Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Lutovi Investments Pty Ltd [1978] FCA 26; (1978) 37 FLR 209 * (Editor'S Note: An Appeal To the High Court Was Allowed, See Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) v Lutovi Investments Pty Ltd (1979),; 53 Ajlr 152) (7 July 1978)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

FEDERAL COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION v. LUTOVI INVESTMENTS PTY. LTD. (1978) 37 FLR

209

*(EDITOR'S NOTE: An appeal to the High Court was

allowed, see Commissioner of Taxation (Cth.) v.

Lutovi Investments Pty. Ltd. (1979), 53 AJLR 152.)

Income Tax - Companies

COURT

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

GENERAL DIVISION

Bowen C.J.(1), Franki(2) and Deane(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Bonus shares - Dividends capitalized from revaluation of assets - Assets not acquired for resale at profit - Whether dividends excluded from assessable income of shareholder - Whether "redeemable shares" - Issue of bonus shares preceded by reduction of capital - Whether issued pursuant to "arrangement" - Whether purpose of enabling payment by reduction of capital - Enabling Stock Exchange listing - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth.), ss. 6, 44 (2) (b) (iii), 44 (2D) (b).

Income Tax - "Arrangement" - "Oral or in writing" - Number of parties required - Requisite circumstances - Whether directors, members or company parties - Purpose of arrangement - "Enabling" payment of money - Income Tax Assessment Act

Companies - Corporate acts - Resolutions of directors and members - Whether constituting directors, members or company parties to arrangement. Section 44 (2) (b) (iii) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 excluded from the assessable income of a shareholder in a company dividends paid to the shareholder "wholly and exclusively out of . . . profits arising from . . . revaluation of assets not acquired for the purpose of resale at a profit . . . if the dividends from such profits are satisfied by the issue of shares (other than redeemable shares) of the company declaring the dividend".

Section 6 defined a dividend to include "any amount credited by a company to any of its shareholders as shareholders".

Section 44 (2D) (b) deemed a share to be a redeemable share "if . . . the share was issued in pursuance of, or as part of, an agreement or arrangement, whether oral or in writing . . . that had the purpose, or purposes that included the purpose, of enabling the company, by means of . . . a reduction in the paid-up value of that share or of any other share in the company, to pay, transfer or apply to . . . the person to whom the share was issued or any other person . . . any money or other property other than shares in the company".

The taxpayer was a shareholder in a listed public company (the company). The company revalued assets (not acquired for the purposes of resale at a profit) and a large sum stood to the credit of its assets revaluation reserve.

A plan was formulated by the executive director and approved and adopted by resolution of the board of directors. It was then carried into effect in accordance with resolutions at successive meetings of the members to the effect, inter alia: (i) that the company's ten-cent fully paid issued shares be consolidated into fifty-cent shares and the issued capital of the company be reduced by the return to shareholders of forty-nine cents per share; (ii) following the court's approval of the reduction and despatch of cheques in return of capital - that (in addition to an amount credited to the share premium reserve, not relevant in these proceedings) $816,000, being part of the amount credited to the assets revaluation reserve, be capitalized and applied in paying up in full 81,600,000 shares of one cent each for distribution on a twenty-four for one basis between existing members and that, following allotment, there should be a consolidation of one-cent shares into fifty-cent shares.

The company had sufficient surplus funds to satisfy the reduction of capital. For it to retain stock exchange quotation of its shares their par value had to exceed ten cents. Ultimately, each member received forty-nine cents in cash and held (following the bonus distribution) one fifty-cent share in respect of every five ten-cent shares originally held.

The Commissioner of Taxation included in the assessable income of the taxpayer for the year ended 30th June, 1972, the value of those bonus shares issued to it which had been capitalized from the assets revaluation reserve. He contended that by force of s. 44 (2D) (b) those shares were redeemable shares and so not excluded from the taxpayer's assessable income under s. 44 (2) (b) (iii).

The taxpayer's objection was upheld in the Supreme Court and the commissioner appealed.

Held: (1) Bowen C.J. dissenting - The appeal should be dismissed. The bonus shares capitalized from the assets revaluation reserve were not to be deemed pursuant to s. 44 (2D) (b) to be redeemable shares. Thus the application of s. 44 (2) (b) (iii) was not excluded and it operated to exclude from the assessable income of the taxpayer the dividends received in the form of those bonus shares.

(2) Bowen C.J. dissenting - Any arrangement pursuant to which the relevant bonus shares were issued did not in the circumstances have any purpose of enabling the company by means of a reduction in the paid up value in any share to pay money to any person because: (a) for the arrangement to have the requisite purpose, it had to be established that the corporate capacity, power or ability of the company to undertake the act of returning capital would in some way be supplemented or enhanced by reason of the arrangement being put into effect; (b) the company had, independently of any arrangement, the means and ability to return the capital that was returned; (c) any arrangement did not in any relevant way supplement or enhance the company's capacity, power or ability to return that capital, the steps taken to meet the need to retain stock exchange quotation not being relevant in this regard.

Per Bowen C.J. contra - All that is required is that the arrangement should involve a purpose of facilitating or assisting the return of capital.

(3) Per curiam - The words "whether oral or in writing" in s. 44 (2D) (b) should not be construed restrictively so as to necessitate the existence of an agreement or arrangement which is solely oral or in writing.

(4) Per Bowen C.J. and Deane J., Franki J. not deciding - An arrangement within the meaning of s. 44 (2D) (b) must be between two or more persons.

(5) Per Bowen C.J. and Franki J., Deane J. contra - What was planned and carried into effect constituted an arrangement within the meaning of s. 44 (2D) (b) notwithstanding that it consisted of or was evidenced by no more than the exercise of ordinary corporate functions, such as:

Per Bowen C.J. - The resolution of the directors whereby they assented to the plan and put it into effect.

Per Franki J. - The notices decided upon by the directors calling the two meetings and the passing of the resolutions by the members at the meetings.

(6) Per Bowen C.J. and Franki J. - The relevant bonus shares were issued in pursuance of or as part of an arrangement.

Characteristics of an "agreement" and "arrangement" considered and discussed.

DECISION

July 7.

The following written judgments were delivered.

BOWEN C.J. This is an appeal from a judgment of the Supreme Court of New
South Wales upholding an objection by the respondent to an assessment of tax for the income year ending 30th June, 1972. The respondent was a shareholder in a company I.C.D. Ltd. In his assessment, the commissioner included in the assessable income of the respondent the sum of $25,915 being the value of certain bonus shares issued to the respondent by I.C.D. Ltd. The bonus shares fell into two classes: first, bonus shares capitalized from a share premium reserve and, secondly, bonus shares capitalized from an assets revaluation reserve. The commissioner did not argue in the Supreme Court that the assessment in relation to the bonus shares capitalized from the share premium reserve could be maintained, but did seek to maintain the assessment in respect of the sum of $12,693.36, being the value of the bonus shares capitalized from the assets revaluation reserve. (at p211)

2. The circumstances leading up to the issue of the bonus shares by I.C.D. Ltd. are set forth in the judgment of Franki J. and I will not recite them here. (at p211)

3. The assessable income of a shareholder in a company, where the shareholder is a resident of Australia, ordinarily includes dividends paid to him by the company out of profits (Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s. 44 (1) (a)). In an appropriate case the paid up value of bonus shares themselves is included in the meaning of "dividend" in par. (c) of the definition in s. 6 and thus is included in assessable income. The commissioner here relies upon par. (b) and it is not disputed that that is the appropriate paragraph in the present case (cf. Curran v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1974] HCA 46; (1974) 131 CLR 409 ). (at p212)

4. The taxpayer relies on s. 44 (2) (b) (iii) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1972, for his contention that the dividend does not form part of his assessable income. It is conceded that the taxpayer is entitled to the benefit of this provision if the bonus shares are not redeemable shares within the meaning of that provision. That meaning is to be found in s. 44 (2D) (b). Thus, it is conceded that the assets revaluation reserve represented profits that satisfied the description of profits in s. 44 (2) (b) (iii). (at p212)

5. Section 44 (2D) (b) provides: (at p212)

6. "(2D) For the purposes of sub-paragraph (iii) of paragraph (b) of sub-section (2) of this section, a share issued by a company shall be deemed to be a redeemable share if - . . . (at p212)

7. (b) the share was issued in pursuance of, or as part of, an agreement or arrangement, whether oral or in writing and whether entered into before or after the commencement of this sub-section, that had the purpose, or purposes that included the purpose, of enabling the company, by means of the redemption, purchase or cancellation, or of a reduction in the paid-up value, of that share or of any other share in the company, to pay, transfer or apply to, on behalf of or at the direction of the person to whom the share was issued or any other person, whether upon the exercise of an option by the company or by any other person or not, any money or other property other than shares in the company." (at p212)

8. The case to which this provision is obviously directed is one where a company wishes to make a cash distribution to shareholders from profits which satisfy the description of profits in s. 44 (2) (b) (iii) and seeks to do so by issuing bonus shares which are later redeemed, or are the subject of or included within a reduction of capital. That is not the present case. However, the provision in deeming a share to be redeemable, does so to limit the operation of s. 44 (2) (b) (iii), and does not denote the share as one having all the characteristics of a redeemable share. The provision talks of the redemption, purchase or cancellation or reduction in the paid-up value of "any other share" in the company. Thus if the words of the provision are satisfied, it is no answer to its application that the share which is deemed redeemable has not itself been redeemed, purchased, cancelled or reduced in value. (at p213)

9. In the present case, the reduction of capital was resolved upon and the moneys were distributed pursuant to the resolution, before the bonus shares issued. In the Supreme Court it was held that this temporal distinction was important as the bonus issue could not be said to have enabled I.C.D. Ltd. by means of a reduction in the paid-up value of the pre-existing ordinary shares, to pay money to the shareholders. The issue of the bonus shares did not enable the company to achieve the requisite ends. (at p213)

10. The decision in this case turns on the proper construction of s. 44 (2D) (b). The particular questions which arise are: 1. whether there is an agreement or arrangement pursuant to which or as part of which the bonus shares were issued; 2. whether such agreement or arrangement is or is required to be solely oral or in writing; and 3. whether such agreement or arrangement had a purpose of enabling the company by means of the reduction in capital to distribute money to the shareholders. (at p213)

11. It was not argued that for there to be a reduction in the paid-up value of "any other share" within s. 44 (2D) (b), the other share must exist and stand at its unreduced value at the time the demed redeemable share is issued. That is, there is no argument that the reduction in capital was not a reduction in the paid-up value of "any other share" within the meaning of s. 44 (2D) (b). (at p213)

12. In the Supreme Court proceedings, in reply to a request for particulars of the agreement or arrangement alleged to have been entered into, the solicitor for the commissioner stated that the commissioner would be alleging the existence of an arrangement between various parties enumerated in the alternative. (at p213)

13. The word "arrangement" has been the subject of much discussion in recent times; most importantly in the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Newton (1957) 96 CLR 578 and in the Privy Council in Newton v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1958) 98 CLR 1 . The meaning of "arrangement" was there discussed in its context in s. 260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 which refers to "contract, agreement or arrangement made or entered into orally or in writing . . .". In the Privy Council it was said: "The word 'arrangement' is apt to describe something less than a binding contract or agreement, something in the nature of an understanding between two or more persons - a plan arranged between them which may not be enforceable at law" (1958) 98 CLR, at p 7 . (at p213)

14. The characteristics of "an arrangement" which are emphasized are its lack of formality and precision and its consensual nature. A similar finding as to the meaning of arrangement in s. 80B (5) was made by Mahoney J. in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. K. Porter & Co. Pty. Ltd. (1974) 22 FLR 344 , (see K. Porter & Co. Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 52 ALJR 41 for the decision of the High Court on appeal). There his Honour also held that there must be an element of commitment between the parties to the arrangement. (See also Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Students World (Australia) Pty. Ltd. [1978] HCA 1; (1978) 52 ALJR 298 ). (at p214)

15. We were referred by counsel to a number of decisions under the Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1956 (U.K.), s. 6 (1) and (3). Section 6 (1) refers to "any agreement between two or more persons" and by s. 6 (3), "agreement" is said to include any agreement or arrangement whether or not it is or is intended to be enforceable by legal proceedings. Except that the cases referred to emphasize that the common expectations of parties to an arrangement arouse mutual moral obligations, I do not regard these cases as taking the matter very much further. See British Basic Slag Ltd. v. Registrar of Restrictive Trading Agreements (1963) 1 WLR 727 ; Schweppes Ltd. v. Registrar of Restrictive Trading Agreements (1965) 1 WLR 157 ; and Re Association of Crane Makers' Agreement (1965) 1 WLR 917 . (at p214)

16. One matter however that does bear mention is that in the British Basic Slag case, Willmer L.J. said: "I think it is highly significant that Parliament did not see fit to include any definition of 'arrangement'. I infer from this that it was intended that the word should be construed in its ordinary or popular sense" (1963) 1 WLR, at p 739 . (See also Diplock L.J. (1963) 1 WLR, at p 746 .) (at p214)

17. Their Lordships were there concerned with arrangements "between two or more persons", which necessarily supposed that the arrangements in question would be consensual. If one is free to give to the word "arrangement" in s. 44 (2D) (b) its popular or ordinary meaning, it would include an informal agreement or understanding, but would also include the settling or ordering of a plan or scheme unilaterally: see The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary and Tetley v. Taylor per Jervis C.J. [1853] EngR 8; (1853) 1 El & Bl 521, at p 540; [1853] EngR 8; 118 ER 530, at p 537 . The word "arrangement" is frequently used in other contexts in the sense of plan or scheme without any qualification that the arrangement must be consensual: see, for example, Income Tax Act, 1952 (U.K.), s. 403, discussed in Crossland (Inspector of Taxes) v. Hawkins (1961) Ch 537 . See also the power to approve "arrangements" under the Variation of Trusts Act, 1958 (U.K.), adopted in Trustee Act 1958 (Vic.), s. 63A; Trusts Act 1973 (Q.), s. 95 and Trustees Act, 1962 (W.A.), s. 90; Re Steed's Will Trusts Lord Evershed M.R. (1960) Ch 407, at p 419 . (at p214)

18. If such a meaning is found to be appropriate to s. 44 (2D) (b) it is not necessary to construct an arrangement in one of the ways set out in the commissioner's particulars. It would be enough that a scheme was drawn up by the board of directors and carried into effect. (at p215)

19. In Newton's case [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1958) 98 CLR 1 the Privy Council held that in the context of s. 260, "arrangement" meant an arrangement between two or more persons. However, in that section, the word forms one of a class with contract and agreement (Bell v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 34; (1953) 87 CLR 548, at p 573 ), and the consensual element of these can be said to attach to the word "arrangement". That is, even if "arrangement" is not being read ejusdem generis with "contract" and "agreement", its meaning is at least affected by its textual connexion with those terms. (at p215)

20. In s. 44 (2D) (b) the language used is "agreement or arrangement, whether oral or in writing and whether entered into before or after the commencement of this sub-section". The words "agreement or arrangement" do not form as distinct a class as "contract, agreement or arrangement" as found for example, in ss. 80B (5), 260 and 262 at the material date. On the other hand the provision does not use the words "made or entered into" as in ss. 260 and 262, nor does it follow the language of s. 6(4) and say ". . . agreement or arrangement made . . .". (at p215)

21. If the draftsman had intended that the word "arrangement" should include a plan or scheme devised by one person, one would have expected him to have referred to that arrangement as having been "made", or "made or entered into", rather than as having been "entered into" alone. The words used suggest that the draftsman intended it to bear the same meaning as in s. 80B (5) as it then stood and in s. 260, that is, an arrangement between two or more persons. (at p215)

22. It is necessary therefore for the commissioner to show an arrangement between two or more persons, as set out in his particulars. It appears from the evidence that the course of conduct eventually taken was proposed to the board of directors by the executive director at the board meeting of 6th December, 1971, and it was there resolved by the board that the proposal be adopted. Thereafter the Stock Exchange was informed and a letter summarizing the proposal and enclosing notice of an extraordinary general meeting was sent to shareholders. At the extraordinary general meeting which was held on 2nd February, 1972, that part of the proposal which related to the reduction of capital was adopted and the chairman of the board said that a subsequent general meeting would be convened to deal with the remainder. When the approval of the Supreme Court to the reduction in capital had been obtained, the chairman caused notices to be sent to the shareholders of a further extraordinary general meeting to deal with the bonus issue. At that meeting held on 20th March, 1972, resolutions were passed effecting the bonus issue. (at p216)

23. In attempting to identify an arrangement for the purpose of s. 44 (2D) (b), one helpful guide is to determine whether any party who failed to take one of the steps set out above, would thereby have become liable to censure by another party for breach of a moral obligation arising under the arrangement, whether or not the latter party could avail himself of a legal remedy. This, though, need not be an essential feature of an arrangement. There may, for example, be a consensus as to a convenient course of procedure, a subsequent dissent from which might carry no obloquy. It can nevertheless be an arrangement. (at p216)

24. It is necessary to bear certain other matters in mind. If a proposal were devised by one person and another person signified his assent to it, there would, I consider be a sufficient consensus to constitute an arrangement for the purpose of s. 44 (2D)(b). Of course, a supposititious failure of such a party to signify his assent will not involve a dereliction of moral duty, as at that stage there will be no arrangement by which the duty is imported. Further, the arrangement need not require all parties to take steps under the arrangement. Where that is so, it will be no objection to the existence of an arrangement that one cannot point to a case where such a party would be in default of any moral obligation. Finally, steps which a party or other person is required to take under the arrangement may be limited expressly or impliedly to steps which are not in breach of any other duty imposed by law. (at p216)

25. The arrangement alleged by the commissioner in his particulars was: (1) between I.C.D. Ltd. and its shareholders, or (2) between the shareholders, or (3) between the directors, or (4) between the directors and shareholders. (at p216)

26. I see no basis for finding an arrangement between either of the sets of parties in the first two particulars. The decision taken by the board of I.C.D. Ltd. and the decisions taken by its shareholders at the general meetings were decisions of that company. But they were internal matters. No arrangement between I.C.D. Ltd. and any other party was involved. The reduction of capital and the issue of the bonus shares were acts of that company but were done pursuant to a plan which was already in existence. The shareholders met in general meeting and a sufficient number of them voted in favour of the reduction of capital and, later, the issue of the bonus shares. But they were concerned in the carrying into effect of a plan already arrived at. They were not concerned in the formulation of or entry into an arrangement to give effect to the plan. At the most it may be said they by assent, indicated by their votes, took part in implementing the plan. In this sense, it might be argued they became parties to an already formulated arrangement. However, it does not appear to me that there was an arrangement of the kind in question, between I.C.D. Ltd. and its shareholders, or between its shareholders. (at p217)

27. The third particular refers to the directors. It was the directors who played the active role. On 6th December, 1971, they had before them the recommendation of the executive director that a reduction of capital and a bonus issue be effected. They decided to accept this recommendation and to put in train the calling of an extraordinary general meeting to carry it into effect. In voting as they did, the directors were not doing any extraordinary or unusual act. They were carrying out their duties as directors towards I.C.D. Ltd. However, it is quite possible that directors in the exercise of their usual functions may come to an arrangement between themselves. It is true that a director who at first approved of the plan would, if he later changed his mind, be not only free but obliged to voice a dissenting view. But that is consistent with the existence of an arrangement to which he was a party at the time he signified his original approval. An arrangement of the kind in question need not require him to act in breach of any other duty. (at p217)

28. Such an arrangement may appear from discussions taking place between the directors at the time. That is not the case here as the evidence did not show any such discussion. However, I think it may properly be inferred that by resolving as they did, the directors assented to the plan and to put it into effect. This was not an agreement in a contractual sense. It was not an agreement intended to have legal consequences. But it does appear to me that implicit in what they did, was involved the kind of arrangement between them which is referred to in s. 44 (2D) (b). (at p217)

29. A sufficient number of shareholders voted in favour of the proposals, as has been noted, at two stages. The actions of the shareholders were but stages in the carrying out of the plan. They gave their assent and then accepted the money and the shares which were distributed. (at p217)

30. If one should regard the plan made between two or more persons and all the transactions by which it was carried into effect as comprehended in the "arrangement" (see Peate v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1964) 111 CLR 443, at p 469 per Kitto J., and, on appeal[1966] UKPCHCA 1; , (1966) 116 CLR 38 ) then perhaps one should hold there was an arrangement as stated in the fourth particular. It is unnecessary to express a concluded view on this. In my opinion there was an arrangement between the directors. (at p217)

31. The next issue is whether an arrangement to fall within s. 44 (2D) (b) must be solely oral or in writing. Counsel for the respondent argued that the words "whether oral or in writing" qualified "agreement or arrangement" and that the arrangement here in question was not correctly described as either oral or in writing or both, as it could only be inferred by looking also to the conduct of the various parties. However, if that construction is correct it would apply equally to s. 260. Yet in Newton's case, Williams J. said, in a passage cited with approval in the Privy Council: "The purpose of a contract, agreement or arrangement must be what it intended to effect and that intention must be ascertained from its terms. These terms may be oral or written or may have to be inferred from the circumstances" (1957) 96 CLR, at p 630 . (at p218)

32. In my view the words "whether oral or in writing" used in s. 44 (2D) (b) were intended by the draftsman to be words of extension not of limitation, cf. Bank of New South Wales v. Commonwealth per Dixon J. [1948] HCA 7; (1948) 76 CLR 1, at p 383 . Thus it is not necessary to attempt to classify the arrangement as being oral or in writing. (at p218)

33. Then it was argued that any arrangement did not have the purpose of enabling the company to achieve the results which s. 44 (2D) (b) specifies. It was said that the company had share capital available to be returned to the shareholders, and the funds in the assets revaluation reserve were not needed for that purpose. Therefore, the application of those funds to the issue of bonus shares did not enable the company to effect the reduction of capital. Accordingly, an arrangement that comprehended these steps did not have as a purpose the enabling of the company to return money to the shareholder by means of a reduction in the paid-up value of shares. The argument is highlighted by, but not dependent upon, the circumstance that the reduction in capital was effected before the bonus shares were issued. (at p218)

34. It seems that this was the argument that found favour with the learned judge below, who held that the issue of the bonus shares did not enable the company to achieve an object of reducing the paid-up value of the shares already in existence. It must be remembered though, that in s. 44 (2D) (b), the subject of "enabling" is not the issue of the bonus shares but the agreement or arrangement pursuant to which the shares were issued. Strictly the inquiry is not, did the bonus issue enable the company to do something?, but, did the arrangement have a purpose of enabling the company to do something? (at p218)

35. It would seem that a consequence of the argument that the bonus issue did not enable the company to effect a reduction of capital because the reduction was made with moneys other than the moneys capitalized as bonus shares from a fund satisfying s. 44 (2) (b) (iii) would be that s. 44 (2D) (b) could rarely be satisfied unless there were a reduction in the paid-up value of the bonus shares. By narrowing the field of inquiry in such a case to the machinery used to effect the reduction of capital, the argument in essence requires that "enable" be read in the sense of supplying a power which otherwise is not available, rather than merely facilitating the return. (at p219)

36. I find myself unable to accept this argument. First, s. 44 (2D) (b) talks of the reduction in the paid-up value of "any other share" that is, a share other than the issued bonus share. That supposes that the bonus share may not be included in a reduction of capital, so that the moneys returned to the shareholders would then have a source other than in the moneys capitalized as bonus shares from a fund satisfying s. 44 (2) (b) (iii). That means that s. 44 (2D) (b) contemplates that the company would have the power and means to return money to the shareholders quite apart from the funds used in the bonus issue, yet still be capable of being "enabled" to do so by the arrangement pursuant to which the bonus shares were issued. (at p219)

37. Secondly, when it is borne in mind that the enabling of the company must be a purpose of the arrangement, and it is not the bare fact of the issue of the bonus share that is to enable the company to make the return, it is easier to see that "enable" should be read to include assisting or facilitating the return in a broad sense (cf. Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Students World (Australia) Pty. Ltd. per Mason J. dissenting [1978] HCA 1; (1978) 52 ALJR 298 ). If the reason the arrangement was entered into was of general or commercial importance, rather than one directed to the machinery by which the return of capital was made or to the conditions necessary for the return to be made, that reason should nevertheless be taken into account in determining whether the arrangement enabled the company to make the return. (at p219)

38. The question then is whether it was a purpose of the arrangement pursuant to which the bonus shares were issued that the arrangement which comprehended that bonus issue would assist the company to make the payment to shareholders by means of the reduction of capital which took place. To answer that inquiry, I turn to examine the share structure of I.C.D. Ltd. at the conclusion of the first extraordinary general meeting of 2nd February, 1972. At that time, the approval of the shareholders had been given to the reduction of the issued ordinary share capital of the company to 3,400,000 shares of one cent each. After the approval of the Supreme Court of New South Wales to the reduction had been obtained, the register of members was closed to determine the entitlement of shareholders to the return of capital. That was done at 5 p.m. on Friday 17th March, 1972, and the cheques in prepayment of capital were posted on that day. (at p219)

39. It was an agreed fact between the parties that: "As a matter of policy, at the relevant time the Sydney Stock Exchange would have required the one-cent shares of a listed company occurring as a result of a reduction in capital to be consolidated into shares of a minimum par value of ten cents or possibly twenty cents." (at p220)

40. On Monday 20th March, 1972, trading in shares of I.C.D. Ltd. on the Sydney Stock Exchange was suspended. On that day the resolutions effecting the bonus issue were carried, and the shares of I.C.D. Ltd. were again of par value of fifty cents. Trading in those shares re-commenced on Tuesday 21st March, 1972. (at p220)

41. I consider that by means of the bonus issue, the company was assisted in retaining its position as a listed company on the Sydney Stock Exchange. It may be that the same result could have been reached had it consolidated its one-cent shares into shares of a par value of ten cents or twenty cents. However, there is a large measure of speculation in that assumption. There may have been difficulties caused by shareholdings which were not in multiples of ten or twenty. Certainly the retaining of a share structure not materially different from that obtaining before the reduction in capital obviated an inquiry into such difficulties. In any event, as counsel for the commissioner submitted, the fact that I.C.D. Ltd. might have been able to achieve a similar position with regard, for example, to the trading in its shares, to that in which it stood on 21st March, 1972, without the bonus issue, does not mean that the bonus issue did not enable the company to achieve that position. That other modes of proceeding might or might not have been adopted does not, in this case, affect the significance which should be attached to the mode of procedure in fact adopted. (at p220)

42. In my view, because the bonus issue enabled the company to continue its relationship with the commercial world unchanged, it can properly be said that the arrangement which included the bonus issue assisted the company by means of the reduction to make the payment to its shareholders. Were it not for the arrangement that after the reduction in capital had taken place there would be a bonus issue that would for all material purposes restore the share structure of I.C.D. Ltd. to its original state, the company might have experienced difficulties in its external dealings. That would have rendered it more difficult in a commercial sense to have made the reduction in capital. I conclude therefore that it was a purpose of the arrangement that by its implementation, the company would be enabled to return money by means of the reduction to its shareholders. (at p220)

43. In the result I conclude that the elements of s. 44 (2D) (b) have been made out. The taxpayer can thus not avail itself of s. 44 (2) (b) (iii). I would allow the appeal with costs. (at p220)

29. It is necessary to consider first whether there was any agreement or arrangement within the meaning of s. 44 (2D) (b). The words "agreement or arrangement" or somewhat similar words appear in various other Acts, and for example in s. 260 of the Act. These words have been considered by the Privy Council in Newton v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1958) 98 CLR 1 in which the following passages appear: "Their Lordships are of opinion that the word 'arrangement' is apt to describe something less than a binding contract or agreement, something in the nature of an understanding between two or more persons - a plan arranged between them which may not be enforceable at law. But it must in this section comprehend, not only the initial plan, but also all the transactions by which it is carried into effect - all the transactions, that is, which have the effect of avoiding taxation, be they conveyances, transfers or anything else" (1958) 98 CLR, at pp 7-8 . (at p226)

30. "Applying these principles to the present case, the first question is - Was there an arrangement? The answer is "Yes'. The whole complicated series of transactions must have been the result of a concerted plan; and the nature of the plan is to be ascertained by the overt acts done in pursuance of it" (1958) 98 CLR, at p 9 (at p226)

31. In the United Kingdom Restrictive Trade Practices Act, 1956, s. 6, the types of agreement to which that Act applied are set out and agreement is defined broadly to include any arrangement between two or more parties whether or not it is intended to be enforceable by legal proceedings. In British Basic Slag Ltd. v. Registrar of Restrictive Trading Agreements Lord Diplock said: (at p226)

32. "I think that I am only expressing the same concept in slightly different terms if I say without attempting an exhaustive definition, for there are many ways in which arrangements may be made, that it is sufficient to constitute an arrangement between A and B, if (1) A makes a representation as to his future conduct with the expectation and intention that such conduct on his part will operate as an inducement to B to act in a particular way, (2) such representation is communicated to B, who has knowledge that A so expected and intended, and (3) such representation or A's conduct in fulfilment of it operates as an inducement, whether among other inducements or not, to B to act in that particular way" (1963) 1 WLR, at p 747 (at p226)

33. It is necessary to consider whether the words of s. 44 (2D) (b) of the Act ". . . whether oral or in writing and whether entered into before or after the commencement of this sub-section . . ." restrict an agreement or arrangement to one which is wholly oral or in writing. In my opinion, they do not. I consider that in using the phrase the legislature intended to embrace all forms of agreement or arrangement and that the words are words of extension and not of constriction. (at p227)

34. Whatever construction is placed on the words "arrangement or agreement" in the section, I consider that the relevant shares were issued in pursuance of or as part of an agreement or arrangement. If one has to specify the precise agreement or arrangement, and indeed there may have been more than one, I consider that the notices decided upon by the directors calling the two meetings and the passing of the resolutions by the appropriate majority of shareholders at those meetings constituted an arrangement between the directors and shareholders within the meaning of the section. (at p227)

35. I pass now to consider the purpose of the arrangement. (at p227)

36. Section 44 (2D) (b) of the Act deals with the position of a share issued by a company and provides that that share shall be deemed to be redeemable if: (i) the share was issued in pursuance of, or as part of, an agreement or arrangement, whether oral or in writing and whether entered into before or after the commencement of the subsection, (ii) the agreement or arrangement had the purpose, or purposes that included the purpose, of enabling the company to pay . . . any money or other property other than shares in the company, (iii) the method of payment . . . provided is by means of the redemption, purchase or cancellation or a reduction in paid-up value, of that share or any other share in the company, and (iv) the person concerned with the receipt of the money is "the person to whom the share was issued" or "any other person". (at p227)

37. In relation to the facts in the case before us the essential features of the section for consideration are whether: (i) the issue of the shares was in pursuance of, or as part of, an arrangement, within s. 44 (2D) (b), and (ii) the purpose or purposes of the arrangement was, or included, the purpose of enabling the company, to pay money by means of a reduction of capital. (at p227)

38. As I have said I am satisfied that the shares were issued in pursuance of a relevant arrangement. (at p227)

39. It is next necessary to consider whether the arrangement had the purpose, or purposes that included the purpose, of enabling the company, by a reduction in the paid-up value of any share in the company, to pay money to any person. (at p227)

40. At all relevant times the company had the power under its articles of association, the surplus assets and it was in a position, subject to confirmation by the Supreme Court of New South Wales, to repay that portion of its capital which it repaid pursuant to the resolution of 2nd February, 1972. The arrangement, in my opinion, did not have the purpose or include a purpose of enabling the company to pay any money. The arrangement neither added nor was calculated to add to the powers of the company or to its capacity or ability to pay money by means of a reduction of capital to any person and so it did not enable it to so pay money to any person. (at p228)

41. An argument was presented for the commissioner that some difficulty may have been experienced by the company in retaining the listing of its shares on the Stock Exchange when the shares were reduced to a par value of one cent unless the bonus issue had been made. Whether that be so or not, I consider that ease of retaining stock exchange listing is not relevant to a consideration of the question of whether the arrangement had a purpose of enabling the company to pay money to any person. (at p228)

42. I would dismiss the appeal with costs. (at p228)

DEANE J. The issue between the appellant commissioner and the respondent taxpayer which remains for determination in this appeal is whether the amount of $12,693.36 representing the taxpayer's entitlement in respect of a dividend declared by I.C.D. Ltd. ("the company") from the amount standing to the credit of its assets revaluation reserve and satisfied by the issue of 1,269,336 ordinary fully paid shares of one cent in the company's capital constituted assessable income of the taxpayer of the tax year ended 30th June, 1972. That issue will be resolved conformably with the answer to the question whether those shares ("the bonus shares") were, within the meaning of s. 44 (2D) (b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1971 ("the Act") "issued in pursuance of, or as part of, an agreement or arrangement . . . that had . . . purposes that included the purpose, of enabling the company, by means of . . . a reduction in the paidup value" of any other shares in the company to pay money to the person to whom the bonus shares were issued or any other person. If they were, the commissioner is entitled to succeed in the appeal. If they were not, he should fail. (at p228)

2. There is no real dispute between the parties as to the primary facts and circumstances in the context of which that question falls to be answered. Those facts and circumstances and the relevant legislative provisions appear from the judgments of the Chief Judge and of Franki J. which I have had the benefit of reading. It is unnecessary that I repeat them. I agree, for the reasons which he gives, with the Chief Judge's conclusion that the "arrangement" to which s. 44 (2D) (b) refers, like that mentioned in s. 260 of the Act, is an arrangement between two or more persons (see, for example, Hancock v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1961] HCA 90; (1961) 108 CLR 258, at p 282 ). (at p228)

3. Section 44 (2D) (b) assumes that a relevant arrangement will be "entered into" by the parties to it. It does not follow that it is necessary that there be a formal acceptance of, or committal to, such an arrangement by such parties. A plan can be propounded without prior arrangement and be constituted as an arrangement by acceptance of or adherence to it implicit in the performance of steps which it encompasses or the acceptance of benefits which result from its implementation. The arrangement to which s. 44 (2D) (b) refers is, however, a real one and not one which the Act imputes regardless of the reality. The relevant question, for the purposes of s. 44 (2D) (b), is whether there was, in fact, a relevant arrangement not whether the facts are such that such an arrangement should be imputed to parties even though, in truth, no such arrangement may have existed between them. (at p229)

4. The case which was put on behalf of the commissioner was not lacking in variety. The relevant "arrangement" for the purposes of s. 44 (2D) (b) was said to be an arrangement between: (a) the company and its shareholders, or (b) between the shareholders (without the company), or (c) between the directors, or (d) between the directors and shareholders. The arrangement was said to have the requisite purpose in that it had a purpose: (i) of persuading the shareholders to support the resolution for the reduction of capital, (ii) of overcoming commercial disadvantages which would otherwise have been consequent upon such a reduction of capital and (iii) of facilitating the carrying into effect of what was called "a cohesive plan" involving, among other things, the reduction of capital. (at p229)

5. A company is a figment of the law. It cannot perform a positive act either of its own initiative or independently of the action of those who constitute its corporate organs or act as its agents on its behalf. The two primary corporate organs or instruments are the shareholders in general meeting and the directors in meeting. Those corporate organs do not make decisions or act for the company: they make decisions and act as the company (see, e.g., Tesco Supermarkets Ltd. v. Nattrass [1971] UKHL 1; (1972) AC 153, at p 170 ). Ordinarily, a decision made or corporate action effected through such corporate instruments will be made or effected by a resolution. Where such a resolution is carried with the support of more than one director or shareholder, it will ordinarily be possible to find a consensus among at least some directors or at least some shareholders supporting the course of corporate activity which the resolution authorizes or effects. The existence of such an underlying consensus does not of itself, however, indicate that there has been "an agreement or arrangement" entered into by either individual directors or individual shareholders. On its own, it means no more and no less than that some directors or some shareholders have, in the performance of their functions as such, voted the same way on a particular resolution (see, generally, O'Brien v. Dawson [1941] NSWStRp 39; (1941) 41 SR (NSW) 295, at pp 307-308 , and, on appeal[1942] HCA 8; , (1942) 66 CLR 18, at pp 32, 34 , and G. Scammell & Nephew Ltd. v. Hurley (1929) 1 KB 419, at pp 443, 449 ). (at p230)

6. Thus, in the present matter, there was clearly a meeting of minds or a consensus among at least some of the directors of the company on the executive director's proposal that a number of steps be taken by or on behalf of the company. Those steps included the consolidation of existing issued ten-cent shares into fifty-cent shares, the repayment of money by means of the reduction of capital and the issue of the bonus shares. The consensus between the directors was evidenced, and indeed was constituted, by the passage of the resolution that the recommendations of the executive director be accepted and that an extraordinary general meeting of the company be convened. The proposals which the executive director had recommended could only be effected by the company acting through its shareholders in general meeting. It was within the scope of the directors' powers to determine whether an extraordinary general meeting of the company should be convened to consider and, if thought fit, give effect to those proposals. It was within the authority of the directors, in deciding to convene the meeting, to give consideration to the content of the proposals. It has not been submitted on behalf of the commissioner (and there is nothing in the evidence to suggest) that, at any relevant time or in relation to any relevant act, either the executive director or any of the other directors was acting otherwise than in his capacity as such and in accordance with the fiduciary duties of the office in the company which he held. (at p230)

7. The directors of a company are not, in the performance of their functions as such, properly concerned to pursue their own independent purposes or interests. It is their fiduciary duty to decide where the company's interests lie and how the company's interests are to be served in relation to any matter which arises for their consideration and to pursue those interests as they see them. When there is a conflict of interest and a director is not precluded from acting, he is obliged to subordinate his personal interests to those of the company. It is no part of the function of the directors of a company to reach "an agreement or arrangement" among themselves. The function of the directors is, as regards the matters entrusted to them, to determine and give effect to what is in the interests of the company as a whole. (at p230)

8. The resolution which the directors carried did not involve "an agreement or arrangement" between all or any of them that the issue of the shares or the return of the capital be effected. The decision on those matters was beyond their powers. The resolution which they passed involved a decision by them that those matters be brought before the appropriate organ of the company for consideration. The directors' resolution embodying their decision was, if looked at from outside the company, within internal management. It none the less constituted both corporate decision and corporate act. The powers which the directors exercise under the articles of a company are corporate powers. The resolution calling the extraordinary general meeting to consider the proposals was an act of the company authorizing both the action by the company's officers and the expenditure of the company's money necessary to implement it. In my view, neither the resolution itself nor the consensus underlying it constituted, for the purposes of s. 44 (2D) (b) of the Act, "an agreement or arrangement" entered into by or made between some or all of the directors or entered into by or made between some or all of the directors and the company (see O'Brien v. Dawson [1941] NSWStRp 39; (1941) 41 SR (NSW) 295 and Williams v. Hursey [1959] HCA 51; (1959) 103 CLR 30, at pp 128-129 ). (at p231)

9. Nor, in the view I take, did any of the resolutions which were passed at the two extraordinary general meetings of shareholders involve, in itself, any such agreement or arrangement between the shareholders or such of them who supported it or between shareholders and the company. The shareholders who voted for those resolutions were not under a fiduciary duty of the type which applied to the directors. They were, however, acting as part of a corporate instrument and, in the absence of any suggestion that they were motivated by impermissible collateral purposes, they were acting to achieve a corporate purpose (Ngurli Ltd. v. McCann [1953] HCA 39; (1953) 90 CLR 425, at p 439 ). The shareholders who voted to support the resolutions no doubt favoured the proposals which the resolutions embodied. It cannot however, in my view, properly be said that by reason only of their so voting they entered into or made an agreement or arrangement or that, as a consequence of their so voting, an agreement or arrangement was entered into or made. The resolutions which they passed were corporate acts performed by the company through the appropriate corporate organ. Their support of the resolution as being, in the relevant sense, in the interests of the company as a whole did not, without more, involve "an agreement or arrangement" being entered into or made for the purposes of s. 44 (2D) (b). (at p231)

10. The acceptance by shareholders of the moneys payable to them on the reduction of capital and of the shares issued to them upon the bonus issue fall into a different category. The payments were made and the shares were issued to shareholders in their personal capacity and not in their capacity as constituting a corporate organ. The payments of money and the issue of the bonus shares had, in one sense, been the object of the overall plan which had initially been propounded by the executive director. They were the result of the implementation of that plan and, indeed, in themselves were its culmination. It can be argued that, by acceptance of the payments and by acquiescence in their names being added to the list of shareholders in respect of the shares the subject of the bonus issue, the shareholders in question became parties to an arrangement embodying the whole overall plan. The conclusion which I have reached is that the evidence simply does not warrant a finding that such acceptance or acquiescence involved such shareholders in being parties to any such overall arrangement between the company and themselves. (at p232)

11. There is, in my view, nothing in the other material before the court which, taken either with or in the context of the resolutions which were passed, the payments which were made and the shares which were issued, either establishes or justifies the inference that any relevant agreement or arrangement was entered into or made. The notices which were despatched, the correspondence with the Stock Exchange and the application to the Supreme Court for confirmation of the reduction of capital were the result of the company's officers acting in pursuance of one or more of the resolutions which had been passed. No suggestion has been made that any facts have been concealed or that all of the relevant material is not before the court. In the result, the proper conclusion on the evidence is that there was not, for the purposes of s. 44 (2D) (b), any relevant agreement or arrangement. It follows that the amount of $12,693.36 in question did not constitute assessable income of the taxpayer. (at p232)

12. Even if I had been of the view that there was a relevant agreement or arrangement encompassing the proposals which were carried into effect by the resolutions passed by the two extraordinary general meetings, I would, nonetheless, have been of the view that the provisions of s. 44 (2D) (b) were not applicable. In the view I take, it could not properly be said of any such agreement or arrangement that it had "the purpose, or purposes that included the purpose, of enabling the company, by means of" a reduction of capital to pay money to any person. (at p232)

13. It is relevant to note that there is to be found in s. 44 (2D) (b) a distinction between the relationship between the requisite agreement or arrangement and the issue of the bonus shares on the one hand and the relationship between the requisite agreement or arrangement and the payment of money by way of reduction of capital on the other. It is unnecessary that the relevant agreement or arrangement have either a purpose or effect of enabling the company to issue the bonus shares. All that is required is that the bonus shares be in fact issued pursuant to the agreement or arrangement. On the other hand, it is not sufficient that there be a payment of money by means of a reduction of capital in pursuance of the agreement or arrangement or that such a payment was a purpose or the purpose of the agreement or arrangement. The requirements of s. 44 (2D) (b) will not be satisfied unless it can properly be said that the agreement or arrangement had a purpose "of enabling the company" to make such a payment. The question whether an agreement or arrangement can properly be said to have had such a purpose is a question as to the purposes of the arrangement itself rather than a question as to the purposes in the minds of the parties to it (cf. Peate v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1964) 111 CLR, at p 469 ). (at p233)

14. The payment of moneys by means of a reduction of capital was proposed on the basis that the relevant capital was in excess of the needs of the company. Such a payment of money by means of a reduction of capital could only lawfully be made by the company in accordance with the requirement of, and the procedures laid down by, the Companies Act, 1971 (N.S.W.), s. 64, namely, that the proposed reduction of capital was authorized by the articles of association of the company, was effected by special resolution of the shareholders and was confirmed by the court. In other words, the three essential formal prerequisites of any valid payment of money by the company to shareholders by way of reduction of capital were authority in the articles, effective action by the company acting through its shareholders in general meeting, and confirmation by the court. The fourth prerequisite was the practical one that the company had available the money necessary to make the payment to its shareholders and that such money was, in truth, surplus to its requirements. (at p233)

15. The evidence establishes that the company did, in fact, have the cash funds necessary for the payment by way of reduction of capital. There is no suggestion that, in the circumstances which existed, those cash funds were not, in truth, surplus to the requirements of the company. There is no suggestion that the necessary authority was not to be found in the articles of association of the company. It is apparent that, in the circumstances which existed, the application to the Supreme Court for confirmation of the proposed reduction of capital would be a straightforward one. Again, no suggestion to the contrary has been made on behalf of the commissioner. The remaining requirement for the payment of money by means of a reduction of capital was the essential corporate act, namely, the passage of a special resolution. The passage of such a resolution was clearly within the competence of the company acting through the relevant corporate organ, the shareholders in general meeting. In the circumstances which existed, the company was clearly able to make the proposed payment by means of reduction of capital regardless of the purpose or effect of any agreement or arrangement entered into or made by or between any one or more of its directors, its shareholders or itself. (at p233)

16. On the assumption that there was an agreement or arrangement encompassing the overall proposals, it can be seen that it may have operated to persuade some of the shareholders who supported the proposed reduction of capital to vote in favour of the relevant resolution. The issue of the bonus shares and the restructuring of the company's capital after the reduction of capital avoided failure to comply with the listing requirements of the Stock Exchange and resulted in a capital structure which was commercially more satisfactory than the capital structure which would have existed without them. In these respects, it is possible, in a loose sense, to see in the assumed agreement or arrangement a purpose of persuading the shareholders to pass the resolution for the reduction of capital and a purpose of providing an overall context or setting for the proposed reduction of capital and of overcoming commercial difficulties and inconveniences which might have resulted if the reduction of capital had been effected in isolation. Such a purpose is, however, more properly to be seen as a purpose in the minds of some or all of those who propounded, or were parties to, the assumed arrangement rather than a purpose of the arrangement itself. In any event, the perception of such purposes does not justify the conclusion that a purpose of the assumed agreement or arrangement was the enabling of the company to effect the reduction of capital. (at p234)

17. In a case where the validity of a resolution is in question, it may be important to distinguish between the actions of the individual shareholders responsible for its passage and the corporate act which the resolution, if valid, effects. In the present case however such a distinction is not, in my view, a relevant one. The special resolution that the company's capital be reduced was itself an act of the company. It was that resolution which effected the relevant reduction of capital. Its operation was subject to the confirmation of the Supreme Court of New South Wales in that the resolution itself did not take effect until the lodgment of the office copy of the Supreme Court's order (Companies Act, s. 64 (6)). On the lodgment of that copy of the order however, it was the resolution which itself operated to effect the reduction of capital. A consequence of that corporate act was that the company became indebted to its shareholders for the amount which, under the reduction of capital, they were entitled to receive (Archibald Howie Pty. Ltd. v. Commissioner of Stamp Duties (N.S.W.) [1948] HCA 28; (1948) 77 CLR 143, at p 157 ). The persuasion of a necessary majority of those constituting the appropriate corporate organ to perform a corporate act may, in these circumstances, properly be regarded as the persuasion of the company itself in that those constituting the corporate organ can properly be seen as acting, for relevant purposes, as the company. Those constituting the corporate organ will, however, only be able to perform the corporate act if performance of it lies both within corporate power and the authority of the corporate organ. Persuasion of performance cannot, in such circumstances, properly be seen as equivalent to enabling performance. (at p235)

18. The word "enabling" is appropriate to refer to matters which relate to the conferring or enhancement of ability or capacity to perform an act. It may mean "making able" in the strict sense of conferring ability or capacity. It may also, in an appropriate context, connote facilitating actual performance in the sense of conferring or contributing to the means of performance. It is not however appropriate to comprehend matters relating to persuasion or inducement of an act which is already completely within competence and capacity. If enabling were necessary, persuasion or inducement, without more, would of necessity, be sterile. In my view, the word "enabling" in s. 44 (2D) (b) has its ordinary meaning and refers to the conferring or enhancement, in the sense mentioned, of ability or capacity to perform an act. If the word is given that meaning, the assumed agreement or arrangement did not have a purpose of enabling the company to make the relevant payment to its shareholders by means of a reduction of capital. The company had the independent ability and capacity to do that and the agreement or arrangement neither enhanced that capacity nor contributed to the means of performance. The most that the assumed agreement or arrangement did was to bring about a situation in which the company, by special resolution passed by its shareholders in general meeting, performed that corporate act in association with other corporate acts which, by the same procedure, it had the unquestioned capacity or ability to perform. (at p235)

19. It was submitted on behalf of the commissioner that to construe the word "enabling" in the manner in which I would construe it would have the result that s. 44 (2D) (b) was ineffective to achieve the purpose which it was obviously designed to achieve and that the words "or any other share" were devoid of utility. I do not consider that this is so. It is not apparent to me that s. 44 (2D) (b) was designed to cover the situation where a company which has the necessary capital profits available to enable it both to make a payment of money by means of a reduction of capital and independently to declare a dividend from a capital fund such as assets revaluation reserve and satisfy the dividend by the issue of bonus shares, does both of these things. It seems to me that the provisions of s. 44 (2D) (b) may well have been intended to meet the situation where a company, which has one amount of capital profits which it wishes to distribute to its shareholders, declares a capital dividend, applies the amount of that dividend to pay up the capital on a "bonus issue" of shares and then makes the desired payment to its shareholders by means of redemption, purchase or consolidation of, or reduction of the capital paid up on, those shares with the result that the shareholders receive the funds which were applied in satisfaction of the dividend without being liable to pay the tax which they would have been liable to pay if the same funds had been received by way of that dividend. The words "or any other share" would apply, for example, in a situation where the bonus shares are replaced by other shares either issued in exchange for them or resulting from subdivision or consolidation before the reduction of capital or other means utilized for the payment of moneys to the shareholders. In either case, of course, it will not suffice that a company has actually done these things. The provisions of s. 44 (2D) (b) are only applicable when there was, in truth, an arrangement having the designated purpose (as to payment) and effect (as to issue of bonus shares). (at p236)

20. The appeal should be dismissed with costs. (at p236)

ORDER

Orders accordingly.
