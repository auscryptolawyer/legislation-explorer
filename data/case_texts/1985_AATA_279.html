HEARING

CANBERRA

18:10:1985

ORDER

The Tribunal decides to set aside the decision of a delegate of the above-named respondent made on 12 March 1984 and confirmed on reconsideration to issue in respect of the above-named applicant pursuant to s.16(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 a benefit classification certificate specifying the condition "History of superficial spreading melanoma".

DECISION

This application for review seeks review of a decision of a delegate of the above-named respondent to issue, pursuant to s.16(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"), in respect of the applicant a benefit classification certificate ("BCC") specifying the condition "History of superficial spreading melanoma". Unfortunately, the progress of the matter has been marked by considerable bitterness, which is a pity because in the end the Tribunal has come to the view that the resolution of the dispute depends not upon a conclusion as to the relative merits of medical opinions or upon any point of fact, but rather upon a point of statutory interpretation. In other words, there need have been no real dispute save as to the meaning of a statutory phrase, and such a dispute need not have been the cause of the kind of heat that has, regrettably, arisen between the parties.

2. Section 16 of the Act, so far as relevant, is as follows:

"(2) The Commissioner may, for the purposes of

this section, require a person (other than a person

to whom section 184 applies) who proposes to become

or becomes an eligible employee to undergo, within

such period as the Commissioner specifies, such

medical examination or examinations by an approved

(3) A report or reports of the result or results

(4) The Commissioner shall consider the report or

the report or reports, to continue to be an

a benefit classification certificate to that

effect, being a certificate in which the relevant

3. The applicant was born in February 1948. He was from 1976-78 a member of the Commonwealth Public Service, and was accordingly during that period of time a contributor to the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund. From 1978 to 1983 he was a member of the Tasmanian Public Service. On 16 August 1983 he was permanently appointed to the Australian Public Service, with effect from 1 September 1983, involving a move with his family to Canberra and compulsory re-entry into the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund.

4. For the purposes of his appointment to the Australian Public Service, and of his entry to the Superannuation Fund, the applicant was medically examined on 15 August 1983, having completed the then applicable form (Form S6/PSB 9A) in which he was required to answer a number of questions as to his medical history. He made a completely full and frank disclosure of his medical history. What is relevant is that in answer to question 10(a) of Part B, enquiring whether he had ever suffered from "Cancer or any kind of tumour", the applicant answered "Yes", adding "Small melanoma removed three years ago". He also answered affirmatively to question 12(a), which asked whether he had had "any operations, surgical treatment or accidents". In a further space provided for details of "Yes Answers" he wrote: "Small skin cancer removed", and "Removal of melanoma". The comments of the Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO"), a Dr Frost, were: "Dr Carney removed 'freckle'. Has had regular follow-ups". Under this appeared:

"Letter to Mr. John Carney: Superficial spreading

melanoma. Excellent prognosis. Gland fields and

scar clear 12/12 ago. Likely to continue to

normal retiring age. Depth 0.75 mm".

5. In his "Report", appearing on the same form, Dr Frost ticked a box indicating that the applicant met the General Medical Standard. Under the heading "Comments", he first deleted by crossing out, but later reinstated by writing the word "STET", the printed words "After clinical examination, medical tests and specialist advice, as appropriate, I consider the applicant does not meet general medical standard due to the following mental/physical condition(s)". Under these words a space is provided preceded by the words "Mental/physical condition(s) suffered", and in this space Dr Frost wrote:

"History of superficial spreading melanoma ((R)

scapula region)".

In a box provided for insertion of the number applicable to the condition as categorized in the International Classification of Diseases ("ICD") (Manual of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Injuries, and Causes of Death, 1975 Revision, World Health Organization, Geneva, 1977) the CMO inserted the figure "172.6", which refers to "Malignant melanoma of skin - Upper limb including shoulder".

6. On the evidence before him, the CMO would not have been able to find that the applicant met the General Medical Standard, referred to below, which provided, relevantly, in s.19 as follows:

"19. MALIGNANT DISEASES

(1) Cancer, Leukaemia and Hodgkin's disease.

(a) Standard not met where a person suffers

from a malignant disease such as cancer,

leukaemia, Hodgkin's disease etc.

(b) Standard met where a person underwent

treatment with eradication of disease at

least 5 years prior to the examination

provided a specialist reports there is no

evidence of recurrence and the person could

be expected to give effective and

continuous service until maximum age for

retirement.

(2) Basal cell carcinoma or epithelioma, etc.

Standard met where a person who has

suffered with such a condition has been

treated and cured 2 years before the

examination. Other cases should be

referred for specialist advice."

7. It is noteworthy that this standard has since been amended. It now reads:

"19. MALIGNANT DISEASES

(1) (a) Standard not met where a person suffers

from a malignant disease such as cancer,

leukaemia, Hodgkin's disease, melanoma,

etc.

(b) Standard may be met where disease has been

eradicated at least 5 years prior to the

examination provided a specialist reports

that there is no evidence of recurrence and

the person could be expected to give

effective and continuous service until

maximum age for retirement.

(2) Basal cell carcinoma, epithelioma and skin

cancers other than melanomas.

Standard may be met where a person who has

suffered with such a condition has been

treated and cured 2 years before the

examination. Other cases should be

referred for specialist advice."

There is thus now a specificity of reference to melanoma that was not present in August 1983 when the applicant was examined. As Dr B.P. Kean, who is presently Senior Adviser, Executive and International Branch, Department of Health, said in evidence:

"The position before, perhaps, was not as clear as

it should have been ... The heading under the

original section 19, basal cell carcinoma or

epithelioma, etcetera, could have implied that all

skin tumours were lumped into that category."

8. The expression "the General Medical Standard" refers to a set of standards that was brought into being in 1976, as was explained to the Tribunal in evidence by Dr Kean. The Standard is set out as Part IV of the Handbook for Commonwealth Medical Officers. Dr Kean explained that the production of the Standard accompanied the introduction of the new superannuation scheme, as provided for by the Act. The Standard was based on standards applied under the old scheme and was produced after extensive consultation between the Department of Health, the Commissioner for Superannuation, and the Public Service Board. Reliance was placed on Brackenridge, The Medical Aspects of Life Insurance, First edition, 1962, (as then current); current life assurance tables; and clinical consultation with professional colleges and eminent professionals in various specialities. Dr Kean explained that the Standard was used as the basis for medical examination for entry to the Superannuation Fund. The explanation which follows relates to what was required of the examining medical officer by the form S6/PSB 9A that was then in existence. Since the applicant was examined, in fact probably very soon afterwards, a completely new form, S6, was issued and brought into use. In relation to the old form, those conducting the examination were in effect required to assume that the question was whether the person under examination did or did not meet the Standard. There seems to have been in the old form no invitation to the examining CMO to expound the grounds of a favourable prognosis in the particular case except for the provision of the boxes, with supporting annotation, that are discussed in paragraph 6 above. The use of the Standard was purely for the purpose of the Department of Health's advice to the Commissioner. The decision to accept the person to the fund, and on what terms, is that of the Commissioner.

9. The administrative procedures which are undertaken, and which Mr Peters, who appeared for the Commissioner, explained to us, are as follows. When a person applies for entry to the Australian Public Service, the Public Service Board ("PSB") and the respondent Commissioner request the Commonwealth Medical Officer to conduct an examination, the PSB under the Public Service Act 1922 and the respondent under s.16(2) of the Act. "The" Commonwealth Medical Officer delegates this duty to "a" Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO") who carries out the clinical examination, commencing with the questionnaire which the applicant will by then have completed. The CMO recorded the results of his examination and then had to address his attention only to the question whether the applicant met the General Medical Standard. If the applicant did not, the alternatives were to find that he:

(a) did not meet the General Medical Standard but was fit for

permanent appointment;

(b) met the "standard for appointment" but "should be

re-examined in ... months to determine whether General

Medical Standard is met";

(c) did not meet "the fitness standard for appointment" and a

recommendation "should be deferred for ... months pending

re-examination"; or

(d) was medically unfit for permanent appointment.

This was all set out in the block headed "Commonwealth Medical Officer's Report". This was followed by a block headed "Commonwealth Medical Officer's Comments" and was to be signed by the examining CMO: See paragraphs 4 and 5 above for Dr Frost's completion of the block in the present case.

10. The final block to be completed was that headed "Decision of Delegate of the PSB and Recommendation to Commissioner for Superannuation". This had two separate sections, each requiring one of two boxes to be ticked. They were as follows:

"Appointment

( ) I determine that the health and fitness of

the applicant justifies his/her acceptance

for permanent appointment under the Public

Service Act.

( ) I consider that the health and fitness of

the applicant does not justify his/her

acceptance for permanent appointment under

the Public Service Act.

Superannuation

( ) I consider that the applicant meets general

medical standard.

( ) I consider that the applicant does not meet

general medical standard for the reasons

given in the C.M.O.'s comments."

We were told that it was in fact completed by a Senior Medical Officer ("SMO") mentioned, and that it was to the SMO that the examining CMO reports.

11. The completion of this last section explains the dual function of the examination. But neither an examining CMO, nor an SMO, nor "the" Commonwealth Medical Officer determined anything for the purposes of s.16(4) of the Act. For a similar position under other legislation see Newman v Mulock & Anor (1977) 2 NSWLR 12. The only question for them was whether the applicant met the General Medical Standard. And as previously seen, the General Medical Standard has no statutory basis. It is, and can only be, a guide, and no doubt in the broad sweep of cases it is a very useful guide. The question however for the Commissioner or his delegate under s.16(4) is whether he should, after considering the report or reports of the medical examination, be of "the opinion that (the applicant) is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition ... referred to in the report ..., to continue to be an eligible employee until (the applicant) attains his maximum retiring age". The new form S6B, referred to above, poses a different question for the CMO. He is required to answer "Yes" or "No" to the following question:

"As a result of your examination and consideration

of the applicant's answers and other material

available to you do you consider (having regard to

the General Medical Standard as set down in Part IV

of the Handbook for Commonwealth Medical Officers)

that the applicant is NOT LIKELY TO CONTINUE to be

an eligible employee until attaining the relevant

maximum retiring age by reason of, or for a reason

connected with, any physical or mental condition(s)

referred to in this form or the Medical Report Form

S6A?"

The question of course remains one for the Commissioner or his delegate to answer.

12. The examining CMO in the present case had before him, as has been seen, a report from Dr Carney, a surgeon specialising in plastic and reconstructive surgery, dated 5 August 1983 which reads as follows:

"I first saw Mr. Bewley on 16th January, 1981 with

a pigmented lesion on his right scapula.

Excisional biopsy confirmed a diagnosis of

superficial spreading melanoma. The depth of

invasion is well within the limit of .75mm.

Because of this his prognosis is excellent. I

last saw him approximately one year ago and I noted

that his gland fields and scar were clear.

I am certain that he will be able to fulfil service

requirements and is likely to continue until the

normal retiring age."

The letter from Dr Frost, the examining CMO, requesting this report, had asked for "details of (the applicant's) case including if possible your opinion as to whether he can be expected to give at least three years satisfactory service, and if so is this likely to continue until the normal retiring age".

13. Having considered the reports of the examining CMO and of Dr Carney, and the information provided in the Medical Report Form, including, presumably, the conclusion of the SMO, the delegate formed the opinion that the applicant was "not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with the undermentioned physical or mental condition(s), to continue to be an eligible employee until he/she attains his/her maximum retiring age". The "physical/mental condition" specified was "History of Superficial Spreading Melanoma".

14. It is to be noted that this certificate did not issue until 29 March 1984, so that the applicant had been in the Australian Public Service for seven months before he was informed of the issue of the BCC, a fact which was undoubtedly, as far as he was concerned, a bombshell. He had made full disclosure, nothing, he said, had happened at the medical examination to arouse any suspicion that there was a problem, and his move to the Australian Public Service and to Canberra was complete. His bridges were all burnt. The situation was undoubtedly a complex one, but where a person is moving from, and losing the protection of, another Superannuation Scheme, it seems to us that different administrative arrangements are needed, and that they do not exclusively concern the office of the Commissioner for Superannuation. Where the procedures for admission to the Public Service and for acceptance into the Superannuation Fund overlap, there needs to be provision for warning bells to ring in a case like the present. It appears to us to be quite wrong for a person not to know for some months after other options are closed that his move to the Australian Public Service could affect his pension rights.

15. Although this Tribunal reviews not the reasons for the making of the decision under review but the decision itself, it is to be noted that having regard to Dr Carney's opinion as expressed in the letter of 5 August set out above, it is clear enough that the delegate's opinion placed more weight on the failure of the applicant to meet the General Medical Standard than it did on Dr Carney's opinion that the applicant was "likely to continue until normal retiring age". It may also be noted that it would appear that all the material upon which the BCC was issued, and also the decision that the applicant did not meet the General Medical Standard, were both known by 16 August 1983, that is to say before the applicant had taken up his appointment in Canberra.

16. The applicant sought review of the decision to issue the BCC, and after some exchange of correspondence reconsideration under s.154 of the Act commenced. Further medical reports were obtained as follows:

(ii) Dr King 8 November 1984

This report for the most part repeated Dr Carney's

17. On the basis of the material previously referred to, and of the reports of Dr Carney and Dr King referred to in paragraph 16 above, the Commissioner confirmed the delegate's decision to issue the BCC.

18. We pause at this point to observe that in neither the reasons for the original decision of the delegate (T15 1-4) nor in the reasons for the decision taken on reconsideration is there any discussion of what is requisite for the formation of the opinion contemplated by s.16(4). It appears to have been assumed that failure to meet the General Medical Standard, or perhaps the presence of a significant risk of recurrence of the relevant condition (although that is not spelt out), is enough to ground the formation of the opinion. To be fair, this assumption was no doubt grounded upon the decision of the Tribunal in Re Linden and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN 659. The problem is indeed a difficult one, but before attempting to resolve it we shall complete our reference to the evidence.

20. It had been agreed prior to the hearing that all of the medical reports be tendered without cross-examination. At the hearing it transpired that the respondent, in response to the submission of Dr Farnsworth's Report, wished to tender a report from a pathologist, and with some misgiving the Tribunal granted leave for this to be lodged within a short time after the conclusion of the hearing, giving the applicant an option to respond thereto if he so desired. The report, from a Dr Grahame, proved to be a helpful one to the extent that it dealt with medical matters, but a page attached to it entered into argumentative matter related to the meaning of the provisions of s.16(4) and to the consequences Dr Grahame perceived would follow were it not construed in the manner advanced by her. Dr Grahame is not qualified to express an opinion on these matters, and the Tribunal rejected the tender of this page of the report and returned it to the respondent. A lengthy submission was then made in writing as to why its tender should have been accepted but we have decided to maintain our rejection of the tender. To be fair to Dr Grahame, her instructions may be seen to have provoked her intrusion into matters of law although the questions asked of her were in fact perfectly proper. She had been asked to express her opinion on two bases, namely:

"(i) that 'likely' means 'more probable than not';

(ii) that 'likely' means 'such as might well happen -

more than a bare possibility'."

Which of these two bases is correct in terms of the interpretation of s.16(4) is at the heart of this case, but the medical opinion expressed in Dr Grahame's report is expressed with conspicuous clarity and enables an accurate judgment to be made of her opinion on either basis.

21. Dr Grahame's report in relation to the matters of substance is a major one. She had been supplied by Mr Peters with all of the relevant material and she had considered some further articles as stated in her report. It is of such importance that it must in our opinion be set out in full. We would, however, highlight the fact that she finds that "The melanoma under consideration at present obviously falls into the 'low risk' category". And she goes on to say that "Unfortunately there is almost universal agreement that 'low risk' does not equate with 'no risk' where ultimate survival is concerned." This no doubt leaves open the possibility that there might be other grades of risk, but later Dr Grahame says: "With regard to Mr. Bewley's tumour in particular, I would agree that it falls into the category of minimal risk and that it did so on all the dates mentioned". She thus expresses a conclusion that the risk in the applicant's case was both "low" and "minimal". The "dates mentioned" in the letter from Mr. Peters requesting her opinion were: In August 1983 when the applicant was medically examined; in March 1984, when the BCC was issued; and December 1984, when reconsideration was completed. It follows that, accepting the critical evidence profferred by the respondent through Dr Grahame, it is unnecessary to embark upon a full discussion of which date is the critical date. There is not in our view any case for regarding August 1983 as the critical date. The best view is, in our opinion, that it is the date of the decision to issue the BCC: Compare Re X and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1980) 3 ALN N58. But as will appear our conclusion would remain unchanged even if the date of reconsideration and confirmation were found to be the critical date. The full report is as follows:

"I have read and considered all the articles and

documents given me by Mr Peters regarding malignant

melanoma in general, and Mr Bewley's melanoma in

particular.

I have also read and considered a 'Symposium on

Current Views on the Pathology of Melanomas' in the

most recent Journal of the Royal College of

Pathologists of Australasia Vol 17 No 2 of April

1985, which contains some 20 articles, many of

which refer to the articles above.

There are two considerations in the prognosis of

malignant melanoma - local recurrence and ultimate

survival should distant metastases occur in vital

organs, which latter is the mode of death in most

fatal melanomas.

There are numerous histologic, some clinical, and

an occasional socio-economic criteria for assigning

melanomas to either low or high risk categories.

The melanoma under consideration at present

obviously falls into the 'low risk' category.

Unfortunately there is almost universal agreement

that 'Low risk' does not equate with 'No risk'

where ultimate survival is concerned.

All the studies of large numbers of cases show that

deaths from distant metastases can occur even after

a disease free period of 8 or 10 years. I think

this confirms statements of for example

Akerman (J. Path): 'No pathologist can predict when

cells of malignant melanoma in the dermis or

already occurred, or will occur' and McCarthy

(Pathology 1985)

'Our understanding of the pathology of melanocytic

metastasise, when another several mms thick fails

The question of prognosis for local recurrence is

These studies have altered initial melanoma

there is unanimous agreement with other surveys -

'We wish to emphasise that studies of large numbers

Other surveys gave comparable recurrence rates

Sydney Melanoma Clinic: approx. 2% of 708 cases in

10 years

Queensland Melanoma Clinic: less than 5% at 5

In summary, none of the research gives a nil

all refer to 5 year or 8 year or 10 year "cures",

meaning no demonstrable disease at a given point in

They all also stress the point that patients are

when he says 'when analysing the prognosis for

individual melanoma patients the most important

However, an exact prognosis can never be made for

With regard to Mr Bewley's tumour in particular, I

adjacent naevus.

Despite all the statistics, and their analyses,

presented by Mr Bewley and the researchers, the

universal agreement has not yet been reached.

Until further knowledge is gained, there will

the conclusions and opinions expressed by Shaw,

Schmoeckel, McGovern and others.

As R.J. Reed (USA, in Pathology 1985) summarises

'Caution is urged in the translation of

histological patterns and common prognostic

In my opinion, Mr Bewley will probably not have a

However, of every 100 people having a tumour

22. A great deal of other material in the form of studies published in medical journals was submitted to the Tribunal both by the applicant and by Mr Peters, and as previously mentioned they were all placed before Dr Grahame. They are also referred to in the letter from Mr Peters to Dr Grahame. We do not propose to rehearse the opinions expressed in these articles, preferring to rely on the opinions expressed in the various medical reports submitted to us. Were the opinions in these reports at variance with each other in any significant respect, or were there any significant doubt cast upon them by the various publications referred to, we would have a greater problem, but those situations do not arise. In summary, the applicant in effect asks us to find that, at whichever is the critical date, be it the date of the decision to issue the BCC or the date of reconsideration, there was a very low risk of recurrence. We consider that the evidence supports a finding to that effect and we so find. In fact he submits that he should be regarded as having been in all likelihood cured, but it is unnecessary, as will appear, for us to express any finding in relation to this claim. Indeed, from what appears below such a finding would be impossible. Mr Peters on the other hand asks us to find, as we understand the submission, that there was here a history of a physical condition the very nature of which gives rise to a definite if small risk, and thus to a likelihood (in the sense of "that which might well happen") and not merely to a bare possibility, of either local recurrence or recurrence elsewhere in the body, leading to a disabling incapacity. We consider that the evidence supports, indeed compels, such a finding, in particular the evidence of Dr Grahame. Only Dr Farnsworth in fact spoke of a "100 per cent cure rate", and we do not consider it open to us on the evidence to find other than that there was, at whichever is the critical date, a risk, albeit a very low risk, of recurrence, and we do so find. It follows that, as forecast earlier, the crucial issue in the case is the meaning to be ascribed to the words in s.16(4). What is meant by the expression "not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical ... condition ... referred to in the report ..., to continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age"?

23. The meaning of the words in question seems to us to be clear. It is that in order to form the relevant opinion there must be a positive satisfaction that the person "is not likely to continue" etc. There must be a greater likelihood that the person will not so continue than that he or she will. Unless this be the case, how can it be opined that the person is not likely to continue? It is in our view crucial that the phrase "not likely" be kept intact, and be regarded as a composite expression. The word "not" should not be detached and emphasis placed on the meaning of the word "likely". In consequence we consider that it is not possible to form the opinion in question until it can be said that it is more probable than not that the person will not continue until attainment of maximum retiring age. This being what seems to us to be the plain meaning of the statutory expression, no further inquiry is really necessary. In deference however to Mr Peters' careful argument and to previous decisions, and mindful of the seriousness of the question for the purposes of the administration of the Act, it is necessary to set out and to consider that argument and the various decisions that were referred to in it.

24. What was said was that in the context of s.16 as a whole, and of the administrative process called for by its provisions, s.16(4) should be seen as setting up a preliminary or screening process, alerting the administrator of the Fund, at the stage of examination for permanent employment and the person's first entry to the Superannuation Fund, to the existence of something that might lead to reduced benefits being payable. Reliance was placed on Re Linden (above). That case of course involved not a s.16(4) question, but a question under s.16(8), the applicant having sought revocation of a BCC specifying obesity and hypertension. The Commissioner accepted that the applicant no longer suffered from obesity and deleted that condition from the BCC. The Tribunal affirmed the refusal of the Commissioner to delete the condition hypertension from the BCC.

25. The opinion to be formed under s.16(8) is that the person "is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with (the condition specified in the BCC), to cease to continue to be an eligible employee before he attains his maximum retiring age", whereupon the Commissioner shall revoke the certificate. It must be observed that the question is not reported accurately in the words of the note at the foot of p.659 which introduces the extract from the Tribunal's Reasons for Decision in Re Linden. Interestingly enough, it appears from that extract that in relation to s.16(8) it was there contended for the Commissioner that the word "likely" meant " a better than 50 per cent chance".

26. The Tribunal in Re Linden went on to consider Re Waddy and Delegate of the Secretary, Department of Transport (1978) 1 ALD 199 and then said as follows:

"Section 16 of the Superannuation Act 1976 is

obviously concerned with providing some protection

for the superannuation scheme established by the

Act against claims due to the premature invalidity

or death of a person who at the time when he

becomes an eligible employee is suffering from a

potentially disabling or fatal physical or mental

condition or conditions. The protection afforded

to the scheme is not absolute because a benefit

classification certificate ceases to have any

effect at all after completion of 20 years'

contributory service (see s 16(5)) and has a

diminishing effect in the event of death or

retirement on grounds of invalidity where the

period of contributory service is not less than

eight years (see ss 66 and 81). When, in sub-ss

(4) and (8) of s 16, parliament has referred to a

person who is not 'likely', by reason of or for a

reason connected with some physical or mental

condition, to continue to be an eligible employee

until attaining maximum retiring age, it seems to

us that, in the context, parliament must be

referring to conditions which give rise to a 'real

risk' and not merely to conditions which create a

'probability' or 'better than even chance' that the

person will not continue until attaining maximum

retiring age.

There is no question in our minds that where the

medical evidence establishes a probability or

'better than even chance' that a particular

condition will prove disabling before a person

attains maximum retiring age, the Commissioner is

entitled, if not obliged, to issue a certificate.

But we are unable to accept that the section limits

the Commissioner to issuing a certificate only

where such a degree of certainty is established.

The general medical standards developed for the

guidance of Commonwealth medical officers, which

were designed at establishing acceptable levels of

risk, are as we understand them, consistent with

that broader view. Failure to meet the general

medical standards may not render it 'probable' that

a person will not continue in employment until

maximum retiring age. But such failure does

suggest that, if the condition persists early death

or retirement on grounds of invalidity is an event

which might well happen."

27. We do not cavil with the result reached in Re Linden, which was a case of a continuing condition. But we have to say that, with the greatest respect, we are unable to accept the reasons there set out insofar as they suggest that a condition giving rise to no more than a "real risk" will enable the issue of a BCC. In the final paragraph set out above there appears to us to be an elevation of the role of the General Medical Standard, which is entirely non-statutory, into a criterion for interpretation of the statute, leading to a construction which holds that an event that "might well happen" is to be treated as one entitling the formation of an opinion that the person is "not likely" to continue to maximum retiring age.

28. We have already noted the piquant circumstance that in Re Linden in relation to s.16(8), where the application of what we may call the "probability" rather than the "real risk" criterion would lead to a failure to form in favour of an applicant the opinion that the person is not likely to cease to be an eligible employee, the Commissioner submitted that "likely" meant "a better than 50 per cent chance". Mr Peters in this case contended that there was an essential difference between s.16(4) and s.16(8), the former provision being said to be, as previously stated, a "screening mechanism", analogous to procedures adopted by any prudent insurer. But the difference here is that the Commissioner cannot under the Act refuse entry to the Fund to a person accepted as a permanent employee. Instead he issues a BCC. In turn, the employee then has the right under s.16(8) to challenge the issue of the BCC by invoking s.154 of the Act, as the present applicant has done. Alternatively he may, under s.16(8), request variation or revocation of the BCC. Under this provision a person may be required to undergo further medical examination, and the argument was that the emphasis here is quite different. The "likelihood" here was said to be directed to the incapacity itself, whereas under s.16(4) the likelihood is a likelihood relative to the disease itself, with the question under s.16(4) being related to likelihood based on the nature of the physical or mental condition.

29. We have given earnest consideration to Mr Peters' argument, but we can find no basis for it. He certainly made the best that could be made of the argument, and we can readily appreciate the point which he made as to what is an appropriate burden to place upon the manager of a fund which has, we were told, some 30,000 applicants seeking to become members of it in each year. A large scale investigation could not, it was said, be expected of the Commissioner under s.16(2), fairness being maintained by the insertion of the provisions of s.16(4) and s.16(8). There are three immediate comments on this:

(i) The aptness of s.16(8) to deal with the situation in a case

such as this is very questionable. If revocation is not

called for, the person who has left the shelter of one fund

where his or her rights are unqualified is left high and

dry;

(ii) The analogy with a private insurer is not as apt as might

seem. As already stated, a person accepted for permanent

appointment to the Public Service cannot be refused entry

to the Superannuation Fund;

(iii) Most important of all, it should be stated that in Re Hare

and Commissioner for Superannuation (1981) 3 ALN N81; Re

Watts and Commissioner for Superannuation (1981) 3 ALD 267;

Re Z and Commissioner for Superannuation (1983) 5 ALN N251

(on appeal to Federal Court sub nom Fry v Commissioner for

Superannuation [1984] FCA 211; (1984) 6 ALD 229) the Tribunal adopted an

approach very much related to the drawing of an analogy

between the role of an insurer and the role of the

Commissioner under the Act, but the judgments on appeal to

the Federal Court in Fry v Commissioner for Superannuation

make it abundantly clear that there is no substitute for

interpretation of the words of the Act according to the

familiar principles of statutory interpretation, and that

that interpretation should not be influenced by

implications drawn from analogies with the conduct of

private insurance based on contract.

30. Mr Peters referred the Tribunal to a number of authorities on the meaning of the word "likely". In Tillman's Butcheries v AMIEU (1979) 27 ALR 367 the phrase in question appeared in s.45D(1)(a) of the Trade Practices Act 1974, and referred to a situation "where the conduct is engaged in for the purpose, and would have or be likely to have the effect of causing substantial loss or damage to the business of the corporation". At pp.375-6 Bowen CJ said:

"The word 'likely' is one which has various shades

of meaning. It may mean 'probable' in the sense of

'more probable than not' - 'more than a 50 per cent

chance'. It may mean 'material risk' as seen by a

reasonable man 'such as might happen'. It may mean

'some possibility' - more than a remote or bare

chance. Or, it may mean that the conduct engaged

in is inherently of such a character that it would

ordinarily cause the effect specified.

In Australian Telecommunications Commission v Kreig

Enterprises Pty Ltd (1976) 14 SASR 303, Bray CJ had

to consider the meaning of the word 'likely' in s

139B of the Post and Telegraph Act 1901-1973 (Cth).

The context, of course, was different. However,

Bray CJ concluded it meant 'more probable than not'

in that context. His Honour expressed the view

that that was the natural and ordinary meaning of

'likely', though he referred also to the rules of

construction applicable where the statute being

interpreted is a penal statute or one which, as in

the case of s 139B, imposed an additional liability

beyond the liability in tort.

It was suggested by Joske J in Trade Practices

Commission v Total Australia Ltd (1975) ATPR 40,014

that 'likely' in s 47(5) of the Trade Practices Act

was equivalent to 'calculated' and he referred to

Thurley v Hayes [1920] HCA 28; (1920) 27 CLR 548. However, in

that case 'calculated' was interpreted to mean

'likely' in order to rid the word of its overtones

of intention.

The American cases in this area are not overly

helpful, as s 3 of the Clayton Act uses the word

'may'. The only certainty is that 'may' means more

than a mere possibility. The standard varies from

a 'potential' in Anchor Serum Co v FC of T 217 F 2d

867 at 872 to a 'probability' in Standard Fashion

Co v Magrane-Houston Co [1922] USSC 78; 258 US 346. In the latter

case reference was made to the effect of the word

'substantial' on 'may'. It was considered that

this should lift the requirement of proof because

the effect was not simply lessening of competition

but 'substantial' lessening of competition.

The circumstances to which s 45D may apply are so

various, that I hesitate to place a gloss on the

section by preferring one meaning of 'likely'

rather than another for the determination of this

particular case. It is unnecessary to do so,

because I have formed the view that whichever

meaning is adopted the evidence leads me to the

conclusion that the likelihood of substantial loss

or damage has been established"

At pp.380-1 Deane J. said:

"The word 'likely' can, in some contexts, mean

'probably' in the sense in which that word is

commonly used by lawyers and laymen, that is to

say, more likely than not or more than a 50 per

cent chance ('an odds-on chance': per Lord Hodson,

Czarnikow Ltd v Koufos (1969) 1 AC 350 at 410 and

see, as to the meaning of the word 'probable',

Eggleston, Evidence, Proof and Probability, 1978, p

10ff). It can also, in an appropriate context,

refer to a real or not remote chance or

possibility, regardless of whether it is less or

more than 50 per cent. When used with the latter

meaning in a phrase which is descriptive of

conduct, the word is equivalent to 'prone', 'with a

propensity' or 'liable'. When so used, it is

sometimes equated with the concept of

foreseeability in the law of negligence (see, for

example, Overseas Tankship (UK) Ltd v Miller

Steamship Co Pty Ltd (Wagon Mound No 2) (1967) ALR

97; [1966] UKPC 1; (1967) 1 AC 617 at 634-5; Callaghan v Wm C

Lynch Pty Ltd (1962) 79 WN (NSW) 830 at 835). Thus,

if I fire a rifle through drawn curtains into a

quiet lane in a country village, it is not likely,

in the sense of more likely than not or an odds-on

chance, that I will injure anyone. It would,

however, be difficult to deny that there was a real

chance or possibility (or likelihood in that sense)

that an occasional passer-by would be wounded by

the bullet. Plainly, the act of firing a rifle

through drawn curtains into a lane used by

pedestrians would be an act which was, in the

circumstances, prone or liable (likely in that

sense) to cause injury to a passing pedestrian.

In Australian Telecommunications Commission v Krieg

Enterprises Pty Ltd (1976) 14 SASR 303 at 308-13,

Bray CJ, after an instructive consideration of

relevant authorities, expressed the view that the

ordinary and natural meaning of the word 'likely'

is synonymous with the ordinary and natural meaning

of the word 'probable' and both words mean that

there is an odds-on chance of the thing happening.

His Honour added that statutes containing the words

have usually been construed that way, particularly

so where the statute is a penal statute or is

imposing an additional liability in tort. This

view, if accepted, would lead to the conclusion

that, prima facie, the word 'likely' in s 45D(1)

means probably in the sense of more likely than

not. While dictionary definitions and examples of

judicial and lay usuage can be adduced to offer

strong support for that view, I am unable to accept

that likely is synonymous with 'more likely than

not' or that if there is a 49.9 per cent chance of

an event occurring it would ordinarily be denied

that it was likely (or 'quite likely') that the

event would occur. Nor does it appear to me that

there is a presumption that, in a legislative

provision proscribing conduct that is likely to

cause loss or damage to another, the legislative

intent was that conduct which had a 49.9 per cent

chance of causing such damage was to be outside the

proscription."

31. Instructive as these passages are, they were stated in a wholly different context and do not govern the meaning of the phrase presently under consideration. In Tillman's Butcheries the phrase under consideration was "likely", and thus what was there at stake was a question of purpose and of likelihood, and not of an event being more likely than not as a matter of probability. Mr Peters also referred us to the decision of Sheppard J in the Administrative Law Division of the Supreme Court of NSW in Newman v Mulock and ors, but while the judgment is helpful in various respects, it does not touch on the present problem.

32. The decision most nearly in point is the decision of the Tribunal in Re Augustine and Commonwealth of Australia (1984) 2 AAR 34. The issue there concerned s.49(5)(a) of the Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act 1971 ("the Compensation Act"), under which provision the power of the Commissioner to determine that liability to make further payments of compensation in respect of partial incapacity be redeemed by payment to the employee of a lump sum is made dependent on the Commissioner being satisfied that, inter alia, "the injury is not likely to result in the employee becoming totally incapacitated for work". The Tribunal considered that the word "likely" in this context meant "substantially possible" and not "more probable than not". At pp.45-6 the Tribunal said:

"30. A further question to be considered in

relation to the interpretation of section 49(5)(a)

is the meaning of the phrase 'is not likely to'.

In Australian Telecommunications Commission v Krieg

Enterprises Pty Ltd (1976) 27 FLR 400 at 406-410, a

case in the Supreme Court of South Australia, Bray

CJ discussed the various meanings which 'likely'

has been held to have in particular contexts by

courts in England and Australia. He thought that

the normal meaning of the word was that there was

more than a 50 per cent chance of the event

happening. However, he pointed out that 'likely'

is often synonymous with 'probable' and that in

Overseas Tankship (UK) Ltd v Miller Steamship Co

Pty Ltd (Wagon Mound (No.2)) [1966] UKPC 1; (1967) 1 AC 617 at

634-635 Lord Reid said of the word 'probable':

'It is used with various shades of meaning.

Sometimes it appears to mean more probable than

not, sometimes it appears to include events

likely but not very likely to occur, sometimes

it has still a wider meaning and refers to

events the chance of which is anything more

than a bare possibility, and sometimes, when

used in conjunction with other adjectives, it

appears to serve no purpose beyond rounding off

a phrase.'

31. In Re Waddy and Delegate of the Secretary,

Department of Transport (1978) 1 ALN 199 the

Administrative Appeals Tribunal referred to a

discussion of the meaning of the word 'likely' by

McMullin J of the Supreme Court of New Zealand in

Transport Ministry v Simmons (1973) 1 NZLR 359 at

363, where His Honour said:

'The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary gives

one meaning of the word "likely" as being

"probable", but it also gives us another "such

as might well happen". That "likely" may mean

something less than "probable" is suggested by

the fact that very often it is accompanied by

the use of "very", "most" or "more". In my

view the meaning to be given to the word

"likely" where it is used in a statute or

regulation will depend upon the statute or

regulation and the context in which the word is

used. An event which is likely may be an event

which is probable but it may also be an event

which, while not probable, could well happen.

But it must be more than a bare possibility.'

The Tribunal in Waddy's case, having regard to the

context in which the word 'likely' was used in the

Air Navigation Orders which it was required to

construe, decided that it should be interpreted as

'such as might well happen'.

32. It is to be noted that the phrase used is

'not likely' rather than 'unlikely'. Although in

some circumstances they may be synonymous, in the

context of section 49(5) I am satisfied that what

is required of the Commissioner is that he is to

consider whether or not the injury is likely to

result in the employee becoming totally

incapacitated for work, and that the phrase 'not

likely' is to be construed not by reference to the

meanings which the word 'unlikely' may bear, but

rather by considering those which the word 'likely'

may bear. Section 49(5) is clearly intended to

protect the applicant from the risk that at some

future time he may become totally incapacitated for

work and that the amount payable to him then under

s.50 of the Act may be insufficient for his

support. I am satisfied, therefore, that 'likely'

should be construed fairly broadly, that is to say

that it should bear the meaning 'substantially

possible' and not 'more probable than not'. Unless

the Commissioner is satisfied that there is no

substantial possibility that the injury will result

in the employee becoming totally incapacitated for

work he is not to exercise the discretion conferred

by s.49(3)."

33. What has been often emphasised is that the word "likely" is to be construed in its context: See the passages cited above from Tillman's Butcheries v AMIEU and the cases therein referred to, and the passage from Re Augustine. The context of s.49(5) of the Compensation Act, involving the future support of an injured worker, would with respect tend to support the construction there adopted by the Tribunal. Unless it were so construed, there would be a danger that a decision could be too lightly made that an injured worker might receive a lump sum payment which could then be dissipated only to be followed by a lapse into total incapacity. We need however say no more than that these considerations do not apply here. We have to say also that we do not consider it correct to adopt an interpretative approach which discards the word "not" and construes the word "likely" standing on its own, in so doing introducing connotations of that word that are quite inapposite when the total phrase, in particular the expression "not likely", is in question. The presence of the word "not" makes it very difficult to read into the word "likely" the connotation "such as may well happen". It follows that in our opinion the requisite opinion cannot be formed unless it is found that it is more probable than not that the person in question will not continue to his or her maximum retiring age.

34. The point was referred to again in Re Amess and Commissioner for Superannuation (1984) 7 ALD 290 especially at pp 297-9, but the Tribunal's decision was that the issue of the benefit classification certificate in that case was justified whether the word "likely" bears the meaning "something that might well happen" or the meaning "more probable than not", so that the point did not have to be decided.

35. Finally, reference should be made again to Re Waddy. It has to be borne in mind that the expression there in question appeared in medical standards set out in Air Navigation Orders, which required that there be "no abnormality of the heart which is likely to interfere with the safe operation of aircraft". The applicant had had a myocardial infarction. As the Tribunal pointed out at p.200:

"Taken literally, it seems clear that the

abnormalities from which the applicant suffers

could not of themselves nor would they be 'likely

to interfere with the safe operation of aircraft'.

... The clear assumption ... is that until a

period of two years has expired the abnormalities

giving rise to and consequential upon the

infarction may lead to further medical events which

would be likely to interfere with the safe

operation of aircraft".

In this situation therefore, emphasising that the Air Navigation Orders are not, as the Tribunal pointed out, drafted with the precision of an Act of Parliament, and that the context of that problem was air safety, the Tribunal, not surprisingly, adopted as the meaning of the word "likely", unaccompanied by any form of negative, the meaning "such as might well happen".

36. In conclusion, we refer to paragraph 22 above and to the findings that we there made on the evidence. We observe that had we been able to endorse the construction of the statutory phrase that was contended for by Mr Peters, we would have supported the formation of the relevant opinion. But since we are of the contrary view, it is our conclusion that the opinion should not have been formed that it was not likely that the applicant would continue to his maximum retiring age, and that this is so whether the critical date for the formation of such opinion was 12 March 1984, being the date of the original decision, or 7 December 1984, the effective date of completion of reconsideration of that decision. At the date of the original decision, the only reports before the delegate were those of the examining CMO and of Dr Carney, and on our construction of s.16(4) the requisite opinion could not have been formed on the basis of those reports. Nor was there any information then unknown, but later revealed, which would have justified its formation. By the time of the reconsideration, the reports of Dr Carney and of Dr King set out in paragraph 17 above were also available, but the situation in our view remained unchanged. For the purposes of this review we have looked at later reports, a course which we regard as justified on the basis that such reports were relevant for the purpose of considering whether the reports earlier available should be qualified or supported. Dr Farnsworth's report was of course in a special case because it analysed tissue preserved from the original excision and she alone spoke of a "100 percent cure rate". But all of the reports before us are entirely consistent with each other, and confirm the evidence that was before the delegate in March 1984, and before the Commissioner on reconsideration in December 1984.

37. In all the circumstances the decision to issue the benefit classification certificate must be set aside.
