ORDER

Tribunal affirms the objection decision under review.

DECISION

In this case, the applicant (henceforth referred to as "W") had, prior to her divorce, operated a farming partnership with her husband ("the H and W partnership"). The parties intermarried in 1973. No evidence being called, it was agreed that all the relevant facts were to be found in the judgment of McCall C.J. of the Family Court (WA) in the matrimonial proceedings between W and her husband ("H"), a copy of which was included in the "T" documents (exh "A").

2. That judgment states that at the time of the marriage, H was employed as a farmhand by his parents, who farmed in the Bizet district. H's sole asset at the time of the marriage being a motor vehicle. W, who was then unemployed, brought a motor vehicle and some $3000 into the marriage. At or about the time of the marriage, H's parents gave the couple $10,000 which they spent mainly on purchasing household equipment. For about the first 2 1/2 years of the marriage, the parties lived rent free on two properties leased by H's parents.

3. In 1976, the couple purchased a home in Bizet for $10,000. The deposit ($2000) was gifted to the couple by W's parents, H's parents meeting all the mortgage payments thereafter. Prior to this, H had traded in his motor vehicle on another one, H's parents meeting the repayments on this replacement vehicle. Later that year, the couple purchased an investment property in Dutton Park for $17,500, the whole of this amount being given to the couple by H's parents. This property was sold about two years later for $22,500, the couple retaining for their own purposes some $2,500 and investing the balance ($20,000) in Carmen Nominees Pty Ltd ("Carmen"). Carmen carried on the farming business as trustee for the family trust established by H's parents in 1977 trading as F.S. Black and Co, Bizet. Dealing with this $20,000, McCall C.J. held that as no shares were issued by Carmen, this amount was to be treated as a loan by the parties to the company.

4. At the end of 1976, W sold her motor car, retaining the proceeds in a separate account. At this stage, the couple had no children and W commenced part-time work for about 7-9 months as a nurses aide at the local hospital . Throughout this period and up until the parties commenced farming on their own account in 1984, H worked for his parents for wages paid by Carmen. In 1980, H traded in his vehicle on a station wagon, his parents providing $6,600, being the balance of the finance needed for this replacement vehicle. In 1985, the couple sold the domestic home in Bizet for $18,000, using the proceeds as part payment for another house in the same town costing $30,000, H's parents once again paying all the mortgage payments on this new home. The couple lived in this home until their separation on 16 September 1988, when W left H.

5. From this point onwards, I can do no better than restate the judgment of McCall C.J. to the extent that it impinges on the issue I have to decide.

The husband's parents, Francis Black and Eileen

Black, have been farming in the Bizet area for some 30

years. ... The parents acquired two farming properties -

Blackacre and Whiteacre - the former being owned by

Carmen, the latter by the parents. ... However, although

the ownership of the two farms was different, the

business of farming the two properties was carried on by

Carmen.

The directors of Carmen were and are Mr and Mrs

Black Senior and the husband, and the beneficiaries of

the Trust were and are, the children of Mr and Mrs Black

Senior. From the time of the marriage up until the

beginning of 1984, the husband worked on his parents'

properties. ... (t)he husband was paid a wage. By

early 1984 this amounted to $80 per week and was

increased thereafter. The Black Seniors lived in the

township of Bizet as also did the husband and wife. It

was the practice of the two men to go out to the farm

and work each day, returning in the evening. Mrs Black

Senior was responsible for keeping the books for Carmen

whilst her husband and the husband in these proceedings

did the farm work. The Black Seniors had three other

children who are beneficiaries under the Family Trust

but none of them in recent years has been employed in

the farming business.

Throughout the period up to 1984, it was Carmen that

owned all the machinery necessary to run the farms. The

husband and wife owned no machinery.

Tanner's Lease

In 1983 a nearby farm owned by one Don Tanner became

available for leasing. This farm consisted of 3,000

acres. It was all arable land and about 1812 acres

could be, and were, cropped. It is apparent to me that

the Black Seniors were by this time anxious to see their

son start farming on his own account. He had worked for

them for many years and they wished to see him on his

own farm. I am satisfied that they encouraged him to

take on the lease of Tanner's farm and commence his own

farming operation. I am also satisfied that there was a

discussion with the husband and wife and the husband's

parents about the whole project and it was decided that

the husband and wife would take on the lease of Tanner's

property and commence farming on their own. However, it

is also apparent to me that the husband and wife were in

no financial position to do this without considerable

assistance from the Black Seniors.

The lease rent was $27,000 per annum. In addition,

moneys were going to be needed at the outset for what

could be broadly referred to as "start up" costs, namely

money to purchase sheep, chemicals, fuel, etc. In fact,

the Black Seniors advanced the husband and wife the

following:

$ 2,700 lease deposit

5,000 fuel

1,000 chemicals

2,000 workmen

8,694 sheep

$19,394 TOTAL

There was no disagreement between all the parties

concerned that these moneys were advanced to get the

husband and wife started and that they were repaid in

full within about 24 months.

There was, however, total disagreement as to the other

arrangements alleged to have been made between the Black

Seniors and the husband and wife relating to payment for

other matters concerning the working of Tanner's lease.

The husband and wife formed a partnership to work

Tanner's property ("H and W"). In the discussion

leading up to the leasing of Tanner's property, the

husband's parents both said that it was agreed that the

husband and wife could use Carmen's machinery for

working Tanner's property and that Mr Black Senior would

work with the husband and their labour would be employed

in working both farms, that is, the two properties

belonging to Carmen and Tanner's property. Their

evidence was that the husband and wife were expected to

pay for the use of this machinery and also for the use

of Black Senior's labour. There was never any attempt

to quantify what this was to be worth, nor was any

repayment date fixed. The arrangement, according to

them, was that the husband and wife would repay them

when they could afford to do so, namely when their farm

became profitable enough or the repayment could take the

form of the husband and wife purchasing machinery in

due course, which in turn could be used on the

properties of F.S. Black and Co.

The Black Seniors were quite adamant in their evidence.

There was no intention to make a gift to the husband and

wife of the use of Carmen's machinery, nor was there any

intention that Black Senior would work on the Tanner's

property gratuitously. They expected to be repaid when

the financial circumstances of the husband and wife

permitted, or any debt due as a result of the use of the

machinery and labour could be offset and probably

satisfied if, in due course, the husband and wife

purchased machinery. An example was, if they bought a

header for $100,000 or $165,000, which in turn could be

used in farming the Blackacre and Whiteacre. The husband

agreed that this was the arrangement. The Black Seniors

put the arrangement as high as being an agreement

reached.

As against this evidence, the wife denied that there was

any agreement to pay for the labour and machinery. Her

evidence was that she understood that they were allowed

to have the use of the machinery because the husband had

some interest in Carmen which, in turn, owned the

machinery. Much time was taken during the trial in

cross-examination of all parties regarding the

discussion and arrangement arrived at at this time.

There was no recording of any such agreement in the

books of Carmen and in my view this is understandable.

The picture I get from the evidence is that here was a

couple who had been farming for many years and for whom

their son had been working. They were anxious to see

their son starting off with his own property. They were

anxious to help him and his wife, with whom they had a

good relationship at that time, as much as they could.

They had a good year in 1982, but 1983 had not been a

good year, resulting in a loss on their farming

operation. There was no dispute that the start-up costs

were not being given to the young couple, but moneys

lent which had to be repaid, and in fact they were

repaid.

It was intended that the two men would work both farms.

Each would help the other. However, Carmen had all the

machinery and the husband and wife had none. I am also

satisfied that it would not have been possible for the

husband and wife to commence farming operations had they

had to either purchase or lease machinery. Accordingly,

it was necessary for the Black Seniors to make available

their machinery to assist their son start his farm.

However, I am also satisfied that this could not go on

indefinitely, nor was it intended to be gratuitous. I

am satisfied that the arrangement arrived at between the

husband and his parents and whether or not his wife was

present, she was subsequently made aware of the

arrangement and agreed to it, was that the use of the

machinery and labour was to be paid for either in cash

or subsequently by the purchase of machinery.

I arrive at this conclusion because I am more impressed

with the evidence given and the way in which the

evidence was given by the Black Seniors than by the

wife. But further, annexed to the affidavit of F.S. and

E.P. Black sworn 19 October 1989 was a handwritten sheet

which the wife admitted was in her handwriting. She

agreed it must have been in 1984, 1985 or 1986 that this

was written and it was a compilation of expenses for the

running of the farm. The document reads as follows:

Expenditure

Lease $27,000

Super 10,000

Shearing 2,500

Insurance 500

Accountancy 600

Petrol 6,000

Petrol 30,000

Bank 10,000

Sprays 2,000

Wages 88,600

Not included any living expenses

any wages for Father in Law

any usage farm machinery

Paying F.S. and Co for sheep

8,694

only paid 3,436

$5,258

House repayments

Because it refers to the initial payment for sheep of

$8,694 and showing a repayment of $3,436, the document

must have been compiled within the first two years of

the husband and wife taking over Tanner's property. The

evidence was that they had repaid this money outlaid for

sheep within 24 months.

The significant items on the sheet are that the expenses

do not include wages for Father-in-Law or any usage of

farm machinery. In my view, this document corroborates

strongly the evidence of the Black Seniors that the

arrangement made was that the parties were to pay for

the use of farm machinery and Mr Black Senior's wages.

I am satisfied that in November 1983, when discussions

took place between the Black Seniors and the husband and

wife, that an agreement was reached that if the husband

and wife took over Tanner's property, the Black Seniors

would not only advance them money for start-up costs

which were to be repaid, but would provide the use of

machinery and the labour of Mr Black Senior, but that

the latter had to be paid for in due course either in

cash or by the acquisition of machinery by the husband

and wife which in turn could be used on F.S. Black and

Co's farms. (my italics).

I have considered all the evidence leading up to this

finding as I appreciate that this finding forms the

basis of the claim by the intervener. I am satisfied on

the basis of the credibility that I attach to the

evidence of the Black Seniors, supported by this

document, that an agreement was reached at this time in

the terms I have outlined above. I do not accept the

evidence of the wife that this document was a draft that

she prepared for discussion with her husband. I do not

accept her evidence that this was only a thought as to

whether they should or should not pay the Black Seniors.

1984 onwards

On 28 February 1984 the husband and wife leased Tanner's

property. They went into possession and began farming

Tanner's property on 28 February 1984. The lease was

initially for three years at a rent of $27,000 per

annum. The rent was subsequently reduced by half

because of poor seasons. According to the wife, after

the initial three year period the parties made a

decision whether to carry on year by year. The parties

separated on 16 September 1988 and the lease continued

in their name until the end of February 1989, when it

was surrendered. The Black Seniors then took over the

lease for a period of one year commencing March 1989.

During the period from February 1984 to September 1988,

whilst the parties were living together, the three

properties, Blackacre (owned by Carmen), Whiteacre

(owned by the husband's parents) and Tanner's (leased by

the husband and wife) were more or less worked together.

On Blackacre and Whiteacre, F.S. Black and Co planted

and took off crops and ran sheep. On Tanner's the

partnership of the husband and wife ran sheep and

planted and took off crops. The husband and his father

worked together each day on all of these properties. On

Tanner's there were 1812 acres under crop and on F.S.

Black and Co's property there were 2200-2500 acres under

crop. The sheep of each party were kept separate even

though they used the same brands. The plant, machinery

and equipment for ripping up the soil, seeding,

fertilising and harvesting was all provided by Carmen.

This meant tractors, trucks, boom sprayer and seeder and

header were provided by Carmen. In addition, the

shearing was done in Carmen's shed.

The husband drove the tractors for ripping up the soil

and drove the header at harvest time and did the

maintenance on it. He also did the spraying. The

husband's father was in attendance at all times and

assisted by driving the trucks and keeping the seed and

super to the seeder. The two men worked together and

from time to time employed additional hands to assist

them. The accounts for the two farming operations were,

however, kept separate. Mrs Black Senior kept the

accounts for Carmen and the F.S. Black and Co

partnership and the wife did the same for the

partnership between her and her husband relating to the

farming operations on Tanner's.

Exhibits 7 and 8 show the trading results between the

years 1982 and 1988 for the two farming operations. In

1983 the parents lost $8,800.

It is significant for what follows later to see the

comparative net profits of the two farms for the years

ending 30 June 1984 to 30 June 1988. F.S. Black and Co

(the parents' farms) showed the following trading

results:

1983 1984 1985 1986 1987 1988

$8,852 $29,420 $10,158 $4,748 $75,353 $41,296

(loss) (loss)

By comparison the H and W partnership had the following

trading results:

1984 1985 1986 1987 1988

Nil $22,814 $8,924 $60,788 $47,299

...

When the wife left the matrimonial home she took with

her a quantity of the furniture from the home and as

well the sum of $58,000 from her own and the parties'

bank account. Of this sum she spent approximately

$11,000 on acquiring a motor car and pursuant to an

order made by the Court on 27 October 1988 she was

allowed to withdraw the sum of $5,000 which she said was

spent in acquiring items for the home at Harrow. The

balance remaining ($40,000) was by that order frozen.

The husband by the same order was allowed to uplift

$5,000 from the proceeds of the wool and wheat crop.

Otherwise, all moneys coming into the farming

partnership from such sources was also frozen. All

moneys coming into the partnership by an order made on

11 October was to be invested in an interest bearing

account in the joint names of the parties pending the

determination of the proceedings. ...

What happened after the separation was that the husband

was left with the farm with growing crops and sheep to

look after. He continued to work the farm with the

assistance of his father and Carmen's machinery. The

harvest was taken off at the end of 1988. The husband

continued running the farm until February 1989 when, I

believe because of his unsettled state, he decided to

give up farming the partnership property. He

surrendered the lease to Tanner, which was taken over

for 12 months on 1 March 1989 by Carmen. Proceeds from

the crop went into the partnership account but was not

available to be used by the husband or the wife. When

he gave up the lease, the husband and wife partnership

owned a number of sheep that were running on that

property. There were approximately 1110 ewes and 128

hoggets. The husband entered into an agreement with his

parents whereby Carmen would receive the wool from the

sheep and any lambs they produced in return for agisting

the stock on Tanner's which was now being leased by

Carmen. Carmen in fact surrendered this lease on 1

March 1990. During the year from 1 March 1989 to 1

March 1990, the wool was shorn from the parties' sheep

and these sheep produced some 700 lambs. Later 100 were

lost in a flood. The result was that the husband's

parents got the benefit of the wool clip and also the

lambs which were sold. Because ultimately they were

giving up Tanner's lease, all the sheep belonging to the

husband and wife were sold and the proceeds put into the

partnership account.

The result of the sale of the wheat and wool was that

although at the time of separation the parties had

little money in the partnership bank account,

particularly as the wife had taken most of it with her,

by the end of that year (1989), the proceeds of the farm

produce had built up a credit in the accounts of over

$150,000. This money was, of course, frozen and not

available to either party.

The husband, from early 1989 had, I am satisfied, given

up full time work for the partnership. Particularly was

this so after he surrendered Tanner's lease. He

thereafter had no income except what was being provided

to him by his parents for whom he was working more or

less on a casual basis. I am satisfied that he ceased

to receive the payments from Carmen that the parties

received during the course of their holding the Tanner

property. He was being supported by his parents who

provided him with money when (he) needed to come to

Perth for the purpose of access. His mother gave

evidence that she purchased for him any necessities that

he may have required.

There was some criticism of the husband for the

arrangement entered into between himself and his parents

for the continued care of the partnership's sheep. I am

satisfied that the effect of the breakdown of the

marriage on the husband was such that the steps he took

to ensure the preservation of the sheep were reasonable.

I am also satisfied when looking at the cost of agisting

sheep, that the arrangement he made was not one which

unduly favoured the parents. They took the risk with

respect to the number of lambs and the price of wool.

The husband was restrained from disposing of the

partnership property but was not in a position to look

after the sheep himself, particularly after he gave up

the Tanner lease. It is another area in which, on

looking at the overall conduct of these parties, that I

believe the wife was being unduly obstructive in not

co-operating with the proper ongoing business of the

farm.

Eventually, prior to the husband's parents surrendering

Tanner's lease in March 1990, the sheep belonging to the

husband and wife partnership were sold. The proceeds

were accounted for to the partnership. The total net

proceeds came to about $9,700 and were paid into the

parties' joint cheque account at Bizet. The sheep were

sold at recognised sheep sales by auction. Although an

attempt was made to show that a proper price was not

obtained for the sheep, I am satisfied because of the

way in which the sheep were sold at sheep sales, that no

complaint can be made about the prices received for the

remaining sheep of the partnership. I am also satisfied

that as Tanner's lease had been surrendered and the

husband's parents were giving up farming, that there was

no alternative but to sell the sheep. There was nowhere

for them to agist.

Before turning to the property of the parties as at the

present time, there are two items of property concerning

which I wish to make a comment. The first is a debt due

by the interveners to the parties with respect to an

International Header. In short, the interveners had a

Deutz Header which was not performing. They acquired an

International header for $60,000. This was used for the

1987/88 harvest. After the harvest the parties took

over the liability for this header. However, a new one

was bought subsequently and the old Deutz and the

International traded in on it. The arrangement was that

the husband's parents (i.e. Carmen) were to repay

$30,000 to the parties at the end of 1988/89 harvest and

the intervener would indemnify the parties with respect

to their further liability to Westpac for monies

borrowed for it. This debt due by the intervener to the

parties is taken into account as a credit to be allowed

to the parties in the claim by the intervener against

them and will be referred to later when dealing with the

intervener's claim.

TOTAL LIABILITIES $33,037

With respect to the property of the parties the major

dispute between them was the question of whether there

was any liability to the intervener and, if so, the

extent of such liability. The intervener claimed the

sum of $150,375, being, it said, the amount due to it

pursuant to the agreement reached between the intervener

and the husband and wife for payment for the use of the

intervener's machinery and for the labour of the

husband's father and other goods supplied. In the

alternative, if there was no binding agreement between

the intervener and the parties, the intervener said it

was entitled to recover this amount of money from the

parties by way of restitution and in either event, what

was the proper quantification of the intervener's claim.

This was a case in which the intervener sought to

establish a debt due to it by the parties to the

marriage and sought an order that this debt be repaid

before the property of the parties became the subject of

an order under Sec. 79 (Family Law Act).

In my view, this was an appropriate case in which the

claim by the intervener should be recognised, and as far

as possible, determined as a liability of the husband

and wife. The magnitude of the claim was such that it

was not appropriate for me to ignore it, and as far as

possible I should attempt to determine the debt due from

the parties to the intervener and make an order that

this debt be paid, and accordingly deal with the balance

of the property then remaining pursuant to the

provisions of Sec. 79 of the Family Law Act. The claims

of the intervener cannot be ignored. The course I have

adopted in this case in my view is consistent with the

approach of the Full Court in Zdravkovic and Zdravkovic

(1982) FLC 91-220 and was not objected to by either of

the parties, apart from the application made on the

tenth day of the hearing.

Intervener's claim

The intervener alleged that there was a debt due by the

husband and wife to it arising either out of contract or

by way of restitution for unjust enrichment. The

intervener alleged that during the discussions about the

husband and wife taking on Tanner's lease, it was agreed

firstly that the Black Seniors would advance the start-up

costs which had to be repaid, and in fact were

repaid, and secondly, that the husband and wife, in

running Tanner's property, would be allowed to use the

machinery belonging to Carmen, and also Mr Black Senior

would spend part of his time helping the husband work

Tanner's property. The agreement alleged was that

payment was to be made for the use of the machinery and

Mr Black Senior's labour when the parties were in a

position to afford this payment. There was no attempt

at that time to establish a rate or to quantify the

value of the provision of machinery and services. Both

Mr and Mrs Black Senior and the husband made it clear in

their evidence that the provision of the machinery and

services was not gratuitous, that Carmen had to be

reimbursed at some stage in the future. Reimbursement

could take the form of the husband and wife in turn

purchasing machinery in the future which could be used

on Carmen's property but, failing that, a time would

come when payment was expected. I have already dealt

earlier in these reasons with the conflict of evidence

surrounding this agreement.

I repeat what I said earlier about the question of

credibility and I accept that such an agreement or

arrangement was entered into prior to the husband and

wife taking on Tanner's lease and I also believe that

the wife knew of this arrangement. I do not accept her

explanation that there was only a moral obligation to

make some payment to the Black Seniors. I accept and

believe the evidence of the Black Senior's that the

provision of this machinery was not gratuitous nor

intended to be gratuitous. (my italics)

The difficulty with formulating a claim in contract is,

in my view, the uncertainty surrounding the time for

payment and the rate for payment. These uncertainties

regarding the terms make it impossible to ascertain what

the actual contract was. However, the intervener's

claim, in my view, falls within the doctrine of unjust

enrichment, entitling it to a reasonable remuneration

for the work done, goods supplied or services rendered.

The doctrine is dealt with in detail by the High Court

in Pavey and Mathews Pty Ltd v Paul (1986-1987) 162 CLR

221. Even though there is an unenforceable contract,

nevertheless the intervener is entitled to succeed on

its claim based on restitution or unjust enrichment

arising from the husband and wife's acceptance of the

benefits accruing to them from the intervener's

performance of the unenforceable oral contract. The

contract as I have said, in my view, would fail for

uncertainty, but this does not prevent the intervener

from recovering because the evidence satisfies me that

the husband and wife accepted the benefits provided by

Carmen. I am also satisfied that such benefits were not

intended to be a gift to the husband and wife. As Deane

J said in Pavey's case:

What the concept of monetary restitution involves is the

payment of an amount which constitutes in all the

relevant circumstances, fair and just compensation for

the benefit or enrichment actually or constructively

accepted. Ordinarily, that will correspond to the fair

value of the benefit provided (e.g. remuneration

calculated at a reasonable rate for work actually done

or the fair market value of materials supplied). (at p

263)

In this case, it is significant that the wife recognised

16. Just what is the nature of an obligation to make restitution in reliance on the doctrine of unjust enrichment was not adverted to by Mr Gunson and largely ignored by the Commissioner's representative, the latter submitting that the outgoing was not "incurred" until delivery of the judgment and contenting himself with a passing reference to the passage in the decision of McCall C.J. where his Honour noted the "difficulty" he faced in attempting to formulate the intervener's claim in contract in view of the uncertainty surrounding the time and rate for payment. As will now have become apparent, his Honour overcame that difficulty by concluding that the partnership's obligation to the intervener was ultimately grounded in the doctrine of unjust enrichment, "entitling it to a reasonable remuneration for work done, goods supplied or services rendered." It was sufficient for the learned Chief Justice's purposes merely to note that the "the doctrine is dealt with in detail by the High Court in Pavey and Mathews Pty Ltd v Paul" [1987] HCA 5; (1986-1987) 162 CLR 221. As I see the problem confronting me, the luxury of mere incorporation by reference to Pavey is, alas, not one open to me.

17. In Pavey, the High Court had to consider whether a builder who had completed building works for a customer pursuant to an oral contract was entitled to sue on a quantum meruit for the reasonable cost of the work done notwithstanding that according to the relevant State Act, a building contract carried out by a licensed builder is unenforceable against the other party unless the contract is in writing, signed by the parties and sufficiently describes the building work. The High Court held (by majority) that although the contract could not be enforced by the builder against the defendant, nevertheless the execution of the work for which the unenforceable contract provided and its acceptance by the customer was sufficient to found an obligation to make restitution, not in reliance on an implied contract, but based on the theory of restitution or unjust enrichment. Mason, Wilson and Deane JJ. concluded that a claim grounded in unjust enrichment is an independent claim, arising from the acceptance of the benefits accruing to the claimant from the execution of the work for which the ineffective contract provided. Thus the existence of the oral contract is of evidentiary value only, used merely to show that it was not the builder's intention to make a gift of his work to the defendant. Thus Mason and Wilson JJ. concluded that:

Once the true basis of the action on a quantum

meruit is established, namely execution of work for

which the unenforceable contract provided, and its

acceptance by the defendant, it is difficult to regard

the action as one by which the plaintiff seeks to

enforce the oral contract. (p 227).

18. What, then, is involved in a finding that one party is liable to compensate another in the absence of any enforceable agreement? At the outset, it would seem that Australian law in the area of unjust enrichment has departed from the jurisprudential theories applied by English judges in this area. Thus, the facts in Pavey bear a striking resemblance to those which fell for consideration before the House of Lords in Orakpo v Manson Investments Ltd (1978) AC 95, a case in which moneys were lent by moneylenders on the security of first mortgages. When the borrower fell into arrears in its repayments, the lenders sought to enforce their mortgages. Alas, it was held that the transactions were unenforceable because they were in breach of sec 6(1) of the Moneylenders Act 1927 (UK) which provided that "no contract for repayment by a borrower of money lent to him ... by a moneylender ... shall be enforceable unless a note or memorandum in writing of the contract be made ...". Lord Diplock, albeit with deep regret, noted in his speech:

My Lords, there is no general doctrine of unjust

enrichment recognised in English law. What it does is to

provide specific remedies in particular cases of what

might be classified as unjust enrichment in a legal

system that is based upon the civil law. (at p 104).

19. It seems to me that this approach is irreconcilable with the views expressed in Pavey in the judgment of Deane J and the joint judgment of Mason and Wilson JJ., particularly the observation of Deane J that:

To identify the basis of such actions as restitution

and not genuine agreement is not to assert a judicial

discretion to do whatever idiosyncratic notions of what

is fair and just might dictate. The circumstances in

which the common law imposes an enforceable obligation

to pay compensation for a benefit accepted under an

unenforceable agreement have been explored in the

reported cases and in learned writings and are unlikely

to be greeted affected by the perception that the basis

of such an obligation, when the common law imposes it,

is preferably seen as lying in restitution rather than

in the implication of a genuine agreement where in fact

the unenforceable agreement left no room for one. That

is not to deny the importance of the concept of unjust

enrichment in the law of this country. It constitutes a

unifying legal concept which explains why the law

recognises, in a variety of distinct categories of case,

an obligation on the part of a defendant to make fair

and just restitution for a benefit derived at the

expense of a plaintiff and which assists in the

determination, by the ordinary processes of legal

reasoning, of the question whether the law should, in

justice, recognise such an obligation in a new or

developing category of case: see Muschinski v Dodds

[1985] HCA 78; (1985) 160 CLR 583 at 619-620; Goff and Jones, The Law

of Restitution, 2nd ed (1978), p 11ff. In a category

of case where the law recognises an obligation to pay a

reasonable remuneration or compensation for a benefit

actually or constructively accepted, the general concept

of restitution or unjust enrichment is, as is pointed

out subsequently in this judgment, also relevant, in a

more direct sense, to the identification of the proper

basis upon which the quantum of remuneration or

compensation should be ascertained in that particular

category of case. (pp 256-257).

20. Pavey appears not only to have buried the "implied contract" theory which was used to underpin an action on a quantum meruit; cf Turner v Bladin [1951] HCA 13; (1951) 82 CLR 463, 474, but have moved towards the creation of a unifying legal concept which recognises "in a variety of distinct categories of case" an obligation on the part of a defendant to make a fair and just restitution for a benefit received. Thus the Full High Court in Australia and New Zealand Banking Group Ltd v Westpac Banking Corporation ((1987-1988) 164 CLR 662) noted:

"The basis of the common law action of money had and

received for recovery of an amount paid under

fundamental mistake of fact should now be recognised as

lying not in implied contract but in restitution or

unjust enrichment. ... In other words, receipt of a

payment which has been made under a fundamental mistake

is one of the categories of case in which the facts give

rise to a prima facie obligation to make restitution, in

the sense of compensation for the benefit of unjust

enrichment, to the person who has sustained the

countervailing detriment: cf. Pavey and Mathews Pty Ltd

v Paul. ... It is a common law action for recovery of

the value of the unjust enrichment...." (p 673).

21. The above does not pretend to be an exhaustive analysis of the theory underlying a claim grounded on unjust enrichment, I have set it out what I perceive to be the law merely in order to determine the punctum temporis when the liability in this case can be said to have been "incurred".

22. It was submitted on behalf of the Crown that W's liability did not arise, i.e. was not "incurred" until the determination by the Family Court on 14 May 1990. Support for this submission is said to be found in a series of cases, beginning with New Zealand Flax Investments Ltd v F.C. of T. [1938] HCA 60; (1938) 61 CLR 179, followed in F.C. of T. v James Flood Pty Ltd (1953) 88 CLR 494 and Nilsen Development Laboratories Pty Ltd and Ors v F.C. of T. [1981] HCA 6; (1981) 144 CLR 616.

23. I am satisfied that the Crown's submission is correct. Indeed, the problem McCall C.J. had in formulating the intervener's claim in contract, viz. the uncertainty of its terms, supports the Crown's case that nothing could be said to have been incurred before his Honour's determination. Thus, if the intervener's claim does not rest in contract, but in unjust enrichment, a liability for services rendered and freely accepted by the recipient cannot be said to be "incurred" until the benefit is ascertained by measurement against the market price of the work performed. And that calculation took place on 14 May 1990, i.e. at a time well after the partnership business had come to an end. It follows that W's liability was incurred on or after May 14, 1990.

24. Since writing the above, the House of Lords in Woolwich Equitable Building Society v Inland Revenue Commissioners (1992) 3 WLR 366 - published in the UK as recently as ten days ago - re-examines some aspects of the doctrine of unjust enrichment. The facts of that case are too far removed from those before me to warrant a closer examination of their Lordship's speeches, including the powerful dissents of Lord Keith of Kinkel and Lord Jauncey of Tullichettle. Suffice it to say that the effect of the decision of the majority (Lord Goff of Chiveley, Lord Browne-Wilkinson and Lord Slynn of Hadley) is to the effect that although the common law had previously only admitted recovery of money exacted under an unlawful demand by a public authority where the payment had been made under a mistake of fact or under limited categories of compulsion, the nature of a demand for taxation imposed on the citizen by the state - with the perceived economic and social consequences of non-payment stemming from the inequality of the parties' respective positions and the unjust enrichment falling on the state - where the citizen paid an unlawful demand to avoid those consequences warranted a reformulation of the law of restitution so as to recognise a prima facie right of recovery based solely on payment of money pursuant to an ultra vires demand by a public authority.

25. Albeit remote from the issue I have to decide, I have extracted two excerpts from Lord Goff of Chieveley's speech to illustrate how fragile is the base on which the law of restitution rests.

There is however a (second) objection to the

recognition of such a right of recovery. This is that

for your Lordships' House to recognise such a principle

would overstep the boundary which we traditionally set

for ourselves, separating the legitimate development of

the law by the judges from legislation. It was strongly

urged by Mr Glick, in his powerful argument for the

revenue, that we would indeed be trespassing beyond that

boundary if we were to accept the argument of Woolwich.

I feel bound however to say that, although I am well

aware of the existence of the boundary, I am never quite

sure where to find it. Its position seems to vary from

case to case. Indeed, if it were to be as firmly and

clearly drawn as some of our mentors would wish, I

cannot help feeling that a number of leading cases in

your Lordships' House would never have been decided the

way they were. For example, the minority view would

have prevailed in Donoghue v Stevenson (1932) AC 562:

our modern law of judicial review would have never

developed from its old, ineffectual, origins; and

Mareeva injunctions would never have seen the light of

day. (p 392).

In all the circumstances, I do not consider that Mr

Glick's argument, powerful though it is, is persuasive

enough to deter me from recognising, in law, the force

of the justice underlying Woolwich's case. Furthermore,

there are particular reasons which impel me to that

conclusion. The first is that this opportunity will

never come again. If we do not take it now, it will be

gone forever. The second is that I fear that, however

compelling the principle of justice may be, it would

never be sufficient to persuade a government to propose

its legislative recognition by Parliament; caution,

otherwise known as the Treasury, would never allow this

to happen. (p 395).

26. It remains for me to determine whether a liability incurred after the income earning activity of the business has ceased can be said to be loss or outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income or is/was necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, and, if the answer to either of the above propositions is in the affirmative, whether the loss is nevertheless excluded by virtue of being an outgoing of capital or of a capital nature.

27. A useful starting point of any enquiry as to whether or not an outgoing related to income derived in earlier years if there has been a complete cessation of business in the course of which the income was produced is the decision of the High Court in AGC (Advances) Ltd v F.C. of T. [1975] HCA 7; (1974-1975) 132 CLR 175; 75 ATC 4057. The facts in that case (quoting the headnote in the Commonwealth Law Reports) were as follows:

Until December 1968 a taxpayer carried on business

as a financier lending money and financing the purchase

of goods under hire-purchase agreements. In the course

of its business debts became owing to it which were

brought into account as assessable income. In 1969 a

scheme of compromise and arrangement under the Companies

Act (SA) took effect under which a special manager was

appointed for five years to act as agent of the company,

to take possession of its assets and wind up its

affairs, and to realise any of its assets or write off

any of its debts. Under the scheme the special manager

was to pay the amounts of debts received into a special

bank account, to be dealt with under the scheme for

payment to creditors; but there was no provision that

uncollected debts should be beneficially held for any

person other than the taxpayer.

The taxpayer wrote off as bad during the years of income

ended on 30th June 1970 and 30th June 1971 a number of

debts that it had not been able to recover. It claimed

to be entitled to deductions of the amounts of those

debts under s.63(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act

1936-1971.

It was held by majority (Barwick C.J. and Mason J; Gibbs

J dissenting) that on the proper construction of the

scheme the debts continued to be owned beneficially by

the taxpayer until the time (if any) of their

collection, and, accordingly, it was entitled to

deductions under s.63(1) in respect of the debts it had

written off.

28. I had occasion to analyse the AGC case at length in Case S30 85 ATC 280, and since I am unrepentant of the view I reached in that case, it seems to me that for the sake of economy of space, it is sufficient if I merely refer to my reasons in that case. Thus, it seemed to me then, as it seems to me now, that the result in the AGC case depended on the different findings of fact each of the three judges reached on the evidence. The Chief Justice concluded that the business of the taxpayer was, in the relevant year "substantially" the same as that which had been carried on before; Mason J held:

that on the facts recited in the stated case I am

satisfied that the business carried on by the appellant

after its share capital was acquired by Australian

Guarantee Corporation Ltd was the same business as that

which the appellant formerly carried on. (p 198).

Gibbs J (diss) found that there had been a complete cessation of the business in the course of which the income was produced. This finding persuaded his Honour to conclude that neither limb of sec 51(1) enabled the taxpayer to claim deductions for losses relating to income derived in earlier years.

29. The above sets out what I perceive to be the ratio of the AGC case. However, I would be remiss if I failed to include the strong dicta of Mason J who stated:

... In the Ronpibon Case [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 the Court

suggested in the passage to which I have already

referred that the second limb may have a slightly wider

operation than the first limb and that it may authorise

the deduction of losses incurred in a distinct business.

At first glance it may be thought that these

observations overlook the possible limitations inherent

in the words "incurred in carrying on a business for the

purpose of gaining or producing such income", viz.

assessable income generally. It may be argued that if

the taxpayer has ceased to carry on a particular

business, a loss subsequently sustained in relation to

that business cannot be described accurately as a loss

incurred in carrying on that business, or at any rate

one incurred in carrying it on for the purpose of

gaining or producing assessable income. But the

soundness of the argument depends on what is meant by

"incurred". A loss constituted by the writing off of a

bad debt is no doubt incurred, in the sense that it is

sustained, at the time when the debt is written off, and

that may occur in a given case after the taxpayer has

ceased to carry on as a going concern the business in

which the debt was created. Yet even in such a case it

may be correct to speak of the loss as having been

incurred in the carrying on of the business. This is

because the occasion for the loss is to be found in a

transaction entered into in the carrying on of the

business for the purpose of producing assessable income,

that is, in the agreement by which the debt was created.

Because the loss had its origin in such a transaction

the loss may be said to be one which was incurred in the

carrying on of the business for the purpose of producing

assessable income, notwithstanding that its true

character as a loss is not finally ascertained until the

debt is written off.

What I have said is, I think, in accord with the

observations made with respect to s.51(1) in the later

cases. Thus in the Ronpibon Case (1949) 78 CLR at p 57

the Court stated that "to come within the initial part

of the subsection it is both sufficient and necessary

that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be

found in whatever is productive of the assessable

income". So also it may be said that it is enough to

satisfy the second part of the subsection that the

occasion of the loss or outgoing is to be found in the

carrying on of a business for the production of

assessable income. In my opinion, therefore, the

outstanding terms charges not brought to account as

assessable income fall certainly within the second limb

of s.51(1); it may be that they fall within the first

limb but this is not a question which needs to be

pursued.

If this conclusion involves a departure from the literal

meaning of the words used it is to be justified by

reference to the context and to the unlikely and

arbitrary consequence which would flow from the adoption

of a literal interpretation. That a loss having its

origin in the course of carrying on a business for

profit should only be deductible whilst the business is

still in operation, though the loss may not be

ascertained until a later date, seems to me to be a

strange result; so strange indeed that an intention to

bring it about should not be imputed to the legislature

when the statute is susceptible to a sensible

alternative interpretation. (132 CLR pp 197-198, 75 ATC

pp 4071-4072).

30. The view I expressed in Case S30 as to the ratio of the AGC case has since been followed (albeit (dubitante) by Mrs Balmford in Case X3 90 ATC 114.

31. I am therefore satisfied that once I reach the conclusion that the obligation of the H and W partnership to compensate Carmen in reliance on the doctrine of unjust enrichment was "incurred" for tax purposes eo instanti with the Order of the Family Court, i.e. when the partnership had well and truly come to an end, I find myself unable to conclude that the payment to Carmen can be said to have been incurred either in gaining or producing the assessable income of the partnership, or that it was necessarily incurred in carrying on that business for the purpose of producing such income.

32. On that finding, it is only out of an abundance of caution that I propose to deal briefly with the alternative submission, viz that the payment was, in any event, an affair of capital. When the payment to Carmen is looked at in slow motion, it is clear that it arose out of an obligation by the partnership to compensate Carmen by way of a single, once and for all, largely undissected (and undissectable) sum, and having its origin in an Order of the Family Court, it answers positively all the "classic" criteria habitually applied by the Courts in determining whether an outgoing is an affair of capital.

33. For the above reasons the objection decision under review is affirmed.
