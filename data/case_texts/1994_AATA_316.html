Re Taxation Appeals [1994] AATA 316; (1994) 29 ATR 1238 (1 November 1994)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. ST93/27

AAT No. 9813

Number of pages - 16

Sales Tax

[1994] AATA 316; (1994) 29 ATR 1238

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

MATHEWS J (President), B. LOCK (Member) AND D TROWSE (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Sales Tax - photocopy services - whether photocopies are "goods" - whether photocopying constitutes "manufacture" - whether photocopies incidentally produced in the performance of skilled services - property in confidential information - decision affirmed.

Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930 ss 3(1), 3(4), 17(1), 17A

Adams v Rau [1931] HCA 43; (1931) 46 CLR 572

Baumgartner v Baumgartner [1987] HCA 59; (1987) 164 CLR 137

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Jax Tyres Pty Limited 85 ATC 4001

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Phillips [1978] FCA 28; (1978) 20 ALR 607

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Rochester [1934] HCA 17; (1933) 50 CLR 225

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Totalisator Administration Board of
Queensland 89 ATC 4877

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Totalisator Administration Board of Queensland [1990] HCA 48; (1990) 170 CLR 508

Muschinski v Dodds [1985] HCA 78; (1985) 160 CLR 583

Nanus Asia Co Inc and Anor v Standard Chartered Bank (1990) HKLR 396

Pacific Film Laboratories Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 70 ATC
4104

HEARING

ADELAIDE, 5 September 1994

1:11:1994

Counsel for the Applicant: Bernard O'Brien

Solicitors for the Applicant: C Rankine, Rankine Shaw and Co.

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr Nettle QC

Solicitors for the Respondent: A Macdonald, Australian

Government Solicitor

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

MATHEWS J, B. LOCK AND D TROWSE The applicant, L, seeks review of a decision of the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, dated 28 April 1993, in which the applicant's liability to pay sales tax under section 17 of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930 ("The Act") was affirmed. In essence, the dispute relates to the respondent's refusal to refund the amount of sales tax paid for the period 1 September 1987 to 31 August 1990. (herein referred to as the "refund period")

2. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by Mr Bernard O'Brien of counsel, and the respondent was represented by Mr Nettle QC.

3. The factual material before the Tribunal consisted of the documents lodged under section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (the T documents) together with the following:

An amendment, dated 5 September 1994, to the applicant's

Objection of 15 February 1991.

The Sales Tax Returns of the applicant from 1 September 1987 to

31 August 1990.

No oral evidence was called at the hearing. Despite an earlier

indication that certain facts might be in dispute, the factual

basis of the claim was, as it transpired, entirely subject to

agreement by the parties.

Factual History

4. The applicant was incorporated as a proprietary company on 10 October 1973. Its principal activity was to be trustee of a unit trust set up to provide administrative and other services to a firm of chartered accountants, P.

5. On 26 March 1979, the applicant obtained registration as a manufacturer of photocopies for sales tax purposes.

6. During the period 1 September 1987 to 31 August 1990, the applicant engaged in the provision of photocopying, computer services and other managerial and administrative services to P. These services were rendered pursuant to a management agreement entered into between the applicant and the partners of P on 1 November 1986. The terms of this agreement are central to the issues in this case, and a copy of it is annexed to these reasons.

7. It was conceded by the applicant at the hearing that the principal object of the management agreement between P and the applicant was to obtain the tax advantages outlined by the Federal Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Phillips [1978] FCA 28; (1978) 20 ALR 607.

8. During the refund period the applicant managed the business affairs of P which in turn rendered professional services to its clients. Paragraph 5.1 of the Statement of Agreed Facts described the services which the applicant provided to P:

"5.1.1 the provision to P of photocopying services;

5.1.2 the typing and printing of income tax returns and annual returns

of clients of P;

5.1.3 the typing and printing of letters and reports to clients and

statutory authorities and other persons;

5.1.4 the entry of data into computer terminals for the purpose of

preparing financial statements and reports for clients;

5.1.5 secretarial and other assistance to partners and staff of P; and

5.1.6 recording messages and filing of documents."

9. The professional services which P provided to its clients are listed in paragraph 5.3.1 of the Statement of Agreed Facts:

"income tax advice; insolvency management, including receiverships and

liquidations; asset protection planning; litigation support; valuation

of shares in companies, units in unit trusts and goodwill of

businesses; negotiation of income tax disputes and company and business

takeovers; the financial management and reporting and control of

business enterprises; and auditing of companies and other businesses,

social or domestic entities."

10. Paragraph 5.3.2 of the Statement of Agreed Facts details what is required of P to perform these services:

"In providing these services, the professional staff (of P) must

produce either a set of accounts, reports, tax returns, annual returns,

liquidator's and receiver's statements, detailed letters of advice,

spreadsheets, entries into company and unitholder registers, minutes of

meetings, notices pursuant to statutes, contracts and articles of

association and general correspondence".

11. Paragraph 5.3.2 of the Statement of Agreed Facts explains how these documents and materials are produced:

"In order to produce the materials referred to above typically the

professional staff (of P) formulate and compose those publications by

the use of a dictaphone, which is then typed and printed by an

employee of the Applicant on a personal computer or workstation,

settled by the professional staff, photocopied and sent by mail or

otherwise by an employee of the Applicant".

12. In accordance with clause 5 of the management agreement, P paid the applicant the amount of $0.26 for each photocopy produced pursuant to the arrangement.

13. The applicant paid sales tax to the respondent on the photocopies made for P during the refund period pursuant to section 17(1) of the Act. These payments totalled $108 865.60.

14. On 18 October 1990, the applicant applied for a full refund of $108 865.60 in respect of sales tax claimed to have been overpaid on photocopies produced by it during the refund period. The claim for refund was based on the applicant's contention that it was not engaged in business as a "manufacturer", and hence had not been required to be registered for sales tax purposes. Inherent in this submission was the proposition that the photocopies were not "manufactured" by the applicant within the meaning of section 17(1). This submission largely relied upon the judgment of the Queensland Supreme Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Totalisator Administration Board of Queensland (89 ATC 4877).

15. On 18 December 1990, the Commissioner refused the applicant's claim for a refund. This decision was based in part on the fact that the High Court had in the meantime overruled the judgment of the Queensland Supreme Court in the TAB Queensland Case ((1990) [1990] HCA 48; 170 CLR 508). The High Court had confirmed in that case that the respondent was liable to pay sales tax upon printing activities notwithstanding the fact that the primary characteristic of its business was not that of manufacture. The respondent had printed various items for its own use in connection with the gambling business it conducted and in association with its betting shops. Those items included stationery, office supplies, display material, betting cards, form guides, race lists, and dividend cards.

16. On 15 February 1991, the applicant lodged an objection to the decision to refuse to refund the sales tax.

17. On 28 April 1993, the Commissioner disallowed the applicant's objection. In determining the applicant's objection, the Commissioner took the view that the process of photocopying constituted "manufacture" under section 17(1) of the Act. Accordingly, the photocopies produced and supplied by the applicant to P were "sold" within the meaning of that section.

18. On 1 June 1993, the applicant applied to the AAT for review of the respondent's decision of 28 April 1993.

The Legislation

19. The respondent claims that sales tax was payable pursuant to section 17(1) of the Act. That section reads:

17(1) Subject to, and in accordance with, the provisions of this Act,

the sales tax imposed by the Sales Tax Act (No 1) 1930 shall be levied

and paid upon the sale value of goods manufactured in Australia by a

taxpayer and sold by him or treated by him as stock for sale by retail

or applied to his own use.

20. There was no suggestion at the hearing that the photocopies had been treated as stock for sale by retail, or had been applied to the applicant's own use. Similarly, there was no dispute as to the rate used as the basis for the calculation of the sales tax.

21. Accordingly, the only issues which remained outstanding at the hearing were:

Whether the photocopies constituted "goods" under section

17(1) of the Act.

Whether the process of photocopying constituted "manufacture"

under that section.

Whether it was the applicant which manufactured the

photocopies.

If the applicant manufactured the photocopies, whether it

sold them to P, given that confidential information was

involved.

Each of these issues shall be dealt with in turn.

Are photocopies "goods"?

22. Section 3(1) of the Act gives the following definition of "goods":

s 3(1) "Goods" includes commodities, but does not include:

(a) goods which have, either through a process of retailing or otherwise,

gone into consumption in Australia;

(b)......

23. Counsel for the applicant did not seriously urge that photocopies fall outside the primary definition of "goods" in section 3(1). Nor could he do so, for there is ample authority to the effect that photocopies are goods or commodities according to ordinary conceptions and are thus goods within the meaning of the Act: (TAB Queensland, in the High Court, supra).

24. However, it was submitted that photocopies could not be goods by virtue of the fact that the blank paper and toner, which were the raw materials in the process, had already "gone into consumption in Australia", and might already have been subject to sales tax before being purchased by the applicant. This submission, however, lacks foundation. It is the goods themselves which must have gone into consumption in order to attract the exclusion in paragraph (a). It would be an unwarranted extension of the exception to apply it here to the raw materials which were used in the manufacturing process.

25. Accordingly, we find that photocopies are "goods" within the meaning of the Act.

Were the photocopies manufactured?

26. Section 3 (1) of the Act defines manufacture, as far as it is relevant, as including:

(a) production

(b) the combination of parts or ingredients whereby an article or

substance is formed that is commercially distinct from those parts or

ingredients;...

(f) the copying or reproduction of visual images or sounds, or visual

images and sounds, so as to embody the images or sounds, or images and

sounds, as the case may be, (whether in the same material form or in a

different material form) in goods (including goods and commodities of

the kind referred to in paragraph (a) of the definition of "Goods");

27. It was conceded by the applicant at the hearing that had the photocopying services been provided by a professional photocopying bureau, then it would have constituted "manufacture" under the Act, and would have attracted sales tax. In making this concession, it was acknowledged that the process of photocopying falls within the definition of "manufacture" in section 3(1) of the Act. This was an appropriate concession, for the production of photocopies would, in our opinion, clearly be capable of falling within any one of paragraphs 3(1)(a), (b) or (f).

28. Paragraph 3(1)(a) was discussed by Lockhart J in FCT v Jax Tyres Pty Limited (85 ATC 4001), at 4004:

""Production" which is included in the definition of "manufacture" (sec.

3(1)(a)), is a word of wide import; but it still involves the element of

producing something different from the materials from which it was made.

It is not possible to formulate precise definitions of such general

terms as "manufacture" and "production"; but they do not bear a

restricted meaning. Whatever answers the description of "manufacture" or

"production" of goods according to accepted usage of the English

language is within the Act."

In this broad sense, the process of photocopying which clearly produces a different entity from the materials which went into its making, amounts to "production" in paragraph 3(1)(a).

29. Further, under paragraph 3(1)(b), the process of photocopying involves the combination of two ingredients, namely blank paper and toner, to produce an article which is commercially quite distinct from the original ingredients.

30. Finally, under paragraph 3(1)(f), photocopies can be characterised, by ordinary concepts, as being the result of a copying or reproduction of visual images.

31. Accordingly, it was not disputed that the process of photocopying constituted manufacture. The real issue related to the context in which this took place.

32. The applicant's central argument rested on the assertion that it was a provider of services, in much the same way as the respondents were in Adams v Rau [1931] HCA 43; (1931) 46 CLR 572. In that case, the respondents were professional shorthand writers who conducted a business of transcribing court proceedings. The High Court held that the transcripts thus produced were not "manufactured" within the meaning of the Act.

33. Adams v Rau has since been treated as authority for the proposition that if a person incidentally produces goods in the course of work which essentially involves the performance of skilled services, that person is not engaging in manufacture. The High Court affirmed this principle in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Rochester [1934] HCA 17; (1933) 50 CLR 225. It was the essence of those cases that the production of goods and the provision of services could not be isolated from each other, but that they were part of the same process, which was essentially one for the provision of skilled services.

34. Counsel for the applicant submitted at the hearing that, from the point of view of a client of P, the provision of photocopies was part of the overall professional services provided to it by P. The client, he urged, had no knowledge of the existence of the applicant nor of its arrangements with P. Therefore, the provision of photocopies merged into the professional accounting services provided by P. However, we must reject this submission. It entirely ignores the legal realities, namely that P and the applicant were separate legal entities, whose dealings were regulated by a legally binding management agreement. Indeed, the separate legal existence of P and the applicant were relied upon by both parties in order to obtain income tax relief under the Phillips principles. These legal identities cannot be relied upon in one context, and then denied in another.

35. The applicant's main argument pursuant to Adams v Rau was that the total management services performed by the applicant for P could not be "disintegrated" into separate entities, so as to treat the photocopying aspect of them in isolation from the others. He urged that insofar as the High Court in the case of Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Totalisator Administration Board Queensland (supra) might be taken to have permitted such a course, this was confined to situations where the manufacturer of the goods applied them to its own use, and was thus inapplicable here.

36. It is strictly unnecessary to determine this question in this case, for we would, in any event, have found that the management agreement brought this case outside the principle in Adams v Rau. But we find it very difficult to accept that the process of reasoning to be adopted when ascertaining whether goods which were for self use had been "manufactured" under the Act should be any different from that to be applied when making precisely the same enquiry in the case of goods which were intended for sale.

37. Further, the facts of this current matter are entirely different from those in Adams v Rau. In Adams v Rau, the recording of court room proceedings involved a great deal of skill. The transcript of the proceedings was the material manifestation of the provision of these skilled services. This lies in stark contrast to the mechanical process of converting a blank page into a photocopied page. This process, it is acknowledged, involves a low level of skill and minimal training.

38. In any event, the terms of the management agreement distinguish and separate the provision of photocopies from the management and administrative services which the applicant was to perform for P. Under the management agreement the applicant agreed to perform three distinct functions:

Under clause 2 of the agreement, to provide management

services to P for which it was to be paid in accordance with

clause 3;

Under clause 6 of the agreement, to provide computing

facilities to P, for which it was to be paid in accordance

with clause 6; and

Under clause 5 of the agreement, to make photocopies of

documents for P, for which it was to be paid at an agreed

price per page in accordance with clause 5.

This structure clearly separates the provision of photocopies from the other services which were to be performed. Further, the payment for the photocopies is entirely divorced from the payment for any of the other services.

39. In the result, we are firmly of the view that the photocopies were "manufactured" within the meaning of section 17(1).

Were the photocopies manufactured by the applicant?

40. Section 17(1) of the Act imposes sales tax upon "the sale value of goods manufactured in Australia by a taxpayer..." (emphasis added) It was the applicant's submission that by virtue of subsections 17A(2), (3) and (4) of the Act, P is to be deemed to have manufactured the photocopies and not the applicant.

41. Section 17A(2) of the Act provides:

For the purpose of this section, where a person has procured the

manufacture of goods for a person (in this subsection referred to as

the "customer") by another person (in this subsection referred to as

the "third person") in whole or in part out of materials supplied by

the customer, the person who so procured the manufacture of the goods

shall be deemed to have been the manufacturer of the goods and the

third person shall be deemed not to have been the manufacturer of the

goods.

42. Section 17A(3) of the Act provides:

For the purposes of this section, goods manufactured for a person (in

this section referred to as the "customer") shall be taken to be

manufactured in whole or in part out of materials supplied by the

customer if the goods are manufactured in whole or in part out of

materials:

(a) which are supplied by the customer or by another person at the

request of, or under an agreement with, the customer; or

(b) which the customer has purchased, or agreed to purchase, from the

manufacturer.

43. Section 17A(4) of the Act provides:

In this section, "agreement" means any agreement, arrangement or

understanding:

(a) whether formal or informal;

(b) whether express or implied; or

(c) whether or not enforceable, or intended to be enforceable, by legal

proceedings.

44. The applicant submitted that when subsections (3) and (4) are read in conjunction, an agreement should be implied between P and its clients that P would supply the paper and toner to the applicant for the purpose of making the photocopies. As a result, it was submitted that subsection (2) deems P, not the applicant, to be the person who manufactured the photocopies.

45. This argument, however, fails to place the deeming provisions of section 17A in their proper context. Subsection 17A(1) specifically limits the operation of the section to the question of whether there has been a "sale" of goods, and reads as follows:

"Where:

(a) goods have been manufactured in Australia by a person for another

person (in this subsection referred to as the "customer"); and

(b) the goods were manufactured in whole or in part out of materials

supplied by the customer;

the manufacturer of the goods shall, for the purposes of this Act, be

deemed to have sold the goods to the customer at the time when the

goods were delivered to the customer, or were delivered under an

agreement with the customer to some other person, and the customer

shall, for the purposes of this Act, be deemed to be the purchaser of

the goods."

The deeming provision in subsection 17A(2) operates for the purposes of this section alone. Accordingly, subsection 17A(2) is confined to the context set out in subsection 17A(1), namely, for the purpose of establishing a "sale", notwithstanding that materials are supplied by the customer. Subsection 17A(2) cannot be used to affect the scope of section 17(1). Accordingly, the submission that P should be deemed to have manufactured the photocopies must necessarily fail on this basis.

46. In any event, all the evidence indicates that the paper and toner were purchased by the applicant, not by P. Paragraph 15.1.1 of the Statement of Agreed Facts states that it was the applicant which purchased paper and other products for use in providing photocopy services. And there could be no suggestion that there was an implied agreement between P's clients and the applicant for the purchase of the materials. As Mr O'Brien himself acknowledged, albeit in a different context, P's clients would not have known of the existence of the applicant.

47. Accordingly, we find that the photocopies were manufactured by the applicant.

Were the photocopies sold by the applicant?

48. Section 3(4) of the Act provides that:

For the purposes of this Act, a person shall be deemed to have sold

goods if, in the performance of any contract (not being a contract for

the sale of goods) under which he has received, or is entitled to

receive, valuable consideration, he supplies goods the property in

which (whether as goods or in some other form) passes, under the terms

of the contract, to some other person.

49. As the photocopies were sold under the management agreement, this section would appear to deem the applicant to have sold them to P for the purposes of section 17(1).

50. The applicant submitted, however, that because 80% of the material which was contained in the photocopies was confidential, the applicant did not have property in the photocopies, and therefore could not sell them to P. This proposition was based on the argument that equity would impose a constructive trust on the material photocopied, so that the beneficial interest would remain with P and their clients. The central authority relied upon for this argument was Nanus Asia Co Inc and Anor v Standard Chartered Bank (1990) HKLR 396 at 415-417.

51. In Nanus Asia Co Inc, the plaintiff companies had deposited funds with the defendant bank that had been obtained through insider trading. The United States Federal District Court of New York had issued restraining orders purporting to freeze the assets of the president of the companies. The plaintiff companies brought an action in the High Court of Hong Kong seeking to recover their deposits. In denying the plaintiff relief, the Court held that the funds were subject to a constructive trust arising from the breach of trust in relation to the confidential information. If the bank gave knowing assistance to the plaintiff in releasing the funds, the bank would be liable to the beneficiaries of the trust.

52. The applicant's submission fails to place the doctrine of the constructive trust in its proper context. Equity may impose a constructive trust in circumstances where it would be inequitable to allow the legal owner of property to assert full beneficial ownership in the property. Muschinski v Dodds [1985] HCA 78; (1985) 160 CLR 583 at 614 and 616-7; Baumgartner v Baumgartner [1987] HCA 59; (1987) 164 CLR 137 at 148 and 152. In Nanus Asia Co Inc, the funds were subject to a constructive trust by virtue of the fact that they were obtained illegally. In contrast, there are no inequitable circumstances in the current matter which would warrant the imposition of a constructive trust. The legal and beneficial interests in the photocopied pages thus remained with the plaintiff up to the point of sale.

53. The judgment of the High Court in Pacific Film Laboratories Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1970 ATC 4104) provides further authority for the proposition that the applicant has property in the photocopies. In that case, the applicant submitted that as a result of the Copyright Act 1912, it had no property in the prints and duplicates that it made from developed negative film, and thus was not liable to sales tax. However, this proposition was not accepted by the High Court. The Court confirmed that property in a chattel may be in one person, and the copyright in another. The Copyright Act 1912 will deem the owner of the copyright to have property in the reproduction or copies only where they have been created in contravention of the copyright. Where a print or duplicate is produced with the authority of the owner of the copyright, the Copyright Act 1912 does not have any operation. Thus, the applicant in that case had property in the photographic prints, and was capable of selling them to the owner of the negatives.

54. Accordingly, we find that the applicant had property in the photocopies, and was thus capable of selling them as provided in section 3(4) of the Act, and did so sell them to P.

Conclusion

55. A determination in favour of the respondent must follow. In our finding, the photocopies provided by the applicant to P under clause 5 of the management agreement, constituted goods manufactured and sold in Australia pursuant to section 17(1) of the Act. Accordingly, we affirm the decision under review.
