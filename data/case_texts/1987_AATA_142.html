NT85/5085-86 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 142; (1987) 18 ATR 3507; 87 ATC 519 (15 April 1987)

Last Updated: 16 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - allowable deductions - company proposing to build premises to be at by the company - outgoings of interest and rates pending commencement of -construction - whether incurred in gaining or producing assessable income - whether of a capital nature.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) section 51

Income Tax Act. (Papua New Guinea) section 68(1)

Herald and Weekly Times Limited v FCT (1922) 43 CLR 113

Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Limited v FCT [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295 at 309.

Travelodge Papua New Guinea Limited v Chief Collector of Taxes (35 ATC 4, 432)

Sun Newspapers Limited and Associated Newspapers Limited v FCT [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337

Law Shipping Co v LRC (1923) 12 TC 521 and W Thomas and Co v FCT [1965] HCA 54; (1965) 115 CLR 58

Inglis v FCT Full Bench of the Federal Court of Australia 1977 ATC 4,305

REASONS FOR DECISION

Peter M Roach (Senior Member)

1. In these references the Applicant is a company which claims deductions in relation to the years of income ended 30 June 1981 and 1983 for interest incurred ($4.665 and $8,759); and rates (£822 and Si,498) in relation to its ownership of two blocks of land. They were situate in a mountain-side residential area and favoured with magnificent scenic aspects. At all material times the land was undeveloped. The company was at all material times wholly under the control of a family comprising parents and three children and the controlling mind and will of the company was to be identified with the mind and will of the husband and father as Managing Director of the company.

2. The Managing Director (MD) gave evidence. He is an international consultant. and his services have been utilised on large scale mineral projects both in Australia and overseas. By December 1980, he had experienced the chill winds which can affect persons who do not enjoy the security and privileges of a tenured position. Following changes in more senior management, his services became redundant to the organisation which had employed him. Upon the termination of his services he received a sum of the order of $92,000, including approximately $45,000 representing superannuation entitlements. As a married man responsible to provide for the welfare of a wife and three daughters, when he came to he unemployed he set about reorganising his affairs and those of his family in order to make provision for the future of the family. His first step in doing so was to use approximately $12,000 to pay off the mortgage on the home. About $80,000 remained to be put to other purposes.

  1. A company - the Applicant upon these references - was incorporated and its shares came to be held by the five members of the family. The Memorandum of Association of the company was not produced in evidence but unquestionably its principal activity was in the field of "Energy Consultants": the term used in its two returns of income to identify "business or income producing activity". The MD lent to each of the children $10,000 (said in each case to be advanced by payment to the credit of a Building Society Account); and the balance of his redundancy monies were lent to his wife. The monies so borrowed were "on-lent" to the Company at interest. The rates of interest were not disclosed. Some monies were advanced to the company to provide working capital and no dispute arises as to any claim to interest paid in relation to monies so borrowed and used.
  2. Concerned that the benefit of monies which he characterised as "superannuation" should be set aside and preserved in an "inflation insensitive investment" to the benefit of the family, the MD looked for investment opportunities to be achieved through the family. In due course, in pursuit of that ambition, it was arranged that the company would purchase two adjoining blocks in the mountain-side residential area. One block was a standard house block comprising approximately a quarter acre with an eighty-foot frontage to a gravel road; and the other was a block of approximately two acres which adjoined the first upon a side boundary. The latter block had a frontage of approximately forty-feet to the gravel road and extended downhill to a cliff-face. The evidence of the MD was that, at the time of purchase he had not had - for himself or the company - any thought of re-selling the lands at a profit. I accept that without question. As to intended future use of the land, his evidence was "simply that the company intended to use the lands in one of many possible ways, but nothing had been finally decided upon at the time of purchase". Insofar as the contention is that it was his intention that the lands would be held into the indefinite future; that they would be developed in such a way as to become income-producing; and that they would be so held indefinitely as income-producing, I accept it. However, I also find that at the date of purchase and, indeed, at all material times since, there has never been any particularly defined or precisely formed intention as to how the lands were to be developed to bring them into an income-producing condition. At the time of purchase it was believed that one cottage suitable for single family occupancy could be constructed on each site. It was also believed that permission existed from the Council which would have enabled a residence with "granny-flat" attached to be built on each site. It was thought that it was highly probable that future development would be along one or other of those lines. However, it was also recognised that it was a possibility that the lands would be re-zoned, in which case it could have been permissible to develop a block of flats on the site which might have been let to tenants. The evidence was and I accept it - that it was not the intention of the company that the family of its shareholders should occupy the premises as a residence.

5. Having so purchased the properties, the company continued to hold them in an undeveloped state and did not endeavour to advance its intentions either by seeking re-zoning or seeking any building or. other development permit. The deterioration in the Australian economy proceeded so swiftly that by the time the company had found its feet in the consulting business, interest rates had risen as high as 22% per annum, a figure which, in the judgment of MD, made construction with a view to residential letting quite impractical.

6. In the years following the company has persisted in the field of its principal endeavour as Energy Consultants. It responded to and benefited from demand as and when opportunity arose in a market which was subject to substantial fluctuations. The significance of the fluctuations can be seen in the circumstance that from time to time the family had to draw on its capital to meet ordinary living expenses; and by 1986 it had to respond to long-term pressures from the Bank to eliminate its overdraft, notwithstanding the circumstance that the overdraft (then approximating $50,000) was secured by a mortgage over the family home. In response to the pressures from the Bank, the company endeavoured to sell both properties: the asking price being $30,000 for the residential lot and $50,000 for the larger parcel. There was a greater buyer interest in the larger parcel and it was sold for $50,000. The remaining residential lot remains as an undeveloped asset of the company.

  1. No evidence was presented as to payment of interest in other years in relation to monies so borrowed from within the family. It was agreed that, if the holding of the land in the circumstances of the Applicant justified the claim to interest and rates deductions, then the deductions as claimed should be allowed.
  2. Having recounted the foregoing facts it is appropriate to record that the company is to be assessed in accordance with its circumstances and not on the basis of any alternative hypothesis which might have been. That being so, it is unnecessary to consider what would have been the situation had MD used his retrenchment monies to purchase the land and had continued to hold the lots without any interest commitment; or to consider what the situation would have been had he lent the monies interest-free to the company rather than to his wife and children. However, I do observe for the sake only of keeping matters in perspective that, had it been proper to assess any "profit" on the sale of the larger allotment as assessable income, and had interest and rates continued to be paid at rates approaching those which appear in evidence, it may well be that there would have been no "profit" at all. On the other hand, as is illustrated by reference to the sale of the larger allotment, if the contention advanced by the Applicant is correct an entitlement to an interest deduction would have arisen even though the property was disposed of at a profit and even though it had never been committed to any course of development which, upon successful completion, was likely to lead to the generation of any assessable income.

9. It was submitted for the Applicant that outgoings such as interest upon borrowings and rates upon property, being recurrent in their nature and incidence, belong to revenue account rather than capital. I agree, but that is not the essential question for determination upon this reference. In order for the Applicant to establish that it is entitled to the deductions claimed pursuant to section 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act"), the Applicant must establish of the losses and outgoings claimed that they were either " incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, ". As the Applicant expressly disclaims being in any business of or involving land dealing or development it must establish that the expenses were "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income." That draws attention to the need to identify the assessable income being derived, or to be derived in the future. In that regard the Applicant points to its intention, subsisting at all material times throughout the two years of income in question, to develop the properties by bringing them to a condition suitable for letting to rent-paying tenants and so derive income. The Commissioner responds by referring to the fact that not only was there no development in either year which had put the property into a lettable condition, nor had the Applicant in either year embarked upon any course of development which upon its completion would have left the properties in a condition suitable for letting so as to generate assessable income. The Applicant responds by pointing to the circumstance that it is not necessary that expenditure should generate income within the year in which expenditure is incurred (Herald and Weekly Times Limited v FCT [1932] HCA 56; (1932) 48 CLR 113). That is commonly so in relation to a continuing business. As Dixon J. (as he then was) said in Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Limited v FCT [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295 at 309.

"In a continuing business, items of expenditure are commonly treated as belonging to the accounting period in which they are met. It is not the practice to institute an inquiry into the exact time at which it is hoped that expenditure made within the accounting period will have an effect upon the production of assessable income and to refuse to allow it as a deduction if that time is found to lie beyond the period." (The emphasis is mine).

10. The same point had been previously made in the decision of the High Court of Australia in Herald and Weekly Times Limited (ante). In that case the proprietor and publisher of a newspaper claimed to deduct from its assessable income legal expenses incurred and damages paid in respect of defamatory matter appearing in newspapers which it had published and sold. Gavan Duffy CJ and Dixon J (as he then was) said at page 118:-

"No point is made of the fact that the publication took place in a former year, and properly so. The continuity of the enterprise requires that the expenditure should be attributed to the year in which it was actually defrayed." (The emphasis is mine).

  1. The Applicant supports its contention by asserting that its entitlement to the deduction claimed is to be assessed by reference to the state of affairs subsisting up to and at the close of each year of assessment. It contends that if during those periods and at those dates it had the intention of proceeding with development and thereby the generation of assessable income, and provided that a consideration of subsequent events did not establish the contrary, that would suffice to establish the entitlement. The Applicant points to the decision of the National Court of Papua New Guinea in Travelodge Papua New Guinea Limited v Chief Collector of Taxes (85 ATC 4,432) as authority for that view. Further he contends that the circumstance that subsequently the development had not proceeded upon either lot and that prior to the date of hearing the proposal had been abandoned in relation to the larger lot something necessitated by its sale - could not operate to defeat an entitlement which had existed at the relevant earlier date. If the premis is correct, I agree with the conclusion. I now proceed to consider the Travelodge decision.
  2. In that case a company had been incorporated in 1971 with a view to building, managing and operating a hotel in Port Moresby. The company successfully tendered to the Government for the lease of two blocks of land, paying a premium to do so. A Construction of the hotel commenced 21 March 1974 and the construction reached practical completion on 23 October 1978. The first guest was accepted two days later. In the company's balance sheets for the years ended 31 December 1974 to 197B it capitalised interest, rates and rent prior to the earning of any income. It claimed that the capitalised interest, rates and rent incurred prior to the hotel earning income were losses and outgoings under section 68(1) of the Income Tax Act (Papua New Guinea). That section is based on and, for present purposes, indistinguishable from section 51 of the Australian Income Tax Assessment Act.

13. That decision has since been relied on by a Taxation Board of Review in an unreported decision which is the subject of a Ruling of the Commissioner (IT 2,374). As the decision is unreported I refer to it only insofar as its terms are disclosed to the public by the text of the Commissioner's Ruling. It appears that in April 1978 a married couple purchased a residential property which was in a suitable condition to be let. The taxpayers decided to do some painting work before making it available to be leased. The house remained vacant for about two and half years and during that period the taxpayers did some painting work and generally maintained the property. No attempt was made to lease the property until it was placed in the hands of a real estate agent in October 1980. It was leased shortly thereafter. The husband gave evidence (which was accepted) that the property had been purchased with the intention of leasing it to gain rental income. He also said that the painting work had taken longer than expected because of personal and sporting commitments. The taxpayers claimed deductions for interest, rates, borrowing expenses, depreciation of furnishings; insurance and standing charges for electricity and gas in the years ended 30 June 1979 and 1980. No rental income was derived from the property in those years. Relying on the Travelodge decision (ante), the Board found that outgoings for interest, rates, insurance and standing charges were deductible.

14. But for those decisions I would have affirmed the decision of the Commissioner upon these references without reserving my decision. However, the decisions do exist and in decision of the Commissioner upon these references without reserving my decision. However, the decisions do exist and in fairness that requires of me that I should carefully consider the principles applied in their determination. It may even make it appropriate that I should subordinate my view as to the correct principles of law to be applied to the authority of those decisions so that there may be consistency in decision-making, and in the appreciation and application of the relevant principles of law to be applied. But the consistency which is a desirable attribute of the administration of justice is not a narrow concept. It is not to be found simply in following principles expounded in a recent decision. Rather it is to be achieved by the application of principles consistently applied over many years by judges in many Courts and by members of relevant Tribunals. 3y that standard I am certain that the main stream of authority in Australia requires that I should not follow either of the decisions referred to.

15. I reach that conclusion by having regard to two concepts. The first is that income tax is an annual imposition arising out of the events and transactions of a fiscal year. Whether a liability to tax arises in respect of any particular year requires that the facts of that year be established. Those facts will not include matters which, at the close of the fiscal year in question, were still matters for the future and, for that reason, matters which might or might not eventuate. In determining whether evidence as to facts and events asserted to have been true at a given date is to be accepted, regard is properly had to future conduct and events to test whether those future matters can be explained consistently with the state of affairs contended for at the earlier date. But such considerations do not alter the facts relevant to the assessment. They merely assist in determining what were at the material time the facts relevant to assessment. Applied to a consideration of the Travelodge decision, it follows that the critical finding as to eligibility for the deductions claimed by reason of entitlements arising during the years of income ended 31 December 1974 to 1978 (inclusive) was the existence throughout each of those years and at the close of each of those years of an intention to build in the future or, as the case may be, to continue and complete the building project in hand and, in either case thereupon to commence conducting the business of a hotelier on the premises so constructed. The evidence that the taxpayer company commenced construction of the hotel in March 1974 and continued with it until practical completion on 23 October 1978, and thereupon immediately commenced trading as a hotelier lends strong support for a finding that from March 1974 it was the intention of the company to carry the construction project to completion and thereafter commence business as a hotelier. Yet again the findings to be so made in relation to the period March 1974 to October 1978 lay a foundation for a finding that the taxpayer company had those intentions from as early as 1 June 1973 or even, although in more general terms, from its incorporation in 1971.

16. However, the facts of the present case illustrate a significant difference. In this instance, I am persuaded that the Applicant intended to construct lettable residential premises on the sites and to let them and thereby gain assessable income. To that extent the state of mind of the Applicant was akin to that of a promoter interested in the development of tourist facilities on a freehold site who might intend to build a licensed hotel, or an unlicensed motel, or other facilities serving the tourist trade; but who has yet to settle on basic features of the design of any of those particular developments. As to the hotel project, matters which might lie undetermined would be the number and size of bedrooms and suites; their standard of construction and fitting out; the siting of the building; the percentage of the site to be occupied; the number of stories; what service facilities in the way of lounges, bars, dining rooms, sporting facilities and entertainment complexes should be provided; and so on. In that regard the only significant difference between the circumstances of the present Applicant and those of Travelodge is that the design of its hotel complex was probably at a more advanced stage when its tender was accepted. In the Board decision, the distinction is that the property was already in a lettable condition when it was purchased although the taxpayers chose not to commit it to an income-earning course at that time.

17. I next turn to consider other principles laid down over many years by the Australian Courts. Fundamental to income tax law is the distinction which must always be drawn between what Dixon, C.J. called "the profit-yielding subject" and the processes of operating it. (Sun Newspapers Limited and Associated Newspapers Limited v F.C.T. [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 C.L.R. 337). As his Honour said (page 359):

"The distinction between expenditure and outgoings on revenue account and on capital account corresponds with the distinction between the business entity, structure, or organisation set-up or established for the earning of profit and the process by which such an organisation operates to obtain regular returns by means of regular outlay, the difference between the outlay and returns representing profit or loss. The business structure or entity or organisation may assume any of an almost infinite variety of shapes and it may be difficult to comprehend under one description all the forms in which it may be manifested. But in spite of the entirely different forms, material and immaterial, in which it may be expressed, such sources of income contain or consist in what has been called a "profit-yielding subject," the phrase of Lord Blackburn in United Collieries Limited v Inland Revenue Commissioners. As general conceptions it may not be difficult to distinguish between the profit-vielding subject and the process of operating it. In the same way expenditure and outlay upon establishing, replacing and enlarging the profit-yielding subject may in a general way appear to be of a nature entirely different from the continual flow of working expenses which are or ought to be supplied continually out of the returns or revenue. The latter can be considered, estimated and determined only in relation to a period or interval of time, the former as at a point of time. For the one concerns the instrument for earning profits, the other the continuous process of its use or employment for that purpose. But the practical application of such general motions is another matter." (The emphases are mine).

18. If there is any difficulty in this case, it is in identifying what his Honour would have called the "profit-yielding subject". In my view, in the circumstances of this Applicant that was to be the holding of real estate developed to a condition in which it would be suitable for residential letting. There is no suggestion in the evidence before me that the undeveloped land was acquired as the "profit-yielding subject", such as might have been the case if the lands had been acquired as, and had been used for, the growing of crops; the agistment of stock; or even as a car-park or storage area. It follows in my view that the costs of establishing the "profit-yielding subject" are expenses of capital. The expenses of establishing it in such a condition as to be able to "operate (it) to obtain regular returns by means of regular outlay" will not be deductible. Such costs could be wide ranging: the costs of seeking out the site; of organising re-zoning or, if appropriate, of resisting re-zoning; of arranging planning permission; of securing the release of restrictive covenants limiting development; of procuring the design of an appropriate development; of arranging finance; of securing and supervising construction; and of borrowing monies needed to effect the construction.

  1. Consistently with the foregoing the authorities recognise that where expenditure incurred in bringing a newly acquired "profit-yielding subject" up to a condition which makes it more fit for use in income-earning operations and in which the work done does not arise out of the operation of the profit-yielding subject, the expenses so incurred may be appropriately characterised as an expense of capital (Law Shipping Co v IRC (1923) 12 TC 621 and W. Thomas and Co v FCT [1965] HCA 54; (1965) 115 CLR 58).
    1. Having identified what constitutes or, in this case, what was intended to constitute the "profit-yielding subject" it is then necessary to consider whether the "losses and outgoings" in question were expenses incurred in operating the profit-yielding subject, for it is only if they are that the expenses will be allowable pursuant to the provisions of section 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act"). It is not enough that they be recurrent in character. So it is that recurrent expenses such as statutory annual charges arising out of the earlier operation of a mining business no longer carried on (Amalgamated Zinc De Bavav's - ante - High Court of Australia) interest on the mortgage of a farm property and depreciation on farm chattels (Inglis v FCT - Full Bench of the Federal Court of Australia 1977 ATC, 4,305) are not allowable if the business is not being carried on.
  2. Similarly, the expenses incurred in bringing a capital asset into existence and of holding it and maintaining it or of developing it and improving it to a condition suited to be applied to profit-yielding operations will not be allowable if the expenses were not incurred "in carrying on" the business (cf De Bavay's - ante).
  3. In my view those considerations essentially determine the matter. It is not a question as to whether the expenditure incurred was capital ("once for all" or non-recurrent in nature"). What is not deductible - special statutory provisions apart - is the cost of acquiring the asset committed to the profit-earning activity. It does not matter whether the operator of a hotel business bought his hotel as a going concern or bought a newly constructed hotel in a condition ready to commence business; or bought an old hotel and remodelled it; or developed a hotel complex on a vacant site or on a previously developed site cleared for the purpose. In none of those cases is the expense of acquiring a "profit-yielding subject" deductible and in my view that extends to the interest costs and all other charges, whether recurrent in nature or not, which are costs of establishing the profit-yielding subject as distinct from the costs of operating it.
  4. I also observe that in my view nothing in this case turns on the question whether the outgoings were incurred or the obligations so arising discharged "while" the profit-yielding subject was being operated. The interest would not have been deductible if it was liable to be paid only in relation to the holding period and only due for payment after operation of the "profit-yielding subject" commenced. Conversely, the rates relevant to the period in which the "profit-yielding subject" was operated with a view to the derivation of assessable income would not have been non-deductible only because they were paid in advance of the period of operations.
  5. I affirm the determination of the Commissioner upon the objection.
