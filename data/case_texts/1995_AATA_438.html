Schofield and Commissioner for Superannuation [1995] AATA 438 (14 February 1995)

Last Updated: 23 September 2008



SUPERANNUATION - deferment of benefits - election outside 21 days of retirement - discretion to recognise - whether “desirable” - failure to advise employee or rights - incorrect advice given in employer’s statement of terms and conditions and correspondence - discretion exercised - decision set aside.



Superannuation Act 1922

Superannuation Act 1976 - Sections 137, 137(1), 157

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 - Section 37

Superannuation Act 1990



Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 19 AAR 450

Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 19 AAR 563



AAT Decision No 10006

_____________________________________________________________



DECISION AND REASONS

_____________________________________________________________



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) Q93/256

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )



Re: ANTHONY WILLIAM SCHOFIELD



Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION



Respondent



Tribunal: Miss S A Forgie (Deputy President)

Brigadier T R Gibson (Member)

Miss A M Brennan (Member)



Place: Brisbane



Date: 14 February, 1995



The Tribunal:



1. sets aside the decision under review; and



  1. substitutes a decision that, pursuant to section 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976, the applicant’s election pursuant to section 137 of that Act shall be treated as if it had been made in the period allowed by section 137.

S A FORGIE

Deputy President

REASONS FOR DECISION



On 11 August, 1960, the applicant, Mr Anthony William Schofield, became a contributor to the Superannuation Fund (“the 1922 Fund”) established by the Superannuation Act 1922 (“the 1922 Act”). He did so after being permanently appointed as a lineman with the then Post-Master General’s Department (“the PMG”). He continued to be a permanent officer of the Commonwealth Public Service and was an “eligible employee” when the Superannuation Act 1976 (“the 1976 Act”) came into operation on 1 July, 1976. Mr Schofield continued to make contributions under the 1976 Act until he resigned on 27 January, 1978. On 21 March, 1978, he was paid a superannuation benefit amounting to $3,045.08 and comprising his accumulated contributions and interest. On 10 December, 1978, he was paid a further $886.15 as part of the distribution of a surplus following the termination of the 1922 Fund in 1976.



2. Some six years later, Mr Schofield resumed his work in the Commonwealth Public Service. He did so with Telecom which had by then taken responsibility for the telecommunications work previously undertaken by the PMG. On 4 October, 1984, he again became an eligible employee for the purposes of the 1976 Act as he was permanently appointed as a line serviceman. He again made contributions under the 1976 Act.



3. On 9 August, 1990, Mr Schofield wrote to the Retirement Benefits Office (“RBO”) about “buying back ... [his] previous paid Superannuation ... from 1960 to 1978” (T documents, page 47). His letter was treated by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation (“the Commissioner”) as an election under sub-section 137(1) of the 1976 Act to preserve his superannuation rights. After considering the matter, a delegate of the Commissioner decided that Mr Schofield’s election should not be accepted. Mr Schofield applied to the Commissioner for review of that decision but another delegate of the Commissioner confirmed the original decision on 26 March, 1993. On 29 April, 1993, Mr Schofield lodged an application in this Tribunal to review the Commissioner’s decision.



4. At the hearing, Mr Schofield was represented by Mr Ayling of Counsel and the Commissioner by Mr Merrilees, an officer in the RBO. The documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the T documents”) were admitted in evidence together with Mr Schofield’s statement regarding his service in the PMG and Telecom, a booklet produced by Telecom relating to conditions of employment dated 1977 and a similar booklet dated 1988. Mr Schofield gave oral evidence in support of his case.



LEGISLATION



5. There was no dispute between the parties, and we find, that at all relevant times Mr Schofield was an eligible employee within the meaning of the 1976 Act. Pursuant to Division 3 of Part IX of that Act, an eligible employee may, in certain circumstances, and upon ceasing to be an eligible employee, preserve his or her rights under the superannuation scheme established by that legislation. He or she may only preserve his or her rights by making an election pursuant to section 137 which sets out certain time limits. In so far as it is applicable to this case, section 137 provides:



“137(1) A person who -



(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which paragraph (b) does not apply and, upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or



(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue of which the person is to be deemed, under sub-section 58(3), to have retired involuntarily,



may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner, that this Division apply in relation to the person.



(2) ...



(3) Subject to sub-section (4) an election under this section is of no effect unless the person who made the election gives notice in writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, after the expiration of the period that is the prescribed period in relation to him -



(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of the prescribed period and, if so, the name and address of his employer; and



(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the prescribed period a member of the superannuation scheme applicable in relation to that employment and, if so, specifying the scheme concerned.”



THE EVIDENCE



6. Mr Schofield gave evidence both orally and in writing outlining his career in the Commonwealth Public Service. His first position was as a lineman in training. The training course covered a two year period and was followed by some 12 years of work in various positions relating to cable jointing and line service. In 1974, he was transferred as a technical instructor to the External Plant Training Centre for Telecom at Chermside (“the Centre”), a suburb of Brisbane. At the Centre he first underwent a six month training course. He then taught both new recruits and existing employees who needed to acquire new skills. The subjects he covered included basic materials, applied electricity, cable jointing and the installation of Telecom plant.



7. New recruits to Telecom attended induction sessions before commencing their technical training. They were given their safety gear and told about the training course. They were then given a series of talks relating to their employment. Those talks were given by representatives from the union, medical benefits funds and from the social welfare section and the personnel section of Telecom. Representatives from the personnel section would talk about such matters as long service leave, sick leave and recreation leave entitlements. They would also talk about superannuation but the most that they would ever do was to get the recruits to sign the relevant papers to deduct superannuation contributions. If the personnel section’s representatives were ever asked about a person’s entitlements if he or she resigned, they would say that he or she was entitled to a refund of contributions together with accumulated interest. Never, to Mr Schofield’s recollection, was preservation discussed. Although recruits and trainees were given written material on a number of matters, Mr Schofield had never noticed or read any statement that discussed deferred benefits.



8. Although the personnel section was responsible for advising the trainees on personnel matters, questions on those issues would arise from time to time during the course. If the technical instructors, including Mr Schofield, could answer the question, they would do so immediately. If they could not, they referred it to the personnel section through the mail bag or by telephone and waited for the response. If several trainees were concerned about a matter, arrangements would be made for a representative of the personnel section to attend the Centre to talk to them.



9. Mr Don Simpson, who was also a technical instructor at the Centre from 1974, gave similar evidence to that of Mr Schofield regarding the induction programme for new recruits. In particular, he said that the personnel section dealt with superannuation matters. A broad overview was given of the concept of superannuation. That entailed the recruits’ being told that, once they had been accepted as permanent officers, they would pay contributions and would, on retirement, be paid their contributions and a pension. If they were to resign, they were told, they would receive their contributions together with interest. Nothing was said about preservation. In his time as a technical instructor, Mr Simpson said that trainees often asked him about resignation and he always said that they would receive only their contributions and interest. That was his understanding. It was not until the early 1990s that he learned of the right to preserve superannuation entitlements. At that time, a new superannuation scheme was introduced and he learned of the additional rights at a familiarisation session conducted by the Commissioner’s office.



10. Although Mr Schofield said that he did give advice on personnel matters from time to time, he did not consider himself to be well versed in the subject. Most queries relating to superannuation related to the amount of the contribution that had to be paid and not to benefits which might accrue in the future. Mr Schofield felt confident regarding the superannuation advice he gave as it was in the booklet.



11. The booklet to which he referred was that entitled, “Conditions of Employment”, and subtitled, “All you need to know or where to find out”. It was produced by Telecom and readers were advised to “Use this book as a handy guide to ... [their] entitlements and conditions of employment in the Australian Telecommunications Commission.” It went on to say:



“Some of those which are more complex or subject to frequent changes are dealt with in general terms only. Should you require more detailed information use the references given throughout the book.”



If you are unsure about your entitlements or other matters affecting your employment, your supervisor or staff clerk will be able to assist you.” (Exhibit C)



12. The booklet goes on to deal with matters such as separations (which encompass resignation, retirement and termination), promotions, transfers, discipline, allowances, leave, accidents and compensation and staff organisations. In relation to some matters, such as pay, the employees were advised to contact their staff clerk. In relation to a number of matters, they were referred to particular Administrative Orders.



13. One section of the booklet was headed, “Superannuation” (Exhibit C, pages 23-27). After describing the essence of the scheme under the Act, the booklet went on to state that the



“simple description given here is intended mainly for new contributors to the new scheme introduced on 1 July 1976. If you were a contributor to the superseded scheme you should refer to more detailed documents for carry-over conditions.” (Exhibit C, page 23).



14. Although a warning was given in relation to contributors to the superseded scheme, no warnings were given regarding the information given in relation to the scheme established after 1 July, 1976 i.e. under the Act. No references were given to manuals or directives to which employees could refer and they were not advised to consult anybody, such as their staff clerks, if they had any queries.



15. The booklet gave quite detailed information about contributions, the position when an employee took leave without pay and the benefits he or she received on retirement, retrenchment, invalidity retirement, death and resignation. With regard to resignation, it said:



“On resignation a contributor is refunded all his or her contributions, plus interest.” (Exhibit C, page 27)



A similar statement was made eleven years later but this time there was added a reference to a Human Resources Department Guideline (Exhibit B, page 30)



16. At or about this time, the information disseminated from the Commissioner’s office regarding a person’s rights on resignation quite clearly advised employees that they had a right either to receive accumulated contributions and interest or to preserve their superannuation rights. Preservation could take the form of deferred benefits or the transfer of the value paid to some other eligible scheme. (T documents, page 39) It is clear that the Commissioner has continued to give similar advice in the Information Statements he makes to contributors each year. We have copies of those given for the years ending 30 June, 1987 and 1988.



17. Mr Schofield stayed at the Centre for almost four years. He resigned on 21 January, 1978 due, he said, to Telecom’s refusal to grant him leave without pay for a period of twelve months. His request for leave resulted from his being offered an opportunity to work in Nigeria in the communication industry. The offer was very generous. As part of the package, the fares would be paid for his family to move to the United Kingdom to live while he worked in Nigeria. The offer stipulated that he would have to stay in Nigeria for twelve months before he was given married status and his family could join him. He would be able to visit them regularly every few weeks. This part of the package was particularly attractive as his wife’s parents lived in the United Kingdom and had not seen their daughter or grandchildren for twelve years. The offer would enable the family to be reunited for a short period of time. That was something which he would never have been able to afford on his Telecom salary as it did not permit overseas’ travel.



18. Mr Schofield said that he had not wanted to resign from Telecom. When his application for leave without pay from 6 February, 1978 to 3 August, 1979 was refused initially, he lodged an appeal against the decision. Others in Telecom had been granted leave without pay to take up similar offers in Nigeria. In that letter, he pointed to his continuous service of 18 years and went on to say:



“I would like to point out that although T. Cas is a Private Company, they are only a consultancy Firm and have been requested by the Nigerian Government to obtain personnel with experience in British Post Construction, as is the existing construction to be installed is to be to Telecom Australian standard mainly in the installation of filled cables and all other external plant construction.



T. Cas has at this time obtained other staff from

Southern States with the full consent of State Managers.



I feel that the position would give a great deal of personal satisfaction as Nigeria is a developing nation, and also my experience gained, would I think benefit my position as a Technical Instructor with Telecom Australia.



It is with deep regret that to obtain overseas experience that I be placed in a position whereby I would have to tender my resignation.” (part of Exhibit A)



19. Following the rejection of his appeal, Mr Schofield wrote a letter of resignation on 19 January, 1978 (T documents, page 72). That took effect from 27 January, 1978. He said in his evidence that even at this time he intended to return to Telecom after his time in Nigeria although he knew that there was no guarantee that he would be re-employed. He was very annoyed that he had to resign as he felt that he was losing 19 years of contributory service by doing so. He felt that he was losing the “employer’s part of the contributions”.



20. Mr Schofield has no recollection of being given a form numbered S2A (“Form S2A”). By the same token, he could not categorically state that he had not received it. He could recall going in to Telecom’s personnel section and collecting his long service leave pay and he could have been handed Form S2A then.



21. A sample of that form appears in the T documents (page 37). Telecom’s letter to Mr Schofield on 8 February, 1978 suggests that such a form was completed in relation to Mr Schofield but Mr Schofield’s superannuation file has been destroyed in accordance with usual archival procedures. Consequently, neither Mr Schofield, the Commissioner nor the Tribunal has had an opportunity to see whether Form S2A was completed and, if so, by whom.



22. Mr Schofield did not approach personnel for advice about his superannuation rights on resignation. He did not do so as he considered at the time that he knew that the only avenue was that stated i.e. to be given a refund of his contributions and accumulated interest.



23. The letter dated 8 February, 1978 was a standard letter and advised Mr Schofield that Telecom had accepted his resignation. It related to furlough entitlements and advised Mr Schofield of the balance of furlough due to him and that a cheque for the amount involved would be sent to him in the near future. The letter also had a section relating to superannuation and that section read:



“REFUND OF SUPERANNUATION (Ticked box indicates action taken or required)



◻ Your application for refund of superannuation of contributions has been forwarded to the Superannuation Board. A cheque for the amount involved will be posted to you in due course by the Department of the Treasury, Canberra.



◻ You are advised in your own interests to complete and return at an early date the application form for refund of superannuation contributions which is attached.” (part of Exhibit A)



The first box had been ticked.



24. On the day before, 7 February, 1978, Telecom had written to the Commissioner (T documents, page 69). It had done so by means of a standard form headed “Notification of Separation of (Permanent Staff)”. The form showed such standard information as Mr Schofield’s name and date of birth, his designation and salary and the date of his separation. The form went on to advise the Commissioner that “The resignation of the abovenamed officer has been accepted as from the time and date shown ... above.” (T documents, page 69) No mention was made on the form to Form S2A and no reference was made to any attachment.



25. By the time that the letter and the form were sent, Mr Schofield had left Australia. He arrived in Singapore on 6 February, 1978 and in the United Kingdom a few days later on 10 February, 1978. He was very nervous about travelling half way around the world as he did not know what he was getting himself into. From a financial point of view, he was not concerned as he had $4,500 from the sale of the family’s two cars and caravan and another $6,000 from his long service leave pay. He would only be without work for four or five weeks.



26. The family home in Brisbane was leased on a three year lease to an overseas Consulate. It was a very attractive lease arrangement as far as Mr Schofield was concerned. With Mr Schofield’s permission, the Consulate had spent “rather a large amount” on modifications to it. When asked why he had leased it for two years but only sought leave without pay for one year, Mr Schofield said that he had intended to live in rented accommodation on his return to Brisbane for the last two years of the lease.



27. On the family’s arrival in the United Kingdom, they had to work out where to live. Mr Schofield would be based in Nigeria and return on leave periodically. His wife and children could have stayed with his wife’s parents or brother. They decided not to accept any of these invitations in the long term but to purchase a cottage in Northern Wales and close to his wife’s family in Manchester. They purchased it with the superannuation refund of $3,045.08 which Mr Schofield had now received.



28. Mr Schofield said that the superannuation refund was not essential to their plans. He had spoken with the bank manager and had been told that he would have been able to borrow the money even if he had only paid a deposit of £250. His salary package was such that he could live on the generous living allowances in Nigeria and send half of his salary to his wife and half back to Australia. The loan was paid in 14 months. Had he not used the superannuation refund to purchase the house, he would have left it in the bank.



29. After approximately eighteen months, his wife and daughter moved to Nigeria. By then he had been given married status by the firm. The firm paid for his two sons to return to Brisbane and also paid their boarding school fees. Mr Schofield was offered a further eighteen month contract on even more attractive terms than before. He accepted and remained another eighteen months.



30. Mr Schofield rejoined Telecom but had to start again at the most junior level as he had lost all seniority by resigning. Superannuation was not relevant to him as a temporary employee. After six months as a temporary employee, he was offered permanency and he accepted it. The letter of offer, which was undated but which referred to Mr Schofield’s date of appointment as 4 October, 1982, advised him of his obligation to contribute to the superannuation scheme. It made no mention of any rights of preservation (part of Exhibit A). Another letter, dated 3 October, 1982 and offering an employee other than Mr Schofield the opportunity to become a permanent employee, advised that a number of changes would occur were that employee to accept the offer. Of relevance in these proceedings is the following passage which said:



“You will contribute 5% of your salary to Superannuation and this is refundable with interest if you should leave the Commission.” (part of Exhibit A)



No mention was made of the employee’s right to preserve his or her superannuation entitlements were he or she to leave Telecom.



31. Mr Schofield was quite clear that, had he known of his right to preserve his superannuation entitlement, he would have exercised it. He had no need of the money and would not have lost his 19 years of contributory service. When he sold the house in the United Kingdom, he placed the proceeds in a snow ball account until it grew to approximately $22,000 in 1992. He then invested it by assisting his daughter to purchase a house. The snowball account now stands at $5,700.



32. Mr Schofield became aware that he could have preserved his superannuation entitlement when he had to choose whether to join the superannuation scheme established under the Superannuation Act 1990 (“the PSS Scheme”) or under the Act (“the CSS Scheme”). He read an article in the Telecom Officers’ Association (“the Association”) news magazine about deferred benefits. He then contacted the Association’s representative. On discovering that he might be eligible to make a late election, he applied to do so.



CONSIDERATION



33. In considering this case, we have taken into account the principles enunciated by the Federal Court in Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 19 AAR 450 (Black CJ, Davies and Cooper JJ) and Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 19 AAR 563 (Von Doussa J).



34. The first matter we have considered is whether Mr Schofield completed and returned the Form S2A. He has no memory of doing so but, equally, cannot state unequivocally that he did not. Telecom’s letter to the Commissioner on 7 February, 1978 (see paragraph 24 above) does not refer to its being attached. The only reference to the form’s having been completed and sent is in Telecom’s letter to Mr Schofield on 8 February, 1978. Even if we were to accept this letter as sufficient evidence on which to base a finding that Form S2A had been completed and sent to the Commissioner, there is no evidence on which to base a finding that Mr Schofield completed the form himself. It was acknowledged by Mr Merrilees, and known to the Tribunal, that there have been occasions on which a Form S2A has been completed by persons other than the contributor. It follows that we are only satisfied that it is more likely than not that a Form S2A was sent to the Commissioner in relation to Mr Schofield but that we cannot find one way or the other way whether Mr Schofield completed that form himself.



35. We found Mr Schofield to be an open witness and accept his evidence that he always intended to return to Australia and, more particularly, Telecom after his period of service in Nigeria. He realised, of course, that his re-employment with Telecom was dependent upon its accepting him but resumption of his work with them was his intention and his hope. We base that finding not only on his oral evidence but also find it supported by, and consistent with, the terms of his appeal regarding Telecom’s refusal to grant him leave without pay. He stated that he saw the overseas’ experience as benefiting him as a Technical Instructor with Telecom. Our finding is also consistent with his applying for work with Telecom when he did return from Nigeria.



36. We have considered whether his leasing the family’s home to the Consulate for three years makes any difference to our finding that he always intended and hoped to return to Telecom. At the time that he signed the lease, he was intending to be overseas only for a year. We do not think that the difference between the two periods alters our conclusion as we accept Mr Schofield’s explanation that the terms of the lease were so generous that he thought it worth his while to rent on his return to Brisbane.



37. We have also considered whether his staying away for a period much greater than the year for which he initially applied makes any difference to our finding of fact. Again, we have decided that it does not. We accept as reasonable his explanation that, having been required to resign, he did not feel that he had to return to Australia after only a year.



38. We also find that he resigned from Telecom solely because it would not grant him leave without pay to pursue the overseas’ experience. This finding is again based on his oral evidence but also upon his letter of resignation and upon the evidence of his attempts to be granted leave without pay.



39. On the same basis, we find that he sought leave without pay in order to enhance his experience in the telecommunications industry. Certainly, his accepting the offer in Nigeria had other benefits to him and his family. Those benefits were both financial and personal. We are satisfied that the financial and personal benefits were in addition to the professional benefits to Mr Schofield.



40. We are also satisfied that Mr Schofield was very reluctant to resign. He did not want to cut his ties with Telecom and, furthermore, he did not want to lose what he described as 19 years of employer’s contribution to his superannuation. This is apparent both from his oral evidence and also from his letter of appeal and letter of resignation written at the time.



41. This leads us to the question of whether Mr Schofield would have elected to preserve his benefits had he been given the option to do so. We have concluded that he would have. He has not only said so but all of his actions at the relevant time point to that conclusion i.e. his reluctance to resign, his reason for resigning, his desire to gain overseas’ experience that he could use to his benefit in his work for Telecom and his desire to return to Telecom at the conclusion of his time in Nigeria. In addition, it was clearly of concern to him that he had lost 19 years of employer’s contributions and the only way in which he could have saved them was to preserve his superannuation entitlements. We also find that he had no need of the money. He had sufficient funds from the sale of his cars and caravan to tide him over until he took up his position in Nigeria. Certainly, he bought a house with the refund he received. We are, however, satisfied on the basis of his evidence and on the basis of the evidence we have as the very generous terms of his employment in Nigeria, that Mr Schofield did not need the refund to purchase the house. He could have done so quite easily without it. The decision to purchase the house was a decision made once the family had reached the United Kingdom and not one that he had made in Australia when he was resigning and arranging his affairs.



42. Mr Schofield made no attempt to seek advice from Telecom’s personnel section regarding his entitlements on resignation. That is clear on his own admission and on all of the evidence. Like many other people who find themselves in a similar situation, he did not believe that he needed to. He thought that he knew that he would be paid his contributions and accumulated interest and needed to know no more. Unlike many other people, he had some firm foundation for his belief. Telecom’s own personnel section gave that advice at each of the induction sessions held at the Centre. Mr Simpson remembered those sessions as clearly as did Mr Schofield. Furthermore, Telecom’s own booklet for its employees, which had been printed in 1977 and so was very current in 1978, clearly gave the same advice. Unlike some of the other advice it gave, it gave advice regarding superannuation matters without any qualifications and without any suggestion that employees should contact either the staff clerk of the Commissioner’s office.



43. It is quite clear to us that the advice given by the Commissioner himself at this time was accurate. He correctly states the position in his pamphlet. The difficulty for the Commissioner was that the information, for some reason, was not known to the personnel section of Telecom or to Telecom generally. This leads us to conclude that even if Mr Schofield had decided to approach the personnel section for advice, he would never have been told that he had an option to preserve his superannuation benefits. We are supported in this finding by Telecom’s letter to Mr Schofield on 8 February, 1978. That letter clearly contemplates that a person who resigned had only the option of applying for a refund of contributions and accumulated interest. Indeed, the letter told those who had not done so that it was in their “own interests to complete and return at an early date the application form for refund of superannuation contributions” (emphasis added, see paragraph 23 above). Furthermore, even had Mr Schofield approached the personnel section in 1982 when he resumed employment with Telecom, it is apparent that he would have been fortified in his incorrect view. This is apparent from Telecom’s letter dated 3 October, 1982 and offering employment to a person other than Mr Schofield (see paragraph 30 above).



44. When Mr Schofield discovered that he could make a late election to preserve his rights, he took action quickly. We find that he made that discovery in 1990 through the Union’s publication. He did not discover it earlier when he resumed permanent employment with Telecom on 4 October, 1982. We accept Mr Schofield’s oral evidence in that regard and also note that Telecom’s letter to him of 3 October, 1982 would not have enlightened him. Indeed, its statement that his contribution to the superannuation scheme was refundable with interest if he left Telecom, made it most unlikely that he would have found out any earlier.



45. Taking all of the circumstances into account, we are satisfied that it is desirable that Mr Schofield’s late election should be recognised. Had he known of his right to elect to preserve his superannuation rights he would have done so. While he did not make any enquiries as to his rights, it would not have made any difference if he had for his own opinion would merely have been confirmed as Telecom itself (or at least in its Brisbane office) was not, at the time, aware that such rights existed. Mr Schofield’s understanding of his rights, although incorrect, was reasonably based on sources he should have been able to rely upon - his employer’s personnel section and his employer’s statement of the conditions of his employment.



46. For the reasons we have given, we



  1. set aside the decision under review; and

  2. substitute a decision that, pursuant to section 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976, the applicant’s election pursuant to section 137 of that Act shall be treated as if it had been made in the period allowed by section 137.
