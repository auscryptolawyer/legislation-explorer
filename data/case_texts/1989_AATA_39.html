Re Robert James Semmens and Commissioner of Taxation [1989] AATA 39 (24 February 1989)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: ROBERT JAMES SEMMENS

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Nos. ST.86/519, 520 and 521

AAT No. 4937

Taxation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

R.A. Layton(1) (Deputy President), Dr D.B. Williams(1) (Member) and

D.J. Trowse(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - Allowable Deductions - plan to attract Export Market Development Grant and tax deductions - whether purported payments of export market consultancy fees and accountancy fees were actually expended, or a sham or, alternatively, not deductible under s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act because expenditure of a capital nature - purported payment of fees funded by a series of alleged loan transactions and flow of payments and receipts.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s.51(1)

Export Market Development Grants Act 1974

Snook v London and West Riding Investments Ltd (1967) 2 QB 786

John Fairfax & Sons Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30

John Smith & Son v Moore (1921) 2 AC 13

Vallambrosa Rubber Co Ltd v Farmer (1910) STC 529

Case Q42 83 ATC

New Zealand Flax Investments Ltd v F.C. of T. [1938] HCA 60; (1938) 61 CLR 179

HEARING

ADELAIDE

24:2:1989

ORDER

The decision of the Commissioner of Taxation under review is affirmed.

DECISION

This review concerns three references related to the years of income 1979, 1980 and 1981. The matters referred to in the applicant's objections and disallowed by the Commissioner applying the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Assessment Act") are as follows:

2. As to these items, it should be noted that in respect of the omitted income in these years, the Commissioner imposed substantial additional tax for incorrect returns and that such action was also the subject of objection, but those matters were settled by the parties prior to the hearing and is not the subject of review. Consequently, the reference relating to the 1980 year need not be considered by the Tribunal.

3. The applicant's claim for a deduction of carry forward losses in the 1981 year also requires further comment. The 1979 partnership returns as lodged for Sheepskin No. 3 and Trojan No. 9 revealed substantial losses and the sharing of same between the respective partners. It was the sharing of those losses together with the claim for export marketing consultants' fees which created a carry forward loss of $38,486 in the applicant's 1979 return. That deficit, which was converted into a surplus as a result of the adjustment made by the Commissioner, was claimed as a loss pursuant to s.80 of the the Assessment Act in the 1980 return which, after bringing to account various items of income, disclosed a loss of $32,764 to be carried forward into the next year. The point is that whilst the applicant objected to the disallowance of the loss of $32,764 in the 1981 year, no formal objection was made to the disallowance of the amount of $38,486 in 1980. The Commissioner has not taken the view that the loss perished in the 1980 year and was incapable of subsequent resuscitation and is prepared to accede to the right of the applicant to object to the 1980 loss as carried forward in the 1981 year. The Tribunal accepts the Commissioner's concession.

4. In essence, therefore, this review relates to deductions claimed for export marketing consultants' fees allegedly paid by the applicant on his own behalf (a sum of $11,410) in the financial year ending 30 June 1979, as well as export marketing consultants' fees of $70,000 allegedly paid by each of Sheepskin and Stones Exporters No. 3 and Trojan Exporters No. 9 (in which the applicant was a partner). Also in issue is deductibility of accounting fees to both partnerships in the sum of $2,000 each. It was alleged that those latter payments were either paid or accrued by those partnerships in favour of the applicant's accountancy firm.

5. At the commencement of the hearing, the applicant requested that the hearing be public, to which the respondent had no objection. The Tribunal granted the applicant's request that the review be a public hearing and accordingly, it is not necessary for the Tribunal in these Reasons for Decision to preserve anonymity.

6. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by Mr Robertson, who was also a participating party to the matters under review and the Commissioner was represented by counsel, Mr Lawson. The applicant gave oral evidence and called the following witnesses:

7. The applicant submitted that the amounts sought to be deducted were appropriate and had been expended for the purposes of derivation of income by the applicant and/or by the relevant partnerships. For the respondent, it was contended that all of the transactions occurring within the partnerships were a sham and merely devices for the obtaining of export grants and the avoidance of tax. In the alternative, it was claimed that only the transactions giving rise to the individual items claimed as deductions were either shams or that the expenditure relating to those items had not been incurred. In the further alternative, it was claimed that if such expenditure had been incurred, all items were of a capital nature and were not deductible pursuant to sub-section 51(1) of the Assessment ACT

8. The facts surrounding this review are complicated. In an endeavour to give a general overview, but at the risk of being initially simplistic, we set out the broad background as follows.

9. At all relevant times the applicant was an accountant conducting a practice in the suburbs of Adelaide in the name of R.J. Semmens and Co. He was entrepreneurial in outlook and met two fellow spirits, namely, Mr Robertson, an exporting consultant who was the managing director of J.R. Export Pty Ltd, an Australian-based company and of J. Robertson Exports Ltd, a New Zealand-based company, as well as managing director of other companies which will be referred to later in these Reasons for Decision, and Mr Konstas, manager of Trans Tasman Co-ordinators, a business name registered in Australia, and managing director of Trans Tasman Co-ordinators Ltd, a New Zealand company.

10. Mr Robertson and Mr Konstas through their various companies were interested in co-ordinating an export plan based on financial advantages obtained through grants made available pursuant to the Export Market Development Grants Act 1974 ("the Export Grants Act") in combination with income tax deductions perceived by them to be available under the Assessment ACT The role of Mr Robertson in the export plan was as an export consultant, that of Mr Konstas was as finance adviser and administrator and the applicant became involved as an accountant. The general plan was for the applicant to seek interested persons through his accountancy clientele who may wish to become involved in partnerships. The aim was for the formation of ten partnerships. The participants would join in the export plan by payment of an introductory fee - no capital required - and would obtain tax relief in the financial year ending June 1979 and thereafter a possible profit in the following year upon receipt of various grants through the Export Grants ACT

11. In describing the manner in which tax relief for participants would be achieved, it should be noted that "eligible expenditure" incurred under the Export Grants Act prior to 31 December 1978 attracted a grant of 85% for those outgoings but thereafter the percentage figure was reduced to 70%. "Eligible expenditure" under that Act included expenditure incurred in overseas market research and development, including advertising, the cost of participating in overseas exhibitions, fares incurred in overseas travel and expenditure attributable to the provision of free samples to potential or existing overseas buyers for promotional purposes. Therefore, moneys expended by the partner ships prior to 30 June 1979 on various items such as export consultancy fees could prima facie attract a grant under the Export Grants Act and may also qualify for deduction pursuant to s.51 of the Assessment Act in the year incurred. In this way, in the first year of operation, there would be a tax loss to the partnerships which, when claimed by participating members in their individual returns, would hopefully provide targeted tax relief. In relation to subsequent years, a possible profit in part resulting from the export grant, as well as in effect a deferment of income tax on that profit into the following year, could be achieved. Therefore, the timing of expenditure as well as the amount and nature of the expenditure, was critical to the economic success of the plan.

12. The two partnerships with which we are concerned in this review were two of nine partnerships formed as a result of the above plan. The two products designated for export under the plan were opal and sheepskin, and the intended market was the United States of America ("USA").

13. Apart from the applicant, Mr Robertson (and his companies) and Mr Konstas (and his companies), another important party involved in the plan was a chartered accountant, Mr Noolan. A former colleague of Mr Konstas, Mr Noolan became an employee of R.J. Semmens and Co. and was mainly responsible for the accounting of the partnerships as well as being a partner himself in one of the nine partnerships (Emu Exporters No.2).

14. Having set out the plan in general, we will now turn to our particular findings of fACT

15. In May 1978, the applicant responded to an invitation appearing in the daily press to attend a meeting called by Mr Robertson for the purpose of explaining the financial advantages of becoming involved - no cash required - in exporting various goods to the USA. The invitation was headed, "Reduce or eliminate tax bill while making profits" (Exhibit A1, p 26). Indeed, the theme of the meeting appears to have been "Learn how to make money by being in partnership with the Federal Government". It was claimed that these advantages could be achieved by the receiving of grants in terms of provisions contained in the Export Grants Act and the deferment of income tax under the Assessment ACT

16. The applicant attended the meeting in May 1978 and met Mr Robertson and possibly Mr Konstas. Notwithstanding a favourable impression, the applicant did not at that stage agree to participate in the plan, however, he did remain in contact with Mr Robertson and, as will be seen later in these Reasons, he did in the following financial year become involved in a plan based on the original concept as described at the public meeting, and as contained in a series of documents collectively called "The Yellow Book" (Exhibit A1). Mr Noolan also attended the public meeting in May 1978.

17. Following the May 1978 meeting, in about July or August 1978, Mr Robertson, through a company operated by him, Opal Syndicates Pty Ltd, arranged for a Mr Garran McPhee to travel to the USA. Mr McPhee was engaged to ascertain the market for various products including sheepskin, as well as to look for a possible site for the opening of a shop for Mr Robertson operating on behalf of Opal Syndicates Pty Ltd. A site was located by Mr McPhee in conjunction with a former colleague, Ms Gail McMurray; that site became the first leased shop called, "A Thousand Oaks" in Los Angeles.

18. Mr McPhee returned to Adelaide. The shop was leased by Ms McMurray, who as an American citizen was able to enter into the leasing arrangements, and initially was opened in October 1978 by Mr Anton Shaw pending the return of Mr McPhee. The shop was a retail business for the sale of Australian sheepskins and the business was conducted at the shop under the trading name of "Koala and Kiwi's Trading Post". The shop was stocked with sheepskins but there is no definitive evidence before us as to who owned the opening sheepskin stock nor any agreements as to the conditions for its sale. It is reasonable, however, for reasons which appear hereafter, to infer that the initial stock in the shop was probably owned by Opal Syndicates Pty Ltd. The proceeds from the sale of the sheepskins were used to pay the salary/wages of Mr Shaw, Ms McMurray, and then subsequently after January 1979, Mr McPhee on his return to the USA. In addition, the sale proceeds were used to service the shop rental and other business outgoings.

19. At about the time the shop "A Thousand Oaks" was opened, the applicant travelled with Mr Robertson to New Zealand to investigate the potential of opal for export.

20. Mr Robertson was also director of an Australian company called Interior Mining Development Co. Ltd. which owned raw opal. Whilst overseas with Mr Robertson, the applicant met a Mr McK. who operated a New Zealand company called Tai Jade Co Ltd. That company was involved in opal cutting and manufacturing as well as exporting opal from New Zealand to Australia. The applicant and Mr Robertson also met with a Mr McD., a lawyer, and Mr T., an accountant, who together operated as Trade Exports (NZ) Ltd.

21. Following his trip to New Zealand, the applicant agreed to a personal involvement in an export plan and undertook to apply his best endeavours to recruit participants from amongst his clientele. The applicant and other interested participants were to be organised into a series of partnerships. The original proposal was for there to be ten partnerships, however, only nine were in fact formed.

22. The two partnerships relevant to this review are Sheepskin and Stones No. 3 formed on 26 March 1979 and Trojan Exporters No. 9 formed on 23 April 1979. Partnership No. 3 consisted of the following partners: W.H. & A.D. Bartram Nominees Pty Ltd (of which Mr Bartram was a director), 47.5%; R.J. Semmens, 47.5%; and Amazonas (1979) Pty Ltd, 5%. Partner ship No. 9 consisted of: 3 partners each holding 23.75%; R.J. Semmens, also with 23.75%; and Warszawa Pty Ltd, 5%.

25. Subsequently, the nine partnerships which comprised individuals, companies or partnerships were set up and it is of interest to observe that all of the nine partnerships included as one of the partners, a company as trustee of a trust the beneficiaries of which included charitable organisations as well as a company called Soft Wool Products Pty Ltd. This company was a nominee company for a unit trust called The Sheep's Back which in turn was a manufacturer of sheepskins.

26. According to the evidence of Mr Noolan, the decision to introduce a trustee company into each of the partnerships was to enable the performance of a trust strip in the following year when the export grant was received. Mr Noolan maintained that the sharing proportions were to be varied in such a way that the 5% trustee company entitlement in 1980 was to be increased to 95% in 1981. Such a re-arrangement would direct all but 5% of the grant income to the various trusts which at the appropriate time would be stripped. The applicant, however, countered this evidence with the assertion that the only reason for the involvement of a trustee company was to facilitate a possible re-organisation of the business structure in the event of future expansion. In short, in his view, it created greater "flexibility".

27. Bearing in mind in particular the up-front costs associated with the incorporation of nine trust companies and the relative ease in changing from a partnership to a company operation at a later stage, we have difficulty in accepting the applicant's explanation, but instead consider the probability to be the explanation provided by Mr Noolan. Furthermore, an examination of a trust deed tendered in evidence (Exhibit A11) does little to alleviate our doubts on this question. It seems that the provisions contained in all nine trust deeds are identical and that on all occasions the settler was the office secretary to the applicant's accounting company, R.J. Semmens and Co., and that her small contributions totalled $90.00. The nominated beneficiaries were several well known charitable organisations plus Soft Wool Products Pty Ltd as previously mentioned. The Sheep's Back trust had as its unit holders members of the nine partnerships and was engaged in the business of supplying sheepskins to those same entities. Indeed, it was a long and circuitous route.

28. More importantly, in our view, is the rather unique clause contained in the trust deeds whereby any person, company, trustee of any trust who or which is prepared to donate $2.00 to any one of the named charities could, with the support of the trustee, become a beneficiary. It seems at least possible to infer that such an opportunity was designed to cater for the arrival of the strippers' vehicle. When these matters are considered in conjunction with the extent of the premium paid by the participants to join the scheme, we are of the view that the evidence of Mr Noolan on this point is to be preferred. It would therefore appear that tax avoidance rather than tax deferral, was a potential feature of the plan and forms part of the background of the matters the subject of review.

29. Mr Noolan also gave evidence that the amount of the fee payable by each partner was calculated on the basis of their required tax loss which would be encountered by the partner for that year, which was calculated at the rate of 15 cents in the dollar and according to Mr Noolan would give a tax loss of $20,000 (p 328 of the transcript). The only partners excluded from the requirement to pay the $3,000 fee were the applicant, Mr Bartram (through a nominee company) and Mr Noolan. We assume that these three partners were excluded from that requirement on the basis that the applicant and Mr Noolan would be providing accountancy skills which would be used by the partnerships and Mr Bartram would be able to provide knowledge as to the opal market.

30. Therefore the total fees paid by the prospective parties amounted to $54,000, which amount was paid into a bank account headed R.J. Semmens & Co - Clients Account. After formation of the partnerships the same amount was then loaned to the partnerships.

31. After the partnerships were formed, documents record that partners in each of the two partnerships met and the applicant and Mr Noolan were appointed to comprise the management committee for the subject partnerships, as well as probably the remaining seven partnerships (Exhibit R11). It was originally intended that each partnership have a bank account of its own and that all transactions relating to that enterprise be directed through that account. However, it soon became apparent that such a system was cumbersome and thus it was decided by the management committee that an account styled R.J. Semmens & Co - Administration Account, be used. At the conclusion of each financial period, allocations of receipts and payments were made to the nine partnerships by Mr Noolan. In some instances, those allocations were based on invoices raised in the names of the individual partnerships, whereas in others, for example, loan moneys, the apportionments were of an arbitrary nature. The position was further complicated by the extensive use of the previously mentioned bank account R.J. Semmens & Co. - Clients Account. Why such an account was used for purposes of these partnerships was left unexplained.

32. Turning now to the activities related to the partner ships and looking at each of the products of sheepskin and opal in turn.

33. Dealing first with opal. As previously mentioned in para. 23 hereof, there was already apparent trading by some partners prior to the formation of the partnerships. However, after the formation of the partnerships, the situation appears to be that raw opal owned by one of Mr Robertson's companies, Interior Development Mining Pty Ltd, was sent from Australia to New Zealand. It is not possible for us to be definitive as to the progress of the opal following its arrival.

34. Mr Robertson's evidence suggests that both Trade Exports (NZ) Ltd operating from Auckland, and Tai Jade Co Ltd operating from Christchurch, exported "cut and polished" opal back to Australia (transcript, p 52). It would also appear that Trade Exports (NZ) Ltd and Trans Tasman Co-ordinators Ltd each had some arrangement with cutters of opal, namely Tai Jade Co Ltd, which resulted in the nine partnerships being invoiced for parcels of "cut and polished opal" and each invoice had attached to it a promissory note payable at the expiration of six months. These invoices which all follow the same format using similar stationery (see Exhibits R16, R17 and A18) even occasionally having the company name substituted using correction fluid (Invoice Nos. 5072 and 3139 being Exhibit R16), were rendered by all four New Zealand companies to the partnerships and R.J. Semmens & Co.

35. There was specific evidence before us that Tai Jade Co Ltd and Trade Exports (NZ) Ltd were both involved in exporting cut and polished opal from New Zealand to Australia, and also involved in seeking export market development grants in relation to such export pursuant to the equivalent New Zealand legislation. There is also the strong inference that J. Robertson Exports Ltd and Trans Tasman Co-ordinators Ltd had similar interests. Likewise there is a strong inference that Interior Mining Development Co Ltd was also interested in benefits which could be derived from grants for export of the raw opal material from Australia to New Zealand.

36. The financing of the alleged purchase of opal by the partnerships from New Zealand was undertaken by the signing by partners of promissory notes attached to the bottom of invoices. These promissory notes were to be payable within six months. This financing arrangement was put into operation after individual partners were checked for their creditworthiness, so that promissory notes signed by them could be discounted by either banks or a finance company. Such discounting of promissory notes then provided the partnerships with running capital without capital outlay. The export plan was that in turn, the cut and polished opal "purchased" by the partnerships on the basis of promissory notes from New Zealand operators, after export to Australia would be invoiced to USA customers, again using the discounting of promissory notes.

37. Mr Robertson gave evidence that it was essential in his view for the partners/partnerships to be involved in the New Zealand opal trading arrangement in order to establish export activities for the purpose of qualifying for export market development grants.

38. In the months of May, June and July 1979 the partner ships, partners to those partnerships and R.J. Semmens & Co, were invoiced for "cut and polished opal" from the New Zealand companies, which opal came mainly in $5,000 lot consignments from New Zealand in "Sunshine" milk tins. This arrangement was allegedly terminated in about June 1979 when the applicant opened one tin of the opal, whereupon a rapid 20 second visual inspection revealed to him that the quality of the opal was not as it should be (most of it, in his view, being of poor quality and some, if not all, being uncut and unpolished). On the basis of this information, the applicant cancelled invoices. These cancelled invoices include invoices made out to R.J. Semmens & Co and Partnership No. 3 (see Exhibit R16) as well as invoices addressed to persons who through a related partnership or company were partners in one of the partnerships (Exhibit A21). Following that action, Mr Robertson, through his company, Opal Syndicates Ltd, agreed to purchase the rejected opal.

39. The convenience of Mr Robertson, through his company, being prepared to purchase rejected opal raises significant questions. There is considerable doubt in our view as to whether the opal exported from New Zealand was in fact "cut and polished". In any event, the result is highly suggestive that raw opal being sent by Mr Robertson's company to New Zealand was being returned to Australia through the New Zealand operators after little or no processing and the same opal was finally purchased by another of Mr Robertson's companies. As to this possible inference, it is not necessary for us to make a specific finding and we refrain from doing so.

40. In our view, the evidence given by the applicant as to the manner in which he decided that the opal was not up to standard bordered on the farcical and should be rejected. A 20 second visual examination by a non-expert of only one tin being only part of one consignment, which action resulted in apparently all consignments thereafter being cancelled, appears too convenient and furthermore it conflicts with invoices contained in Exhibits R16 and R17 containing 6 month promissory notes due in January and May 1980 which would mean the partnerships and R.J. Semmens & Co were still apparently being invoiced after "cancellation". Alternatively the "cancellation" of opal did not occur until July 1979 as suggested by Exhibit A21.

42. It is to be noted from these financial statements that the purchasing of "cancelled" opal by Opal Syndicates Ltd is claimed to have occurred in the financial year ending 30 June 1979 but the cancellation of some promissory notes appears not to have taken place before 19 July 1979 (Exhibit A21).

43. We also note that "cancellation" of the opal occurred at a time after Mr Bartram had undertaken a trip to the USA in May 1979 after which he informed the applicant that in his view the opal market in the USA was "soft". What effect this may have had in relation to "cancellation" of opal can only be speculation.

44. Taking into account the whole of the evidence regarding alleged purchasing of opal by the subject partnerships and the subsequent alleged cancellation of those transactions, we find the evidence confusing and unsatisfactory.

45. In summary, we are not satisfied that any of the apparent invoicing of opal to the subject partnerships were genuine invoices which resulted in these partnerships in fact purchasing "cut and polished" opal from the New Zealand entities. We therefore are not satisfied that the subject partnerships traded in opal and we are not satisfied that the invoices of apparent purchases to the subject partnerships were intended to create true commercial dealings and legal obligations as between the parties. We are also not satisfied that there was any cancellation of such purported dealings.

46. We now turn to the sheepskin products. One source of supply of sheepskins for the purpose of export to the USA by the partnerships was to be Soft Wool Products Pty Ltd which, as previously mentioned, was a trustee company for a unit trust called "The Sheep's Back". The issued units were owned by three business associates of the applicant each holding 30%, and "investment clients of R.J. Semmens and Co" holding 10%. The 10% interest held by "investment clients of R.J. Semmens and Co." in fact relates back to the units held by all of the 9 partnerships.

47. There was also another planned supplier of sheepskins, Comfort Plus Pty Ltd. There was no evidence which suggested that any persons involved in any of the partnerships were related to that entity.

48. In relation to the activities involving sheepskin products, the first question is whether or not the partnerships and, in particular, these two subject partnerships, ever purchased sheepskins from either Soft Wool Products Pty Ltd or alternatively, Comfort Plus Pty Ltd. There were only two documents placed before us which suggest prima facie that partnership No. 3 purchased sheepskins from Comfort Plus Pty Ltd. The documents were in fact letters from a recovery agent requesting payment of one alleged outstanding invoice for $45.88 (Exhibit R16). No primary documentation was placed before us which evidenced purchase of sheepskin by these partnerships.

49. The balance sheets of the two partnerships refer to sheepskin stock held "on consignment" and refer to "sheepskins purchased" by the partnerships. The absence of documentation evidencing purchases of sheepskins by the partnerships casts some doubt as to whether the subject partnerships ever purchased sheepskins from either of these manufacturers. However, subsequent actions and transactions including letters of recovery as in Exhibit R16, the freighting of sheepskins undertaken by the partnerships and the later attempts to recover freight costs and the eventual liquidation of one of the partner Trust companies in relation to that freight, suggest that sheepskin products were, on the balance of probabilities, purchased by the subject partnerships. It is probable that the various partnerships, including these two partnerships, were invoiced for and purchased batches of sheepskin from the manufacturer Soft Wool Products Pty Ltd on a promissory note basis payable after six months. In relation to Comfort Plus Pty Ltd, we have no evidence as to whether purchases were made on a promissory note basis or some other arrangement, but more probably in our view, the latter, but we are unable to specify what arrangement.

50. Following such purchase, the sheepskins were sent to the USA. The issue which now arises is whether these shipments were sent on consignment to the shops or whether they were invoiced and sold.

51. The evidence on this matter is also vague and confusing. With the exception of the first shipment, all subsequent shipments were ordered by either Ms McMurray or Mr McPhee directly from the manufacturers, Soft Wool Products Pty Ltd or Comfort Plus Pty Ltd. On the evidence of Mr McPhee, he and Ms McMurray assumed that the orders placed by them with these manufacturers and which were subsequently received by them in the USA, were in fact approved by the "purchasers" of those sheep skins by arrangement with the sheepskin manufacturers. Mr McPhee did not know at that time that the "purchasers" were the 9 partnerships as he had only had dealings with Mr Robertson and then later, the applicant.

52. Another question which arises is on whose behalf were Mr McPhee and Ms McMurray acting. The evidence on this issue is similarly conflicting and confusing. Taking into account the whole of the evidence, we are of the opinion that as from January 1979 when Mr McPhee returned to the USA, up until 30 June 1979, he at all times was in the employment of Opal Syndicates Ltd. Similarly, Ms McMurray, although lessee of the first shop and subsequently the lessee of other shops, was also a salaried employee of or agent for, Opal Syndicates Ltd (see Exhibits A22 and A23). Therefore requests for sheepskin stock by Ms McMurray and Mr McPhee were being made for and on behalf of that company.

53. Following those requests for stock, sheepskins were freighted and freight charges were incurred by the partnerships and the partnerships invoiced either Mr McPhee or Ms McMurray for the sheepskins on sending the stock to the USA.

54. There are several possible interpretations as to the ownership of the sheepskins upon arrival and acceptance in the USA. The first possibility is that raised by the applicant's own evidence, namely the sheepskins were acquired and sent by the partnerships acting in turn on behalf of one of Mr Robertson's companies (suggested by the applicant at pp 598-599 of the transcript). The inference, be it ever so slight, was that the sheepskins were to be given away as samples and as such, the costs should be borne by Mr Robertson's company(ies) from the consultancy fees payable by the partnerships.

55. A second possibility is that the sheepskins owned by the partnerships were sent to the USA "on consignment" and therefore ownership remained with the partnerships until such time as they were sold and accounted for by either Mr McPhee or Ms McMurray as agents of Opal Syndicates Ltd, or given away as samples.

56. A third possibility is that the sheepskins owned by the partnerships were invoiced to Mr McPhee and Ms McMurray as agents for Opal Syndicates Ltd., and that upon their arrival and acceptance in the USA, they became the stock of Opal Syndicates Ltd.

57. Taking into account the whole of the evidence, the Tribunal rejects the first of these possibilities, as there was no arrangement or agreement before us such as would satisfy us that the partnerships acted at any time on behalf of any of Mr Robertson's companies. Further, we also note that paradoxically no effort was made by the partnerships to recover the resultant debts, collectively a debt of $170,839, nor indeed to offset this amount from any other of Mr Robertson's companies, nor was any consideration given to the deducting of that amount from the consultancy fee payments allegedly due by the partnerships to Mr Robertson's companies.

58. As to the second possibility, notwithstanding that this is the basis upon which the partnership books were finally drawn up to indicate the goods were "on consignment", we heard evidence from the accountant, Mr Noolan, that this description was used by him upon instructions which he subsequently received from the applicant. The description "on consignment" was therefore a later reconstruction and until that time, Mr Noolan, when acting on behalf of the partnerships, considered that what he had been doing was in fact the third possible interpretation, namely, the partnerships were invoicing Mr McPhee and Ms McMurray who were agents for Opal Syndicates Ltd. so that the sheepskins were in fact on-sold from the partnerships to Opal Syndicates Ltd. We accept Mr Noolan's evidence, and consider that this is the most likely interpretation of the disorganised and ad hoc arrangements which existed between the parties. Also, it seems reasonable to infer that the description "on consignment" was merely later selected to facilitate the concept that the stock was to be used as give-aways and samples and thus ensure the prospect of gaining an export market grant.

59. In summary, on the evidence, and notwithstanding its considerable confusion, it is our opinion that, on the balance of probabilities, the sheepskins were purchased from the manufacturers by the partnerships which in turn invoiced Mr McPhee and Ms McMurray as agents for Opal Syndicates Ltd. Opal Syndicates Ltd then became the owner of those sheepskins upon their acceptance in the USA. This interpretation is consistent with the manner in which the sheepskins were subsequently dealt with in that they were retailed by Mr McPhee and Ms McMurray and the proceeds of the sales were used to pay their salary/wages/ drawings as well as advertising expenses, rent and outgoings in relation to the retailing business of the shops. Therefore, the skins were treated by Mr Noolan acting on behalf of the partner ships, by Ms McMurray and Mr McPhee acting on behalf of Opal Syndicates Ltd. and also by Mr Robertson in allowing the above practice to occur, as if the sheepskins were owned by Opal Syndicates Ltd. upon their receipt and acceptance in the USA.

60. The major invoicing of sheepskins to the USA by the partnerships occurred in about May 1979. It was at about this time that a second shop was opened in Woodlands in the USA (two further shops were opened after June 1979).

61. It was also in or about May to early June 1979 that the economic problems of Mr Robertson and the applicant emerged. The export plan developed by Mr Robertson included that shops set up by his company Opal Syndicates Pty Ltd in the USA would be sold to American residents by way of franchise, and that the 9 partnerships would sell stock to the new American purchasers on a promissory note/discounting basis thereby supplying funds for the continuation of the export plan. However, Mr McPhee was unable to sell any shops and his evidence was that a sale over such a short period was not realistic. The failure to obtain such funding was critical and it was this crisis which led to the extraordinary measure of the borrowing of a sum of $300,000 interest free for a day on 27 June 1979 from a "friendly" finance company, the details of which are set out in para. 93 hereof and following.

62. This borrowing was not part of the original export plan developed by Mr Robertson, but was an ad hoc arrangement devised from economic necessity and provided the basis for a series of some of the alleged liabilities and payments to attract export grants and be tax deductible and which are the subject of this review.

63. Thereafter, the survival of the export plan in the absence of successful franchising and discount of promissory notes in the USA was reliant upon funding to be obtained from export market grants which Mr Robertson thought should be available and paid to the partnerships by September 1979. This was not to be. The grants were refused and economic reality forced the applicant to organise a stocktake of all sheepskin stock in the USA shops in October 1979 preparatory to arranging for the execution of an agreement to sell the remaining stock on hand in November 1979. The partnerships did not receive any payment in relation to the agreement for sale of stock remaining on hand.

64. With regard to the stocktake and the manner in which sheepskin stock was treated in the USA shops, we interpolate by noting that there was no attempt to separate the sheepskin stock received from the partnerships into their individual partnerships which, bearing in mind that the allocation between the partnerships was not equal, would appear to be singularly inappropriate. Furthermore, there was no attempt in the USA shops to distinguish between the stock received from the partnerships from the stock which was already in the first shop prior to the commencement of the partnerships. If in fact Opal Syndicates Ltd became the owner of all sheepskin stock, no matter what its original source, then such failure to separate the sheepskins would be of no consequence. However, it would be of considerable consequence if the goods had been sent "on consign ment" (being the evidence of the applicant), as all stock was inextricably mixed and there would be no means of identifying which stock was sold, which stock had been given away and which stock was on hand at the time of the October stocktake. The failure to identify ownership of the stock would also make it impossible to identify to whom any surplus proceeds were owed. There was evidence before us that a sum of $10,000 was returned from the USA to the partnerships after 30 June 1979, but no indication as to where this money came from, nor indeed what the payment represented. In any event, this payment is after the period which is the subject of review.

65. Returning to the narrative, on the evidence before us, of the stock disposed of prior to the stocktake on 22 October 1979, all but at the most 100 sheepskins were sold by retail from the shops. Mr McPhee gave evidence that a maximum of 100 sheep skins were given away as samples. In making this finding on the evidence, we contrast Mr McPhee's evidence with that given by Mr Robertson who suggested that $200,000 worth of sheepskins was given away as samples, and that this was part of the export plan. We find the evidence of Mr Robertson unconvincing on this point, and merely illustrative of the fluidity with which either he or the applicant tried to explain the activities surrounding the sheepskins depending on whether they thought it preferable to view them as samples or, alternatively, sales having regard to the provisions of the Export Grants Act and/or the Assessment ACT

66. We now turn to address the specific items which are the subject of review, and will deal first with the consultancy fee which was allegedly paid by the partnerships to either J.R. Robertson and Co. or J. Robertson Exporters Pty Ltd. The total amount of consultancy fees for all nine partnerships involved a sum of $630,000 which, when divided equally between the nine partnerships, gave a figure of $70,000 each. The evidence of Messrs Robertson, Semmens and Noolan was that the original plan conceived of ten partnerships contributing $80,000 each in relation to consultancy fees payable to Mr Robertson's companies. However, subsequently, with the reduction in the number of partnerships to nine, a figure of $630,000 was allegedly struck as to the total to be paid by the combined partnerships.

67. The only persons who seem to have any knowledge of either the original plan or, alternatively, the variation are the three persons mentioned, Mr Robertson, Mr Noolan and the applicant. Other persons involved in the partnerships, namely Messrs Bartram, Hill, Kalleske and Karp, did not know of such an amount, did not authorise such an amount to be incurred on behalf of their respective partnerships, nor indeed for that amount to be paid to Mr Robertson's companies. The mere fact that the partnerships appointed the applicant and Mr Noolan as their management committee is not sufficient in our view to authorise the incurring of such a debt by the partnerships, nor the payment of such an amount by the partnerships, particularly having regard to the large amount involved. It is one thing for the management committee to undertake and commit the partners to pay various relatively small accounting and necessary business running expenses, it is another to incur amounts of up to $630,000 without specific authorisation from the partners. The Tribunal therefore rejects that the partnerships entered into an agreement through the vehicle of the management committee comprising the applicant and Mr Noolan to incur expense and pay debts in relation to Mr Robertson's companies in the sum of $630,000, and therefore in the sum of $70,000 for each partnership including the two the subject of this review.

The evidence of the applicant and Mr Robertson suggests that there was secretarial work done by the applicant or his company on behalf of Mr Robertson's companies. There is no suggestion that the salary disbursement was to be treated differently from the other disbursement and therefore passed on through Robertson's companies to the partnerships. To the extent that Mc McPhee was an employee of Opal Syndicates Pty Ltd, it is difficult to under stand why the partnerships should be debited with such a salary disbursement which was clearly not paid on behalf of or for the benefit of, the partnerships.

Instead, at least $10,000 was a payment to assist Mr McPhee with his divorce expenses and for other moneys requested by Mr McPhee in a memorandum dated 14 May 1979, being Exhibit A25. As to the cheque proceeds allegedly paid to an account in the USA, Mr McPhee could not verify that such amounts had been sent to, or received in the USA, nor do the books of account maintained in the USA indicate receipt of those amounts. Further, the amounts incurred for "advertising" by the shops in the USA between March 1979 (when the partnerships were formed) and 30 June 1979 amounted to no more than $5,470.23 (Exhibit A22). 79. In relation to the account of 31 October 1979 which refers to "management and accounting fees - R.J. Semmens 9 months to 30/6/79" of $22,500, it is quite explicit that this amount includes work done prior to the commencement of the partnerships and, in any event, there is nothing to indicate such work which may have been performed after the partnerships commenced, related to the partnerships as distinct from work done for, or on behalf of, Mr Robertson's companies.

96. No explanation was offered as to why H.R. and Associates were paid a total of $350,000 when their debt was $349.827, nor any explanation as to why Trans Tasman Coordinators Ltd was paid $350,000 when its invoice to H.R. and Associates was for $297,800.

97. The information disclosed in the consolidated balance sheet of the partnerships prepared by the applicant as at 30 June 1979 is of assistance in acquiring an understanding of the foregoing transactions, and yet in some aspects it creates new problems. For example, there is no record of the alleged loan of $300,000 made by Trans Tasman Coordinators Ltd to the partner ships on 27 June 1979. However, it does record an indebtedness to that same company of $107,000 (presumably the advances of $31,000, $26,000 and $50,000 made on 21, 26 and 28 June 1979 respectively) and yet it is perplexing to hear the evidence that no claim for payment has ever been made.

98. We do not accept that in the elaborate transactions which took place on 26 and 27 June 1979, a loan of $300,000 from Trans-Tasman Coordinators Ltd to the partnerships was in fact made, nor was any debt incurred by the partnerships. Therefore, considering the amount of $633,000 claimed as export market consultancy fees and which is the subject of review, and $300,000 of that amount allegedly paid by the partnerships for such fees to Mr Robertson's companies, we consider such payment was not a true commercial dealing nor was it intended to be a payment in respect of a true legal obligation. Similarly, with the other payments made, for reasons set out earlier, we consider that each of those payments involved a similar round of cheques in which no debts or credits of any significance were brought into existence, but merely the notional or apparent payment of invoices totally lacking commercial reality. These payments were merely a charade organised for the sole purpose of falsely demonstrating an entitlement to a grant in terms of the Export Grants ACT We take a similar view of the other payments making up the $633,000 and in doing so we are not overly concerned that a precise balance was not always achieved in the exchange of cheques, as between them there existed the opportunity to self-adjust and in any event it is clear that recovery action from one or the other was never contemplated.

99. In summary, it is therefore the Tribunal's finding that the series of transactions ostensibly attending the payment of the $633,000 by the partnerships to the two Robertson companies, constitute a "sham" within the meaning ascribed to this word at common law, and defined by Diplock, LJ in the decision of Snook v London & West Riding Investments Ltd (1967) 2 QB 786 at p 802, namely:

"Acts done or documents executed by the parties to the

'sham' which are intended by them to give to third

parties or to the court the appearance of creating

between the parties legal rights and obligations

different from the actual legal rights and obligations

(if any) which the parties intend to create... For

acts or documents to be a sham ... all the parties

thereto must have a common intention that the acts or

documents are not to create the legal rights and

obligations which they give the appearance of creating."

100. In the alternative, even if we were satisfied that $633,000 was paid to Mr Robertson's companies for his expertise and "knowhow" in the development of an export business, we are nevertheless of the opinion that this amount does not qualify for deduction in terms of sub-s.51(1) of the Assessment ACT. Whilst it is true that expenditure may be incurred in the production of assessable income, it may nevertheless be of a capital nature and thus not deductible (see Dixon CJ in John Fairfax & Sons Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30 at p 34). The outgoings under consideration are, in our opinion, of a capital nature.

101. What constitutes expenditure of a "capital" nature has been well canvassed by the courts and various indicia have been formulated which, if Mr Robertson's evidence is accepted, apply to these transactions and indicate that the expenditure was of a capital nature. In particular Mr Robertson stated that payment was in the form of a lump sum at an agreed figure and there was no suggestion of the expenditure being incurred as part of trading (see Lord Haldane in John Smith & Son v Moore (1921) 2 AC 13). More importantly, Mr Robertson stated it was payment in relation to the setting up of a "beach-head" for export trade to America, and that once this beach-head was established, he would no longer have any involvement in any follow-up. In every sense, then, the payment was a "once and for all" expenditure; it was not to be a recurrent expense. In the words of Lord Dunedin in Vallambrosa Rubber Co Ltd v Farmer (1910) 5 TC 529 at p 536:

"...I think it is not a bad criterion of what is

capital expenditure as against what is income

expenditure to say that capital expenditure is a thing

that is going to be spent once and for all, and income

expenditure is a thing that is going to recur every

year."

102. Similarly, again accepting Mr Robertson's evidence, the expenditure was not incurred in the carrying on of a business but in the establishment of a business (see Q42 83 ATC). The outgoings in question relate to the establishment of the profit yielding structure as distinct from expenditure incurred in the course of conducting that business structure. Menzies, J. in John Fairfax & Sons Pty Ltd (supra) at p 40 explained the distinction in these terms:

"To make a payment to acquire or to defend the

acquisition of a favourable position from which to

earn income or to enter into arrangements that will

yield income is not in general an outlay incurred

either in gaining or in carrying on business for the

purpose of gaining assessable income; such a payment

in the case of a trading company occurs at a stage too

remote from the receipt of income to be so regarded.

To be deductible an outlay must be part of the cost of

trading operations to produce income, i.e., it must

have the character of a working expense."

103. For these reasons, therefore, the claimed fees would not amount to an allowable deduction within the definition of sub-s.51(1) of the Assessment Act as they would be classified as expenditure of a capital nature.

104. Turning now to the issue of the accounting fees claimed as deductions in the returns of the partnerships. We commence by recording the types of services described in the account prepared by the applicant through his accounting company:

"TO: Professional Fees and Services

for period to 30th April 1979 re setting

up of accounting systems, preparation of

partnership agreement, opening partnership

banking accounts, preparation of minutes

of partnership meetings and generally $1,000"

105. While the above wording was repeated in the accounts rendered to the other partnerships within the group, it seems that a belated alteration in amount was called for. It will be recalled that the amount transferred to the applicant was $10,000 (see cash flow statement 15 May 1979) and thus, in order to reconcile the position, it was a simple matter to increase the charge for one of the nine partnerships from $1,000 to $2,000. Whilst there was clearly accounting work done for or on behalf of the partnerships by Mr Noolan, particularly invoicing of sheepskin products to the USA, there is no suggestion contained in these invoices that those were the professional services which were being charged. Instead, we would agree with the Commissioner's submission that the work described, or at least significant parts thereof, relates to the establishment of a business, and that the relevant costs should be evinced as being of a capital nature for similar reasons set out in para.100 hereof.

106. In relation to the partnerships under review, amounts of $1,000 were accrued as at the close of the 1979 financial period, and thus the overall charge of $2,000 to each partner ship. It is notable that no accounts for these accruals were ever rendered to the partnerships by the applicant or his accountancy firm, and that these balances were never in fact paid. In those circumstances, it is our view that the accrual of $1,000 had not been "encountered, run into or fallen upon" (see New Zealand Flax Investments Ltd v FC of T [1938] HCA 60; (1938) 61 CLR 179 at p 207). More precisely, that part of the $2,000 had not been incurred and thus does not qualify for deduction in terms of sub-s.51(1) of the Assessment ACT

107. The final sum is the amount of $11,410 payable by the applicant in relation to "export marketing consultant fees". The evidence on this was also confused. The applicant initially gave evidence that this was an amount paid to one of Mr Robertson's companies for the gaining of Mr Robertson's export consultancy advice. No invoice was produced, and there was evidence given by the applicant that this item was not the subject of a claim for an export market development grant because of the lack of evidence to support such a disbursement. Later in his evidence, however, the applicant seemed to suggest that the amount of $11,410 alleged to have been disbursed to Mr Robertson's companies may have in fact been for work done by himself on behalf of one of Mr Robertson's companies, and that it related not merely to export advice about opals, but also to sheepskins. We found the applicant's evidence highly unsatisfactory and we reject it. In any event, we note that if such consultancy moneys were paid by the applicant in relation to sheepskins, there was no item of income against which that item could be regarded as expenditure. For these reasons, we therefore reject this as an item of expenditure and therefore deductible pursuant to sub-s.51(1) of the Assessment ACT On this issue, the applicant has clearly failed to discharge the onus of proof imposed upon him by sub-s.190(b) of the Assessment ACT.

108. In reaching our findings in this matter, we have had regard only to the evidence and documentation placed before us. In particular, we were not persuaded by, nor have we relied upon, the findings and decisions by another Tribunal or by Courts as to matters related to these issues in making our own findings.

109. We however record that the applicant has been convicted of offences against para.374C(2) of the Companies Act (SA) 1962 for knowingly being a party to carrying on a business of a company, namely Warszawa Pty Ltd and Amazonas (1979) Pty Ltd, for a fraudulent purpose and sentenced to imprisonment. Mr Robertson has also been similarly charged, convicted and sentenced.

110. Further, all 9 partnerships were the subject of proceedings before the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review of decisions by the Export Development Grants Board which refused applications sought for export grants in relation to the $633,000 of alleged consultancy fees. The Tribunal upheld the decision of the Export Development Grants Board.

111. We merely note that the decisions reached by the Tribunal and the Court are consistent with our own conclusions on such issues as are common to all matters.

112. It is appropriate that we comment on the ramifications of our earlier decision that the opal transactions were shams and, more particularly, the need for further amendment by the Commissioner. The disallowance by the Commissioner of the partnership claims for consultants' fees and accounting effectively converted losses to profits, the result of which was to include the following amounts of nett income in the 1979 return of the applicant:

Sheepskins & Stones Exporters No. 3 $2573

Trojan Exporters No. 9 $ 443

As these surpluses represent "nett profit" from opal transactions, we understand that the Commissioner will give consideration to the possibility of amending the assessment to exclude those amounts.

113. The decision of the Commissioner under review is affirmed for the reasons as stated herein.
