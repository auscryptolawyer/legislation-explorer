ST86/608 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 255; (1987) 18 ATR 3704; 87 ATC 844 (6 July 1987)

Last Updated: 23 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION - Whether profit received by applicant upon sale of gold bullion fell within the provisions of s26(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - whether predominant purpose of applicant was to acquire gold for the purpose of prof it-making by sale - gold bullion purchased as long-term investment - onus of proof under s26(1) of Act considered.

Words & Phrases: "hedge against inflation"

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss. 25(1), 25A, 26(1)

Income Tax Regulations, sub-reg. 35(1)

Income Tax Assessment Amendment Act (Mo. 3) 1984 (Act No 47 of 1984), s11

Evans v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1936] HCA 2; 55 CLR 80

REASONS FOR DECISION

6 July, 1987 Deputy Fresident RA Layton

Mr DJ Trowse (Member)

Dr DB Williams (Member)

  1. This is an application for review of a decision of the Commissioner of Taxation, that an amount of $20,422.00, being the profit received by the applicant upon the sale of gold, was correctly regarded by the Commissioner as assessable income in the year of income ending 30 June, 1983.
  2. At the hearing the applicant 'was represented by his tax agent, and gave oral evidence. The respondent was represented by counsel, Mr McCullough. The documentation before the Tribunal consisted of various documents accepted with the consent of the parties.
  3. Whilst the Commissioner's statement which was furnished pursuant to sub-reg35(1) of the Income Tax Regulations, claimed that the said sum was assessable pursuant to sub-s25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1935 ("the Act"), this view was not maintained at the hearing; instead, the Commissioner relied upon s26(a). It was not seriously contested by the respondent that the applicant fell within the second limb of s26(a) of the Act, and therefore the sole issue remaining at the hearing was whether or- not the applicant had acquired the gold which was subsequently sold by him at a profit, for the purpose of profit-making by sale.

4. The relevant legislation in regard to this application for review is s26(a) as it stood prior to amendments contained in s11 of the Income Tax Assessment Amendment Act (No 3) 1984 (Act No 47 of 1984) which omitted para (a). Prior to that amendment, s 26(a) (which has now been essentially incorporated into the current section 25(A)) read as follows:

"26. The assessable income of a taxpayer shall include -

(a) profit arising from the sale by the taxpayer of any property acquired by him for the purpose of profit-making by sale, or from the carrying on or carrying out of any profit-making undertaking or scheme;"

  1. In this application for review the finding of facts depends to a large extent on whether this Tribunal accepts the oral evidence of the applicant, taking into account the applicant's notice of objection contained on page 4 of the documentation supplied, in addition to the submissions made by the applicant's tax agent at the hearing. Having seen and heard the applicant, we have no hesitation in accepting him as a truthful witness and where any submission made on his behalf by the tax agent or, alternatively, where any information contained in his notice of objection may be thought to be contrary to his oral evidence, we prefer the applicant's sworn oral testimony which was subjected to exacting cross-examination by counsel for the respondent, Mr McCullough.
  2. The facts are that the applicant who is now aged 63 has been a bookmaker since 1956. In 1959 he was introduced to a stockbroker with a view to setting up a portfolio for "profitable long term investment" (see page 6a of the transcript). From that time up until the present, the applicant, on the advice of his stockbroker, has built up a diverse investment portfolio which includes industrial and mining shares, real estate and bloodstock in addition to the purchase of the gold bullion which is the subject of this application for review. The aim of the investment portfolio was to build up a capital investment which could be used by the applicant if he retired or became unable to continue his self-employment as a bookmaker due to age or infirmity.
  3. The long-term nature of the applicant's investment portfolio is illustrated not only through his oral evidence of his intention, but also through his evidence that his share portfolio provides a 4% return overall. The applicant also gave evidence, which we accept that sale of portions of his investment portfolio occurs on the recommendation of his stockbroker, whom he visits approximately two to three times per year. Such a recommendation is based on the stockbroker's assessment of whether each investment continues to be good in the long-term or whether, for various reasons including particular problems of a company such as changes in management or perceived future weakness, the investment has weakened and should therefore be sold.
  4. The applicant bought 2 kg of gold on 10 May 1978 for $9,989.00. This purchase was suggested to him by his stockbroker and was considered by the applicant to be an extension and diversification of his investment portfolio to include that commodity. The applicant did not discuss with his stockbroker whether it was anticipated that the price of gold would rise in the future, although he assumed that one of the reasons for the stockbroker suggesting its purchase, was in anticipation of an increase in the value of gold in the future.
  5. The applicant gave evidence that at the time he purchased the gold, he hoped that it would be a good long-term investment and that its value would increase over the years. He specifically said, however, that at the time when he purchased the gold it was not his intention to sell it but merely to incorporate its purchase as part of his overall investment portfolio which could be used, but only if needed on retirement or if he became unable to work through age and infirmity (see transcript, pp 7, 8 and 20).
  6. In particular, the applicant expressed enthusiasm to continue working as a bookmaker for as long as he can and gave evidence concerning a fellow bookmaker who is still operating his bookmaking business at the age of 89 years (transcript p.6a). We accept the applicant's evidence. In our view, the applicant did not, at the time of the purchase of the gold, nor does he have now, any specific intention to retire at a particular age or, indeed, at all. He intends to try and work for as long as he can and unless he is forced to retire through either ill-health or age or some statutory provision affecting his capacity as a bookmaker, he will continue to be self-employed. We also accept that at the time of acquisition, his intention was that so long as he continued to be self-employed, he would not realise any of his long-term investments, including his gold. We accept" that this remains his intention with regard to his other investments, and that it was certainly his intention at the time when he purchased the bullion in 1978.
  7. On 1 October 1982 the applicant sold one kg of his bullion. The reason for this sale was a business liquidity problem as he had not anticipated that the net profit in his business for that year would be so low (subsequently shown to be $19,148.00) and he needed cash for business purposes.
  8. In April/May 1983 the applicant sold the second kg of gold as well as 17 lots of share stocks, as set out in his taxation return. The total proceeds of the sale of the gold were $30,411.00 giving a profit of $20,422.00. In or about September/November 1982 the applicant anticipated that he may become liable for a significant amount of tax and that he may have to sell some of his investment portfolio in order to pay the tax. He later became aware of the specific amount of taxation liability, which was in excess of $100,000.00. He required cash to pay the tax, and following advice from his stockbroker, he decided to sell the remaining kg of gold together with the shares in the 17 companies. Of the shares sold, only 4 realised a net capital gain, the balance were sold at a capital loss, the total selling price of the shares amounted to $99,851.00 (Appendix to 1982-83 Taxation Return) and the nett capital gain only amounted to a sum of $295.00. Therefore, it is apparent that the weakest of his shareholding was sold to pay his tax liability.
  9. It was suggested by Mr McCullough, both in cross-examination of the applicant and later in his submissions, that as at May 1978, the applicant would have been aware that he may have needed to sell some of his investment portfolio in the future, therefore, the sale of gold was anticipated at the time of its purchase. We reject this suggestion and accept the evidence of the applicant that at the time when he purchased the gold he was not considering the sale of that gold or any other part of his investment then or in the immediate future, due to any anticipated business losses or debts to the Commissioner.
  10. In accepting the above evidence of the applicant it is to be noted that his oral evidence and these findings are different from the Notice of Objection which was signed by him on 25 June 1984. The relevant passage in the Notice of Objection which differs from the applicant's evidence is as follows (p 4 of the documentation):

"During 1978 my professional advisor suggested to me, and I concurred, that, by investing some of my capital in gold, I would be protecting some of my capital against the effects of inflation and hence provide me with capital to derive an income during my old age."

Further, at the hearing, the applicant's tax agent, submitted:

".... The applicant acquired two (2) kilograms of gold on 10th May, 1978 for $9,989. His stated intention was to hold the gold as a hedge against inflation. His intentions in relation to the gold were identical to those for the large number of industrial and mining shares also held by him.

[The applicant] is a bookmaker and hence is self-employed. Upon retirement he therefore will be able to accumulate only a relatively small superannuation lump sum. His business is not saleable and hence his income in retirement must be generated from the capital he accumulates during his working life. Hence, the extensive investment portfolio held by him which has included industrial and mining shares, real estate, gold and bloodstock."

(Tribunal underlining)

  1. These statements clearly differ from the applicant's evidence at the hearing in a significant respect. The difference is that the applicant in his evidence emphasised that at no time did he have any planned intention to retire but instead intended and intends to keep on working. Further, he gave evidence that the only time he would consider selling any of his portfolio (which included his bullion), would be in the event of becoming unable to work through age or infirmity or prohibitive statutory provisions. The applicant also emphasised that even in the event of those contingencies occurring, it would not necessarily require a sale of bullion but could instead necessitate the sale of portions of his share portfolio after advice from his stockbroker.
  2. On the information before us, the Notice of Objection signed by the applicant was prepared by his tax agent (see p 29 of the transcript). The submission made on behalf of the applicant by his tax agent, particularly in the use of the words "hedge against inflation", was a gloss made by the tax agent upon what he understood to be the applicant's intention. It is also clear from an exchange which occurred between Mr McCullough and the applicant's tax agent during the hearing, that the tax agent did not appear to appreciate the significance of the difference between an intention to sell on retirement and an intention to sell portions of an investment which may or may not include bullion if the need for retirement ever arose (pp15-16 transcript). For this reason therefore, notwithstanding the statement made in the applicant's Notice of Assessment and the submission made by the tax agent on the applicant's behalf at the hearing of this application for review, we prefer the direct evidence given by the applicant and our findings are based on that evidence.
  3. It is upon these facts that we must now consider the law. It is a well settled doctrine that the purpose spoken of in s26(a), is the main or dominant purpose of acquisition, which must be for the purpose of profit-making by sale (see Evans v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation, [1936] HCA 2; 55 CLR 80; Pascoe v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 11 ATD 108 and Jacob v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 71 ATC 4192). It is also clear that on review, the onus is on the applicant to satisfy the Tribunal that his main or predominant purpose on acquisition was not for profit by sale (see Gauci and Others v Federal Commissioner of Taxation. 75 ATC 4257, Mason J. at page 4261; McCormack v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 79 ATC 4111, and Macmine Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 79 ATC 4133). It is also clear that the purpose which is spoken of in s.26(a) is something more than intention (Plimmer v Commissioner of Inland Revenue (New Zealand) (1957) 11 ATD 480)) and that purpose is different from motive (XCO Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 71 ATC 4158).
  4. The question of whether acquisition of property as a long-term investment "hedge against inflation" is outside the scope-of the first limh of s26(a) has been considered in several cases and with differing results. It could be that the differing results are due to the different facts found by the relevant Courts or Boards.
  5. The case of Firstenberg v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 76 ATC 4141 concerned a taxpayer who purchased a number of allotments of land in an outlying area of a capital city and sold two of those allotments for profit which was assessed pursuant to s26(a) of the Act. McInerney J of the Supreme Court of Victoria held on the facts that the profit from the sale of the land did not come within s26(a). In a decision which carefully canvassed the relevant authorities in relation to that section, His Honour, after citing the views expressed by Fullagar J in Pascoe's case (supra) at page 112, said at 4158:

"To buy property as a hedge against inflation must, of course, have the consequences that money could be raised on the security of the asset by mortgaging it. It would also carry the consequence that the assets so acquired would form a valuable part of the purchaser's estate to be disposed of by' his will or to be divided among his next of kin on intestacy. I do not think that the fact of the purchase of fifteen residential building blocks necessarily requires the conclusion that resale of the properties must have been a constituent element of the taxpayer's purpose in acquiring these properties or that the stipulated purpose of the "creation of a hedge against inflation" could not be achieved otherwise. Nor do I think that it was necessary for the taxpayer to prove that the acquisition was an "investment" within the meaning of that word as understood by Mr Todd, (member). The question under sec 26(a) is not a question of investment: it is simply a question whether a property, on the sale of which profit has ex hypothesi been made, was a property acquired by the vendor "for the purpose of profit-making by sale", or in the course of the carrying on or carrying out of any profit-making undertaking or scheme.

Furthermore, the statement of Fullagar J, in Pascoe v FC of T cited above cannot be regarded as prescribing straight-jacket alternatives into one or other of which every case must fall. Fullager J himself expressly qualified his proposition by saying "it will generally be either with the view to holding and deriving income from it or with a view to realising sooner or later an enhanced capital value". If the evidence shows that that latter alternative was merely a possiblity to which the taxpayer had not in truth applied his mind, that in short it was no more than a possibility, open to the taxpayer only in consequence of his acquisition of the property, and not a purpose with which the property was acquired, then the case cannot be brought under sec 26(a).

For myself, I would certainly not be prepared to affirm that the mere purchase of the property "as a hedge against inflation" must in all cases fall within sec. 26(a)...

In the present case, the Board of Review concluded that the stated purposes of acquiring the land as a "hedge against inflation" could not be achieved unless there was a resale of the land. I do not think that necessarily follows, for the taxpayer could obtain his "hedge against inflation" by the simple process of retaining the land. He had his money in the form of property which would not unlikely depreciate in value even if it were not returning any income. The question whether the taxpayer would sell or retain the land might, in the end depend on whether the value of the land proved to be great enough to compensate for the lack of income. In any event, even assuming that the purpose of having a hedge against inflation could not be achieved without a resale of the land, it certainly would not follow that such resale would be by the taxpayer. It might equally be by some person claiming under his will or his intestacy and the profit on resale is assessable only if the property was acquired by the taxpayer for the purpose of profit-making by sale by him: it is not enough if some other person has made the profit upon the resale.

It will not be enough if the taxpayer's devises or next of kin hereafter effect a resale at a figure which, when compared with the original purchase price, shows a profit. The profit would in that case not have enured to the taxpayer. The profit which enures to the devisee or next of kin is a profit on a property which was not acquired by the devisee or next of kin "for the purpose of profit-making by sale".(See Williams v F.C. of T.72ATC4188; 127CLR226,. See also McClelland v FC of T 69 ATC 4001; [1969] HCA 72; (1968) 118 CLR 353; 70 ATC 4115; [1970] HCA 39; 120 CLR 487, and Steinvberg v FC of T 75 ATC 4221 at 4232 per Gibbs J).

  1. In our view the facts in Firstenberg's case (supra) are very similar to the found facts in this application for review. Although the applicant could not be said to have acquired the gold for the purpose of being "a hedge against inflation", being a phrase which he specifically did not use in his evidence (see p 9 of the transcript), nonetheless it appears to us that at the time of acquiring the gold, the sale of the gold was a possibility which depended on the fulfillment of a number of contingencies (supra at para 15 hereof) which were contrary to the applicant's expectations. Further, the applicant gave evidence that if such contingencies arose necessitating sale of part of his investment portfolio, such sale may not necessarily have included sale of the gold. In our view, therefore, we are satisfied that the predominant purpose of the applicant was not to acquire the gold for the purpose of profit-making by sale. Although the case of Firstenberq (supra) refers to an onus of proof which, having regard to the later authorities of McCormack (supra) and Macmine (supra), may be inaccurate, we do not consider the passages cited earlier at para 19 in these Reasons to be incorrect.
  2. A subsequent decision, Case P27 82 ATC 117 concerned a taxpayer who, after resignation from the Army, invested his money in gold and silver bullion which he sold two and half years later to give him cash to purchase a farming property. The No 1 Board held that although the taxpayer's purpose in acquiring the bullion was to hold it as "a hedge against inflation" his dominant purpose in acquiring the bullion was for the purpose of re-selling it at a profit when he reached the age of 55 years. In our view, this case is distinguishable from the present application as the applicant has no intention to retire at any time and it was not anticipated that any realisation of his investment portfolio may result in sale of the bullion.
  3. In considering this case our attention was drawn to paragraph 30 of the Reasons for Decision, in case P27 (supra) where Mr Pape, with whom Mr Harrowell agreed, stated, after considering the case of Firstenberg (supra):

"Thus in my opinion the reasoning of Mclnerney J who held that the Board of Review was unwarranted in drawing a conclusion from the evidence that the taxpayer had the requisite sec 26(a) purpose would now appear to have been overruled. If the taxpayer's evidence was solely that it was only a possibility that he may eventually sell the property to make a profit, then on the authority of McCormack's case he has not affirmatively discharged the onus of proof. In my view the taxpayer must affirmatively prove on the balance of probabilities that he did not acquire the property for the purpose of profit-making by sale. The mere possibility of sale by itself does not in my view enable the requisite intention to be displaced."

23. Whilst the case of Firstenberg (supra) must be viewed very carefully due to its apparent reliance on the onus of proof expressed by the majority in Gauci's case (supra), we do not agree with the statement made by Mr Pape that "The mere possibility of sale by itself does not in my view enable the requisite intention to be displaced". Whilst it is true that the onus is on the applicant to prove that he did not have a dominant purpose of acquiring the bullion for the purpose of profit-making by sale, it does not mean that merely because the applicant admits that at the time of such acquisition there may have been a possibility of sale of that property for profit, he can never discharge the onus. Each case must depend on its facts as to whether, given the whole of the evidence, the Tribunal is satisfied on the balance of probabilities that the dominant purpose at the time of acquisition was not for profit-making by resale. As with any acquisition for investment, there may be many purposes which are satisfied by an acquisition of an asset and as stated by Hogarth ACJ, in G Williams v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 74 ATC 4237:

"... although the onus is upon the taxpayer to establish on the balance of probabilities that it was not his main or dominant purpose in acquiring the shares, to sell them at a profit, it does not necessarily follow that he must establish what in fact his main or dominant purpose was. If he can establish that it was some other specific purpose, so much the better for him. That is one way of proving that it was not the purpose of resale at a profit. But he may be able to prove the absence of that purpose by evidence which establishes that he had no particular purpose, but merely a vague general hope that the shares would be a good investment. In this case I am satisfied that that was the taxpayer's main or dominant purpose. He hoped to better his financial position in some vague and undefined way... So far as the shares under review were concerned, therefore, I find that the taxpayer did not have the specific purpose of selling them at a profit when he acquired them. The nett proceeds of their sale are therefore not brought to tax under the first limb of sec 26(a)."

The onus is on the applicant to prove that the dominant purpose was not that provided for in s26(a). The fact that there is a "mere possibility of sale by itself" would not, in a given case, be sufficient to contra-indicate that that onus is not fulfilled.

24. The final case to which we will refer is Q109 83 ATC 560. That case also concerned taxpayers who purchased a quantity of silver bullion as a long-term investment to use for the benefit of their children on either their death or alternatively when the children reached maturity. Some two years after the purchase advice was sought from an accountant who recommended that some of the silver be sold, as a result of which half was sold resulting in a profit, and the balance was retained for the original purposes. In that case it was held that s.26(a) did not apply and that the resultant profit on the sale of half the bullion was not assessable. We consider, however, that that case may be distinguished from this application for review in that it was accepted by the Board in Q109 (supra) that the original acquisition of the bullion was for the specific purpose of a long-term investment for the benefit of the children whereas in this case, that purpose is not specific, but is a peripheral consideration. In the present application there is a suggestion in the applicant's evidence that if the capital in his investment portfolio is not needed by him on either retirement or invalidity, it would pass on to his children (see p17 of the transcript). However, we are not satisfied that this was the applicant's main motive for acquiring his investment portfolio including the bullion.

  1. For these reasons we are therefore satisfied that, on the facts in this case, on the balance of probabilities the intention of the applicant at the time of the purchase of the bullion was to purchase it as a long-term investment and that the predominant purpose of that long-term investment was not sale for the purpose of profit-making. Rather the applicant's intention was to retain the bullion as part of his long-term investments, as security for himself in case of future need and for his family on his death. The bullion would only be sold if a number of contingencies occurred (para 15).
  2. For these reasons therefore we consider that the decision of the Commissioner should be set aside and that in lieu thereof substitute a decision that the said $20,422 is not assessable income within the meaning of s26(a) of the Act.
