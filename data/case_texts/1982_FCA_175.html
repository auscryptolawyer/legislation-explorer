Re Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v William Alfred Wilcox [1982] FCA 175; (1982) 63 FLR 210 (2 September 1982)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

And: WILLIAM ALFRED WILCOX [1982] FCA 175; (1982) 63 FLR 210

No. G61 of 1981

Income Tax

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

QUEENSLAND DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Fox(1), Toohey(2) and Fitzgerald(2) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - allowable deductions - whether expenditure incurred in contesting an election - taxpayer unsuccessfully contesting a State Parliamentary election - deductibility of expenses.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s.74.

Income Tax - Allowable deductions - Expenditure incurred in contesting election - Whether deductible if writs for election not issued - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), s. 74. The taxpayer contested an election in November 1980 for membership of the Queensland Parliament. He incurred expenses in respect of the election between November 1978 and June 1979 after he was endoresed as his party's candidate in September 1978. The writs for the election were issued in October 1980. The Queensland Constitution Act Amendment Act. 1890 required that an election had to be held before the end of 1980. The taxpayer claimed a deduction for the election expenses incurred between November 1978 and June 1979 pursuant to s. 74 of the Income Tax Assessment Act but this was disallowed by the Commissioner. A Board of Review allowed the taxpayer's appeal. The Commissioner unsuccessfully appealed to the Supreme Court of Queensland, and then further appealed.

On appeal, the Commissioner argued that because the writs had not been issued, the election had not commenced when the taxpayer incurred the expenditure in question. Section 74 therefore did not allow a deduction.

Held, appeal dismissed, that on the facts there was a sufficient connexion between the expenditure incurred and the contest of the election. The contest for an election may begin before the formal issue of the writs.

HEARING

Brisbane, 1982, August 3; September 2. 2:9:1982

APPEAL.

Appeal from the decision of Sheahan J. of the Supreme Court of Queensland dismissing the Commissioner's appeal from the decision of a Board of Review allowing the taxpayer's appeal against the disallowance by the Commissioner of his objection to an assessment in respect of the year of income ended 30th June, 1979.

G. Davies Q.C. and N. A. Skoien, for the appellant.

F. L. Harrison, for the respondent.

Cur. adv. vult.

Solicitor for the appellant: B. J. O'Donovan, Commonwealth Crown Solicitor.

Solicitors for the respondent: Nagel & Adsett.

J. H. TELFER

ORDER

1. The appeal be dismissed.

2. The appellant pay the respondent's costs of the application for leave to appeal and of the appeal. The costs to be taxed.

DECISION

The facts in this case are stated in the joint judgment of Toohey and Fitzgerald JJ. and their consideration of them and of relevant legal matters enables me to confine myself to what appear to me to be the critical considerations.

The question, and I think the sole question which arises, is whether to be deductible under s.74(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 expenditure must be incurred after the issue of the writ for the election.

It is submitted that there cannot be a contest for an election until there is an election on foot, and, in constitutional and parliamentary practice that is when the writ issues. There is a seductive but, I think, misleading logic about the submission. The English language being capable of an infinite shade of meanings, one must pay close attention to the words used in the sub-section and endeavour to ascertain their meaning according to ordinary usage. The sub-section refers to "expenditure incurred . . . in contesting an election . . .". There is in the first place no plain indication that "election" is being used in a technical sense. Then, the sub-section does not say "expenditure incurred during an election" or even "during the contest for an election". Further, the participle "contesting" is used and not the noun "contest". I think the phrase "in contesting" is suggestive of a longer or more protracted period of time than the formal duration of the election process. This accords with what would seem to have been the Parliamentary intention, and with common sense. Presumably Parliament intended to make deductible the cost to a candidate or potential candidate of his expenses in contesting an election. The period between the issue of a writ and the day of "holding the election" is often very short, and potential candidates, if they are to hope for success, will often have to spend money relative to the election before the issue of the writ. I agree in this respect with what was said by the learned judge and by Dr. Gerber, whose decision as a member of Taxation Board of Review No. 3 in the present case was concurred in by the other members of the Board.

Section 74(1) contains two limbs. I have been discussing the second. The first provides for the deductibility of expenditure incurred in being elected. It has been accepted by counsel for the parties that the first limb refers to a successful candidate who is elected, and the second to an unsuccessful one, and I see no reason to disagree with that view. This is not to say that the second limb may not also apply to a successful candidate. The point which should be made for present purposes is that the phrase "in being elected" does not limit expenses to those incurred during the election process, any more than does the second limb. The construction which I prefer does not create any anomaly or imbalance between the two limbs. In accordance with long-established authority, the words "incurred in", as relating to both limbs, should be read as meaning "incurred in the course of".

It is not necessary to go further for the purpose of the present case. There has been no debate as to the nature of the test which should be applied once it is determined that some expenses which pre-date the issue of the writ may be deductible. I would expect the decisions in relation to s.51(1) to provide the principal guide, notwithstanding differences between that sub-section and s.74(1). A submission that some of the items allowed as deductions in this case by the learned judge and by the Board of Review should not be allowed has been expressly disclaimed.

I am aware that the conclusion I have reached may permit of the deduction of expenditure incurred before the issue of a writ for an election, even although the potential candidate does not nominate as a contestant for the election. This is an unlikely situation, just as it is unlikely that, having nominated, he does not thereafter take any part in the election. The construction of the sub-section cannot be influenced either way by events of such remote possibility. The tests which govern the relationship between expenditure and an election, but which were not debated before us, will limit even further the deductibility of expenses in the situations I have mentioned.

I agree with the orders proposed.

This is an appeal brought by leave by the Commissioner of Taxation from a judgment of the Supreme Court of Queensland. The Supreme Court dismissed with costs an appeal by the Commissioner from a decision of a Board of Review. The Board had held that, in the year of income ended 30 June 1979, the taxpayer was entitled to a deduction in respect of expenditure totalling $1185.00. The basis upon which the deduction was allowed was that such expenditure had been incurred by the taxpayer in that year of income "in contesting an election for membership" of the Queensland Parliament. Expenditure incurred in contesting an election for membership of a State Parliament is an allowable deduction by virtue of sub-s. 74(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936, ("the Act"). That sub-section reads:

"74.(1) Expenditure incurred in the year of income by the taxpayer in being elected as a member, or in contesting an election for membership, of the Parliament, of the Parliament of a State or of the Legislative Assembly of the Northern Territory of Australia shall be an allowable deduction."

The taxpayer had unsuccessfully contested the electorate of Salisbury in general elections for the Queensland Parliament which were held in 1974 and 1977. On 28 September 1978, he was officially endorsed as his party's candidate in the same electorate for the next election, which, by virtue of s.2 of the Constitution Act Amendment Act, 1890, (Qld), had to be held by the end of 1980. The expenditure in respect of which a deduction was claimed was incurred by the taxpayer between November 1978 and 30 June 1979. During that period, no date was fixed for the holding of an election, and no other candidate in respect of the electorate of Salisbury was announced. The taxpayer anticipated that his principal opponent would be the sitting member, who had defeated him in the elections in 1974 and 1977. The writs for the 1980 elections were issued on 27 October 1980; the taxpayer submitted his nomination on 3 November 1980; the closing date for nominations was 7 November 1980; the polling date was 29 November 1980; the poll was declared on 8 December 1980; and the writs were returned on 10 January 1981.

The debate throughout has centred upon whether or not the expenditure by the taxpayer was incurred too early to be deductible under sub-s. 74(1) of the Act. The principal submission for the Commissioner was that the only expenditure in respect of which a deduction is allowable under sub-s. 74(1) is expenditure incurred in the course of the contest which takes place during an election; that an election begins only on the issue of the writ: see Hodge v. The King [1907] HCA 68; (1907) 5 C.L.R. 373, at p.379 and Vardon v. O'Loghlin [1907] HCA 69; (1907) 5 C.L.R. 201, at p. 211; and that an election cannot be contested prior to the issue of the writ since, until then, there is no election in progress. The Commissioner sought to relate the commencement of the election contest to the taxpayer's nomination, a date necessarily subsequent to the issue of the writ.

It is the received view that if a loss or outgoing is to be deductible under sub-s. 51(1) of the Act as "incurred in gaining or producing . . . assessible income", it must be incurred in the course of doing so: see, for example, Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1935] HCA 81; (1975) 54 C.L.R. 295, at pp. 303, 309 and Ronpibon Tin N.L. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 C.L.R. 47 at pp. 56-57. We accept the Commissioner's submission that equally expenditure must have been incurred in the course of contesting an election if, in the present circumstances, it is to be deductible under sub-s. 74(1) of the Act. That, however, is not the same thing as saying that the expenditure must have been incurred in the course of an election in the sense contended for by the Commissioner, i.e., during a period delineated by reference to the issue and return of the writ for the election. The central feature of an election viewed as a contest is the voting at the poll which takes place during the electoral process initiated by the issue of the writ and terminated by its return. We see no reason either in logic or in the language of sub-s. 74(1) of the Act for denying that a contest in respect of an election, at least an election which must occur, may begin notwithstanding that the formal election process has not commenced. Conversely, expenditure incurred after the poll but before the return of the writ is not likely to have been incurred in contesting an election.

The Court was referred to a number of English decisions concerned with petitions alleging corrupt and illegal electoral practices. Some of these recognize that work may begin and expenses may be incurred in connection with an election before the occurrence of a vacancy or the issue of a writ (see for instance Channell J. in The Borough of Great Yarmouth Case (1906) 5 O'M & H 176 at p.188). However the language of the legislation with which the courts were concerned differs from that in the present case and we prefer to base our decision upon the language of sub-s. 74(1) itself.

Counsel for the Commissioner handed up certain material concerning proceedings of the Commonwealth Parliament in 1936 when sub-s. 74(1) was passed in its present form, so far as is now relevant. We have no need to express a view as to the admissibility of such material. Whether or not the separate provision made in respect of a successful candidate by sub-s. 74(1), by which a deduction is allowed of expenditure incurred in the year of income "by the taxpayer in being elected as a member", is either tautologous or a mere historical anomaly, the language of that part of sub-s. 74(1) last quoted does not of itself support the contention that only expenditure after formal commencement of an election is deductible by an unsuccessful candidate. That part of sub-s. 74(1) is in no respect inconsistent with the view which we take that such expenditure may be incurred in contesting an election although the election process has not been commenced.

The Commissioner's alternative submission was also centred upon the time at which the expenditures were incurred by the taxpayer. No real argument was addressed to the question whether sub-s. 74(1) is concerned with the purpose of expenditures or their character or both, and we think that we should not pass on that subject in the circumstances. In the context of sub-s. 74(1) the purpose and character of expenditures may not always be clearly separate questions, and both may be among the relevant factors for consideration.

The Commissioner's submission was that, on the facts of the present case, the proper conclusion was that the taxpayer's expenditures were not made in the course of contesting the 1980 elections in respect of the electorate of Salisbury but preparatory to contesting that election. The thrust of the argument was that, even if it is not possible to define a point of time which marks the beginning of all election contests for the purpose of sub-s. 74(1) of the Act, in each case there must be a time prior to which expenditures of a taxpayer are not properly described as incurred in contesting the election. On the facts of this case, according to the Commissioner, the taxpayer's expenditures can be seen to fall on the wrong side of the boundary, wherever it is to be drawn. Marshalled against the taxpayer are a formidable array of relevant considerations. For example, the expenditures occurred up to two years and not less than about one and a half years before the election in fact occurred. When the expenditures were incurred, no more was known than that an election must take place at some time in the future, certainly no later than when it did. There was no opposing candidate. The electoral rolls had not closed, and there would be some changes to the eligible voters in the electorate. The taxpayer might die, have his endorsement withdrawn by his party, or become ineligible to stand in the electorate. He might simply change his mind and decide not to nominate. On the other hand, the facts that the taxpayer had been chosen as his party's candidate and his candidacy announced are entitled to considerable weight.

The Board accepted that there was a sufficient connection between the expenditures claimed and the taxpayer's unsuccessful attempt to win the electorate, a view shared by the learned judge and not challenged on the facts before us. In the circumstances, it would need to be an exceptionally clear case before this Court could arrive at a contrary conclusion. This is not such a case.

Accordingly, in our opinion the appeal fails and must be dismissed. The Commissioner must pay the taxpayer's costs of the application for leave to appeal and of the appeal, to be taxed.
