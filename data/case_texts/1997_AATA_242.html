REASONS FOR DECISION

17 July 1997 J Block, Senior Member



1. (a) The Applicant is one of three beneficiaries in and the owner of 50% of the issued units in a unit trust (“the Trust”); the Trust is (and has since March 1990) been the owner of a large shopping centre, which is referred to as the C Shopping Centre. In accordance with clause 4 of T2 (page 4) of the T Documents, the C Shopping Centre has an area of approximately 27,000 square metres, approximately 85 tenants, and gross rental of $5.6 million per annum. However, (although nothing turns on this) T4 (page 20) of the T Documents reveals that the C Shopping Centre has two supermarkets, two discount department stores, five food court outlets, two cafes and approximately 90 specialty shops.

(b) A company, which is referred to as “LD”, is the owner of an area of land (the "B site") which is approximately 600 metres to the east of the C Shopping Centre. The B site was originally zoned for retail use; however the relevant zoning approval included a floor area size constraint of approximately 20,000 square metres and in addition a requirement that any development include the incorporation of a department store.

(c) Subsequent to the acquisition of the C Centre by the Trust, LD obtained an amendment to the zoning approval in respect of the B site from a Shire Council in Queensland, which is referred to as the "Council". That amendment both increased the floor area size allowed to approximately 27,000 square metres and in addition removed the requirement as to the incorporation in the development of a department store.

(d) In consequence of the amendment to the rezoning approval, the Trust instituted legal proceedings (“the legal proceedings”) in the Planning and Environment Court held at Brisbane, seeking certain orders as to the rezoning amendment. The Trust contended, inter alia, that the amended rezoning approval did not accord with the original rezoning deed dated 17 September 1995. The legal proceedings were, however, rendered ineffective as a result of legislation passed by the State Government of Queensland and were in consequence withdrawn. In respect of the legal proceedings, the Trust incurred costs of $629,842 (the "costs amount"); the Applicant claimed a deduction on income account for its share of the costs amount in respect of its substituted year of income ending 31 December 1994.

2. (a) A private binding ruling request dated 15 September 1995 was lodged on behalf of the beneficiaries, and including the Applicant, in the Trust; that request, made under section 14ZAG of the Taxation Administration Act ("TAA"), sought a decision from the Respondent as to whether the costs amount was an allowable deduction under section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“ITAA”).

(b) A private binding ruling was issued on 20 May 1996; in accordance therewith the Respondent ruled that the expenditure incurred was of a capital nature; an objection was lodged on 25 June 1996.

(c) On 20 September 1996, a Request for an Objection Decision was made by the Applicant under section 14ZYA of TAA; because the Respondent did not make an objection decision within 60 days after the receipt of such Request for an Objection Decision, the Respondent was deemed to have disallowed the objection under section 14ZYA(3) of TAA.

(d) The Applicant sought the review by the Tribunal of the deemed disallowance of its objection.

3. The Applicant was represented by Mr Brian Lawrence, a tax agent, of Coopers & Lybrand, Sydney office, and the Respondent was represented by Mr Ian Marriott of the Australian Taxation Office. The Tribunal received into evidence the documents (T Documents) lodged under section 37(1)(a) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act and in addition two exhibits; Exhibit A1 is a copy of a letter dated 11 June 1993 addressed by the Manager of the C Shopping Centre to the Applicant and Exhibit A2 is a memorandum referable to the C Shopping Centre addressed to the Property Committee dated 29 September 1994.

4. There was no other evidence and in particular no oral evidence. The matter was thus argued on the papers before the Tribunal, and with particular reference to a number of decided cases. It is convenient, at this juncture, to refer to one agreed aspect, and other relevant matters:

(a) At the commencement of the hearing Mr Marriott on behalf of the Respondent accepted the quantum of the costs amount alleged by the Applicant to have been incurred in respect of the legal proceedings.

(b) The Applicant in the legal proceedings sought orders, inter alia, that the development of the B site, otherwise than in accordance with the zoning described by the rezoning deed dated 17 September 1985, was unlawful, and that purported amendments to the zoning of the B site by three deeds of variation dated 16 May 1986, 23 March 1991 and 25 March 1994 to which the Council was a party were unlawful and of no force or effect.

The legal proceedings were thus directed not against the original zoning approval but rather against the “purported amendments” to the zoning approval made by the three subsequently executed deeds of variation previously referred to.

There was no evidence of any nature before the Tribunal as to whether proceedings of this nature occur frequently or rarely.

(c) It was contended that the legal proceedings did not seek to eliminate competition from the B site but rather to minimise its effect, having regard to the fact that the B site had at all relevant times a zoning approval for approximately 20,000 square metres.

That contention is contradicted to some extent by clause 6 of the private ruling request which (page 4 of the T documents; edited so as to preserve anonymity) reads as follows:

“An area of land referred to as the B site is located approximately 600 metres east of the C Shopping Centre. The B site was originally zoned for retail uses but the zoning approval had a size constraint of approximately 20,000 square metres. The zoning approval also contained various conditions including the need to incorporate a full department store into the development. On that basis the trust considered that the development of the B site was probably not viable and it was unlikely to cause a threat to the C Shopping Centre area. Therefore it was considered that the C Park Shopping Centre would be the main shopping centre in its catchment area.” (Emphasis added)

Reference is also made in this context to the penultimate paragraph on page 2 of Exhibit A1 which queried the feasibility of the development of the B site. Exhibit A1 was issued prior to, although it foreshadowed the institution of, the legal proceedings.

(d) Page 54 of the T documents indicates that in respect of the original rezoning approval (which would have allowed approximately 20,000 square metres of floor area including a department store) a department store would require approximately 8,000 square metres thus allowing only 12,000 square metres for specialty shops.

Argument before the Tribunal indicated that in respect of a large shopping centre development, the rent paid by specialty shops will usually be higher (by reference to area let) than that paid by an anchor department store or other similar tenant. It is in this context perhaps that the Trust did not view the B site, with its original zoning approval, as significant competition or as commercially viable.

(e) It was argued on behalf of the Applicant (and the Tribunal accepts) that a development of the B site in accordance with the amended rezoning approval was not likely to be fatal to the C Shopping Centre, and that therefore the C Shopping Centre was not faced with what was referred to as a "life or death" situation. The perceived threat was however treated as significant, and of sufficient importance to warrant the institution of the legal proceedings. Indeed the Impact Analysis, which commences at page 46 of the T Documents indicated that a significant threat (varying in accordance with three different scenarios) was perceived; sections 3.2 to 3.5 of the Impact Analysis are quoted (edited to preserve anonymity) as follows:

The Impact Analysis makes it clear that in relation to each or any of the scenarios analysed, the Trust was concerned with a threat, which while it was not perceived as fatal, was seen to be significant, and when one takes into account that real property of this nature is often valued by reference to its rental return (capitalised by reference to prevailing capitalisation rates) one can readily see why. (See also page 35, lines 37 and 38, of the Transcript).

(f) There was argument before the Tribunal as to that which would be affected by the competitive threat. The Tribunal considers that relevantly, for the purpose of this matter, the threat perceived by the Trust related to the structure. A decline in turnover would inevitably affect the C Shopping Centre, which is the profit earning structure, indirectly and perhaps after a lapse of time, in respect of its ability to let vacant shops at current or increased levels and its ability to achieve rent increases, and directly in relation to turnover-related rents. (There was no evidence before the Tribunal as to the extent which tenants of the C Shopping Centre paid, at the relevant time, turnover related rentals.) The Tribunal does not accept a contention by Mr Lawrence that “the purpose of the objection here was unrelated to the capital asset i.e. the nature of the property itself.” (See Transcript page 719).

(g) The Tribunal accepts that the legal proceedings would not result in an enlargement of the C Shopping Centre.

(h) Insofar as there were references during argument to the fact that after the legal proceedings were abandoned the C Shopping Centre operated profitably, the Tribunal finds that it must look to the position at the time when the costs amount was incurred. This was accepted by Mr Lawrence (see page 35, lines 33 and 34 of the Transcript).

(i) It was argued on behalf of the Applicants that the costs amount (in respect of the legal proceedings) could be treated as analogous to the costs of an advertising campaign, and generally on income account. The Tribunal does not accept that such an analogy is correct, and considers that the legal proceedings are best and most closely characterised as akin to the proceedings in Broken Hill Theatres (more fully referred to later in these Reasons).

5. The Tribunal now turns to a consideration of the cases, a considerable number of which were listed by the parties, not all of which were referred to in argument.

6. The starting point for a consideration of the issues must always be the decision of the High Court in Sun Newspapers Limited and Associated Newspapers Limited v FC of T [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337. In that case, and in very broad terms, the appellants paid a substantial sum to a rival publisher to prevent the publication of a rival newspaper. The following quotations from that decision are particularly helpful:

(a) Latham J at 355/6:

"It is true that the payments did not result in obtaining a new capital asset of a material nature, but they did obtain a very real benefit or advantage for the companies, namely, the exclusion of what might have been serious competition. When the words "permanent" or "enduring" are used in this connection it is not meant that the advantage which will be obtained will last forever. The distinction which is drawn is that between more or less recurrent expenses involved in running a business and an expenditure for the benefit of the business as a whole: See per Rowlatt J. in Anglo-Persian Oil Co Ltd v Dale (6), where consideration is given to the significance of the word "enduring" in this connection. The effect of the payment was to enlarge the goodwill of the enterprise, which was one of its most valuable assets. There is no doubt that the goodwill of the Sun newspaper became worth very much more as the result of the agreement which prevented the publication of a competitive newspaper within the same area, possibly at a lower price, by persons who had the control of a press and the necessary plant, together with a newspaper organisation in being."

(b) Dixon J (as he then was) at 362/3:

"But the idea of recurrence and the idea of endurance or continuance over a duration of time both depend on degree and comparison. As to the first it has been said it is not a question of recurring every year or every accounting period; but "the real test is between expenditure which is made to meet a continuous demand, as opposed to an expenditure which is made once for all" (per Rowlatt J., Ounsworth v Vickets Ltd (2)). By this I understand that the expenditure is to be considered of a revenue nature if its purpose brings it within the very wide class of things which in the aggregate form the constant demand which must be answered out of the returns of a trade or its circulating capital and that actual recurrence of the specific thing need not take place or be expected as likely. Thus in Anglo-Persian Oil Co. Ltd v Dale (3) the establishment and reorganization of agencies formed part of the class of things making the continuous or constant demand for expenditure, but the given transaction was of a magnitude and precise description unlikely again to be encountered. Recurrence is not a test, it is no more than a consideration the weight of which depends upon the nature of the expenditure.

Again, the lasting character of the advantage is not necessarily a determining factor.”

(c) Dixon J at 363-364:

“The facts of the present case show the following features: (i) The expenditure was of a large sum incurred to remove finally the competition feared from the Star and actually experienced from the World. (ii) It could be regarded as recurrent only in the sense that the risk of a competitor arising must always be theoretically present and that the reality or imminence of the risk depends upon circumstances which can never clearly be foreseen. (iii) The chief object of the expenditure was to preserve from immediate impairment and dislocation the existing business organisation of the taxpayers. (vi) The impairment or dislocation feared involved in lowering of selling price, a loss of circulation, a change in advertising rates and a reorganisation of selling and production arrangements all of a lasting character; that is, the changes would be of indefinite duration and their effects would continue until they disappeared under influences brought by the future the exact nature of which could not be foreseen. (v) The transaction involved the acquisition for a cash consideration of the right to enjoy for three years all the property tangible and intangible of an existing undertaking, that is, the acquisition of a going concern for a period, a thing recognised as a capital asset. The advantage in terms of profit was not to be obtained by the use of the undertaking but by putting it out of use; but in itself it remained a capital asset.

In these circumstances I think that in principle the transaction must be regarded as strengthening and preserving the business organisation or entity, the profit-yielding subject, and affecting the capital structure.”

Sun Newspapers indicates in clear terms that the fact that the expenditure did not result in the addition of an asset did not have the consequence that it was on income account. It also establishes that recurrence is not decisive, but goes rather to weight. Moreover and in the passage quoted in subclause (c), Dixon J noted (in (ii)) that the risk of a competitor arising must always be present at least in theory, and (in (iii)) that the chief object of the expenditure was to preserve the existing structure from impairment.

7. The decision in Hallstroms Pty Limited v FC of T [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634 is now perhaps best known for its dissenting judgment by Sir Owen Dixon, which has been cited with approval in later cases. See in this context Dixon J at 646-647 as follows:

“For myself, however, I am not prepared to concede that the distinction between an expenditure on account of revenue and an outgoing of a capital nature is so indefinite and uncertain as to remove the matter from the operation of reason and place it exclusively within that of chance, or that the discrimen is so unascertainable that it must be placed in the category of an unformulated question of fact. The truth is that, in excluding as deductions losses and outgoings of capital or of a capital nature, the income tax law took for its purposes a very general conception of accountancy, perhaps of economics, and left the particular application to be worked out, a thing which it thus became the business of the courts of law to do. The courts have proceeded with the task without, it is true, any very conspicuous attempt at analysis, but rather in the given case led to a decision assigning the expenditure to capital or to income as the case might be. It is one thing to say that the presence among the circumstances of a case of a particular factor places the case within a specific legal category. It is another thing to infer that the absence of the same factor from some other case necessarily places that case outside the category and gives it an opposite description. But towards that kind of fallacy human reasoning constantly tends, and the decisions upon matters of capital and income contain much reasoning that is quite human. My own opinions upon the question I have attempted to explain in Sun Newspapers Ltd v Federal Commission of Taxation (1) and I shall not re-state them. I shall treat the passage to which I refer as incorporated in this judgment. Once more, however, I shall endeavour to apply what I conceive to be the principles that determine whether an outgoing is on account of capital or of revenue. As a prefatory remark it may be useful to recall the general consideration that the contrast between the two forms of expenditure corresponds to the distinction between the acquisition of the means of production and the use of them; between establishing or extending a business organisation and carrying on the business; between the implements employed in work and the regular performance of the work in which they are employed; between an enterprise itself and the sustained effort of those engaged in it.

The facts upon which the case before us must be decided present in a somewhat unusual aspect the elements or factors governing the distinction. The claim is to deduct legal expenses, and legal expenses, we may assume, take the quality of an outgoing of a capital nature or of an outgoing on account of revenue from the cause or the purpose of incurring the expenditure. We are, therefore, remitted to a consideration of the object in view when the legal proceedings were undertaken, or of the situation which impelled the taxpayer to undertake them.”

The prefatory remark in the passage quoted is of particular relevance; it is necessary to distinguish expenditure (as between capital and income) in accordance with the distinctions there enumerated. While the actual decision in Hallstroms favours the Applicant, it would seem that it is generally accepted that the minority dissent was correct; see in particular Hill J in SmithKline referred to in clause 10(a) below.

8. In Broken Hill Theatres Pty Limited v F.C. of T [1952] HCA 75; (1952) 9 ATD 423:

(a) Dixon C.J., McTiernan, Fullagar and Kitto JJ made it clear that the fact that no asset was brought into existence could not be relevant; moreover (having regard to a reference in the T Documents and argument to the fact that its share of the costs amount was capitalised in the accounts of the Applicant) nothing turns on the matter in which the expenses are dealt with in a company’s accounts.

(b) Webb J at 425 stated:

“I am unable to see why heavy legal expenses incurred in resisting the licensing of a new theatre which, if it came into existence, would impair or destroy the business of an existing theatre, should be capital expenditure in one town and revenue expenditure in another. I think that the propensity of persons in a town to seek such licences cannot turn what would otherwise be a capital outlay into revenue expenditure in that town, and that evidence of such propensity is inadmissible for that purpose. But if it is admissible then it reveals in this case that applications for new licences repeatedly failed. That is not surprising, as the refusal of a licences not only gives immunity for a year certain, but is likely to ensure immunity for a longer period, though not permanently. It is true that, say, the cost of painting business premises is usually a revenue expenditure, although such cost may be heavy and painting is required only every four or five years. Still it is so required as a matter of course. But ordinarily that is not the case with legal expenses incurred in resisting new licences. It cannot at any time be predicted with certainty that applications for new licences will be made in the near future, and that legal expenses will be incurred in resisting them, simply because there is nothing in law to prevent such applications. The provision for annual applications for licences, if desired, imposes a limit on such applications: it does not render them more likely to succeed than if they could be made at any time. (at p436)”

Broken Hill Theatres concerned legal expenses incurred in order to resist competition; the facts in Broken Hill Theatres are thus to some extent analogous with the facts in this case. It may be noted that Webb J noted specifically that it could not be predicted whether at any given applications for new licences in the near future could be anticipated. As set out previously, there was no evidence before the Tribunal as to the likelihood or otherwise of future litigation comparable with the legal proceedings.

9. In FC of T v Consolidated Fertilizers Limited (1991) 91 ATC 4677, a majority of the Full Federal Court (Spender and Lee JJ) found inter alia that the expenses there in question constituted expenditure which the nature of business required as part of the prudent management; see in particular 4681 where their Honours said:

“The crux of the issue thus lies in the distinction between capital and revenue as set out in the classic statement by Dixon J. in Sun Newspapers where His Honour said at p. 359:-

“The distinction between expenditure and outgoings on revenue account and on capital account corresponds with the distinction between the business entity, structure, or organization set up or established for the earning of profit and the process by which such an organization operates to obtain regular returns by means of regular outlay, the difference between the outlay and returns representing profit or loss.”

See also Hallstroms case at A.T.D. pp. 194-195; CLR pp. 646-647.

The contrast is between the acquisition and extension of a profit-yielding enterprise, including the assets employed therein and the regular use of that enterprise, the carrying on of the business, for the purpose of producing a profit. Thus it was that in Sun Newspapers and Broken Hill Theatres, the Court held expenditure to be of a capital nature as it was directed to protecting the taxpayer’s business from competition, to protecting its substantial monopoly. On the other hand, in Snowden and Willson, the expenditure was directed to maintaining the reputation and goodwill of the taxpayer and was not concerned with the profit-yielding structure as such. As Dixon C.J. said at 437:-

“An examination of the facts does not support the views that the proceedings in Parliament and before the Royal Commission imperilled the existence of the business or the capital assets of the company. The proceedings were not necessarily directed at a winding-up of the company or a stoppage of the business. Precise definition or distinctions are difficult in such an affair. But what the company had most to fear was the embarrassments in the present and future conduct of its business and, no doubt, a decline in its custom. There is no satisfactory ground for saying that the expenditure was an affair of capital.”

Legal costs undertaken in relation to the protection of patent rights or of confidential information are not necessarily of a capital nature simply because, in the course of the litigation, a challenge is made of the validity of the patent rights or to the confidentiality of the information. It is as much a part of the regular operation of a business to protect items of an intangible nature as it is to maintain and repair buildings and plant. The cost of fire protection is not of a capital nature simply because a fire, if left unchecked, might destroy a building. Just as the distinction between the capital expenditure involved in the renovation of a building and revenue expenditure spent in its maintenance and repair may be a fine one, so, when in litigation a taxpayer seeks to protect and enforce intangible rights while the other party seeks to challenge those rights, the distinction between profit and revenue may not be easy to draw. Yet in the end, the crucial distinction is, as Dixon J. said, “between the character and organization of the profit-earning business” and “the operations by which it is carried on”. See Hallstroms case at 648.” (Emphasis added)

The majority judges in Continental Fertilizers found in effect that the expenses there in question were incurred as part of the operations of the business.

10. (a) Mr Marriott in particular urged consideration by the Tribunal of the decision of Hill J in SmithKline Beecham Laboratories (Aust) Ltd v FC of T (1993) 93 ACT 4629 because it admirably sums up a number of relevant aspects of the law involved; see in this regard His Honour’s decision at pages 4633-4644:

“It may be recalled that in Hallstroms the taxpayer had expended money on legal fees to oppose the extension of a patent of a competitor. The majority of the Court found the expenditure to be deductible but it is the judgment of Sir Owen Dixon in dissent which has in later cases been referred to with approval. In Broken Hill Theatres Pty Limited v FC of T [1952] HCA 75; (1952) 9 ATD 423, (1951-1952) 85 CLR 423 the High Court had to consider the deductibility of expenditure on legal fees to oppose the grant of a licence under the Theatres and Public Halls act 1908-1946 (NSW) which would have had the consequence of permitting a competitor to show motion pictures at Broken Hill in opposition to the taxpayer. Not only was Hallstroms case distinguished, but the view of Dixon J disapproving the decision of Lawrence J in Southern (HM Inspector of Taxes) v Borax Consolidated Limited [1941] 1 KB 111 was specifically affirmed. That affirmation carried with it presumably the disapproval of the judgments of Latham CJ and Williams J, two of the majority in Hallstroms, both of whom relied, at least to some extent upon Southern v Borax Consolidated Limited.

But whatever the correctness of its facts of Hallstroms case, there can be little doubt that expenditure incurred to preserve or to protect a business as such will ordinarily be expenditure of capital: cf FC of T v Consolidated Fertilizers Limited 91 ATC 4677 at 4687-4688; (1991) 101 ALR 385 at 399-400 per Spender and Lee JJ. On the other hand, where legal costs are incurred in defending the taxpayer and its officers from criticism of its methods of trading, the outgoings will clearly be on revenue account: FC of T v Snowden & Willson Pty Limited [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 11 ATD 463 at 465; [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431 at 437 per Dixon CJ.

The resolution of the question whether an outgoing is of capital or revenue is often greatly assisted by reference to the judgment of Dixon J in Sun Newspapers Limited v FC Of T [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 5 ATD 87; (1938) 61 CLR 337. In that case His Honour referred (at ATD 94; CLR 360) to the distinction between income and capital as dependent upon the distinction between the profit yielding subject on the one hand and the process of operating it on the other. Relevant to the distinction, as His Honour points out, is a difference between an outlay which is recurrent, repeated or continual on the one hand, and outlay which is final or made “once and for all” on the other. Expenditure made to bring into existence or procure an asset or advantage of a lasting nature to ensure for the benefit of the profit-earning subject would ordinarily be on capital account as distinct from expenditure which falls:

“within the very wide class of things which in the aggregate form the constant demand which must be answered out of the returns of a trade or its circulating capital ...” (at ATD 95; CLR 362).”

(His Honour then referred to the classic statement, which has been quoted previously in these Reasons.)

“In the present case the expenditure in question was incurred to prevent, so far as possible, a competitor obtaining marketing approval and thereby being in a position to compete with the applicant, with the subsidiary motive that if this was not possible competition would at least be delayed. Such expenditure could not be regarded as recurrent except in the sense that the risk of a competitor arising may always be theoretically present. The chief object of the expenditure was thus to preserve from impairment and dislocation that part of the goodwill of the business of the applicant as was concerned with the cimetidine products so as to prevent loss of market share.” (Emphasis added)

It is thus apparent that there is a clear distinction to be drawn between expenditure incurred by a taxpayer in defence of criticism of its business methods (which will generally be on income account) and expenditure to preserve or protect a business (and including through the restraint of competition) which will generally be on capital account. The passage to which emphasis has been added is directly relevant to the current proceedings.

(b) The decision in Magna Alloys & Research Pty Ltd v FC of T (1980) 80 ATC 4542 involved legal expenditure incurred in defending various charges brought against the directors and agents of the company. See in this context the passage from Magna Alloys quoted in SmithKline at pages 4634-4635 as follows:

“In support of his submission, counsel for the applicant referred to the decision of the full court of this Court in Magna Alloys & Research Pty Ltd v FC of T 80 ATC 4542 per Brennan J at 4554 and per Deane and Fisher JJ at 4562. In the first of those passages, Brennan J in holding that the legal expenditure in that case incurred in defending various charges brought against the directors and agents of the company was not on capital account said:

“The capital of the business was in no way increased by the expenditure incurred. True it is that the expenditure protected the reputation and goodwill of Magna’s business, but the attack which was made arose out of the day to day selling activities of the business and it was the business purpose of vindicating the methods by which it was conducted that brings the expenditure within sec. 51(1).

Though goodwill is a capital asset of a business it is frequently earned and maintained by the daily activities of those engaged in the business. The valuable if intangible asset of goodwill frequently grows out of activities the cost of which is a charge on revenue account (see Sun Newspapers, supra, at pp. 360, 361). Expenditure incurred in attempting to vindicate the business methods of the taxpayer, overcoming the obstacle to its trading, which had been raised by the prosecutions is properly to be regarded as a cost on revenue account.””

Magna Alloys involved expenditure which, so it was held, was incurred in protecting against an attack made against the activities of the business, and those engaged in the business.

11. In W. Wehbe Pty Limited & Anor v FC of T (1993) 93 ATC 5015, Gummow J furnished a helpful summary of the judgment of Hill J in SmithKline at 5018 as follows:

“Hill J. pointed out that (i) where the expenditure sought to be deducted is incurred in the course of litigation, what is required is consideration of the purpose of the taxpayer in undertaking the litigation, (ii) expenditure incurred to preserve or to protect a business “as such” ordinarily will be an expenditure of capital, (iii) where the purpose of the litigation is to stave off a loss which would be fatal, causing the business to cease, the costs expended will be on capital account; this may also be the case if the object of the expenditure is to preserve the business from impairment and dislocation; (iv) legal costs will be on revenue account if incurred in defending the taxpayer and its officers from criticism of the taxpayer’s methods of trading, (v) for an expenditure to be on capital account it is not necessary for a capital asset to be acquired, and (vi) had the litigation by SmithKline been successful, it was highly likely that the advantage sought of freeing the taxpayer from competition in respect of cimetidine would have been virtually permanent because without access to the information in question the possibility of the regulatory authority being able satisfactorily to evaluate generic products would have been quite remote.”

12. See also Dixon CJ in John Fairfax & Sons Pty Limited v FC of T [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30 at 36 where Dixon CJ said:

“It is not in my opinion right to say that because you obtain nothing positive, nothing of an enduring nature, for an expenditure it cannot be an outgoing on account of capital What Viscount Cave L.C. said in British Insulated and Helsby Cables Ltd v. Atherton [1926] A.C. 205 was this, ‘But when an expenditure is made, not only once and for all, but with a view to bringing into existence an asset or an advantage for the enduring benefit of a trade, I think that there is very good reason (in the absence of special circumstances leading to an opposite conclusion) for treating such an expenditure as properly attributable not to revenue but to capital; [19620 A.C. at 213, 214. That is an affirmative proposition. But it is hardly necessary to say that it is a logical fallacy to run it round and say that an expenditure cannot be attributable to capital unless it is made once and for all and is made with a view to bringing into existence an asset or an advantage of the enduring benefit of a trade. Nor did Viscount Cave L.C. mean to say that no expenditure falling outside his proposition would be of a capital nature. No doubt it is not often that an outgoing is voluntarily incurred without anything to show for it. The cynical might say that it is a phenomenon so rare that for illustrations of such an outgoing you must look to the costs of litigation. It is, however, a feature that is always likely to occur when the purpose of the expenditure is to limit or buy off opposition or forestall or get rid of some present or threatened disadvantage. Of course, you can have expenditure of that kind which on the soundest principles of accounting is chargeable against revenue. But you might confidently expect to find that such expenditure of the kind was undeniably on capital account.” (Emphasis added)

13. Mr Lawrence relied to some considerable extent on FC of T v Rothmans of Pall Mall (Australia) Ltd (1992) 92 ATC 4508. See in particular Lockhart J at 4515 as follows:

“A company which manufactures and supplies tobacco products these days is under fire. Community attitudes towards smoking have changed considerably in recent times. Pressures are exerted upon legislatures, both Federal and State, to prohibit or reduce the sale and consumption of tobacco products. On the other hand, the market for the consumption of tobacco products is large. Pressure groups are at work. As legislatures react to public and scientific opinion there are repercussions on the businesses of the tobacco producers and suppliers. But this is to-day an ongoing part of the setting in which those companies carry on business. Expenditure of the kind with which this case is concerned is on revenue account. The capital of Rothmans’ business was in no way increased by the expenditures in question. The expenditure arose from Rothmans’ commercial activities in the course of carrying on its business. It was incidental to the carrying on of Rothmans’ business. There is a clear relation between the expenditure and the carrying on of that business. It was incurred in carrying on its business and was not of a capital nature. If legislation is proposed that will, if enacted, totally ban the sale of tobacco products in the future, Wards Case would have something to say about the deductibility of expenditure incurred by tobacco companies in influencing public opinion against the passage of the legislation. But that is for another day and another case.” (Emphasis added)

The Tribunal considers that the decision in Rothmans is clearly distinguishable on its facts. As His Honour said, Rothmans was a company “under fire”; accordingly he concluded that the expenditure was incidental to the business and incurred in carrying on the business. The legal proceedings in this matter were not.

14. The decision in Creer v FC of T (1994) 94 ATC 4454 was also one on which Mr Lawrence relied. Mr Creer was a partner in a large firm of solicitors, and in respect of whom a performance evaluation committee of the firm reduced his share of profits by a substantial percentage. Burchett J at 4457 said:

“No attack was involved at that point upon his position as a partner, nor upon his right to practise the profession from the practice of which his earnings arose. It is true he had been asked to resign, but that was a request which acknowledged his right to choose, and upon his refusing, there the matter rested. The object he had in view when the legal proceedings were undertaken was undoubtedly the establishment of his right to the full amount of his income entitlement, and the situation which impelled him to undertake the proceedings was the denial of a proportion of his hitherto accepted share of income. The reason why he was eager to pursue that object, and the strategic implications he saw in that situation, do not detract from the reality of these facts. From a practical and business point of view, whatever motivations the parties may have had and however their rights should be classified from a juristic point of view, the expenditure of the legal fees was calculated, at the time they were expended, to achieve a restoration of the level of the applicant’s income, and not to vary by one hair’s breadth his position as a solicitor in practice as a partner in the firm to which he belonged.” (Emphasis added)

It is perhaps relevant to note that Burchett J referred in Creer’s case to Magna Alloys and the minority judgment of Dixon J in Hallstroms, but not to the decisions in Sun Newspapers and SmithKline. It is, in the view of the Tribunal, not easy to reconcile Creer’s case with the earlier decisions.

The proceedings, in which Mr Creer was involved, related so His Honour found, to his income rather than the structure which produced that income. In this matter the legal proceedings related clearly to the protection of the structure against what was perceived to be a significant threat. The Tribunal considers in any event that if Creer can be said to differ from the earlier cases, and in particular Sun Newspapers, the minority judgment in Hallstroms and Broken Hill Theatres, it should follow the line of authority established in those earlier cases.

15. Focussing on the summary formulated by Gummow J in Wehbe’s case, and using the numbering in that summary:

(i) the purpose in undertaking the litigation was to prevent or limit potential competition, perceived to be a significant, if not fatal, threat;

(ii) no comment is required;

(iii) the objective was to preserve a significant impairment;

(iv) there is no suggestion that (iv) is relevant;

(v) the cases make it clear that the acquisition of a capital asset is not necessary;

(vi) Broken Hill Theatres in particular indicates that permanence of freedom from competition is not a necessary element.

16. The Tribunal does not think it necessary to refer to certain other cases cited. In summary:

(a) there was, as indicated previously, no evidence as to the frequency with which legal proceedings of this type occur; however, it is doubtful whether actions alleging invalidity in deeds made by a Council as to rezoning occur with any degree of frequency.

(b) the Applicant in its Statement of Facts and Contentions stated at clause 19 as follows:

“However, if recurrence is seen as a critical aspect of revenue expenditure it should be noted that given the continual development of retail shopping centres in high growth areas of Brisbane, there is an increased likelihood of objections which will be similar in nature to the current case.”

There was no evidence to support this contention, and the Tribunal doubts its correctness;

(c) if the expenditure was indeed of an isolated nature, its non-recurrence, while not determinative, lends weight to the view that it was incurred on capital account;

(d) the expenditure was incurred in order to restrain competition of a significant nature and the decision in Broken Hill Theatres is of particular relevance. The Tribunal considers that the competitive threat related to the income-earning operation (the C Shopping Centre) owned by the Trust. The fact that the competitive threat was not perceived as fatal is not to the point; that it was perceived as significant is sufficient; the fact that in the result the costs amount incurred was abortive, is not relevant.

(e) there is no suggestion that the expenditure was incurred to defend against criticism of business methods;

(f) the legal expenditure was not incurred in the course of the relevant business by those engaged in it;

(g) the weight of authority is, in the opinion of the Tribunal, strongly in favour of the view that the costs account was incurred on capital account.

17. Accordingly, the Tribunal considers that the objection decision was correct, and that it should be affirmed.
