REASONS FOR DECISION

19 January 1995 Mr B.H. Pascoe, Member



1. There are applications for review of decisions of the Commissioner of Taxation to disallow objections against the refusal by the Commissioner to accept claims for refunds of sales tax made by the applicant. The applications for refund were made on the basis of a claim that sales tax had been overpaid on the purchase of certain equipment, which the applicant argued, constituted “aids to manufacture”.

2. The applicant carries on a business of the maintenance and overhaul of jet engines used in large passenger aircraft. The dispute related to whether the relevant equipment purchased was primarily and principally used in the manufacture of goods.

3. Mr D. Bloom Q.C. assisted by Mr E. Mayne appeared for the applicant and Mr G. Nettle Q.C. with Ms A. Richards appeared for the respondent. The principal witness for the applicant was “A”, the Controller Inspection/Planning for this particular business of the applicant. Further witnesses for the applicant were “B”, the Engine Test Facility Controller, ‘C’, the manager of the business and ‘D’, a Manager Module Line of a similar business carried on by a competitor. One witness was called by the respondent. He was “E”, a consultant aeronautical engineer who had been employed until 1981 as Director and General Manager of a Hong Kong Company which carried on similar operations to those of the applicant. A video was provided as an exhibit to the witness statement of A and sereened during the hearing to provide a visual explanation of the operations conducted by the applicant.

4. The evidence provided showed that the business in which the applicant was involved was the maintenance and restoration of aircraft jet engines, modules of such engines, auxiliary power units (APU’s), accessory components of jet engines and component parts of such engines, APU’s and accessories. The applicant has the capacity to deal with several different makes of engine such as Pratt & Whitney, General Electric, Rolls Royce Spey and CFM1. Each of these engines is made up of five modules:-

(a) fan module

(b) compressor module

(c) combustion chamber module

(d) high pressure turbine module

(e) low pressure turbine module

The core engine is made up of the compressor module, combustion chamber module and high pressure turbine module. Modules are interchangeable between engines of the same type. Modules can be further divided in to sub-assemblies.

APU’s are engines which are used to run air-conditioning, supply electrical power and for starting main engines in aircraft. Accessory components include items such as fuel control units, fuel pumps, fuel/oil coolers, auxiliary gearboxes etc.

5. Engines are received into the applicant’s work shop as a result or either a “scheduled” removal from the wing of an aircraft or an “unscheduled” or “on-condition” removal. The former occurs when certain parts of the engine or module have reached the time limits set by the manufacturer of the jet engine.

Unscheduled or on condition removals occur when, as a result of on-wing checks and inspections or engine failure, the engine is deemed to be unserviceable. A small proportion of engines are brought into the workshop because a repair, special inspection or modification is not possible on-wing. On arrival at the workshop, an Engine Receipt Condition Report is generated containing the history of the engine, why it has come off-wing, its operating cycles and the status of life limited parts in the engine. The applicant’s Engine Review Committee determines a workscope for each engine, including:-

(a) the extent of disassembly required,

(b) the service period required until the next scheduled shop visit,

(c) the required replacement of life limited components

(d) the disassembly required to incorporate modifications.

The Engine Review Committee also approves any subsequent revision to the initial workscope. The age of all life limited components is checked and relevant changes are made on all engines brought into the applicants workship whether the engine is removed as the result of a scheduled or unscheduled removal. The vast majority of engines or modules which are restored have some modifications incorporated in them as specified by the original manufacturer.

6. All engines and modules which are to be completely restored are totally disassembled. All parts are kept together on multi-layered bins which are labelled with the engine type, serial number and job number and with the engine module. These bins are moved around as the parts pass through the required processes. All parts are cleaned in accordance with the engine manufacturer’s approved procedure. After cleaning each part is inspected and a decision made whether the part is serviceable, whether it needs replacing with a new or restored part or whether it is possible and economical to restore the part to the required specifications or limits as set out in the engine manufacturer’s manual. A rework card is prepared for each part which is to undergo restoration or modification. Some restoration of parts is done in the applicant’s own workshops and some parts are forwarded to a local or overseas contractor for restoration.

7. The applicant’s workshop comprises several areas through which component parts of engines or modules may pass:-

(a) Cleaning shop in which all parts are thoroughly cleaned

(b) Machine Shop in which metal cutting, grinding and milling machines are used to modify or machine park prior to restoration of surfaces. Manufacture and fitting of new bearings, bushes, studs, inserts, etc., into worn or damaged parts is carried out in the machine shop.

(c) Non Distinctive Test Area where parts are checked for cracks

(d) Grinding Machines Area where the blade tip grinding/rotor balancing machine is used to grind compressor and turbine blades.

(e) Balancing Machines Area where rotating parts of the engine are balanced.

(f) Sheet Metal Welding Shop where parts made of sheetmetal, such as combustion chambers, exhaust pipes, large pneumatic ducts, are restored. Any parts requiring welding of cracks and heat treatment are restored in this area.

(g) Plating Shop where parts requiring electroplating such as Nickel/ Cadmium plating for corrosion protection and chrome plating of bearing journals to restore worn dimensions are restored.

(h) Testing. After an aircraft jet engine or module is assembled the engine will be transported to the Engine Test Cell where testing equipment is used to determine if the engine will perform to standard. If not performing to standard and the defect cannot be rectified in the Test Cell, the engine is returned for further work.

8. After the relevant parts have been restored or modified, the engine or module is then assembled. This process may involve the assembly of:-

(a) parts from the same engine or module which have been cleaned, inspected and deemed to be serviceable,

(b) parts from the same engine or module which have been restored in the applicant’s workshop,

(c) parts from the same engine or module which have been restored by outside contractors,

(d) new and restored parts requisitioned from store to replace parts which cannot be restored or which have been sent to outside contractors and may not be returned in time to meet the time schedule.

(e) serviceable parts removed from other engines

(f) modified parts which may be either restored or new parts.

Because of the time involved in sending parts overseas, in most cases the part will be replaced with one from store or with a serviceable part from another engine. When the part is returned from overseas it is placed in stock or in the engine from which the replacement part was taken.

9. Assembly of an engine or module is usually undertaken by two or three trained and qualified aircraft mechanics and can take from three to five days for assembly of a module and from ten to fifteen days for a complete engine.

All stages of disassembly, restoration of parts, requisition of new or restored parts assembly and testing are accompanied by extensive paper work so that the identification and history of all parts can be monitored and checked. The restoration of engines and modules can take anywhere from three weeks to three months to complete. Restoration of APU’s and accessory components follow a similar pattern to that for engines and modules except that accessory components are disassembled, inspected, assembled and tested in a separate area of the workshop.

In addition a further seventeen Pratt & Whitney engines were brought in of which fourteen were sent overseas for restoration. For the remaining three, the high pressure turbine modules were restored by the applicant.

Where an engine was brought in but no restoration of the complete engine or a module took place, the work consisted of either the restoration of one or more sub assemblies, the repair of one or more sub-assemblies where such repair was not accessable on wing or a special inspection or modification which could not be done on wing.

11. In his witness statement, A estimated the number of complete engine or module restorations which resulted in the replacement of greater than 50% in value or quantity of new and restored parts. This number was said to be 6 of the 30 complete engines and 65 of the 323 modules. In his verbal evidence he stated that these figures were based solely on value of major parts and were not based on quantity nor included the value of smaller less expensive parts. A recalculation was provided based on quantity of new and restored parts including small items such as nuts, bolts, blade retainers, blade locks, etc., which increased the number of restorations involving the replacement of more than 50% in quantity of new or restored parts to 22 out of 30 engines and 182 out of 323 modules. It is not clear what particular significance was placed on these calculations. It appeared that they were seen as relevant having regard to a ruling, ST 2170, issued by the respondent in relation to reconditioned motor vehicle engines. The ruling regards reconditioned engines as “second hand goods” unless the value of new components (including any imported component which may have been used overseas but not in Australia) clearly predominates. Mr Bloom did not concede that more than 50% was a relevant figure and that 20%, even 10% of replacement parts would be sufficient to conclude that the operation conducted by the applicant result in the manufacture of goods.

12. From a sample work package for the restoration of a Pratt & Whitney JT8D-15 engine, A calculated that, of the total of 4500 parts in the engine, 2033 were replaced with new or restored parts, 1254 were restored and refitted and the balance were serviceable and refitted. No attempt was made to differentiate between new and restored parts as restored parts were regarded ‘as new’. The evidence of C was that the price of a restored part was generally 60% of the price of a new part.

13. It was the applicant’s case that the process of combining new, restored and still serviceable parts to build and test an engine or module is a process of manufacture as defined in the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 1) 1930 (“the Act”) and that the resulting engine or module are goods for the purposes of the Act. It was submitted that the engine or module is a different article from the parts from which it is made up. As a consequence the equipment used in that process was said to be aids to manufacture, the applicant was entitled to have quoted his certificate on the purchase of such equipment and, not having done so was entitled to a refund of sales tax paid. The respondent argued that the process engaged in by the applicant did not constitute manufacture of goods within the meaning of the Act. It was argued further that if it was found that there were instances of manufacture of engines or components they were a small minority out of the total number dealt with by the applicant and the equipment was not used principally and primarily in manufacture.

14. The definition of aids to manufacture is found in Regulation 4(1) of the Sales Tax Regulations (“the Regulations”) and in Clause 1 of the First Schedule to the Sales Tax (Exemptions & Classifications) Act 1935 (“Exemptions and Classifications Act”). This definition, so far as it relates to this application, is:-

“aids to manufacture” means goods for use by a registered person in the course of carrying on a business (where that use is exclusively, or primarily and principally, for the purposes of that business), being:

(a) machinery, implements and apparatus for use exclusively, or primarily and principally:

(i) in the actual processing or treatment of goods to be used in, wrought into or attached to goods to be manufactured;

(ii) in any processing or treatment by which the goods to which that processing or treatment is applied are used in, wrought into or attached to goods to be manufactured;

(iii) in any processing or treatment for the purpose of bringing goods into, or maintaining goods in, the form or condition in which they are to be marketed or used by the manufacturer of the goods;

........

(v) in testing or checking the quality or specifications of goods processed or treated by him as specified in subparagraph (i), (ii) or (iii);”

Section 26(3) of the Act provides that the Commissioner of Taxation may refund sales tax paid where he is satisfied that tax has been paid on the sale of goods to a registered person who was required to quote his certificate and failed to do so. Applications for refund must be made within three years from the date upon which the goods were sold. Regulation 12 requires a registered person to quote his certificate in respect of the purchase of goods by a registered person for use by him as aids to manufacture. As a result the first question to be answered is whether the applicant is engaged in a process of the manufacture of goods. Section 3(1) the Act contains the definitions with which this question is concerned and, to the extent that they are relevant to these applications, are:-

““Goods” includes commodities, but does not include-

(a) goods which have, either through a process of retailing or otherwise, gone into use or consumption in Australia; or

(b) goods which are sold as second-hand goods and are manufactured exclusively or principally from goods which-

(i) have, whether alone or as parts of other goods, gone into use or consumption in Australia; and

(ii) in the opinion of the Commissioner, in their condition as parts of the goods so manufactured, retain their character as goods or parts of goods which have gone into use or consumption in Australia”.

“Manufacture” includes-

(a) production;

(b) the combination of parts or ingredients whereby an article or substance is formed that is commercially distinct from those parts or ingredients;

......

but does not include:

(g) a combination falling within paragraph (b) (other than a combination whereby a prescribed article or substance is formed) if, in the opinion of the Commissioner, the combination is of a kind that is customarily undertaken by persons who use the articles or substances formed by combinations of that kind for the purposes for which the articles or substances are intended to be ultimately used;”

“Manufacturer” means a person who engages, whether exclusively or not, in the manufacture of goods, and includes a printer, publisher, lithographer or engraver, and a person (not being an employee) who manufactures goods, whether or not the materials out of which the goods are manufactured are owned by him, but where one person manufactures goods for another, wholly or in part out of materials supplied by that other, and the goods are not for the use of, but are for sale by, that other, the person supplying the materials shall be deemed to be the manufacturer, and the person who so manufactures the goods shall be deemed not be the manufacturer;”

  1. In relation to this first question of whether the applicant is engaged in the manufacture of goods, the applicant argued that the process of restoring engines, modules APU’s, accessories or components constituted manufacture within the ordinary meaning of the word and, in particular, within paragraph (b) of the definition. The respondent argued that the applicant simply received aircraft engines to refurbish them by repairs and, where necessary, the incorporation of new or refurbished parts so as to restore them to near new condition. It was further submitted that the restored engine, incorporating parts which came originally from that engine and some replacement parts is not commercially distinct from the original engine and the parts which were incorporated. Finally, the respondent submitted that, if the process can be described as manufacture the goods were not goods within the meaning of the Act because they were second hand goods composed of second hand parts which retained their character after incorporation into the engines overhauled.
  2. The principal cases on which both parties sought to rely were Adams v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1948) 8 ATD 332, Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Jack Zinader Pty Ltd [1949] HCA 42; (1949) 78 CLR 336, Jax Tyres Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1984] FCA 278; (1984) 3 FCR 252 and Commissioner of Taxation v Jax Tyres Pty Ltd (1985) 5 FCR 257. In the Jack Zinader case (supra), a furrier company received from customers fur garments which had become too badly worn and damaged to be repaired, and, after removing the defective parts, remodelled for those customers respectively, by various processes, what was left into modern styles of coats, fur capes, fur collars, fur coats and stoles having regard to the extent shape and nature of the available materials. The materials used by the company in remodelling were, except about five per cent of the linings, confined to those available from the customer’s garment. If new linings were required the customer supplied them. It was held that the articles which resulted from the remodelling were goods manufactured.

Dixon J. Said (at p. 343):-

“The commissioner claims sales tax in respect of fur coats, stoles, capes and collars which the defendant company has remodelled on the ground that the remodelling of fur garments for customers is hit by s.17A. An inspection of s.17A will show that the claim depends on the remodelling amounting to a manufacture of goods and upon its being done for the customer “wholly or in part out of materials supplied by him.”

“The essence of making or of manufacturing is that what is made shall be a different thing from that out of which it is made” (per Darling J., McNicol v Pinch (1906) 2 K.B. 352 at p.361). The first and, it may be thought, the decisive question in the case, is therefore whether the garments which result from the process of remodelling are different things, that is are different “goods,” from the garments that the customer hands over”.

And later (at p. 343 & 344):-

“On the side of the taxpayer it is contended that these procedures do not change the identity of the garment but only some of its characteristics. The customer hands in a fur garment and takes away a fur garment. It is altered and renovated but it is still a fur garment; it is her fur garment; it is the fur garment she brought to the furriers. On the side of the commissioner it is said that a different fur garment has been brought into existence. The old fur garment has been used only to provide the materials or some of them from which the new fur garment has been made. It is a thing of a different description both commercially and from the point of view of the wearer. It is a different entity and has a new identity. “Goods” have therefore been produced.

On the whole the commissioner’s view appears to be the more correct. The work of the furrier is to use skins to form garments. In skins he works with materials often of great value and usually of some permanence. His skill lies in the use he can make of them and the descriptions of garment he produces. Fashion, commercial usage and his customer’s tastes combine to distinguish the various descriptions of garment he makes and to compel the recognization of them as separate categories of “goods”. When he takes skins made up into one description of fur garment and produces another, he cannot be treated as having altered an existing thing without producing a new one. He has made a different article.”

Williams J., in also agreeing that the process was manufacture, said (at p. 350)-

“The ordinary meaning of the verb manufacture is to work up materials into forms suitable for use. Where new materials are supplied there would plainly be a notional manufacture of goods within the meaning of the Act. Where old materials are supplied there would only be such a manufacture if the work done was more than a mere repair or modification of the old materials and was such as to change the old goods into goods of a different character.”

A question to be answered in this matter is whether the engines, modules, etc., which were received become goods of a different character after the process carried out by the applicant so as to constitute manufacture and not a repair or modification of the original goods.

In the Jax Tyres case (supra) Beaumont J., in the first instance and the Full Federal Court on appeal were required to determine whether the process of retreading tyres was manufacture. It was held that it was not manufacture either according to the meaning of the word or the statutory extension of that meaning as contained in Section 3(1) of the Act. In his decision in the first instance Beaumont J., said (at p. 260):-

“In my view, the reasoning in Zinader is decisive in the present case. When applied, that reasoning leads to the conclusion that no “manufacture” has occurred here. The question, one of fact and degree, is whether a different thing has been produced. In my opinion, it has not. Although the tread on the tyre has been replaced, the application of the retreading processes has not produced anything that is different in character from the worn tyre submitted to those processes (cf. Mayor of Guidford v Brown [1915] 1 K.B. 256 at 259).

And later (on the same page):-

“The question is necessarily one of degree and thus one of general impression based upon a common usage of ordinary language (see, for example, M.P. Metals Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1968)117 CLR 631 at 649). It is possible, in the present context, to imagine a case, as happened in Zinader where a retreading process is applied to a worn tyre with the result that a different type is produced. For example, if the worn tyre is one originally designed for use on a motor car but, by some retreading process, its tread is not only replaced by significantly widened so as to be adapted for use on, say, a tractor, it may well be that a different thing is produced so that “goods” would then be “manufactured”. If the old tyre is completely dismantled and then reconstructed, a different thing may well be produced. A “manufacture” may be involved if the taxpayer produces something, not part of the retreading process, yet brought into existence as a separate and distinct commercial commodity or article (see Case No. R 37 [1965] 16 T.B.R.D. 158 at 161). But there can be no suggestion that the plaintiff’s operations extend to activities of this kind. Nor is this case where differences in utility for some purpose can be demonstrated (see M.P. Metals at 638).”

And (at p. 261)

“In short, since the retreading process involves no more than the repair of a product, the original identity of which is maintained throughout the process, no manufacture arises.”

On appeal to the Full Court, His Honour’s decision was upheld. Lockhart J., said (at p. 264).

“A new tread has no identity or existence apart from the tyre. The original tyre has not become a component or part of a new product; rather the tread rubber and other substances have become components or parts of the tyre. There has not been the manufacture of a new article but the repair or remodelling of an old one. Retreading is not manufacturing as that word is generally understood or as the extended statutory definition provides. The essence of manufacturing implies a change from which a new and different article must emerge having a distinctive character or use. This is not what happens with a retreaded tyre. The fact that the taxpayer’s activities appear to be conducted on a fairly large scale does not affect my conclusions in this case. It is a relevant, though by no means a decisive, consideration.”

Sheppard J., after referring to the comments of Dixon and Williams JJ., in the Jack Zinader decision (supra) said (at p. 267):-

“The application of these tests may sometimes be a difficult exercise. The question is one of fact and degree. An exercise in judgment is involved. Having given this matter consideration. I have reached the conclusion that the process here is one, if not of repair, then of renovation. It is not manufacture. The essence of retreading is that , while the area that comes into contact with the road may be in need of renewal, the rest of the tyre remains sound and useful. A suitable worn tyre is rendered fit for use by the application of a new tread”.

  1. The applicant relied heavily on the decision in Adams (supra). In this case the taxpayer carried on a business of “replating” batteries. The decision of Williams J. was by way of case stated from an interim decision of the Board of Review reported as 11 CTBR Case 122. His Honour found that there was no question of law arising from the decision of the Board and dismissed the reference. However he did embark on a consideration of the argument. The process taken from the report of the decision of the Board of Review was stated as:-

“The starting point in every case is an old battery which, although useless or practically useless as such, is still, commercially, a battery. The first step (apart, possibly, from pouring out the acid) is to cut the straps through (and evidently also the parts of the posts which project above the covers) to facilitate the extraction of each cell assembly from the case. For the same purpose the pitch by which the covers are sealed to the case is scraped off. The acid is poured off when (if not before) the cell assemblies have been extracted from the case. The straps, the upper parts of the posts and the covers are removed and the plates are severed from the carriers. The plates and separators are all scrapped, but the case and the covers are retained. The straps, posts and carriers are melted down together with the similar parts of half-a-dozen or more old batteries, and new straps, posts and carriers are moulded from the molten metal. The acid is also retained and together with the acid from other old batteries is put into a large precipitation tank from which after the sediment has settled, it is siphoned into a lead-lined chamber where it is stepped up to the required specific gravity. While a battery is charging the acids in the plates are forced into the solution which thereby becomes heavier. While it is not charging the acids return to the plates and the solution becomes lighter. The specific gravity of the acid from an old battery is from 1100 to 1150 (the standard being, assumably, 1000 for ordinary water at a certain temperature). The specific gravity required by the taxpayer for a replated battery is from 1250 to 1275. This is obtained by adding to the old acid in the lead-lined chamber a sufficient quantity of acid which has a specific gravity of 1850. According to the taxpayer the quantity required to be added varies from 5 per cent to 10 per cent of the quantity of the old acid. The Commissioner’s representative forecasted evidence which might show that this is an understatement, but, in our opinion, the point is immaterial to the present issue. The only other ingredient retained is the scraped-off pitch which is melted down (probably with pitch from other old batteries) for use in sealing the replated battery.

“It follows from these facts that, just prior to the commencement of the operations of combination whereby the replated battery is formed, the parts or ingredients for that purposes consist of-

(a) the cases and the covers of the old battery;

(b) pitch which may or may not be identical with that scraped off the old battery;

(c) acid which was obtained by the reconditioning of acid from old batteries but which nevertheless, is not identifiable with the acid taken from any particular old battery;

(d) new straps, posts and carriers made from the material obtained by the melting down of the similar parts which were obtained from old batteries and which, of course, lost their identity in the process; and

(e) new plates and separators.

These parts and ingredients are combined in the manner described above.”

After commenting on the taxpayers contention that the rebuilt batteries were second hand goods pursuant to paragraph (a) of the definition of goods His Honour said (at p. 334 and 335):-

“The batteries left by customers for renovation are, at the time they are left, clearly goods which fall within this exception. If therefore all that the taxpayer did was to perform some work on the batteries which could be fairly described as a repair, as for instance if he recharged them or supplied some minor part, it would be unreasonable to hold that the batteries had lost their original identity and exemption as second-hand goods. But the batteries were completely dismantled, and each internal compartment completely rebuilt. Part of this internal reconstruction was composed of second-hand materials, although the whole of the plates were new, but the alterations in the components of the batteries returned to the customers compared with their components when handed in and the work done to effect these alterations was substantial. The batteries were reconstructed substantially of materials supplied by the customers, but a person is a manufacturer within the meaning of the definition if he manufactures goods whether or not he owns the materials out of which the goods are manufactured.

The question of the meaning of an ordinary English word such as “manufacture” used in a Statute is one of fact and the question whether the facts proved in evidence come within this meaning is also one of fact. Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Broken Hill South Ltd [1941] HCA 33; (1941) 65 CLR 150; The Commissioner of Taxation v Miller [1946] HCA 23; (1946) 73 CLR 93. The definition of “manufacture” in the Act is not exhaustive. It merely states what manufacture includes. So far as its meaning is not defined in the Act, the word must be given its ordinary grammatical meaning at the date the Act came into force. But the definition expressly includes the combination of parts or ingredients whereby an article is formed which is commercially distinct from those parts or ingredients. This part of the definition would appear merely to incorporate an ordinary grammatical meaning of the word which is to work up materials into forms suitable for use.”

A significant part of the evidence in this case was the degree of careful recording performed by the applicant to document the identity and source of every part incorporated in the reassembly of the engine, module, APU or component. In Adams case (supra) the only components of the old battery which retained their identity in the replated battery were the cases and covers. The pitch, acid, straps, posts and carriers from the old battery lost their separate identity and it could be seen that the taxpayer there manufactured new components which were major items in the replated battery. In this case it can be said that the applicant disassembles the engine, module, etc., examines each part, restores or sends out for restoration specific parts which at all times retain their separate identity, replaces some parts with identical new parts and reassembles the engine, module, etc. There may be some manufacture of minor parts but I am unable to find that the parts which are restored by the applicant in his own workshop can be regarded as manufacture nor do I believe that the applicant was submitting that this process could be so described. Whilst submitting that the reassembly process was manufacture within the ordinary meaning of the word, the applicant relied primarily on paragraph (b) of the definition on the basis that the combination of parts produced a commercially distinct article. Some discussion ensued during the hearing as to whether there was any substantial difference between the terms frequently used to describe a restored engine such as “as new” , “rebuilt” or “wholly serviceable”. It would seem that very little turns on the description applied to the end product. Similarly, a typical invoice of the applicant to an outside customer which was for “Engineering Charges” made up of labour at a hourly rate, material per the attached six pages listing individual parts and fuel used during testing, is not necessarily the correct description of the actual process performed. Nevertheless they are all indicative of the view that what the applicant does is to take an engine, disassemble parts and reassemble the engine so that it may perform in accordance with the original manufacturer’s specifications.

  1. Evidence was given that all engines have a serial number under which all recording of the history of parts replaced or restored, hours of operation, etc is maintained. Evidence was also given that on occasion the part to which the plate containing that serial number is affixed is replaced and the plate is removed and refixed to the new part. The respondent took the view that this serial number indicated that, after the process performed by the applicant, it was the same engine which was simply refurbished or restored back to its fully operational condition. The applicant regarded this serial number as solely a convenient and practical method of maintaining the identification of the history of the parts which were combined by the applicant into the new and commercially distinct engine. One difficulty with this latter view is that it was hard for both counsel and witnesses not to refer to an engine coming back for several overhauls. For example, during cross examination of A the following questions and answers were produced:-

“There might be a number of parts changed as a result of a complete engine overhaul?--There would be lots of parts changes in complete engine overhaul.

And after there had been more than one engine overhaul, there would be lots and lots of parts which would have been changed ultimately?--Yes, definitely.

And so that if one went on with enough complete engine overhauls, there will not even be a majority of parts in there which were not in there to begin with?--It can be nearly all of them, such as a 30 year old JD8D engine that we have had in our fleet. There would be probably no original parts remaining against that serial number.

But that takes, usually, some considerable period of service life?--It can do, yes”.

Evidence was given that, in many instances, there are modifications made to an engine during the overhaul as required or suggested by the original engine manufacturer. Some of these modifications arise from recommendations of the applicant or by other engine overhaulers. A few are major modifications but the majority are minor. Witness D took the view that the rebuilt engine has different characteristics to the original engine but was reluctant to describe it as “a new engine”. In the case of Commissioner of Taxation v Jax Tyres Pty Ltd (supra) the Court saw it as relevant that tyres are manufactured with the inherent capacity for retreading. As Lockhart J., said (at p. 263 and 264):-

“A tyre is manufactured with an inherent capacity for retreading. Indeed, Toyo Australia Ltd, a manufacturer of truck tyres, specifically contemplates that a retread may be carried out on tyres manufactured by it because included in the warranty which it gives against defects in workmanship or materials, there is a provision that such warranty subsists for a period of three years “from the date of manufacture or up to the buffing stage of the second recap, whichever comes first, provided that the recap has been carried out by one of Toyo’s own retread factories”.

The manufacturer knows that once a tyre is fitted it must meet and continue to meet the safety standards which are prescribed by law and that once the tyre becomes bald it can continue to be used on public roads only if it is retreaded”..

It is equally clear that aircraft jet engine manufacturers produce engines with the capacity and, indeed, requirement for them to be disassembled with parts to be checked and restored and replaced at regular and specified intervals. In my view that is what this applicant does. I find that this process cannot be described as manufacture within the ordinary meaning of the word. There is no general or trade usage of the word that has been demonstrated as being applicable to the operations described in evidence.

  1. I also find that the applicant’s reliance on paragraph (b) of the definition of manufacture in Section 3(1) of the Act cannot provide the answer sought. In my view the restored engine, module APU or component is not a commercially distinct article from that which was brought in for restoration and the new or restored parts which were included in the reassembled item. Whilst both parties spent much time in the hearing on percentages of new or restored parts included in the process, I do not see this as having the significance sought. Firstly, the applicants witness included both new and restored parts in his calculation of the number of engines or modules which resulted in the replacement of more than 50% of parts. No differentiation was provided between new and restored nor those restored by the applicant and those restored by outside contractors. Like Mr Bloom, I have difficulty seeing that any specific percentage of replaced parts, whether new or restored, is sufficient to demonstrate that the applicant’s operations constitute manufacture. It may have relevance to whether the restored engines, etc, are “goods” within the definition in the Act or are excluded by paragraph (a) and (b) of that definition. However, in view of my finding that what the applicant does is not manufacture it is unnecessary for me to consider whether the end result answers the description of goods.
  2. It is accepted that there may well be instances in which the applicant could be said to manufacture engines or modules.

This could occur when the extent of restoration and modification does result in an end product which is commercially distinct from that with which the applicant started. The evidence was not adequate to allow me to form an opinion of the proportion of restorations which could be so described. However the basic question which I am required to answer is whether the equipment purchased by the applicant during the relevant period was for use primarily and principally in the manufacture of goods. Whatever proportion of restoration work could be regarded as manufacture, I am satisfied that it is a minor part of the activities of the applicant so that the equipment cannot satisfy the definition of aids to manufacture. Consequently, I find that the decision under review should be affirmed.
