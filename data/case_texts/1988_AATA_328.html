Income Tax - asset betterment statement - additional tax -whether penalty should be remitted further

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION ) AT88/111-114

Applicant

REASONS FOR DECISION

11 November 1988 Deputy President R.K. Todd

  1. In these proceedings there were applications before the Tribunal for review of objection decisions in relation to four years of income in respect of each applicant, namely the fiscal years 1982, 1983, 1984 and 1985.
  2. The applicants, who are husband and wife, had lodged partnership returns in the relevant years in relation to a business described therein as "Primary Producers/Taxi Operators". The wife, W, was a public servant and had derived income by way of salary from that employment. In fiscal 1982 she also returned an amount of interest derived from a joint account with her husband in a building society. In the other years such interest was included in the partnership return. In the case of the husband, H, his return for fiscal 1982 showed the same amount of interest from a joint account or accounts. His personal income-earning activity was as a taxi operator, the income from which was reflected in the partnership returns. Apart from this item of interest in fiscal 1982, his income in the three later years was composed entirely of that reflected in the partnership returns.
  3. Notices of Assessment were issued in May 1986 in respect of each applicant for each of the four years in question on an assets betterment basis. Amended assessments were issued in June 1987.
  4. By the time the matters came on for hearing, the ambit of the dispute had been much narrowed, with the original betterment of approximately $131,000 having been reduced by some $52,000. The amounts remaining in issue were -

(i) an unexplained withdrawal of $2,070

(ii) a suggested amount ($3,910) of cash on hand, said to have been in existence at the commencement of the relevant period (30 June 1981)

(iii) an amount of $12,000 said to have represented the proceeds of a sale of land in Italy and which, it was claimed, should be brought into account in fiscal 1984 as a "deduct" item.

  1. At the conclusion of the hearing I gave oral reasons for finding against the applicants in respect of the three claims referred to in paragraph 4 above. Those reasons appear on pp.131-133 of the transcript and need not be repeated here.
  2. The issue upon which I reserved my decision is that of penalties. The additional tax imposed by the Commissioner comprised -

(i) a 10 and a 20 per cent interest component on an annual basis, reflecting the fact that up to nearly four years had passed since the tax in question should notionally have been paid, so that the applicants had had the use of the money involved

(ii) a so-called "culpability" component of 40 per cent flat on the total understatement.

  1. The applicants complained about the extent of the additional tax imposed, and also contended that in respect of fiscal 1982 and 1983, when the former provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the ITAA") in relation to penalties were still in force, the principles enunciated in F.C. of T. v Sahhar 85 ATC 4072 should be applied.
  2. As to the first point, the applicants' case was that the so-called "standard" penalties had been applied. Mr. Hollands, for the applicants, rightly emphasised that there can be dangers in speaking of a "standard" penalty, and that the question of penalty should be approached taking into account individual circumstances. I was not however, as I understood it, asked to embark on any general survey of the propriety of the so-called "standard" penalty, and I do not consider that I am, on the facts of this case, put on notice of any material that should lead me to embark upon any such consideration. I consider that I should approach the matter on the footing of a consideration of the particular circumstances of the case and the penalties that were in fact applied. To this end, I must bear in mind the extent of the remission that was involved in the penalties that were imposed.
  3. It was claimed that the degree of co-operation shown by the applicants had been such that they should be treated more favourably in the matter of penalties than had been the case. I am not persuaded of this. I am satisfied from the evidence that at early stages there was what was described as "good" co-operation, but I consider that this was reduced, though not withdrawn, when it was perceived that the Commissioner was not minded to be totally satisfied by the applicants' claims. Overall, I do not consider that the co-operation displayed was either significantly above or significantly below that which could reasonably and properly be expected.
  4. I have to say in addition that it cannot escape remark that I was unimpressed with the way in which the applicants gave evidence of the matters referred to in paragraph 4 above. There was a perceptible disposition on the part of each applicant, particularly in relation to the question of cash on hand, to say whatever seemed advantageous to them in terms of the particular question asked, a fact which led to more than one change of ground in the evidence. It was conceded that the credit of the applicants was relevant to the question of penalty, and I do not consider that this aspect was anything but somewhat negative.
  5. I now go to the point dealt with in Sahhar's Case. There the Federal Court upheld a judgment of Fullagar J. in the Supreme Court of Victoria (84 ATC 4167). The taxpayer had sought to deduct the sum of $50,000 as a share of a partnership loss, not as expenditure, or as a share of expenditure, incurred by him. It was held that a partner's interest in a partnership loss could not be described as expenditure incurred by the partner, and the taxpayer's claim for his share of the partnership loss not being a claim in respect of expenditure incurred by him, the old s226(2) of the ITAA had no application.
  6. Here, the applicants' representative contended that as the betterment had been prepared on the basis that there was a partnership, the amounts representing that share could not, on the principles set out in Sahhar's Case, be made the subject of penalties.
  7. There are in my opinion two answers to this submission. The first is that put by Mr. Coles of counsel for the Commissioner, namely that whereas in Sahhar's Case it was held that a share in a partnership loss could not fall within s226, the position here was that taxpayers had been found to have omitted assessable income from a return. The assets betterment statement had shown that the income had been larger than that returned. That conclusion would have arisen whether the present applicants had been partners or not. The reality was that each applicant had omitted income. It did not necessarily follow that, because the applicants were partners in various respects, the income which they had omitted should be regarded as having been partnership income. The omission of income, in these circumstances, did not give rise to the problem that had arisen in Sahhar's Case, namely that of a claim to a deduction.
  8. For the applicants, Mr. Hollands responded that the proposition could not be sustained that the income in question had some source other than through the partnership, and the partnership loss could not be held to be expenditure that had been incurred by the applicants because the partnership had incurred the expenditure: see Sahhar's Case.
  9. I prefer the case put for the Commissioner, as put by Mr. Coles. It is true that the assets betterment statement bulked the two applicants together, quite possibly to their advantage. But it does not seem to me to be possible to conclude that the omitted income should be seen as income of the partnership business of taxi operator and primary producer as proposed by the applicants. For the purposes of his argument Mr. Hollands sought to isolate sums which as calculated by him could be attributed to losses incurred by the partnership as such. I do not consider that this is how the matter should be treated, for the reasons already stated and for the second of the reasons which, as forecast above, have influenced me and to which I now refer.
  10. It is true that an assessment of taxable income prepared on an assets betterment basis should reflect exactly the same result as an assessment prepared on a conventional basis. But its underlying philosophy is different. That philosophy is set out in the reasons of Board of Review No.2 in Case D47 72 ATC 272, where at 276-277 it was said:

"8. An assets betterment statement is, of course, nothing more than an alternative means of calculating taxable income. Normally this is done by aggregating the amounts of assessable income derived during a fiscal year and taking therefrom all allowable deductions. But it is possible to obtain the same result by a different process of calculation from a different commencing point.

9. Let us take an example. If it is known how much a person has saved during a year and what he has expended during that time without recourse to what he had at the commencement of the period, his taxable income can quite easily be calculated. As a first step the total receipts for the year are determined by adding together what he has saved and what he has spent. It is necessary then to convert the total amount received to assessable income derived during the year. This is done by excluding amounts representing gifts, legacies, lottery wins and the like, which although received do not represent assessable income. Then there are added amounts of assessable income derived but not received. The next step is to dissect the outgoings into (a) outgoings incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, that is to say, outgoings which are deductible under the general provisions of the Act; (b) outgoings to acquire assets or to reduce or extinguish liabilities; and (c) all other outgoings which for convenience may be described as private expenditure. At this stage the taxable income is calculated merely by subtracting the outgoings in category (a) from the assessable income derived as calculated in the manner described. For the sake of simplicity, we have assumed that there are no deductions such as depreciation which are not represented by actual outgoings during the year, and no concessional deductions. It can be seen that, in the calculations described above, the outgoings in category (a) played two parts. Firstly, they were a part of what was added in the process of determining assessable income derived and then subtracted for the purpose of determining taxable income. The ultimate result would not be altered in any way if they were complete[ly] ignored. It follows that the taxable income could be determined in the circumstances described merely by adding to the amount saved the expenditure in categories (b) and (c) and making adjustments for receipts of a non-assessable nature and for assessable income derived but not received.

10. What has just been described represents the concept upon which an assets betterment statement is founded. In such a statement the excess of assets over liabilities (i.e. net assets) at the end of a year is compared with the corresponding excess at the beginning of the year and the increase determined. If this increase represents the amount saved by the taxpayer from the receipts of the year then it corresponds with the first amount taken in the example discussed above. However, if the increase in net assets is not wholly so attributable suitable adjustments have to be made. It is at this point that confusion is most likely to arise because the necessity for and the effect of the adjustments are not always immediately apparent. To the increase in net assets (or adjusted figure if applicable) there has to be added private expenditure, i.e. expenditure of the type in category (c) above. In the same way as mentioned in the example discussed, adjustments have to be made for receipts of a non-assessable nature and for assessable income derived but not received. The resultant figure is the taxable income, again on the assumption that there are no deductions such as depreciation not represented by outgoings and no concessional deductions. Perhaps it should be mentioned that in an assets betterment statement there is no necessity to be concerned with the source of the funds expended during a year, for if recourse is had to what was on hand at the beginning of the period the assets at the end are pro tanto reduced. Similarly, expenditure of the type in category (b) can be ignored for such expenditure is also automatically reflected in the amount of the net assets at the end of the period.

11. Provided [that] an assets betterment statement is properly prepared and is based upon accurate information the taxable income it discloses will precisely equal that calculated in the conventional manner from properly kept records."

  1. There is further guidance at 300-302, and none of the philosophy expressed in these passages is affected by the fact that an appeal from the Board's decision was successful in part in certain specific respects: See L'Estrange v FC of T 78 ATC 4744, including at 4774 where McGarvie J specifically approved comments made by the Board about the presentation of assets betterment statements. The bottom line of the analysis set out above is to make it, as it seems to me, inappropriate to segregate out a loss perceived as having the imprimatur of a partnership loss, and then to ascribe to it the continuing character of a partnership loss in the context of an assessment of increase in assets by the betterment method.

18. Although the claims made by the applicants all fail, it will be necessary, since by the time of the commencement of the hearing further concessions had been made by the Commissioner, in formal terms to set aside the objection decisions under review and to remit the same to the Commissioner for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the adjustments agreed upon between the parties as set out in Exhibits 3, 4, 5 and E be given effect. This will of course involve repayment of the lodgment fees that were paid, not an insubstantial amount when eight years are in question.
