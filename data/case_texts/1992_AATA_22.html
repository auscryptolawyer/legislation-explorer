Re Roger Alan Maxsted Harris and Commissioner of Superannuation [1992] AATA 22; (1992) 15 AAR 37; (1992) 26 ALD 37 (23 January 1992)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: ROGER ALAN MAXSTED HARRIS

HEARING

PERTH

23:1:1992

ORDER

The Tribunal decides to vary the decision under review by revoking the substitute BCC issued by the delegate of the respondent on 4 December 1990 and in its place directs the respondent to issue a substitute BCC specifying the condition of "disturbance of the circulation of the brain in the area of the left parieto-occipital region either causing cerebral infarction or subarachnoidal haemorrhage" as a physical condition of the applicant in respect of which the Tribunal is of the opinion that there is a real risk that the applicant by reason of, or for a reason connected with, that condition will not continue to be an eligible employee until he attains his maximum retiring age. The Decision under Review

DECISION

This is an application for review of a decision made by The delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation on 4 December 1990. That decision is in the following terms:

"I, Ronald Charles WHITHEAR, Delegate of the

Commissioner for Superannuation (the Commissioner),

being of the opinion for the purposes of

subparagraph 16(8)(b)(ii) of the Superannuation Act

1976 (the Act) that there is a real risk that Mr

Harris, by reason of or for a reason connected with

the condition "History of Intra Cranial

Haemorrhage", will not continue to be an eligible

employee until he attains his maximum retiring age,

VARY, pursuant to subsection 154(4) of the Act, the

decision taken by a delegate under subparagraph

16(8)(b)(ii) on 9 March 1988 to revoke the benefit

classification certificate (BCC) in force in

respect of Mr Harris and to issue a substitute BCC

specifying the conditions "History of Intra Cranial

Haemorrhage" and "History of Backache". BY

SUBSTITUTING for that decision, a decision pursuant

to subparagraph 16(8)(b)(ii) to revoke the original

BCC and to issue a substitute BCC specifying only

the condition "History of Intra Cranial

Haemorrhage"."

Whether that decision is constituted by the original decision to issue a BCC in respect of the applicant, as subsequently varied, (including the variation effected by the decision of the delegate on reconsideration on 4 December 1990) or whether it consisted of the action taken entirely under s. 16(8)(b)(ii) of the Act, emerged as an issue in the course of the hearing. It may be material to the outcome of this application and is further considered below (paragraphs 42 and 47). Factual Background

2. The following facts are not in dispute and are accepted by the Tribunal. The applicant, who was born on 2 May 1943, became an "eligible employee", as defined in s. 3(1) of the Act, on 10 October 1983. He was therefore required to undergo a medical examination in accordance with s. 16(2) of the Act to determine his fitness for employment.

3. He was examined by a Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO"), Dr J. Montgomery, on 12 October 1983. The CMO considered that the applicant was likely to continue to be an eligible employee until the relevant retiring age and made the comment "Accept as meeting general medical standard." However, on oversighting the CMO's report in his capacity as advisor to the Commissioner, Dr N. O'Brien, a Senior Medical Officer, disagreed with Dr Montgomery's conclusion as he considered the applicant was not likely to continue to be employed until attaining maximum retiring age by reason of:

"Anxiety, Intra Cranial Haemorrhage and Backache."

4. A delegate of the Commissioner issued a BCC in respect of the applicant specifying the conditions "History of Anxiety", "History of Intra Cranial Haemorrhage" and "History of Backache." The applicant was notified of the issue of the BCC by letter sent on 5 June 1985.

5. On 21 October 1985 the applicant wrote to the Commissioner indicating his dissatisfaction with the issuing of the BCC. This letter was accepted by the Commissioner as a request pursuant to s. 16(6) of the Act to revoke the BCC.

6. To assist the review process, the applicant was referred to a neurologist, Dr S.S. Gubbay. In his report dated 15 December 1986, Dr Gubbay stated:

"...This patient was seen by me on 15 December 1986. I understood

there was a question of his being accepted for superanuation benefits

with regard to the possibilty of reccurence of presumed previous

subarachnoid haemorrhage as detailed in a letter by Doctor A. Fisher on

7th October, 1970. Dr Fisher considered that the patient had suffered

from a small intracerebral and subrachnoid haemorrhage. He thought this

dervied from an arteriovenous malformation which had destroyed itself as

the source of bleeding could not be found during extensive

investigations. I assume that the lumbar puncture which Mr Harris had

at the time confirmed the presence of subarachnoid bleeding and I also

assumed that Mr Harris must have been subjected to four vessel cerebral

angiography which had not revealed the presence of either an

intra cranial aneurysm or arteriovenous malformation.

Mr Harris recounted the incident which occured in 1965 when he was

driving a vehicle and his vision suddenly became blurry. The vehicle

was stationary at the time and he was able to see sufficiently to turn

his vehicle around the corner and bring it to a stop even though his

vision was out of focus. Thus he must have had the ability to recognise

shapes and certainly faces as was evident later when he was examined by

the doctor. Within an hour of the visual distubance he developed a

generalised headache which lasted for a couple of days. It was during

this time that he was admitted to Royal Perth Hospital under the

care of Dr Fisher who investigated him and who kept him there for (a)

period of five to six weeks altogether . . .

. . .

I saw his 2 CT head scans of 1983 and subsequently of 1985 and both

of these showed an area of old infarction situated deeply in the

left parieto-occipital region.

On trying to piece everything together logically it seems that this

patient was considered by Dr Fisher to have suffered subarachnoid

haemorrhage probably from an arteriovenous malformation which could not

be identified subsequently angiographically. In most cases of this type

of arteriovenous malformation which destroys itself in the process of

bleeding, no further complication can be expected.

There are some possible alternatives to the conclusion reached by

Dr Fisher. One alternative is that the patient did have an episode of

migrane in 1965 and that the migrane had resulted in cerebral

infarction. I mention this because the patient's first symptom was that

of visual disturbance and he did not have headache until perhaps an hour

later. It would by atypical for patients with subarachnoid haemorrhage to

suffer from a neurological distubance before the onset of explosive

headache. Another reason why this might have been an episode of

migrainous cerebral infarction that the patient on two subsequent

occasions, once in 1983 and again last year had a transient disturbance

of vision which caused him to have difficulty in reading for a period

of hours. Although he did not suffer from headache on either of these

occasions, this would fit in with the theory that is an occasional

visual migrane sufferer and that the first occasion was associated with

an area of infarction in his brain. Apparently he was considered by

Dr Fisher to have had a subarachnoid haemorrhage presumably after blood
was

found in the cerebrospinal fluid. Dr Fisher obviously in his own mind

must have decided that the blood in his cerebrospinal fluid was not

of spurious and traumatic origin but due to subarachnoid bleeding.

If the patient did indeed have a subarachnoid haemorrhage, then one

of the possibilities and perhaps the most likely is that he did indeed

have arteriovenous malformation. However another significant possibility

is that he had a cerebral aneurysm which was never identified

angiographically because of perhaps a degree of cerebral vasospasm at

the time. It might be possible to pick up the presence of an aneurysm at

this late stage but certainly it would be not justifiable to subject him

to risk of angiography particularly as he may be a sufferer of migraine

which compounds the risk of cerebral infarction from that type of

procedure.

Perhaps the most equitable arrangement regarding superannuation is

that he should be precluded from getting benefits only from a

neurological condition which can be proven to have been the result

of a disturbance of the circulation of the brain in the area of the

previous infarction ie., the complication of migrainous infarction or a

further subarachnoid bleed from that area."

7. On 9 March 1988 a delegate of the Commissioner, having considered the available medical evidence, was satisfied that of all the three conditions mentioned in paragraph 4 only the " History of Anxiety" did not pose a real risk of the applicant's premature retirement. Accordingly, the delegate revoked the BCC and issued a substitute BCC specifying the two conditions "History of Intra Cranial Haemorrhage" and "History of Backache."

8. The applicant was notified of the delegate's decision by letter dated 10 March 1988. By letter dated 20 June 1988 to the Commissioner, the applicant advised that he did not accept the delegate's decision in respect of the "History of Backache" and, further, asked him to consider that as a long time had elapsed since 1966 when the incident of Intra Cranial Haemorrhage had occurred, it was unlikely the problem would recur.

9. In accordance with s. 154(4) of the Act, the decision of the delegate was reconsidered. On further medical assessment, the delegate came to the conclusion that there was no significant risk of the applicant's premature retirement by reason of the backache problem.

10. As to the condition of "History of Intra Cranial Haemorrhage" the delegate formed the opinion that there was a real risk that the applicant would not continue to be an eligible employee until attaining his maximum retiring age. He therefore made the decision set forth in paragraph 1 above which is now under review.

11. In coming to that conclusion, the delegate of the Commissioner took into account, among other things, a further report of Dr Gubbay dated 11 August 1988 in which he says:

"With regard to ... your letter you have asked me

whether there is a real risk to Mr Harris retiring

before the age of 65 years by reason of his

previous history of cerebral haemorrhage or any

associated condition. He is now 45 years of age

and therefore you are asking me whether anything is

likely to happen to him in the next 20 years which

might disable him to the point where he will have

to retire earlier which is connected with his

previous condition.

My opinion is that he has a five to ten percent

risk of needing to retire before the age of 65

years on the grounds of this condition. He might

still have a small arterio-venous malformation or

aneurysm. It is even possible but unlikely that

the cerebral infarction or previous subarachnoid

haemorrhage could result in late complications such

as hydrocephalus or epilepsy.

I mentioned all the above possibilities only for

the sake of completeness but my honest belief is

that it is unlikely that any complications from

that condition will occur and disable him."

Issue

12. The condition "History Intra Cranial Haemorrhage" on the subsitute BCC is the bone of contention before the Tribunal. The essential issue is whether or not there is a "real risk", within the meaning of s. 16(4) and s. 16(8)(b) of the Act, of the applicant suffering a recurrence of Intra Cranial Haemorrhage that would prevent him reaching his maximum retiring age. Implicit in that is the question of whether Intra Cranial Haemorrhage is the actual medical condition that the applicant relevantly had prior to becoming an eligible employee.

Meaning of "Real Risk"

22. In re Nicholson and the Commissioner For Superannuation (1987) 7 AAR 371 the Tribunal discussed "real risk" in the following terms (p 373):

"Both Mr Lorkin and Mr Whithear drew a distinction

between a bare possibility and a real risk. We

consider that there is a proper distinction to

make. We were invited to examine the meanings of

the individual words "real" and "risk" given in

various dictionaries, and have done so; however, we

are satisfied that the mere conjunction of any of

the meanings of "real" with any of the meanings of

"risk" does not capture the essence of the phrase

"real risk", which is a phrase having connotations

of its own distinct from the dictionary meanings of

the two words comprising it. In the phrase "risk"

refers to a chance that something detrimental will

occur. There is a "real risk" where a factual

situation exists which has inherent in it a

particular potential to result in that detrimental

occurrence. Usually the phrase is used in relation

to the process of making an assessment of whether

precautions are warranted to prevent either the

occurrence of the detrimental thing or the

consequences of it occurring. We are satisfied

that in the context of the Act s. 16 is intended to

provide that a benefit classification certificate

should be issued where an eligible employee is

suffering from an identified condition which has a

particular potential to result in his or her dying,

or becoming so incapacitated as to be unable to

continue in employment, before reaching maximum

retiring age." (emphasis added)

23. In re Gelsthorpe and Commissioner for Superannuation (1988) 9 AAR 361 commenting on the preceding excerpt, the Tribunal stated as follows (at p 377):

"This Tribunal finds some difficulty in applying

only the test which is set out in Re Nicholson.

The Tribunal does not consider that the words

"particular potential" for a condition to result in

a detrimental occurrence, are a substitution for

the words "real risk". The words "particular

potential" in turn would require some definition.

Does the word "particular" mean "specific" or does

it mean "something special"? It could be said that

any mental or physical condition has inherent

within it a particular potential to result in a

detrimental occurrence, and the word "particular"

says nothing about the gravamen or the extent of

that potential. This Tribunal considers it

preferable to view the words "real risk" not as a

term of art but rather to regard them as a matter

of common parlance. The word "risk" refers to a

chance or hazard (see Macquarie Dictionary) and the

word "real" in that context would mean something

other than nominal, imaginary, fictitious or

fanciful. Therefore a "real risk" would be a

chance which is more than nominal, imaginary,

fictitious or fanciful but less than a probability;

how much less than a probability it is not possible

to specify." (emphasis added)

24. The Tribunal in that decision stated 4 principles which provide some assistance in deciding when a condition constitutes a "real risk". These are:

(a) The term "real risk" is not a term of art and

should be interpreted in accordance with the

dictionary meaning.

(b) "Real risk" refers to a chance of an

occurrence which is real and is more than

nominal, imaginary, fictitious or fanciful

but less than a probability.

(c) "Real risk" requires consideration and

assessment of the characteristics of the

person, the nature, gravamen and

characteristics of the condition in the

context of employment to maximum retirement

age.

(d) Although percentages may sometimes assist in

assessing the seriousness or otherwise of

certain conditions in relation to employment,

"real risk" is not a quantitative assessment

such that it can be reduced to percentage

terms in order to determine its existence.

25. In re Bergin and Commissioner for Superannuation (1989) 19 ALD 279, at 281 the Tribunal again addressed the issue and made the following comment:

"The test used in Re Gelsthorpe describes a risk

which is real as one which is more "than nominal,

imaginary, fictitious or fanciful but less than a

possibility". During the hearing of this matter

the Tribunal referred to the Shorter Oxford

Dictionary for the meaning of the word "nominal".

The only meaning which appears relevant is meaning

"4" which is as follows: "Existing in name only, as

dist. from real or actual; merely named, stated, or

expressed, without ref. to reality or fact." A

risk which exists in name only as distinct from a

real or actual risk, is not a risk at all and

neither is a risk which is named, stated, or

expressed without reference to reality or fact.

Neither is an imaginary risk a real risk.

Similarly a fictitious risk and a fanciful risk are

not in fact risks at all.

If the only risk present was a nominal, imaginary,

fictitious or fanciful risk there would be no real

risk. In those circumstances neither the test in

Re Gelthorpe nor that in Re Nicholson would be

satisfied. The factual situation would not exist

which had inherent in it the potential to result in

incapacity or death due to the specified medical

condition. Nor would there be evidence that the

medical condition may directly or though its

complications cause incapacity. We agree with Mr

Eadie that the test formulated in Re Gelsthorpe is

not more favourable to an applicant than that in Re

Nicholson. It was the Tribunal's application of

the test to the facts it found which led to the

more favourable result."

26. More recently, in re Kirby and Commissioner for Superannuation (decision No. 6173; 4 September 1990) at pp 9 and 10, after discussing the above views, the Tribunal then stated (at p 9-10):

"The choice of the word "real" is perhaps

unfortunate, as viewed in isolation it is

imprecise. At one end any risk which is logically

possible is real but when "real" is used in

ordinary parlance as an adjective it has a

connotation of something more than simply "a" or a

"remote" risk. On one view any risk may be said to

be a "real risk" but it may also be true that a

slight risk is not a real risk in common parlance.

Nor is it necessarily an answer to look at the

purpose of the legislation. The interest of the

applicant that he be accepted as a full member of

the superannuation scheme is not necessarily in

harmony with the interests of existing members of

the scheme who may be best served by a narrower

interpretation of the term "real risk". Real is

not a question of degree and the Tribunal has to be

cautious in looking at the risk expressed in terms

of degrees. The answer to the meaning of "real

risk" does not depend on some percentage of risk.

Expressing a percentage is no more than an

indicator of whether there is a likelihood of

premature retirement."

27. Finally, in Re Nix and Commissioner for Superannuation (decision No. 7347; 6 September 1991) an alternative approach was adopted. After discussing Nicholson and Gelsthorpe the Tribunal then continued:

"On further reflection a better approach is to have

regard to definition 2 in the Shorter Oxford

Dictionary of the word "real", namely:

"Actually existing or present as a state or

quality of things; having a foundation in fact;

actually occurring or happening."

and then by defining "risk" by saying that it

implies not that the event postulated, in this case

premature retirement on invalidity grounds, will

happen but that the circumstances are such as that

precautions ought to be taken to avoid the

possibility - Cf Buckley L.J. in the Guildhall (1908)

P 29 at p 41."

28. The Tribunal in this matter agrees with the view expressed in Kirby (supra) that the juxtaposition of the word "real" with "risk" is conceptually inelegant. Risk is of itself something that draws on existing circumstances but contemplates the chance of a particular occurrence in the future. It relates to something that is potential rather than actualised. Nevertheless, the legislative choice having been made, the Tribunal must apply the notion of "real risk" as best as it is able. To that end it should take into account the legislative history of the provision. The substitution of "real risk" for the previous test which was expressed in terms of likelihood is therefore taken to be significant. It should therefore not be taken to depend simply on probabilities. The Tribunal does see validity in each of the tests referred to above that have been offered by way of explanation of the concept of "real risk". In particular it accepts the point made in Gelsthorpe that, irrespective of any particular likelihood that can be expressed in terms of degree or as a percentage, there must be something more than a mere fanciful or speculative possibility of the specified mental or physical condition giving rise, either of itself or by reason of connected or associated factors, to later problems that would prevent an eligible employee completing the term of his or her appointment. Furthermore, drawing upon the insight expressed in re Nicholson, the possibility of recurrence must have some basis in an identifiable, previously existing medical condition. More will be said of this aspect later. Finally, it seems to the Tribunal that:-

(1) the risk of a medical condition giving rise to

later incapacity in an eligible employee must

always be greater than the statistical

possibility applicable to the general

population or normal Commonwealth employees;

and

(2) the risk of a recurrence must be relative to

the nature of the work-tasks an employee might

reasonably be expected to perform over his or

her employment engagement.

Discussion of Evidence and Findings in terms of whether there is a Real Risk of Intra Cranial Haemorrhage

29. From the evidence of the applicant it emerged that as a teenager the applicant suffered from headaches. These episodes in high school days were associated with nausea and vomiting but not visual disturbance.

30. The next episode of significance occurred on 15 June 1966 when, while driving, Mr Harris had a headache and visual disturbance and was admitted to Royal Perth Hospital where he spent 11 days. Investigation at that time indicated subarachnoid haemorrhage which was confirmed by lumbar puncture. Although Dr Gubbay made some comments in his oral evidence about the reliability of these tests, the Tribunal accepts the letter from the Royal Perth Hospital (exhibit 2) and the report made by Dr Anthony Fisher on 7 December 1970. The applicant had a four vessel angiogram undertaken at the time of this occurrence but no aneurysm or arteriovenous malformation was demonstrated. However, if regard is had to the report of Dr Fisher he stated that a arteriovenous malformation was the most likely diagnosis at the time. Dr Fisher did find that a recurrence of the episode was unlikely.

31. The next point of significance is 1985 when Mr Harris, following a further episode of visual disturbance, was seen by Dr Nicoll and an ophthalmologist who referred him to Dr Bajada. At this time the applicant was having significant visual disturbances associated with nausea. He related to Dr Bajada that he had had three or four episodes of visual disturbances of similar type over the preceding years and some of these had been associated with headache. The only abnormal findings that Dr Bajada's examination disclosed was that there was an upper temporal quadrantic restriction of his visual fields on the right side and that the blind spot was enlarged. This basically means there is a defective area in the vision of his right eye. Dr Bajada ordered a CT scan and this showed loss of tissue with atrophic changes in the left occipital lobe. Dr Bajada's conclusion was that the disturbances fitted best with a basilar migraine and although he was of the view the recent visual symptoms could possibly be on an epileptiform basis, he also felt that the original disturbance in 1966 could have had a migraine basis.

32. The next event was when Mr Harris was referred to Dr Gubbay for neurological assessment on the 15 December 1986. At this time the question of superannuation benefits was raised. Dr Gubbay did a very thorough history and examination and his findings were identical with those of Dr Bajada. His conclusion was that it was likely that the original episode could have been a migraine associated with infarction.

33. In response to specific questions put to him by the Tribunal Dr Gubbay indicated that, in his opinion, Mr Harris could fit into one of two categories:

1. He had had a subarachnoid haemorrhage from an

aneurysm or arteriovenous malformation at the

original episode and that he also had migraine

as an ongoing problem. Even though it was

some 20 years since the subarachnoid

haemorrhage there still was some small risk

that he could have a recurrence of his

subarachnoid haemorrhage but that was

unlikely.

2. The applicant had an ongoing migraine problem.

The 1966 episode had probably been a severe

migraine attack leading to vascular spasm and

therefore infarction of the brain which had

showed up on the CT scan. There was certainly

a real risk that there could be further

infarction during migraine episodes in the

applicant.

The Tribunal's assessment of whether there is a "real risk" of the applicant ceasing to be an eligible employee prior to retirement

34. Having had regard to the evidence, and in particular that of Dr Gubbay, the Tribunal comes to the conclusion that as at the time of issue of the original BCC and indeed at the present time there is a real risk of the applicant not continuing as an eligible employee until his retirement by reason of, arising from or connected with a pre-existing medical condition. An appropriate description of the medical condition giving rise to that risk would be:

"disturbance of circulation of the brain in the

area of the left parieto-occipital region either

causing cerebral infarction or subarachnoid

haemorrhage."

35. The Tribunal would see this as covering either of two conditions, one being subarachnoid haemorrhage and the other a condition of migraine with cerebral infarction. The Tribunal, having regard particularly to Dr Gubbay's conclusions, is of the opinion that that there is a risk of sufficient reality (in terms of being more than merely fanciful or speculative and of a greater chance than encountered in the average employee) of Mr Harris suffering a recurrence of one or the other of these conditions before he reaches the relevant retirement date. He also meets both conditions (1) and (2) set forth at the end of paragraph 28. It is recognised that Dr Gubbay considered that recurrence of each of these conditions was unlikely, and in fact, subarachnoidal haemorrhage more unlikely than cerebral infarct associated with migraine. Nevertheless, the Tribunal accepts his undisputed testimony to the effect that each condition could recur to a degree more than encountered in the general community. In coming to that conclusion the Tribunal sees itself in a position, the same being true of the respondent, of having to exercise a judgment based on a reasonable degree of prudence and caution in respect of tolerating risks of premature retirement. That would seem to be the fundamental purpose of the scheme behind issuing BCC's in accordance with s. 16 of the Act.

36. In saying this, the Tribunal has not overlooked the fact that, having regard to Dr Fisher's initial assessment and the 21 years that have passed since then, the chance of a recurrence of subarachnoidal haemorrhage seems fairly slim. Nevertheless, the Tribunal does not see the possibility of recurrence as so remote in the sense used in Kirby (above) that it should be entirely discounted.

The Tribunal's Powers of Rectification

37. Having come to these conclusions, the question then arises as to what the Tribunal can and should do by way of resolving this application. In this respect there are two problems as the Tribunal sees it.

(a) Whether condition of subarachnoid haemorrhage actually occurred

38. The first arises from the fact that the Tribunal is not able to come to a definite conclusion that the applicant did in fact suffer a subarachnoid haemorrhage in the first place. Though that was Dr Fisher's conclusion, in the light of the later evidence, and in particular Dr Gubbay's assessment of the report of Dr Bajada and the CT scan, it cannot be said with any degree of confidence that in fact the applicant actually suffered a subarachnoid haemorrhage. The issue that therefore arises is whether it is necessary, where a mental or physical condition is specified in a BCC, for that condition actually to have existed. The Tribunal raised this with counsel for the respondent. Mr Whithear indicated that the issue whether a "condition" for the purposes of s. 16(8) of the Act must have existed, had not in fact been pronounced upon by the Tribunal in any previous decision that he was aware of. He did submit that since there was no express reference to the word "exist" in s. 16(8), the inference could be drawn that the existence of a particular condition did not have to be established.

39. The Tribunal is of the view, however, that implicitly s. 16(8) contemplates the existence of a specified condition. In this respect it is necessary to distinguish between the existence of a condition, as a matter of fact, and the conclusion that a condition possibly exists based on plausible evidence. Where the actual diagnosis of a condition is not known with certainty, it is sufficient for the purposes of subsections 16(4) and (8) that a conclusion of the latter kind is reasonably open. There must be a basis in the medical evidence for a conclusion to be drawn that a certain medical condition may exist. To see the matter in this way is to have regard to the overall scheme of s. 16 to protect the superannuation fund from the kind of risks that could be reasonably anticipated as being present in identified individual members.

40. Even though Dr Gubbay preferred the explanation that the applicant in 1966 had suffered infarction associated with migraine, and assessed that as more probable than his having incurred a subarachnoid haemorrhage, he did not discount the possibility of the latter. Accordingly, the Tribunal is satisfied that there is plausible evidence pointing to the possibility that the applicant suffered a subarachnoid haemorrhage in the past.

41. The respondent, through Mr Whithear, quite properly in the view of the Tribunal conceded that the description of 'history of intra cranial haemorrhage' was inappropriate. That would cover possible haemorrhages not of the subarachnoidal kind of which there had been no previous evidence nor suggestion. The overbroad description should, if possible, be reduced to the more restricted and more accurate compass of "history of subarachnoid haemorrhage". Leaving aside, for the moment, what should be done in relation to "migraine with cerebral infarction", can the Tribunal substitute "subarachnoid haemorrhage" for "intra cranial haemorrhage"?

(b) Powers of Tribunal to rectify description in BCC

42. This raises a discreet issue, adverted to in paragraph 1, about the nature of the decision under review, and the powers of the Tribunal in relation to it. It was Mr Whithear's submission that the original decision is that made in accordance with s. 16(4) of the Act so that when application is made to the Tribunal under s. 154(6) to review a "reviewable decision" with a view to it being confirmed, revoked or varied under subsection (4) the Tribunal has at its disposal, in accordance with s. 43(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 all the powers that the Commissioner may exercise in respect of such a reviewable decision. This includes the powers available to the respondent on reconsideration under s. 154(4) to confirm, revoke or vary the decision in such manner as the Commissioner thinks fit. On this view, the decision made under s. 16(8)(b)(ii) is in the nature of an intermediate review, and the decision pursuant to s. 154 takes the matter beyond s. 16. Subsection (8) of s. 16 should, according to the respondent, be seen as a review provision, not a primary decision-making power in its own right.

43. In making the submission, Mr Whithear quite fairly (having regard to the fact Mr Harris was not legally represented) put to the Tribunal an alternative view which was to the effect that if the reviewable decision is seen to be one made under s. 16(8), the powers of the Commissioner in that respect are much more confined so that, depending on whether the particular matter falls within paragraph (a), (b)(i) or (ii), or (c) of subsection 16(8) the only powers available to the Commissioner, and hence the Tribunal is to refuse to revoke a BCC or to revoke it and issue in substitution for that certificate a new "BCC" specifying only the condition or conditions in respect of which the Commissioner is of the opinion that there is a relevant real risk. To see the matter in this way would be, according to the respondent, to treat s. 16(8) as a source of separate and distinct powers from that conferred by s. 16(4).

44. In support for the view contended for by the respondent, Mr Whithear referred to the decision of the Tribunal in Nix (supra) and that of Kirby (supra). In Nix, the Tribunal was asked to consider a BCC in which the specified conditions included "injury to back" and "back pain (recurrent)". The question arose whether a further condition, namely "musculoligamentus back-strain" could be added to the BCC. The Tribunal there said (p 10):

"Notwithstanding the difference in terminology

between paragraphs (a) and (b)(i) compared to

sub-paragraph (b)(ii) of Sub-section 16(8) of the

said Act, as to which see Rohde and Commissioner

for Superannuation 18 ALD 791 at p 792, this

Tribunal considers that, semantic arguments apart,

its task is to consider as a practical matter

whether there is a real risk by reason of, or for a

reason connected with, the applicant's back

condition howsoever described, she will be unable

to continue in her employment until reaching age

65."

It then went on to conclude that pursuant to s. 43(1)(b) of the AAT Act the decision under review could be varied by adding the latter condition.

45. In Kirby the Tribunal was of the opinion that a condition specified as "history of polyarthritis" was not a proper description of a condition which should properly have been described as "history of back pain". Having regard to the evidence the Tribunal was of the opinion that it would have been appropriate in the first instance to have described the condition in the second way. It therefore substituted that description for the original.

46. In Gelsthorpe (supra) the Tribunal (9 AAR at 373-4) addressed aspects of the problem of the width of power under s.16(8)(c) canvassed above and said:

"In these circumstances, it is consistent with

s.16(8)(b)(ii) and s.16(4), that in deciding under

s.16(8)(c) to revoke a BCC and issue a new one, the

Commissioner should be of the opinion that the

positive exists, namely that there is a "real risk"

that, by reason of the new condition, the employee

will not continue to be an eligible employee until

the person attains maximum retiring age.

For the sake of completeness, we mention that

before reaching the above interpretation of

s.16(8)(c), we also gave consideration to another

possible interpretation. A possible alternative

interpretation is that pursuant to s.16(8)(c), the

Commissioner may either refuse the request to

revoke the BCC, or the Commissioner may refuse the

request to revoke the BCC and issue a new BCC in

substitution, the latter request encompassing both

the revocation of a BCC and the issueing of a new

BCC. Such an interpretation gains prima facie

support from the wording of s.16(6) which uses the

same wording and as amended reads:

"an eligible employee ... may ... request the

Commissioner to revoke the certificate or to

revoke the certificate and issue in

substitution for the certificate a new benefit

classification certificate" (Tribunal

emphasis)

However, if this interpretation were adopted, the

Act would be deficient in that there would be no

provision which empowered the Commissioner to

accede to an employee's request (pursuant to

s.16(6) to revoke an existing BCC and at the same

time issue a new BCC which specified a condition or

conditions not previously specified. This

situation would arise because the alternative power

of a Commissioner under s.16(8)(c) is to refuse the

request to revoke the BCC and issue a substitute

BCC, and the only other powers are either to revoke

the BCC (s.16(8)(a) and (b)), or refuse the request

to revoke the BCC: s.16(8)(c). There would be no

power to revoke a BCC and issue a certificate in

substitution unless it related to a condition

already specified in the certificate: s.16

(8)(b)(ii).

This was obviously not the intention of the

legislature when it drafted the amendments to

s.16(6) and s.16(8)(c) as contained in Act No. 151

of 1986. Prior to these amendments, the

Commissioner was empowered to "revoke or vary the

certificate"; the insertion of the words "to revoke

the certificate or to revoke the certificate and

issue in substitution for the certificate a new

benefit classification certificate" was in effect a

replacement of the alternative to "vary" with the

alternative to revoke and issue a new certificate."

47. As the Tribunal sees the present case, the actual decision under review is the original decision to issue a BCC, but incorporating the later variations that have resulted in the issue of a BCC in accordance with the terms determined after consideration by the delegate of the Commissioner on 4 December 1990. The decision is that which emerges from the process entailed by the initial issue of a BCC but subject to revision according to the process under s.16(8) prior to reconsideration under s.154(4). Subsequently, where that decision has been reconsidered under s.154(4) the Tribunal is able to revoke the existing certificate and issue one in its place which more accurately describes a condition having the characteristics that satisfy the criteria set forth under s.16(8)(b). In the present case, the Tribunal regards itself as competent, therefore, to revoke the present certificate specifying "history of intra cranial haemorrhage" and to issue a new certificate substituting in its place "history of subarachnoid haemorrhage".

48. The more difficult question is whether in revoking the existing certificate the Tribunal can issue a new one which includes as well as "subarachnoid haemorrhage" the other condition of "history of migraine with cerebral infarction". The Tribunal has no difficulty with the course adopted in Kirby (supra); that is, of substituting an accurate description of a condition which indisputably should have been given to a condition then diagnosed as existing. There was no doubt in Kirby that the original diagnosed condition really was "back pain" and the description "polyarthritis" was never an appropriate description.

49. In the present case there was material available to the delegate of the respondent on the first request by Mr Harris under s.16(6) from which the conclusion might have been drawn that infarction associated with migraine had been the cause of the original incident in 1966. That condition, however, has never been specified as such in any BCC. It is clear that in terms of its character it is something quite separate and different from the condition of haemorrhage. The description of "intra cranial haemorrhage" was therefore never an appropriate specification of that condition. Can the Tribunal now, in issuing a substitute BCC, add that condition?

50. This depends on whether, for the purposes of reviewing, the decision (as varied) pursuant to s.154(6) of the Act, the action of adding a new, previously unspecified, condition could be said to constitute a "variation" within the terms of s.43(1) of the AAT Act of the original decision. This issue does not appear to have arisen explicitly in any other previous decision of the Tribunal besides that in Nix, though some consideration was given by the Tribunal in Lockwood and McMurray (party joined) and Commissioner for Superannuation (1990) 22 ALD 57, at 64, to what constitutes a variation. To "vary" according to the appropriate definition in the Shorter Oxford Dictionary is "to cause to change or alter; to introduce changes or alterations into (something); in later use... to adapt to certain circumstances or requirements by appropriate modifications; to express in different words".

51. In the Tribunal's view, variation certainly includes the rectification of a misdescription and the amendment of a defective description. That is consistent with the approach taken, as the Tribunal sees it, in Kirby. "Vary", however, is not appropriate to describe the substitution of a condition of an entirely different character for a subsisting condition which is completely unrelated to the latter (re Dyer [1935] VicLawRp 50; (1935) VLR 273).

52. Where, however, a medical incident has occurred and there may be uncertainty about its diagnosis, it is, in the opinion of the Tribunal, open to the respondent under s.154(4) upon a reconsideration to issue a substitute BCC adding an appropriately described condition which is related in terms of its physiological location and history to one previously specified, in order more accurately to cover the risks to the Fund.

53. To take this view does not require one to regard s.16(8) as permitting the same scope for substitution of previously unspecified conditions as does s.154(4). The powers under s.16(8) are more restricted: they relate to revocation and issue of certificates on which particular conditions are specified. There is no wide freedom to specify something that has not been previously particularised. But to take a more confined view of s.16(8) than of s.154(4) is not inconsistent with the scheme of the Act.

54. In this regard, it seems reasonable to assume that the original consideration and issue of a BCC under s.16(4) will be executed by a delegate of the respondent of a lower (or at least not higher) rank than an officer of the respondent who reviews the matter under s.16(8). The latter in turn will be lower or certainly not higher than the officer delegated to conduct a reconsideration under s.154(4). If that is not the legislative intent a reviewing officer would be potentially subject to improper influence by the higher officer who has made the decision under review. Given that hierarchical situation, it is not unreasonable to assume that whilst the powers of substitution are carefully codified and limited under sub-sections 16(4) and (8) (ie. up to the stage of intermediate review) there should be no such limitations upon reconsideration at the level of an officer who can be expected to perform the functions conferred by s.154(4).

55. Therefore, the Tribunal's powers on a review pursuant to s.154(6) should not, in the opinion of the Tribunal, be regarded as subject to the same constrictions applied under s. 16(8). Hence, in "varying" a decision of the respondent the Tribunal may include a previously unspecified condition provided it was inherent in the factual substratum of the medical condition(s) notified on the original BCC (re Ball's Settlement Trust (1968) 1 WLR 899).

56. In the present case, the infarction associated with migraine, though not accurately covered by the description "History of Intra Cranial Haemorrhage", was a condition that could plausibly have existed in the vicinity of and at the time of the incident in respect of which the latter description was used. With the benefit of hindsight and better information available to it, the Tribunal is able, as it sees the matter, to add by way of specification, a new description that is capable of including it. The condition of infarction is not so unrelated to the kind of precipitating occurrence that it would not be proper to cover the possibility that it might recur and disable the applicant.

57. To approach the matter in this way does not entail any breach of the requirements of natural justice, as the Tribunal sees it. The condition of a possible infarct associated with migraine has been evident since 1986 on the face of Dr Gubbay's report of 15 December 1986. The applicant cannot therefore be said to be taken by surprise by the Tribunal's present concern about that condition.

Conclusions

58. Accordingly, the Tribunal decides to vary the decision under review by revoking the BCC issued by the delegate of the respondent on 4 December 1990 and in its place directs the respondent to issue a substitute BCC specifying the condition of "disturbance of the circulation of the brain in the area of the left parieto-occipital region either causing cerebral infarction or subarachnoidal haemorrhage" as a physical condition of the applicant in respect of which the Tribunal is of the opinion that there is a real risk that the applicant by reason of, or for a reason connected with, that condition will not continue to be an eligible employee until he attains his maximum retiring age.
