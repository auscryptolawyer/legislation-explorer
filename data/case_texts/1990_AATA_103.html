QT89/160-163 and Commissioner of Taxation [1990] AATA 103; (1990) 21 ATR 3393; 90 ATC 393 (2 May 1990)

Last Updated: 1 June 2009

Income tax - Allowable deductions - Depreciation - transfer of office

furniture already fully depreciated - whether entitled to further

depreciation deduction - whether formation of partnership or transfer of

furniture void.



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 : ss.54, 55, 56, 59AA, 60, 62, 90, 260.



Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 : s.43



Case S63 (1967) 17 TBRD



Case W46 89 ATC 442



Donebus v. FCT 88 ATC 4583



Ferling v. FCT [1966] HCA 85; (1966) 14 ATD 66 - (on appeal 372)



Levy v. FCT [1961] HCA 92; (1960) 12 ATD 231

APPLICANT

DECISION



The decision of the Tribunal is that the objection decisions under review



be set aside and the objections be remitted to the respondent to decide



in accordance with the following directions;



(a) the formation of the Toeco partnership is not void as against



the respondent Commissioner;



(b) section 60 of the Act operates so as to deny the Toeco



partnership any deduction for depreciation in respect of the



office furniture transferred to the partnership by the



applicant on 1{# July 1979;



(c) the vacuum cleaner was not used for the purpose of producing



assessable income;



(d) the Tribunal has formed the opinion that the circumstances are



not such that depreciation based on the actual consideration



of $185,000 in respect of the office furniture should be



allowed;



(e) the rental income derived by the partnership from hire of the



office furniture is assessable income and outgoings incurred



in producing that income are allowable deductions;



(f) the depreciation deduction claimed in respect of the



photocopier is to be allowed as a deduction; and



(g) liberty to apply on two days notice in writing to the other



will be reserved to both parties with respect to these



directions.

REASONS FOR DECISION



Before: Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)



Date: 2 May 1990



Place: Brisbane



The question for decision is whether a partnership of which the applicant



is a partner is entitled to a deduction in respect of depreciation of



various items of office furniture. The partnership leases the office



furniture to an accountancy practice of which the applicant was initially



the sole proprietor. From 1 July 1983 the practice was carried on by a



firm of which the applicant was one of the partners. The applications



before the Tribunal relate to the years of income ended 30 June 1982 to



30 June 1985 inclusive.



2. The applicant conducted his own case and the respondent was



represented by Mr M Pope of counsel.



3. In these applications the relevant statutory provisions are sections



54, 59AA, 60 and 260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act").



Section 54 provides in sub-section 1 for depreciation of articles owned



by a taxpayer and used by him during the year of income for the purpose



of producing assessable income. For the purposes of s.54 the partnership



is deemed to be the taxpayer pursuant to s.90 of the Act. The basis for



depreciation is set out in s.55 of the Act and s.56 provides for the



calculation of the depreciation.



4. Section 59AA provides that where for any reason including the



formation of a partnership a change has occurred in the ownership of



property in respect of which depreciation has been allowed or is



allowable under the Act and there is a continuity of interest in the



ownership of the property by a person or persons who owned the property



before the change then the property shall be deemed to have been disposed



of for the agreed amount.



5. Section 60 deals with the depreciation allowable to a purchaser of



depreciated property. Sub-section (1) provides that where a person has



acquired any property, in respect of which depreciation has been allowed



or is allowable under the Act, that person shall not be entitled to any



greater deduction for depreciation than that which would have been



allowed to the person from whom the property was acquired if that person



had retained the property. The proviso to the sub-section is not



relevant to the facts of these applications.



6. Sub-section 60(2) provided (as at 1 June 1979) that s.60 should not



apply where the Commissioner was of the opinion that the circumstances



were such that depreciation based on the actual consideration given



should be allowed. By Act No 149 of 1979 the sub-section was amended so



as to provide, in the same circumstances, that depreciation in respect of



the property should be calculated without regard to s.60.



7. Section 260 has the effect of making certain contracts agreements or



arrangements void against the Commissioner. Although it was not made



clear to the Tribunal it appears that the Commissioner took the view that



the agreement for sale of the office equipment had the effect of



relieving the partners from liability to pay income tax.



The Facts



8. The applicant is a chartered accountant by profession. For a period



of time ending on 31 March 1978 he carried on practice in partnership



with M. The partnership was terminated on that date when the applicant



purchased M's 50% interest in the partnership. Included in the assets of



the partnership of which the applicant became the sole proprietor was a



number of items of office furniture apparently used in the day to day



conduct of the practice. Exhibit J evidences the fact that the former



partners agreed that no consideration was paid in respect of the office



furniture. That was a commercial decision, apparently based upon the



fact that the furniture had been fully written off for tax purposes.



Exhibit 3 which includes photographs of much of the subject furniture as



it was in 1986 indicates that the furniture would have had a market value



at all relevant times.



9. From 1 April 1978 to 30 June 1983 the applicant conducted the



accountancy practice as a sole trader. With effect from 1 July 1984 the



practice was merged with another practice and the combined practices



conducted by a partnership in which the applicant was a member.



10. The subject office furniture does not appear as an asset in the



financial accounts of the applicant in respect of the year of income



ended 30 June 1979 and I infer did not appear in the accounts for the



year ended 30 June 1978.



11. However, the return of income of the applicant for the year ended



30 June 1979 included a note which read as follows (edited to maintain



confidentiality):



"Capital Gain ... Toeco



On 1 May, 1977 I purchased a 50% interest in the accountancy



practice of Abacus & Co. On 31 March, 1978 I purchased the



remaining 50% interest from ... . In both sales no values were



attached to the office furniture, fixtures and fittings. On



1 June 1979 I introduced these assets on formation of the



partnership into the partnership of Toeco at an agreed value of



$185,000."



The balance sheet of the applicant, as at 30 June 1979 revealed an



investment in Toeco of $185,020. Nothing else was shown in the return in



relation to disposal of office furniture and fittings and no outgoing



appeared for lease of office furniture and fittings but it appears that



an amount of $520 is subsumed in a larger amount claimed for equipment



leasing.



12. The return of income of Toeco for the year ended 30 June 1979



covering the period 1 June 1979 to 30 June 1979 revealed income from



equipment rental of $520, outgoings of $2.50 for bank charges and $27 for



registration of business name and $1,251 claimed for depreciation as per



a depreciation schedule. That schedule set out details of the office



equipment acquired by the partnership from the applicant and also a



vacuum cleaner acquired from the applicant's wife for $15,000. The



resulting loss was distributed amongst the partners of Toeco as to 50% to



the applicant, 25% to the applicant's wife, as to 15% to an employee of



the applicant and as to 10% to another employee of the applicant.



13. The partnership return of Toeco for the period to 30 June 1979 is



marked as having been assessed by an officer of the respondent in



December 1979. The evidence before the Tribunal does not disclose when



the applicant was assessed and notified of that assessment in respect of



the year ended 30 June 1979. Exhibit M includes a letter addressed to



the applicant by a Deputy Commissioner of Taxation dated April 1980.



That was, in effect, a requisition for additional information in relation



to the applicant's 1979 return. Amongst other things it asked detailed



questions in relation to the "Capital Gain on Sale of Furniture -



$185,000". Those questions revealed that the respondent's officers



already had an understanding of the transactions in relation to the



furniture. The questions appear to have been asked for the purpose of



giving effect to the operation of s.60 of the Act.



14. The respondent supplied answers to the respondent's requisition by



letter dated 7 May 1980 (Exhibit M). Those answers do not directly



answer each question but the answers were fulsome and accurately describe



the details of the arrangements entered into. On my reading of the



letter of 7 May 1980 the respondent had been apprised of the relevant



facts insofar as the operation of s.60 was concerned. The information



supplied included the following:-



(a) a copy of the depreciation schedule of the former partnership



between the applicant and M for the period ended 31 March 1978;



(b) a clear statement that the office furniture was deemed to have



a "nil" value when the applicant acquired M's interest in the



furniture and that was the value in the records of that



partnership; and



(c) a clear statement that the office furniture was valued at "nil"



and not depreciated by the applicant prior to disposal to Toeco



although the office furntiure had been used continuously in the



accounting practice.



15. There is no evidence before the Tribunal as to what happened after



that because the notices of assessment are not in evidence before the



Tribunal. It is possible to draw inferences from the evidence that



assessments were made by the respondent's officers.



16. The returns of Toeco and the applicant are marked as having been



assessed on the following dates:



Y/e. 30{% June Toeco Applicant



1979 December 1979 April 1980



1980 July 1980 April 1981



1981 September 1981 March 1982



1982 (Not marked) April 1983



1983 December 1983 February 1984



1984 (Not marked) December 1984



1985 (Not marked) January 1986



It should be added that the returns lodged by Toeco were either lodged



earlier than the applicant's return or at the same time. At all relevant



times the respondent had Toeco's return before making the applicant's



assessment and each return of the applicant specifically directed the



respondent to Toeco's return. Most of the relevant returns bear the



notation "AXM" which apparently means that the assessor proceeded without



reference to the partnership return (cf. Levy v. FCT [1961] HCA 92; (1960) 12 ATD 231).



17. Although not in evidence it appears that the respondent caused a



further requisition for information to be issued on 21 April 1986 in



regard to the Toeco partnership. This can be inferred from the



applicant's response dated 14 May 1986 (T8). That letter supplied



considerable information which had not previously been supplied to the



respondent including:



(a) copy Toeco partnership agreement (Exhibit 1);



(b) copy business name registration (T5);



(c) copy of letter of 1 June 1979 re formation of Toeco



partnership (Exhibit L);



(d) copy of the partnership minute dated 1 June 1979



evidencing the first meeting of the partners and their



acquisition of office furniture at $200,000 to be leased



to the applicant at a rental of $1040 per annum (T4); and



(e) additional information regarding the conduct of the



business of Toeco.



18. Included in that additional information was a statement that as from



1 July 1983 the office furniture had been leased to a new firm of which



the applicant was one of the partners. Apparently the applicant's



accounting practice had been merged with another accounting practice.



19. No explanation was given to the Tribunal as to why the respondent



had been prepared to assess the partnership over a number of years



without this information and then determined to requisition for



information concerning the commencement of the business. As it



transpires the additional information regarding the commencement of



business and also the conduct of business only confirmed the factual



situation represented to the respondent in the income tax returns of



Toeco. Those returns when read in conjunction with the explanation



provided by the applicant in his letter of 7 May 1980 (Exhibit M) were



sufficient for the respondent to make informed decisions in relation to



s.60 of the Act.



20. It follows from this that it is open for an inference to be drawn



that the respondent did consider the operation of s.60 and concluded that



the section should not apply. There in no evidence before the Tribunal



that the respondent did apply his mind to the operation of s.60.



21. Document T9 is a copy of a report made by an officer of the Taxation



Office on the affairs of Toeco. The report is dated 14 October 1987. On



the evidence before the Tribunal this is the first occasion that the



respondent gave serious consideration to the implications of the



arrangements pertaining to the affairs of the partnership. That report



appears to have concluded that s.60 did not operate because it concludes



that s.260 applies to strike down the arrangement, although the actual



arrangement said to be struck down appears to be the agreement for



formation of the Toeco partnership rather than the transactions regarding



acquisition of the assets by the partnership.



22. Before proceeding further I should make findings in relation to



Exhibit 3. That exhibit comprises a number of clear photographs of some



of the office furniture with explanations by an officer of the Taxation



Office. The applicant did not seek to impugn the exhibit in any way. I



will deal with the assets seriatum.



23. The first is a vacuum cleaner to which a value of $15,000 has been



attributed. The vacuum cleaner is an ordinary barrell type domestic



model of plastic construction. Its market value in 1979 could not have



exceeded $300. Apparently the vacuum cleaner was photographed in the



home of a mother of one of the partners. No explanation of this peculiar



circumstance was given to the Tribunal.



24. The second is a small table somewhat euphemistically described as a



desk and valued at $10,000. It's market value in 1979 could not have



exceeded $100. This table was said to be located in an employee's



bedroom. Once again no explanation was given.



25. The third photograph is of two small tables again described as desks



and in an obviously poor state of repair. These were said to be located



at an employee's home and were claimed to be valued at $10,000 each.



Their market value in 1979 could not have exceeded $100 each.



26. The fourth photograph is of two sets of pigeon holes valued at



$3,000 each and a set of card drawers valued at $1,000. All were of



limited commercial value and in 1979 the market value could not have



exceeded $100 for each unit as a second hand unit.



27. The fifth photograph is one of six filing cabinets with a total



value of $31,000. The unit pictured is an ordinary four drawers steel



filing cabinet which would have a second hand market value of no more



than $200 in 1979.



28. The sixth photograph is of a set of stand alone bookshelves of



considerable proportions (floor to ceiling) and apparently in good order.



However, the market value in 1979 would have been considerably less than



the value of $3,000 assigned to the item.



29. The seventh photograph is of a set of pigeon holes and a set of



cupboards each valued at $3,000. These units were in poor repair in 1986



when the photograph was taken and would have had a second hand market



value in 1979 considerably less than the value assigned to them.



30. The eighth photograph is of a reasonably large office safe valued at



$9,000. The safe is operated by a key and probably is a fireproof safe.



I am unable to comment on its likely value in 1979.



31. The ninth photograph is of two tables valued at $1,500 each and a



small set of pigeon holes valued at $3,000. None of the furniture



appears to have any commercial value at the time the photograph was taken



in 1986 and would have had a second hand value of no more than $100 each



in 1979.



32. The tenth photograph requires some additional explanation. It



comprises a small set of pigeon holes valued at $3,000 but of virtually



nil commercial value and no more than $100 value in 1979 and a



photocopier valued at $200 and probably worth more than $200. The



photocopier was acquired by the Toeco partnership on 31 July 1980 for the



purpose of providing photocopying services to the accounting practice



conducted by the applicant. Such services were provided for a fee per



copy which the partnership returned as assessable income. The



arrangements in relation to the photocopier are not connected with the



arrangements entered into on 1 June 1979. Nothing was said to indicate



that the photocopy service was other than a bona fide commercial service



provided by Toeco to the accounting practice.



33. The eleventh photograph is of further filing cabinets valued at



$5,000-6,000 each and pigeon holes valued at $3,000. These items appear



to be in better order than those shown in the fourth, fifth and seventh



photographs and therefore, presumably, had a higher second hand value in



1979. However, the values assigned would exceed by many times the second



hand market value of these items in 1979.



34. In the twelfth photograph is a miscellany of office furniture



comprisng a counter valued at $7,000, pigeon holes of considerable



proportions valued at $3,000, a table described as a desk valued at



$10,000 and a curved back wooden chair valued at $2,000. Once again each



item appears to have been valued at a figure many times greater than its



second hand market value in 1979.



35. The thirteenth photograph is of two sets of pigeon holes with



cupboards valued at $6,000 each. Once again the value assigned appears



to be much greater than the second hand market value in 1979.



36. The same comments apply to the set of bookshelves in the fourteenth



photograph which are valued at $3,000 and the same comments also apply in



respect of furniture in the applicant's office shown in the fifteenth



photograph. A cupboard valued at $3,000, a desk (which desk is not



clear) and a wooden chair have each had clearly excessive values



attributed to them as is the case of the two desks ($10,000 each)



a wooden chair ($2,000), bookshelves ($3,000) and a filing cabinet



($5,000) included in the sixteenth photograph.



37. None of the furniture photographed had any attributes which would be



likely to improve its value as time goes by. It was standard office



furniture of a style sold well before 1979. I mean no disrespect when I



say that the furniture (excluding the photocopier) would generally be



described as "junk furniture" if it were put up for sale.



38. The applicant's case is quite straightforward in its essential



argument. He says that s.59AA operates so as to allow the parties to the



present arrangement (i.e. the applicant as disponor and Toeco as



disponee) to agree to the value of assets transferred. The section



overrides the operation of s.60, being a specific section dealing with



the disposal of depreciated property. He also argued that because s.59AA



allows a clear choice in the circumstances of this case s.260 cannot



operate to void the transactions because they represent the application



of specific provisions of the Act. In relation to the operation of s.60



the applicant went further, relying on the reasoning of a Board of Review



in Case S63 (1967) 17 TBRD 363 to the effect that where assets have been



depreciated to nil, i.e. written off, prior to transfer, then s.60 simply



does not have any operation. The essence of the Board's decision is to



be found in pages 367-8 where the Board said in its reasons for



decision:-



"The jigs and patterns pose a different question. Do they



constitute `property in respect of which depreciation has been



allowed or is allowable under this or the previous Act' for the



purposes of s.60(1)? The sub-section, subject to the proviso,



limits the deduction for depreciation in a case to which the



provision applies, to `that which would have been allowed to



the person from whom the property was acquired if that person



had retained it' and we therefore think that it is necessary to



look at the acquired property in the hands of the vendor to



determine whether the purchaser has in fact acquired property



which satisfies the description of depreciated property in



60(1). For example plant purchased from a second-hand dealer



would not, in our view, qualify as depreciated property in the



hands of the purchaser for the purposes of s.60(1) by reason of



the fact that depreciation had been allowed to the person who



sold it to the second-hand dealer. If this view is correct the



treatment which was accorded the subject plan (jigs and



patterns) either in the old company's books or by the



Commissioner in his assessment to tax of the old company is



immaterial. Turning then to the plant in the hands of the



vendor (i.e., the partnership) we find that although the jigs



and patterns unquestionably constituted `plant, or articles'



for the purposes of s.54, no depreciation was allowable to the



partnership in terms of s.56 for the reason that the plant was



acquired at no cost to the partnership and its `depreciated



value' (s.62) was at all times nil. On this view then the



taxpayer company, in acquiring the jigs and patterns, did not



acquire property in respect of which `depreciation has been



allowed or is allowable' and so s.60(1) does not apply to limit



the deduction otherwise allowable to the company pursuant to



ss.54, 55, 56 and 62 of the Act. The Commissioner argues that



the prerequisites of the section are met if one finds, as here,



an acquisition of property which, being plant or articles, has



the characteristic of being depreciable but we think this



submission is met not only by the clear words of the



sub-section itself but also by the terms of the proviso which



specifically refer to `depreciated value of the property under



this Act'. If no depreciation has been allowed to the vendor



of the property and if no depreciation is allowable, as in this



case, s.60(1), in our opinion, has no application."



39. The applicant sought to distinguish the decision in Donebus v. FCT,



88 ATC 4583 on the basis that there was a clear purpose of tax avoidance



in that case and in any event it was difficult to see how the



refrigerator was being used for the purpose of producing assessable



income.



40. The applicant also argued that he had made a full and true



disclosure of the arrangements in the returns of the partnership, his own



returns and his letter of 7 May 1980 (Exhibit M).



41. On the other hand the essence of the respondent's case was to rely



almost entirely on the operation of s.260 with support from the decision



of the Federal Court in Donebus. Counsel was reluctant to deal with the



question as to whether s.170 authorised the amended assessments, being



content to rely on allegedly false statements in the depreciation



schedule lodged with Toeco's first income tax return. The alleged false



statements were that the assigned value for the assets was included in a



column headed "cost" in the standard form "Depreciation Schedule" printed



by the Government Printer. To suggest that the column should not have



been used because of its title is merely a criticism of the form and does



not reflect falsity on the part of the applicant. I am unable to see how



the partnership could have filled out the depreciation schedule itself in



a more meaningful way and therefore reject this submission. The reality



is that the respondent was not misled by any alleged falsity in the



Depreciation Schedule lodged with Toeco's first return. Rather is it



much more likely that the schedule, which is quite explicit, was not



considered at all or only in a most perfunctory manner by the



respondent's assessing officer. Certainly it is in pristine condition.



No marks have been made on the schedule by the respondent's officers.



42. Whether or not the applicant made a full and true disclosure may be



dealt with first. In Case W46 89 ATC 442 I considered a matter not



dissimilar to the present case in that it also involved a factual



situation where it had taken the respondent many years to come to grips



with the problem. There, as here, there was a blatent assault on the



revenue which seemed to wrong foot the respondent's officers in their



handling of the case. I discussed the authorities in some detail in



Case{% W46 but need not do so here. The reason is that unlike the position



found in Case 46 the applicant has not made a full and true disclosure of



all the facts necessary for the making of the assessment. The facts not



disclosed were the specific identification and description of the



furniture involved in the 1 June 1979 transactions. Until the respondent



was in a position to appraise the furniture he was unable to decide



whether s.60 should or should not operate to deny the deduction. Because



the respondent was not given the information necessary to exercise the



discretion in ss.60(2) it cannot be said that the applicant made a full



and true disclosure.



43. The respondent's counsel did not rely on the operation of s.60 (in



the face of the arguments from the applicant as to why the section did



not operate). I do not understand why the respondent takes that view.



Both parties rely on the decision in Case S63 for the proposition that



s.60 does not operate on the facts of this case. I think they are wrong.



My reasons are as follows:



44. There is an error in the reasoning of the Board in that it did not



distinguish the example of the second-hand dealer from the factual case



before it. What the Board really did was to decide that the



circumstances were such that depreciation based on the actual



consideration given should be allowed. The Board's decision, on its



face, produces a strange result which in my view should not be followed.



If the depreciated value of the assets transferred had been a nominal



value in the hands of the vendor then, on the Board's reasoning, that



value would be the extent of the purchaser's entitlement to a deduction



under sub-section 60(1). If, however, the assets transferred had been



depreciated to nil then on the Board's reasoning sub-section 60(1) does



not operate to prevent the assets being depreciated on the actual



consideration. That view cannot be correct in the light of section 62 of



the Act.



45. In my view sub-section 60(1) says in plain language what it was



intended to say, i.e. on the facts of this case the partnership is not



entitled to any greater deduction for depreciation than the applicant



would have been had he retained the office furniture. This view is



supported by the dicta of Menzies J in Ferling v. FCT (1966) 14 ATD 372



at 385-6 where his Honour said:



"Section 60 deals with the depreciation allowable to a



taxpayer upon the acquisition of depreciated property and



establishes as the general rule that a taxpayer `shall not be



entitled to any greater deduction for depreciation than that



which would have been allowed to the person from whom the



property was acquired if that person had retained it'



(s.60(1)). The allowance for depreciation here referred to is



clearly enough a reference to annual depreciation, because the



subsection takes as the measure for the purchaser the



prospective rights of the seller had he retained his



depreciating property. There are two permitted departures from



this general rule. The first is that allowed by the proviso to



s.60(1) which has regard to the possibility that a person may



acquire depreciated property at a price which exposes the



vendor to tax under s.59, sub.s.(2). In such a case the



purchaser becomes entitled to depreciation calculated upon a



value obtained by adding to the value to which the property



acquired had been depreciated in the hands of the vendor the



amount by which the `consideration receivable' increases the



vendor's taxable income by virtue of s.59(2). This proviso is



also concerned with the calculation of annual depreciation.



The second permitted departure from the standard rule is



provided for by s.60, sub-s.(2), which is in these terms:



`This section shall not apply where the Commissioner is of the



opinion that the circumstances are such that depreciation based



on the actual consideration given should be allowed'.



Notwithstanding the form of words here used, it seems clear



that what is intended is that in any case where the



Commissioner considers that it would be unfair that the annual



depreciation allowable to a purchaser should be fixed by



reference to what depreciation would have been allowed to the



vendor had he retained what he sold, the Commissioner can allow



the purchaser depreciation calculated upon the actual



consideration paid. This is how I think s.60, sub-s.(2),



should be understood. Section 60, therefore, is concerned with



the annual depreciation to be allowed to the purchaser of



depreciated property."



46. Each of the assets transferred on 1 June 1979 to Toeco, with the



exception of the vacuum cleaner, had previously been plant or articles



owned by the applicant and used by him for the purpose of producing



assessable income. Therefore, depreciation was allowable under the Act



unless s.60 prevented such deductions. Similarly s.60 prevented such



deductions when the furniture was so used by the partnership of the



applicant and M.



47. It cannot be the case that s.60 ceases to operate simply because of



another transfer of ownership of the assets. The section is not said to



be subject to the operation of any other section. The whole intent of



the section is to prevent double dipping in respect of depreciable assets



by the expedient, such as is sought to be achieved in the somewhat



bizarre facts of this case, of transferring depreciable assets at



inflated values.



48. I find, by inference, that each of the articles of office furniture



(except the vacuum cleaner) had been depreciated to nil for tax purposes



by allowing depreciation in respect of those assets in years of income



prior to the year of income ended 30 June 1979. Support for this



inference is to be found in the Indenture dated 21 December 1977 between



the applicant and M (Exhibit J), wherein it was agreed that the furniture



in question be transferred at "nil" value. It must follow from that that



the assets had depreciated down to nil and that the applicant was not



entitled to any greater deduction i.e. he was not entitled to any



deduction, notwithstanding that the assets were used by him for the



purpose of producing assessable income.



49. It follows that although Toeco used the assets for the purpose of



producing assessable income the partnership was not entitled to any



greater deduction for depreciation than that which would have been



allowed to the applicant as the person from whom the property was



acquired. As the applicant was not entitled to any deduction it follows



that Toeco was not entitled to any deduction. That finding accords with



and gives effect to the clear intent of ss.60(1). It is unlikely that a



more obvious case of the mischief to which s.60 is directed could be



found.



50. There are no circumstances which would justify a finding, in terms



of ss.60(2), that depreciation based on the actual consideration given



should be allowed.



51. As to the vacuum cleaner there is no evidence that it was used for



the purpose of producing assessable income. Exhibit 3 indicates that



although the vacuum cleaner was introduced by the applicant's wife it was



located at the home of an employee's mother. No explanation was given to



the Tribunal. It would be pure speculation unsupported by any evidence



to hold that the vacuum cleaner was depreciable within the terms of s.54.



52. However, there is no reason to disallow the claims for depreciation



in respect to the photocopier. The transactions in respect of the



photocopier are normal commercial transactions.



53. The evidence before the Tribunal indicates that the applicant has



been allowed a deduction in respect of the rentals paid to Toeco for the



lease of the furniture and the photocopying services. As to how the



respondent rationalises this with the view that s.260 operates to void



the Toeco partnership was not explained to the Tribunal.



54. It was the respondent's case, as already noted, that s.260 operated



to void the Toeco partnership as against the respondent Commissioner. In



the light of the evidence it might have been submitted that one of three



possible scenarios exist for the application of s.260 to the facts of



this case. These are:



(a) to void the formation of the partnership and the transfer



of the office furniture to the partnership as a scheme



directed to relieving the partners from liability to pay



income tax;



(b) to void the formation of the partnership as a scheme to



alter the incidence of income tax, or



(c) to void the transfer of the office furniture to the



partnership as a scheme to relieve the partners from



liability to pay income tax.



55. Although not entirely clear the respondent's case appaered to rest



on basis (a) or possibly (b). However, neither of those scenarios



represent the basis upon which the amended assessments in respect of the



applicant have been made. In each year before the Tribunal the



respondent has merely disallowed the applicant's claim for his 50% share



of the net loss of the partnership as returned by Toeco. The applicant



has been allowed a deduction for the rental paid to Toeco for hire of the



office furniture and Toeco has been allowed deductions including



depreciation to the extent of the income derived from the applicant for



rent.



56. The respondent's action in merely disallowing the applicant's claim



for his share of the net loss of the partnership does not therefore rely



upon the operation of s.260 to void either the formation of the



partnership nor the transfer of the office furniture.



57. In my view the formation of a partnership per se does not constitute



an arrangement to avoid tax. If, however, an existing business or



certain operations are then sought to be transferred to the partnership



for the purpose of altering the incidence of income tax then that



arrangement may be void as against the respondent Commissioner. The mere



formation of a partnership, without more, will not be void.



58. If I am right in this analysis it is the transfer of the office



furniture which is void as against the Commissioner. Setting that



transfer aside will result in the claim for depreciation by Toeco being



disallowed. Because I have come to the view that the arrangement failed



by the operation of the specific provision of the Act it is not necessary



that I consider further the operation of s.260 (c.f. Ferling v. FCT



[1966] HCA 85; (1965) 14 ATD 66 at 74-5 per Windeyer J at first isntance - on appeal the



Full Court was not called upon to consider the operation of the section).



59. The Tribunal is left with objection decisions made on a basis that



cannot possibly be correct. Given this Tribunal's obligation to make a



correct or preferable decision in matters before it for review it would



not be appropriate that I merely affirm the objection decisions under



review. Notwithstanding the wide powers conferred by s.43 of the



Adminstrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 this Tribunal does not have the



power to make assessments and is confined by its jurisdiction to



reviewing the objection decision of the respondent.



60. Because my reasons for deciding rely on sub-section 60(1) and not



the operation of s.260, as said to have been adopted by the respondent in



making the amended assessments in dispute and s.60 requires consideration



of the formation or otherwise of an opinion it is appropriate that the



objection decisions under review be set aside and the Tribunal remit each



matter for reconsideration by the respondent with directions to be taken



into account in that reconsideration.



61. The directions of the Tribunal will be:



(a) that the objection decisions under review be set aside and the



objections be remitted to the respondent to decide in



accordance with these directions;



(b) the formation of the Toeco partnership is not void as against



the respondent Commissioner;



(c) section 60 of the Act operates so as to deny the Toeco



partnership any deduction for depreciation in respect of the



office furniture transferred to the partnership by the



applicant on 1 July 1979;



(d) the vacuum cleaner was not used for the purpose of producing



assessable income;



(e) the Tribunal has formed the opinion that the circumstances are



not such that depreciation based on the actual consideration of



$185,000 in respect of the office furniture should be allowed;



(f) the rental income derived by the partnership from hire of the



office furniture is assessable income and outgoings incurred in



producing that income are allowable deductions;



(g) the depreciation deduction claimed in respect of the



photocopier is to be allowed as a deduction; and



(h) liberty to apply on two days notice in writing to the other



will be reserved to both parties with respect to these



directions.

matters of QT89/160-163.
