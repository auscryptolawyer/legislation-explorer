Fraser and Commissioner for Superannuation [1992] AATA 570 (17 March 1992)

Last Updated: 29 July 2008



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



SUPERANNUATION - Applicant employed as clerk in Commonwealth Department -annullment of probationary appointment - discretion in certain circumstances to deem termination to have been on ground of physical or mental incapacity - whether proper to exercise discretion.



Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) ss.7,47,49(9)

Public Service Act 1922 (Cth) ss.34,47



Commissioner for Superannuation v Hastings (1986) 70 ALR 625

REASONS FOR DECISION



1. When the hearing of this application commenced, neither party appeared to be clear as to what was said to be the reviewable decision before the Tribunal. The Applicant, who was unrepresented, in response to questions from the Tribunal as to the nature of her claim, stated that she did not know precisely what benefits she was claiming under the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth), notwithstanding that she has been pursuing this claim under the Act for some eight years. The Respondent's initial submissions appeared to question whether there was any reviewable decision before the Tribunal.



2. In the opinion of the Tribunal, the reasons for this confusion surrounding the Applicant's claim are to be found in the circumstances in which the claim arose, as well as the particular construction placed by the parties, or one of them, on certain factual situations and upon certain provisions of the Superannuation Act. Since the events in question took place, both the Superannuation Act and the Public Service Act 1922 (Cth) have been relevantly amended, so that the Applicant's claim and its resolution is of historical interest only, due to the changes in the relevant legislation.



3. The Applicant was born in April 1955. In May 1983 she was appointed, on probation, as a clerk with the then Department of Aviation. The effect of ss.34 and 47 of the Public Service Act, as those sections then stood, so far as relevant, was that such an appointment was required, in the first instance, to be on probation with the intention that it was to be confirmed or "annulled" within a certain period by the Public Service Board. All this is now significantly changed in the current legislation, in particular, probationary appointments are no longer "annulled" but "terminated."



4. In any event, the Applicant was no sooner commenced on probationary appointment then she underwent a series of medical examinations. At least one purpose in causing the Applicant to undergo these medical examinations was the then requirement (since omitted) from s.47 that before confirmation of a probationary appointment could take place the Board was required to be satisfied after a relevant medical examination as to the "health and physical fitness" of the probationer.



5. In the result, the Applicant's appointment was never confirmed and in fact her appointment was annulled pursuant to the then section 49(9) of the Superannuation Act on the grounds that the Board was not satisfied as to her health. Her appointment was annulled as from 24.12.1984.



6. Prior to the date of annulment, the Applicant had been off work since February 1984 with complaints that are variously described in several medical reports that were engendered at the behest of the Board. Prior to the date of annulment, the Applicant had been making contributions to the scheme then operating under the Superannuation Act. She contributed in all a total of about five hundred to six hundred dollars. It is common ground that these monies were returned to her shortly after the date of the annulment of her appointment.



7. By letter dated 10 December 1984, on the letterhead of the office of Public Service Board, and under the signature of Mr Greenhalgh, the Applicant was advised of the annulment of her appointment as from 24 December 1984, on the ground that she did not satisfy the health and fitness requirements for confirmation of appointment. The letter included the following paragraph:

"In certain circumstances, persons whose appointments are annulled on the grounds of medical fitness may be eligible for the award of a benefit by the Commissioner for Superannuation in accordance with the Superannuation Act". (T23/87)

This general statement seems to be that which caused the Applicant to become fixed with the notion that she had some entitlement under the Act.



8. Apparently in response to the letter just mentioned, the Applicant wrote a letter dated 27 December 1984 to the Commissioner, quoting the general statement quoted above, and asserting that "the section of the Act referred to is 7(2)...". At about the same time the Applicant submitted a claim form (T25/9) in which she applied for some unspecified entitlement under the Act. This drew a response (T27/93) dated 21 February 1985, from the Commissioner, which informed the Applicant that her last mentioned letter would be "treated as a request to have your annulment considered as an invalidity requirement under sub-section 7(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976."



9. The next step seems to have been that a delegate of the Commissioner reviewed the Applicant's claim which the delegate purported to do under section 7(2) of the Act (see T documents T3/27-T3/37).



10. Section 7 of the Act was, and still is, in the following terms -

(1) A reference in this Act to the retirement of an eligible employee on the ground of invalidity shall be read as a reference to his having been retired, or his services having been otherwise terminated, on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties.



(2) Where a person has, before attaining his maximum retiring age, ceased to be an eligible employee, otherwise than by reason of death or by reason of his having been retired, or his services having been otherwise terminated, on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties, but the Commissioner is satisfied that, at the time he ceased to be an eligible employee, he was by reason of physical or mental incapacity, unfit to perform his duties, the Commissioner may direct that the person shall, for the purposes of the Act, be deemed to have been retired on the ground of physical or mental incapacity.



11. The delegate's decision was that the exercise of discretion referred to in section 7(2) should not be exercised in the Applicant's favour and added the opinion that all that the Applicant was entitled to was "a benefit in accordance with her mode of exit viz annulment of appointment."(T3/37.) Precisely what the delegate meant by this expression of opinion is not clear. The delegate's decision was conveyed to the Applicant by a letter dated "Apr 1987" which enclosed a copy of the delegate's reasons and ended by informing the Applicant that: "As a result of this decision there will be no invalidity retirement benefit entitlement in your case."



12. The Applicant's response to this decision was to write to the Commissioner by letter dated 21 May 1987 (T34/102) challenging the decision and this seems to have led to a "reconsideration" of the decision by another delegate of the Commissioner (T2/2-T2/23). This delegate too, came to the decision that the discretion referred to in s.7(2) should not be exercised in the Applicant's favour.



13. The proper construction of section 7, in particular, of the Act has been twice considered by the Full Court of the Federal Court. The first such consideration was in the case of Commissioner for Superannuation v Hastings (1986) 70 ALR 625, and the second in Scott v Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 8 AAR 20. In Hastings it was held that once the preconditions specified at s.7(2) were satisfied, the Commissioner had a residual discretion as to whether or not the discretion should be exercised. The Full Court in Hastings saw s.7(2) "as a provision designed to avoid injustice in cases where an employee might be thought to be morally entitled to a pension, because effectively forced out of his or her employment for health reasons, but is not within sub s(1)." It should be added that in each of the two cases the Applicant was a confirmed appointee and not an appointee on probation.



14. In Scott, the Applicant after a long period of service, resigned his employment on medical grounds. This gave him a lesser entitlement than he would have had had he proceeded on the grounds of invalidity pursuant to s.66(1) of the Act read in the light of s.7(1) of the Act. Before the AAT, the Tribunal found that he satisfied the preconditions of s.7(2), but nevertheless it upheld the decision not to exercise the discretion in his favour. By a majority, the Full Court held that all the relevant considerations indicated that the discretion should have been exercised in the Applicant's favour.



15. In the reconsideration by the delegate of the Applicant's claim in March 1990, the delegate, after what appears to the Tribunal to be a thoughtful and thorough approach to the issues, concluded, first, that on the evidence before him he was not satisfied that at the time the Applicant's appointment was annulled she was by reason of her medical condition unfit to perform her usual duties. In any event, the delegate further concluded, that there was nothing in the evidence which would justify the exercise of the ultimate discretion in the Applicant's favour. In the opinion of the Tribunal each of those findings was clearly open to the delegate.



16. As to the first finding, the various medical reports which were then before the delegate are set out in his reasons. A report of Dr Hedberg of May 1984 which is referred to is to the effect that the Applicant was fit for normal duties at that stage. Another report referred to, that of Dr D.E Meyrick dated 19 July 1984 stated that the Applicant would not be fit to carry out duties "that require using the arms above shoulder level in performing repetitive movements." In fact the Applicant was at all relevant times employed as a clerk and, as she herself told the Tribunal, her duties did not involve any such use of the arms above shoulder level. When this medical report is read in the light of that evidence then it amounts to a conclusion that the Applicant was at that stage able to carry out her clerical duties. There was before the delegate another report from Dr Flavell (the Applicant's general practitioner) but the content of that report does not seem to cast much light on the answer to the question whether at the date of the annulment of her appointment she was fit for her normal duties. Accordingly, as the Tribunal has said, it was open to the delegate to come to the conclusion that he did. This conclusion would be enough to reject the Applicant's claim. However, in the opinion of the Tribunal, the delegate was on even stronger ground in the decision that he came to that the ultimate discretion should not, in any event, be exercised in favour of the Applicant.



17. The simple fact of the matter is that the Applicant, during the course of her probationary appointment, was unable to satisfy the Board that she met the health and fitness requirements which had to be established before her appointment could be confirmed. The Board had no option under the then statutory framework but to annul her appointment. Her contributions were then returned to her. She has had various employments since December 1984, although some periods out of work, but finally in 1987 she obtained employment with the Australian Broadcasting Corporation and she remains in that employment.



18. Assuming that in a proper case, a person such as the Applicant, under the legislation then in force, could conceivably bring herself within s.7(2), there is, in the opinion of the Tribunal, not the slightest justification on the facts of this case for any recommendation that the discretion ought to be exercised in her favour.



19. Accordingly the decision under review will be affirmed.
