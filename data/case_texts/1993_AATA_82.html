ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

S.A. FORGIE On 24 July, 1992, the applicant applied for review of a decision of a delegate of the respondent, the Commissioner for Taxation ("the Commissioner") dated 19 June, 1992. In that decision, the Commissioner refused to extend the time allowed by the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") for the applicant to lodge an objection against the assessment for the year ended 30 June, 1986.

2. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by Mr Laurie of Counsel and the Commissioner by Mr Bickford of Counsel. The documents lodged by the Commissioner pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") were admitted in evidence as were an affidavit of Gregory John Barry sworn on 23 December, 1991, an affidavit of Fiona Mary Fitzpatrick sworn on 24 July, 1992 and an affidavit of Graham Hughes sworn on 10 December, 1992. Oral evidence was given by Mr Barry.

LEGISLATION

3. Prior to the repeal of Part V of the Act, section 185 provided that a taxpayer, who was dissatisfied with an assessment, might lodge with the Commissioner an objection. The taxpayer had 60 days within which to lodge that objection but, should the objection not be sent within that time, sub-section 188(1) provided that:

"Where the period for the lodgement by a taxpayer of an objection

against an assessment has ended, the taxpayer may, notwithstanding that

the period has ended, send the objection to the Commissioner together

with an application in writing requesting the Commissioner to treat the

objection as having been duly lodged."

4. Sub-section 188(3) went on to provide that

"An application under sub-section (1) ... shall state fully and in

detail the circumstances concerning, and the reasons for, the failure by

the taxpayer to lodge the objection or request as required by this Act."

5. The Commissioner was required to consider each application made under sub-section 188(1) to grant or refuse that application and to notify the taxpayer of his decision (sub-sections 188A(1) and (2)). If the taxpayer was dissatisfied with the Commissioner's decision, he, she or it might apply to this Tribunal for review of that decision (sub-section 188A(3)).

6. Sections 185 and 188A, together with the other provisions of Part V, were repealed by the combined operation of section 113 and Schedule 4 of the Taxation Laws Amendment Act 1991 ("the 1991 Amendment Act) and were effectively replaced by Part IVC, "Taxation Objections, Reviews and Appeals" in the Taxation Administration Act 1953 ("the TA Act"). Part IVC was inserted by section 112 of the 1991 Amendment Act. By virtue of the operation of section 114 of that same Act, the amendments effected by sections 112 and 113 applied

"... in relation to objections where the assessments,

determinations, notices or decisions to which the objections related

were notified, or were first notified, as the case may be, after the

commencement of this section."

7. The day fixed by Proclamation pursuant to sub-section 2(10) of the 1991 Amendment Act as the commencement of section 114, and hence of the amendments effected by sections 112 and 113, was 1 March, 1992.

8. There is no dispute between the parties, and I am satisfied on the evidence in these proceedings, that the applicant is a taxpayer who lodged an income tax return for the year ended 30 June, 1986 on 11 August, 1989. By an assessment dated 16 April, 1991, the Commissioner assessed the applicant pursuant to section 26AAA of the Act on the profit from various property transactions. In view of the date of commencement of the amendments effected by the 1991 Amendment Act, I consider that the application should be considered in the light of section 188 before its repeal. This was the basis upon which the parties argued the case.

THE EVIDENCE

9. On 11 August, 1989, the applicant signed a taxation return for the year ended 30 June, 1986 and it was subsequently lodged on 25 August, 1989 on her behalf by Rosman Taxation Consultants. That return revealed her earnings from G.J. Coles for that year as her only income (T documents, page 18).

10. From October, 1989 until June, 1990, Mr Graham Hassell, a Taxation Officer, was engaged in an audit the applicant's affairs. Mr Hassell requested a number of documents from the applicant, her accountant, Mr Wright, and her solicitor, Mr Barry. By May, 1990, the audit had been extended to take account of the applicant's husband's affairs (affidavit of Mr Hassell, sworn 23 July, 1992).

11. On 6 April, 1990, a meeting took place between the applicant, the applicant's husband, Mr Hassell, Mr Findlay and Mr Barry (Affidavit of Mr Findlay sworn on 24 July, 1992 and transcript page 9). There was some discussion of the husband's affairs as well as of the application of section 26AAA of the Act to certain property transactions. Until that time, Mr Barry had not acted for the applicant in respect of her taxation affairs but he had acted for her husband since early 1987 (transcript page 8). Mr Barry said that he had no knowledge of the transactions which were discussed at that meeting. All that he knew was that the Commissioner had alleged that there were three parcels of land which gave rise to the application of section 26AAA (transcript page 8).

12. On 9 May, 1990, Mr Hassell arranged a meeting between himself, the applicant, the applicant's husband and Mr Barry. The purpose of the meeting was to attempt to negotiate an agreement as to the matters relevant to the application of section 26AAA to the applicant's affairs and to resolve certain matters relating to her husband's affairs (Mr Hassell's affidavit, paragraph 4, affidavits of Mr Barry sworn on 15 July, 1992, (paragraphs 10-11) and 27 July, 1992 (paragraphs 5-7) and the applicant's affidavit sworn on 30 June, 1992 (paragraph 9)). There is disagreement in the evidence as to whether or not an agreement was reached at these meetings but its existence or otherwise is not relevant to these proceedings.

13. In the course of his oral evidence, Mr Barry initially said that at none "of the interviews did they (i.e. the applicant and her husband) contend that they were not liable for an assessment under section 26AAA (transcript page 8). Later, however, he disagreed with the proposition put to him by Mr Bickford that the representatives from the Commissioner's office had made it clear that they were contending that a transaction had taken place which attracted the operation of section 26AAA in so far as the April meeting was concerned. He thought that the officers were only seeking documentation in order to clarify issues but were not specifically making the contention that section 26AAA applied at that stage (transcript pages 10-11). In relation to the second interview in May, Mr Barry said that there was no dispute at that interview that section 26AAA applied to the transaction (transcript page 13).

14. Later, however, when again questioned about the April meeting, Mr Barry agreed with Mr Bickford's proposition that there was no dispute at the meeting that the relevant contracts under consideration were those dated 24 July, 1984 and 15 July, 1985. Furthermore, he agreed with the proposition that there was no dispute at the meeting as to the applicability of section 26AAA to the transaction to which those contracts related. He thought it "most likely to be correct" that the applicant would supply further information in relation to expenses incurred in relation to the property and that she was to make a submission regarding penalties (transcript page 12).

15. There is various evidence as to the conversations which took place, or might have taken place, between Mr Barry and Mr Findlay regarding any agreement which might have been reached at the earlier meeting and the fulfilment or otherwise of any undertakings which might have been given at that meeting (affidavit of Peter Noel Findlay sworn on 24 July, 1992, affidavits of Mr Barry sworn 15 July, 1992 and 27 July, 1992). There was a conversation between Mr Barry and Mr Findlay on 17 October, 1990 (see affidavits above and transcript page 14). There is dispute as to what was said during this conversation but Mr Barry said that he was confused by the position adopted by Mr Findlay during the conversation that he would issue an assessment as he thought that an agreement had been reached and that it was still operative. Mr Barry had not confirmed the terms of the agreement in writing and nor had the Commissioner's office (transcript page 14).

16. There then followed a letter written on 22 January, 1991, on behalf of Commissioner to the applicant care of Mr Barry. In it, Mr Findlay stated that:

"From my understanding of arrangements made at the final interview

those arrangements were not capable of performance as they were not

based on law or the facts presented and lacked necessary authority on

the part of the auditor. This being so, I also point out your client

failed to comply with her part of the agreement." (T documents, pages

186-187)

The letter went on to set how the applicant had been assessed and, finally, to state that the originals of the assessment notice had been served on Rosman Taxation Consultants.

17. There is no evidence of any assessment having been issued in January, 1991 but an assessment was issued on 16 April, 1991 and showed the sum of tax payable as $254,661.79 (T documents, page 19). That assessment was sent to the applicant care of Rosman Taxation Consultants. Typed near the bottom of the front of the assessment was a note referring her to the attached document. That document was an "Income Tax Adjustment Sheet" showing that section 26AAA had been applied to certain property and that her taxable income had been increased from $4,252, as shown on her return, to $304,227.

18. Also at the bottom of the front page of the Assessment was a note stating "See back for notes and other information". On the back appeared 15 notes in fairly small, but quite legible, type. Note 2 read as follows:

"Dissatisfied with your assessment. If you are not satisfied that

your assessment is correct or you do not understand how it was

calculated, please phone, write or call in to discuss the problem. If

you are still not satisfied, you can object to your assessment. Your

objection must be in writing and generally should be received in the

Tax Office within 60 days from the date of service of this notice.

There is more information about disputing your assessment in a free

booklet, Income Tax and resolving your dispute available from Tax

Offices."

19. Mr Barry said that he did not have any discussions with the applicant's tax accountant when the assessment was issued and had no knowledge of whether or not he actually received it. Mr Barry said that he could recall talking to the applicant's husband at about the time that the assessment was issued and thought that it was as a result of the husband's calling him. Mr Barry supposed that it was at this time that he decided that the best avenue was to defend a subsequent writ and explained his decision in the following passage:

"I was focused on the issue of what I understood to have been a

compromise agreement which had been reached and I believed at the time

and I continued to believed (sic) that until late December of 1991 that

the basis for dealing with that issue - and also believe that to have

been the only issue at the time - was to defend action for recovery of

this in the anticipated Supreme Court proceedings." (transcript page

17)

20. Mr Barry did not seek counsel's opinion at this stage and there were no discussions between Mr Barry and the applicant and her husband as to funding any legal action (transcript pages 17-18).

21. On 30 July, 1991, a writ was issued in the Supreme Court of Queensland against the applicant. Her solicitors, Messrs Barry Bateman Clapin, entered an appearance on 29 August, 1991. On 3 September, 1991, the solicitors requested that the time for delivery of a defence be extended. That time was extended with the consent of the Commissioner but no defence was delivered within the extended time. As the applicant had not delivered a defence, judgement was entered against her on 26 September, 1991 for $264,753.10 plus costs. (affidavit of Fiona Fitzpatrick sworn on 24 July, 1992)

22. In his evidence, Mr Barry explained why a defence had not been lodged when he said:

"It was my view at that (sic) I certainly needed the assistance of

counsel with drawing an appropriate defence and I certainly needed to

take extensive instructions in relation to it. I asked ... (the

applicant) to have ... (her husband) contact me about that matter,

particularly as to security for fees in that matter and I didn't have a

conversation with ... (her husband). Nothing occurred." (transcript

page 19)

23. At that time, Mr Barry said that he had been acting for the applicant in relation to the sale of a property of which she was the registered proprietor. The sale was completed in September, 1991 for a sum of $210,000, of which a substantial part possibly amounting to $159,085.28 was paid to Mr Barry's trust account in trust for the applicant and her husband. The applicant's share of that money (assuming that the husband was a beneficial owner even though not named as a registered proprietor) was in the order of $80,000. (transcript pages 19-20 and T documents, pages 248-252)

24. Mr Barry said that he received instructions as to what he should do with the proceeds of the sale due to the applicant but that he did not recall those instructions specifically. In so far as he did recall them, he thought that he had been instructed to draw up a number of cheques in favour of the husband's requirements. Among those cheques was a cheque for a substantial amount payable to a firm of auctioneers in Sydney for a truck which the applicant's husband had purchased. Other cheques were drawn for similar purposes and one was payable directly to the husband. Mr Barry was not given any instructions to use any of the proceeds to defend the Supreme Court writ. He did not receive any instructions on that matter until later (transcript pages 21-22).

25. Mr Barry said that he had acted for the applicant and her husband for a number of years and believed that he was acting for both of them. Whether the applicant was the registered proprietor of property or not, his discussions were always with the husband. In answer to Mr Bickford's question

"you did not think you had any obligation to get any instructions

from ... (the applicant) as to what was to be done with those

moneys?",

Mr Barry replied:

"Well, I did. I received an authority from her." (transcript page

23)

26. Mr Barry said in re-examination that the applicant's husband was authorised to provide instructions on behalf of his wife (transcript page 34). In his dealings with her, she had never shown "evidence whatsoever that she is other than commercially naive" (transcript page 35).

27. He thought that he would have advised the applicant to use some of the money to fund a defence to the writ but could not recall specifically whether he had or not. As to her involvement in giving him instructions, Mr Barry said of the applicant that her

"involvement in terms of providing me with instructions was to say

the least minuscule. She had, in my view, little to no understanding of

what was going on or confidence to deal with it, and I depended as I

have always done in acting for the family upon receiving sensible

instructions from ... (the husband) at the end of the day who has always

been the decision-maker in the family, in my experience" (transcript

page 23).

Mr Barry went on to say that the "standard response" which he would have received from the applicant would have been that she would get her husband to contact him but the husband did not do so (transcript pages 23-24).

28. On 9 November, 1991, a Creditor's Petition naming the applicant as the debtor and the Commissioner as the creditor was lodged in the Federal Court. Mr Barry then lodged in the Supreme Court an application to set aside the judgement on which the petition was founded. On the following day, 17 December, 1991, Mr Barry swore an affidavit in the bankruptcy proceedings deposing to the application in the Supreme Court and the reasons why there had been delay in taking action in the Supreme Court. In his evidence, Mr Barry said that he never served that application (transcript page 22).

29. At paragraphs 4 and 5 of his affidavit, Mr Barry went on to say:

"I am informed by the debtor and verily believe that the reason

that the debtor has delayed making the application to set aside

judgment has been that the debtor has not until yesterday been capable

of providing my firm with security for its fees associated with

applying to set aside the aforesaid judgment and these proceedings. I

am similarly informed that the judgement debtor has not been eligible

for a grant of Legal Aid in connection with these matters.

For similar reasons judgment was obtained by the judgment creditor against the debtor in the Supreme Court of Queensland due to the defendants failure to lodge an appropriate form of defence. The defendant did enter an appearance in those proceedings."

30. At paragraph 7 of that affidavit, Mr Barry said:

"I am instructed by the judgment debtor and verily believe that the

reasons associated with her delay entirely relate to her impecunious

means in pursuing a defence. I believe that these circumstances are of

the nature which might well be considered in the Supreme Court of

Queensland as satisfactorily explaining delay."

31. He went on to set out the basis upon which he believed the Commissioner's assessment had been made and that, were the facts as asserted by the applicant regarding ownership of the property to be accepted, "they would create a complete and successful defence to the judgment creditor's action in those proceedings" (paragraph 10).

32. The creditor's petition came before Registrar Allen in the Federal Court and Mr Barry sought an adjournment on behalf of the applicant to allow her to make an application to the Commissioner for an amendment of his assessment pursuant to section 170 of the Act. In his later affidavit sworn on 23 December, 1991, Mr Barry stated that:

"3. When appearing before this Honourable Court on the 17th

December, 1991 I was made aware that relevant authorities indicated

that an application to set aside judgment was an improper course for

the Debtor to pursue in seeking to reverse the effects of the original

assessment made by the Judgment Creditor in respect of which assessment

judgment was entered.

4. I have been made aware that the proper course for the Debtor to

pursue is either to apply to the Judgment Creditor pursuant to Section

188 of the Income Tax Assessment Act (as amended) for an extension of

time within which to object to the assessment or for her to apply to

the Judgment Creditor pursuant to Section 170 of the Income Tax

Assessment Act (as amended) making request for amendment of the

assessment originally made, in each case stating proper grounds.

5. I am informed by the Debtor that the assessment which was

originally made was brought to her attention by the Commissioner at a

time when she was financially incapable of seeking professional

assistance with the necessary disputation process. I am further

informed by the Debtor that she was not aware of the process involved

in disputation of the assessment.

6. I am aware that the Debtor is a middle-aged woman, a grand-mother

and a person who is unfamiliar with matters of business generally and

the person who is a part-time employee of a supermarket in a relatively

menial capacity. I am informed by the Debtor and her husband and

verily believe that the dealings in respect of a certain property the

sale of which forms the basis of the assessment was a dealing which was

orchestrated and directed by the Debtor's husband ...

7. The process of provision of instructions to myself in the course

of this matter since the 16th December, 1991 has been greatly hampered

by the Debtor being in poor health and the instructions and discussions

have been by telephone only.

8. The Debtor instructs after receiving advices from myself

subsequent to the 17th December 1991 that she seeks to pursue a stay of

this action pending determination of her expeditious application to the

Judgment Creditor of a request for amendment of the assessment pursuant

to Section 170 of the Income Tax Assessment Act (as amended). The basis

for the application will be the Debtor's production to the Judgment

Creditor of documentary and sworn evidence by observers indicating that

she was the owner in equity of the land the subject of the assessment

for a period in excess of 365 days and that the assessment has been made

in error "(Exhibit 2).

33. On 5 March, 1992, Registrar Allen granted the adjournment sought until the Commissioner had responded to the applicant's application for amendment of his assessment (T documents, pages 32-39).

34. Following receipt of the Registrar's reasons, the applicant telephoned Mr Barry on 16 March, 1992 and an appointment was made with her for a date later in the week. Mr Barry was unable to keep the appointment until 3 April, 1992 due to his illness. At that meeting, he was instructed to prepare either an application seeking an amendment of the Commissioner's assessment or an application for an extension of time within which to lodge an objection to the assessment. He asked the applicant for copies of the relevant contracts.

35. On 22 April, 1992, Mr Barry received a letter from the Commissioner's solicitors advising that the creditor's petition would be re-listed for hearing on 6 May, 1992 His attempts to contact the applicant by telephone were unsuccessful and he wrote to her on 27 April, 1992 asking her to contact him urgently. She did so on 5 May, 1992 and advised him that she had been unable to obtain copies of the documents from her former solicitors and asked that he proceed with his instructions in their absence (affidavit of Mr Barry sworn 6 May, 1992).

36. On 14 May, 1992, Mr Barry wrote to the Commissioner setting out his instructions and contending that, despite the dates which appear at the head of the two contracts, they were executed in excess of 365 days apart (Exhibit "A" to affidavit of Mr Hughes sworn on 22 June, 1992). Mr Hughes, an officer in the Commissioner's office, decided that the assessment should be affirmed and advised Mr Barry of that decision in a letter dated 27 May, 1992 (Exhibit "B"). In that letter, he also stated that he did not consider that there had been a valid request for an extension of time as no objection had been lodged in writing and the letter did not state in full detail the grounds upon which the applicant relied in making her request (Exhibit "B").

37. After receiving a further letter dated 9 June, 1992 from Mr Barry, the Commissioner decided to treat the earlier letter of 14 May, 1992 as a proper objection. After considering the matter, Mr Hughes then wrote to Mr Barry on 19 June, 1992 advising him that he had affirmed his decision regarding his refusal to amend the assessment and refusing to allow an extension of time within which to lodge an objection (Exhibit "B")

38. On 23 June, 1992, Mr Barry sought an adjournment of the hearing of the creditor's petition for a further week on the basis that he had only just received the Commissioner's letter dated 19 June, 1992 and was seeking Counsel's advice as to the merits of an application to this Tribunal. At this time, an affidavit of the applicant sworn on 30 June, 1992 was filed in the proceedings. She referred to dealings with the property which led to the Commissioner's assessment. She stated that her husband had attempted to purchase the property in 1984. She and her husband had agreed that the land would be purchased in her name as her he had been bankrupt in the past and thought it would be difficult to obtain finance. They had made other business arrangements on this basis. Her husband raised the necessary capital and serviced the loans and the property was held for their mutual benefit.

39. The land was not in fact purchased in the applicant's name but in a pseudonym used by her husband or his nominee as they did not want the vendor to know that their family was purchasing it after failing to complete a contract with him for the same property earlier in the year. The contract was executed on 13 June, 1984. They had expected simply to add the applicant's name to the contract without paying further stamp duty or executing further extensive documents but later discovered that more complex arrangements had to be made. In her affidavit lodged in the bankruptcy proceedings and sworn on 15 July, 1992, the applicant said that her former solicitors wrote to her husband under his pseudonym and asked the name of the actual purchaser. Upon being told that it was the applicant, they sent a contract of sale from the husband to the applicant and advised them that they could either execute that contract or obtain a valuation to accompany the memorandum of transfer. The applicant stated that she and her husband regarded this merely as a necessary formality. The contract, showing a sale price of $73,000 was executed on 24 July, 1984 and stamp duty paid on 27 July, 1984. The applicant sold the property and two adjoining properties of which she was also the registered proprietor by a contract executed on 15 July, 1985.

40. That contract of 15 July, 1985, for the sum of $525,000, was expressed to be subject to three special conditions which were

"It is a condition going to the root of this contract that the Town

Planning approval from ... Council for extractive rights attaching to

Lot 1 on registered Plan ... is current and valid at the date of

completion.

The Purchaser is to have the benefit of such approval and also the

benefit of the similar extractive application made in relation to Lots

2 and 3 on registered Plan ... All copies of and documents relating to

the said approval and applications are to be delivered to the Purchaser

free of charge on or before completion of this contract.

Vendor will not do nor permit any act or omission which endangers

or is likely to endanger either the said approval or the said

application or which may render them or either of them liable to

cancellation withdrawal or refusal."

41. Also on 15 July, 1992, the husband swore an affidavit to which he attached a copy of a contract for the sale of his quarrying business to a quarrying company for $50,000. The contract was subject to three conditions which were:

"A. The Vendor shall on or before completion sign all necessary

forms and notices to divest himself of the registered Business Name

.... to permit use of that name by the Purchaser for all purposes

associated with the business sold.

B. The Vendor will deliver to the Purchaser on or before completion

such documents (or copies thereof) as are necessary to enable the

Purchaser to carry on the said business and to continue to fulfil

contracts for the supply of extractive material.

C. This contract is subject to the contemporaneous completion of

contract of sale of even date between the Purchaser and .... as Vendor

and breach by either party under one contract shall be deemed to be

breach by the corresponding party under the other contract."

(T documents, page 164)

42. The contract included a clause whereby the husband argued that he would not carry on business of quarrying and extracting material. There is no evidence that the contract was ever executed. On 30 July, 1985, he and the applicant signed a less restrictive undertaking by both the applicant and her husband (T documents, pages 162 and 152). Settlement took place on 2 August, 1985 (affidavit of husband sworn 15 July, 1992, T documents, page 160).

43. Returning to her affidavit of 30 June, 1992, the applicant then went on to say:

"7. The agreement between my husband and myself on this occasion

and on previous occasions occurred always at the suggestion of my

husband who came upon the properties and suggested that purchase. I am

not a person who has expertise in property investments or in business

matters at all and have always been guided by my husband's suggestions

as to these matters.

8. I suffer from a continuing nervous condition which I have suffered

from for several years since first having been hospitalized in 1974 as

a result of a nervous breakdown and I recall my hospitalization for a

period of approximately six weeks. My condition was serious throughout

the events of 1990 and 1991 and I have found the matters now before

this court very difficult to address because of my condition. I have

not previously been involved in court matters and regard myself as

naive in such matters as involve courts and commercial dealings.

During the period of these difficulties I have effectively separated

from my husband which is confounded my difficulty in addressing these

problems.

9. I have read the affidavit of my solicitor Mr Barry sworn today and

am familiar with his account of the meeting held between myself, Mr

Hassell of the Taxation Office, My husband and Mr Barry in early June

1990 which is contain (sic) in paragraphs 17 to 20 of that affidavit.

I say that the account given by Mr Barry of that meeting entirely

accords with my recollection. I also believe although I cannot be sure

that I signed notes prepared by Mr Hassell on that occasion. I recall

I was anxious to sign anything that needed to be signed as I was

anxious to leave the meeting to tend to my grandson.

10. I was not aware that the arrangement which had been with Mr

Hassell had fallen over until I receive notice of an assessment herein.

I was at all times unclear as to what to do next and found the entire

situation confusing and difficult to cope with. I believe (sic) that I

would have an opportunity to defend the matter in the courts."

Mr Barry said that, despite the fact that the applicant and her husband were separated, he understood that they were still on speaking terms and that she still relied upon her husband for a number of things (transcript pages 33-34).

44. Mr Barry said in his evidence that he was not aware that any medical evidence had been produced to support her affidavit (transcript page 24). He went on to say, however, that she showed evidence of her condition in the meeting with the officers from the Commissioner's office in that she was shaking violently and crying (transcript page 26).

45. He said later in re-examination that the applicant was "nigh on impossible to communicate with, an also was not of any assistance in providing a factual background which I was exploring in any event" (transcript page 37). During the conversations which he had with her husband, the applicant would tend to sit quietly and allow her husband to answer all questions. She would give negative responses if asked if she recalled any details of the transactions. Mr Barry relied upon the husband to obtain the documentation from his previous solicitors.

46. On 29 July, 1992, Mr Justice Spender heard and determined a further application to adjourn the creditor's petition. He granted the adjournment pending the determination of any objection proceedings by the applicant or earlier order (ex tempore reasons, supplementary T documents, pages 23-30).

47. Prior to the determination of the application for an adjournment, Mr Barry had written a further letter to the Commissioner. This letter, dated 24 July, 1992, made a further application for an amended assessment pursuant to section 170(6) and, alternatively, to extend the time within which the applicant might lodge an objection. A notice of objection was apparently enclosed although none appears in the T documents (pages 205-214). Mr Barry said that he had no instructions as to whether this later objection or that in May was to be pursued (transcript pages 28-29).

CONSIDERATION

48. Mr Laurie submitted that I should consider the application in the light of the principles set out by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Minister for Home Affairs and Environment (1984) 58 ALR 305 together with a consideration of the blamelessness of the applicant and the responsibility of her solicitor as discussed by Sheppard J in Wedesweiller and Others v Cole and Others ((1983) [1983] FCA 94; 47 ALR 528). He said that those principles had been adopted by the Federal Court in Fardon v Commissioner for Taxation ((1992) 92 ATC 4,339, Sweeney J).

49. Mr Bickford submitted that the Hunter Valley case is an example of the sort of criteria which should be considered in relation to section 11 of the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1976 ("the ADJR Act") for an extension of time. It was not determinative of the criteria to be applied in every case and regard must be had to the power. He relied upon a judgement of Brooking J, with whom Fullagar and Tadgell JJ agreed in the Full Court of the Supreme Court of Victoria in Carmel Dix and John James Dix v Crimes Compensation Tribunal (unreported, 12 August, 1992, No.13382 of 1990).

50. Mr Bickford argued that, on a proper construction of sections 188 and 188A of the Act, a full and detailed explanation of the reasons for the taxpayer's failure to lodge and objection within time is a pre-condition to the exercise of discretion in favour of the taxpayer. He relied upon the words appearing in sub-section 188(3) of the Act and on two decisions of Senior Member Balmford in Case Y57 (1991 ATC 493) and Case Y58 (1991 ATC 498). The first case was taken on appeal to the Federal Court and affirmed in Fardon's case where Sweeney J said:

"As clearly revealed by its reasons set out above, the Tribunal made

findings of fact which were properly open to it on the evidence. Its

discretionary decision was made by the application to those facts of

the relevant legal principles which it carefully considered. It made

no error of law. The appeal is dismissed with costs." (page 4350)

51. Mr Bickford submitted that, in case Y57, Senior Member Balmford had considered that there had not been an adequate explanation of the delay and had not exercised her discretion in favour of granting an extension of time. In Mr Bickford's submission, this gave support to his argument that an applicant for an extension of time under section 188 of the Act must first give an adequate explanation of the delay before other discretionary matters could be considered.

52. Before considering the submissions made, I will set out the principles set out by Wilcox J in the Hunter Valley case when he said:

"... I venture to suggest that from them may be distilled the

following principles to guide, not in any exhaustive manner, the

exercise of the court's discretion:-

(a) Although the section does not, in terms, place any onus of proof

upon an applicant for extension, an application has to be made.

Special circumstances need not be shown, but the court will not grant

the application unless positively satisfied that it is proper so to

do. The "prescribed method" of 28 days is not to be ignored (Ralkon v.

Aboriginal Development Commission [1982] FCA 153; (1982) 43 ALR 535 at 550). Indeed

it is the prima facie rule that proceedings commenced outside that

period will not be entertained (Lucic v. Nolan (1982) 45 ALR 411 at

416). It is a pre-condition to the exercise of discretion in his favour

that the applicant for extension show an "acceptable explanation of the

delay" and that it is "fair and equitable in the circumstances" to

extend time (Duff v. Freijah [1982] FCA 159; (1982) 43 ALR 479 at 485; Chapman v.

Reilly, Neaves J, 9 December, 1983, not reported, at p 7).

(b) Action taken by the applicant, other than by making an

application for review under the Act, is relevant to the consideration

the question whether an acceptable explanation for the delay has been

furnished. A distinction is to be made between the case of a person

who, by non-curial means, has continued to make the decision-maker

aware that he contests the finality of the decision (who has not

"rested on his rights": per Fisher J in Doyle v. Chief of General

Staff [1982] FCA 124; (1982) 42 ALR 283 at 287) and a case where the

decision-maker was

allowed to believe that the matter was finally concluded. Compare

Doyle, Chapman, Ralkon, and Douglas v. Allen (Morling J, 3 April, 1984,

not reported, at p 18 of the transcript) with Lucic at pp 414-5 and

Hickey v. Australian Telecommunications Commission [1984] FCA 176; (1983) 47 ALR 517 at

519. The reasons for this distinction are not only the "need for

finality in disputes" (see Lucic at p 410) but also the "fading from

memory" problem referred to in Wedesweiller v. Cole [1983] FCA 94; (1983) 47 ALR 528.

(c) Any prejudice to the respondent, including any prejudice in

defending the proceedings occasioned by the delay, is a material factor

militating against the grant of an extension: see Doyle at p 287; Duff

at pp 484-5; Hickey at pp 525-7 and Wedesweiller at pp 533-4.

(d) However, the mere absence of prejudice is not enough to justify

the grant of an extension: Douglas at p 18; Lucic at p 416; Hickey at

p 523. In this context, public considerations often intrude (Lucic,

Hickey). A delay which may result, if the application is successful,

in the unsettling of other people (Ralkon p 550; Becerra v. Fowell

(unreported, Morling J, 18 February, 1983) at pp 12-13 of the

transcript) or of established practices (Douglas p 19) is likely to

prove fatal to the application.

(e) The merits of the substantial application are properly to be taken

into account in considering whether an extension of time should be

granted: Lucic p 417; Chapman p 6.

(f) Considerations of fairness as between the applicants and other

persons otherwise in a like position are relevant to the manner of

exercise of the court's discretion: Wedesweiller at pp 534-5."

53. Wilcox J also stated in that case, and it has been stated consistently elsewhere that the Hunter Valley case is not an exhaustive statement of the principles to be considered. The High Court, for example, has commented in relation to the extension of time provisions in section 92FAB(9) of the Broadcasting Act 1942:

"Section 92FAB(9) does not specify any criterion to be applied by

the tribunal deciding whether or not to allow an application for a

further period within the meaning of the provision. Where a statute

confers a discretion which in its terms is unconfined, the factors

that may be taken into account are similarly unconfined, except in so

far as there may be found in the subject matter, scope and purpose of

the statute some implied limitation of the factors to which the

decision-maker may legitimately have regard." (R v Australian

Broadcasting Tribunal; Ex parte 2 HD Pty Ltd ((1979) [1979] HCA 62; 144 CLR 45 at

49-50)

54. Were it not for the Fardon decision, I would consider the power to extend time in the context of the Act as a whole to determine the principles applicable. I am, however, bound by the Fardon case and must apply its principles. Therefore, as Sweeney J said that he considered that the correct legal principles had been applied in Case Y57, I will look at what those principles were.

55. The decision in that earlier case set out the principles in the Hunter Valley case as I have done and then set out the following passage from the judgement of Wilcox J:

"In considering the authorities it is, I believe, important to bear

in mind the point made by Sheppard J in Wedesweiller at 531, relating

to the diversity of decisions of which review may be sought under the

Act: `... there will be some cases which may be decided upon

considerations which affect only the immediate parties. It will be

appropriate to consider whether the delay which has taken place has

been satisfactorily explained, the prejudice which maybe caused to an

applicant by the refusal of an application, the prejudice which may be

suffered by the government or a particular department if the

application is granted and, generally, what the justice of the case

requires. In other cases wider considerations will be involved'.

He went on to mention the reference to public interest made by

Fitzgerald J in Lucic at 416.

It is in relation to the former category of cases, ie those `which

affect only the immediate parties' that the approach adopted by Bray CJ

in Lovatt v LeGall (1975) 10 SASR 479 at 485 in respect of private

litigation but adopted in this context in both Doyle at 287 and Duff at

485, is apposite namely: `If the defendant has suffered no prejudice,

as when he was well within the limitation period of the plaintiff's

claim, or where excess period of time is small, or where he cannot show

that he has lost anything by reason of the delay, it may well be that

the court will not find it difficult to come to the conclusion that it

is fair and equitable in the circumstances to grant extension'.

By contrast, in cases involving public administration, especially day

day matters such as personnel management, the public interest may well

dictate refusal of an extension even after only a short delay." (pages

496-497)

56. Senior Member Balmford then went on to consider the reasons for delay in some detail and then, more briefly, any prejudice which might be caused to the Commissioner by allowing the application for an extension of time and the merits of the substantive arguments upon which the applicant would wish to rely if granted the extension of time. She did not specifically consider the evidence against the principles relating to fairness and established practices which were also discussed in the Hunter Valley case. In addition, she did not seem to regard it as necessary to find that there was a satisfactory explanation of the delay before considering the other principles addressed in the Hunter Valley case. It would also seem from the decision that, whether the reasons for delay in lodging an objection were regarded as particularly significant in that case she did not regard the existence of a satisfactory explanation for the delay as a condition that must always be fulfilled before the discretionary power to extend time may be exercised at all. Rather, she looked at the three factors she considered relevant in that case.

57. It follows that I do not regard Fardon as establishing that the existence of a satisfactory explanation must be established before other factors may be taken into account. In reaching this conclusion, I note that, while sub-section 188(3) clearly intends that some weight be given to the explanation for delay, sub-section 188(1) also requires that the objection be lodged at the same time. That would indicate that some weight must also be given to the merits of the objection and is consistent with what I understand the principles stated in Fardon to be. In view of the Fardon case, I do not consider that I am limited to a consideration of the reasons for the delay and the merits of the objection but must look at all factors relevant to whether an extension of time should be allowed.

58. I shall turn now to the reasons for the delay in this case. Both Mr Laurie and Mr Bickford have made submissions in this regard, as in others, but I will not set them out in detail although I have had regard to them in reaching my decision.

59. On the basis of his evidence, I am satisfied that Mr Barry has acted for the applicant and her husband in various land transactions over a number of years. I am also satisfied, this time on the basis of the contracts under consideration in the application of section 26AAA, that Mr Barry was not the only person from whom they sought advice regarding land transactions. With regard to the taxation matter under consideration, Mr Barry was the only professional person from whom they definitely sought advice. Whether or not they sought any advice from their tax agents who lodged the return in the first place, I can make no finding for they gave no evidence, no particular reference was made to them during the proceedings and there is no evidence that they were involved in any of the discussions with the Commissioner's officers regarding the applicant's affairs.

60. On the basis of Mr Barry's evidence in particular and of the evidence generally, I am also satisfied that it is probably unlikely that the applicant had any particular knowledge of the precise details relating to the transactions into which she was entering or of their consequences, tax or otherwise. It is probably likely that her husband was the person who co-ordinated the family's financial affairs and that the applicant played no active role. While I am prepared to say "probably likely" and "probably unlikely", I am not prepared to state my findings any more highly than that or to take the further step and say that the applicant is "commercially naive" as I was invited to do. I did not have the benefit of hearing from her during the proceedings and have only her affidavit evidence and Mr Barry's opinion of her. Partly for the same reason, but more so because I do not have any medical evidence, I am unable to make any finding that the applicant suffered from any nervous condition from which she may suffer or might have suffered in 1990 and 1991 and of the effect which such a condition might have had on her ability to function normally. Even if I accept that she suffered from a nervous breakdown in 1974, I do not know what she meant by her statement that her condition was serious in 1990 and 1991 (see paragraph 40 above). Presumably, it is something less than a breakdown as she was not hospitalised but I can go no further. On the basis of Mr Barry's evidence, I do find that she was crying at the interview at the Commissioner's office before the assessment was issued but I do not regard this as a sufficient basis on which to make any further finding regarding her health.

61. I am satisfied that, after the assessment was issued, neither the applicant nor any person on her behalf took any action to lodge an objection within the specified time. This was so despite what, in my view, amounts to a clear statement on the back of the assessment setting out what a person should do if he, she or it is dissatisfied with the assessment. The applicant's attention would have been drawn to the notes by the clear statement on the front of the assessment. Whether I note that the notes stated that the objection "generally should be received in the Tax Office within 60 days" I do not consider that the using the word "generally" would reasonably lead the average person to think that he, or she or it should not at least make some enquiries as to whether the 60 day time limit applied.

62. I accept that, at the time the assessment was received in April, 1991, Mr Barry held the view that the best course of action was to defend the writ. This view was, of course, incorrect, but even if it had been correct, neither the applicant nor he did very much to put it into action. Although Mr Barry entered an appearance on the applicant's behalf and obtained an extension of time within which to lodge a defence, that defence was not lodged. In Mr Barry's evidence, some blame is attached for this to the failure of the applicant or her husband to contact him and also to their failure to give him any security for fees. As a consequence he was unable to seek counsel's advice. His evidence is not contradicted in the affidavit evidence of the applicant. I accept that evidence and find that the lack of any action, up to September, 1991 when judgement was entered, even on the wrong course was due to lack of instructions from the applicant or her husband and the lack of money.

63. I cannot take this a step further and find that this inaction was acceptable. Looking first to the financial matters, in the same month as the defence was to be filed (i.e. September, 1991), the applicant settled the sale of a property in her name. Even if she was in fact the beneficial owner of only half of that property and not all of it, I find that she was still entitled to a share which netted her approximately $80,000. I find also that this money was spent on the instructions of the applicant's husband on matters other than securing legal advice regarding the lodgement of the defence in the Supreme Court proceedings or regarding the taxation matters generally. There is no evidence upon which I can find the relative priorities of that other expenditure but I do think that I can safely find that expenditure in relation to the applicant's taxation affairs was afforded no priority at all.

64. There is no evidence that the applicant took any action at this stage to give any instructions. She would have needed to give Mr Barry a copy of the relevant contracts at this stage but there is no evidence that she took any action of this type until April 1992. Even if the applicant's affairs were probably handled by her husband, it is at least surprising that she did not take positive steps to do something when faced with an assessment directed to her alone.

65. No application was made to set aside the judgement until 17 December, 1991 when Mr Barry lodged an application in the Supreme Court and I can find no indication of any action having been taken by or on behalf of the applicant to approach the Commissioner to advise him that this action was proposed. This was three months after the judgement had been entered. I find on the basis of Mr Barry's evidence that the application to set aside the judgement has never been served. I am also satisfied that on that day, Mr Barry first discovered that he was pursuing the incorrect course. Even when the correct course was discovered, no action was taken by either by the applicant or Mr Barry other than to apply for adjournments in the bankruptcy proceedings. Although I find that the applicant did take some action on 16 March, 1992 by telephoning Mr Barry after receiving Registrar Allan's reasons and she could not see him until 3 April, 1992 because he was ill, she did not contact him again until she had heard from Mr Barry at the end of April or beginning of May, 1992. Mr Barry did then proceed with some haste and write to the Commissioner on 14 May, 1992.

66. Overall, I find that the applicant took no real action to do anything herself regarding the Commissioner's assessment until after she received Registrar Allen's reasons. That was approximately 11 months after the assessment was issued. At no time has she, on the evidence I have, made any real effort to pursue the matter. Her only effort was to obtain the contracts from her previous solicitors but even then there is no evidence of the extent of the steps she took. I do not think it a satisfactory explanation to say that she relied upon her husband and that her husband always gave the instructions. She is the taxpayer and, as such, is given rights and duties under the Act. Others may exercise those rights and carry out those duties on her behalf but I must consider also her own activities in ensuring that an attempt was made to comply with the legislative requirements and to protect her interests even if she does not know precisely what those requirements are. As I have already found, those efforts were very small and very late in the piece at a time when the assessment was a year or so old. This is not a case in which the applicant can be said to be blameless. The fact that the applicant was separated from her husband at some stage during these proceedings does not alter my view for, on the basis of Mr Barry's evidence, I am satisfied that the applicant continued to defer to her husband in financial matters.

67. It was submitted on the applicant's behalf that no enquiries had been made regarding the transactions which took place five years before as she and her husband had been led to believe that all issues had been settled by arrangement with the Commissioner. Whatever agreement might or might not have been reached before the assessment was issued in April, 1991, it must have been apparent to the applicant when she received the assessment that the issues were not settled at all. That argument does not have any merit when there is no evidence of any action, whether right or wrong, being until December, 1991.

68. I do accept the argument that, by the time December 1991 arrived, there might have been little point in lodging the objection while awaiting Registrar Allen's decision as to whether or not he would grant an adjournment of the bankruptcy proceedings. To do so, would perhaps have shown good faith even though I accept that there may have been little practical point in doing so. Even when the adjournment was granted, there was a further six weeks of delay between 16 March and 24 May, 1992 even when two weeks is deducted for the unexpected illness of Mr Barry in March, 1992.

69. Mr Laurie has submitted that the Commissioner has also delayed in this matter. He refers first to the extended leave which Mr Hassell took and to the fact that the assessment appears to have been issued first in January but was not actually dated until April 1991. I do not regard either of these factors as relevant. I am looking at the delay which occurred after the assessment was issued in April 1991. If any delay did occur in the Commissioner's office, it did so prior to that date. There is nothing in the evidence to suggest that any delay by an officer before April 1991 has contributed to any delay by the applicant after that time. In this regard, the case is distinguishable from Case W23 (1989) 89 ATC 253 (Deputy President Gerber).

70. Taking all of these matters into account, I can not find that the delay which has occurred in this case has been acceptable. There was, effectively, a delay of over 11 months after the applicant, through her tax agents and her solicitor, was given notice of her rights on the back of her assessment. Of those 11 months, it could be said that two periods, one of 2 weeks when Mr Barry was ill, and one of 3 months when the question of the adjournment of the creditor's petition was under consideration, were explicable. That still leaves a delay of approximately 7 1/2 months. The overall conclusion that I have reached is that there was some activity by Mr Barry during that time but it was always as a rear guard action and, until April/May 1992, incorrectly focussed in its entirety. Apart from those rear guard actions which comprised obtaining an extension of time within which to lodge a defence and obtaining adjournments of the bankruptcy proceedings, absolutely nothing was done by either the applicant or her legal representative to address the substantive issues raised by the assessment. That was not done until April or May 1992. The applicant had not given instructions before that time and had not made any serious attempt to find the contracts on which the assessments were faced before that time. This was so even though the applicant had, through the Commissioner's letter to Mr Barry in January 1991, effectively been placed on notice that an assessment based on those contracts would be made. I cannot find that this is a satisfactory explanation of the delay in this case.

71. Nor can I find, as I have already said at paragraph 66 above, that the applicant is blameless. Even if she were, that would only be one of the factors, albeit, a material factor, to take into account. This follows from Sheppard J's judgement in Wedseiller's case where he quoted from the judgement of the High Court in Sophron v Nominal Defendant ((1957) [1957] HCA 27; 96 CLR 469, Dixon CJ, McTiernan, Fullagar and Taylor JJ) when it said at pages 474-5:

"When the application was before the prothonotary he appears to

have adopted the view that a fixed general rule existed that when the

failure to give notice within time could not be ascribed to the fault

of the claimant but was attributable entirely to fault on the part of

his solicitor, that necessarily amounted to sufficient cause within the

meaning of s30(2)(b)(ii). Such a view is opposed to the principles

laid down by Walsh J in Martin v Nominal Defendant (1954) 74 WN (NSW)

121 and it gained no support in the Full Court in the present case. No

one, of course, doubts that such a consideration as the blamelessness

of the claimant and the responsibility of his solicitor is very

material. But every case must be determined on its own facts. Fixed

formulae cannot be substituted for the wide words of the sub-section,

..."

In this case, the applicant chose to rely upon her husband and took no active role in protecting her own interests by encouraging him to take action or in assisting Mr Barry.

72. Turning now to any prejudice which may be caused to the respondent were I to grant the application, I am unable to find any. The area of dispute between the parties is documented and is not particularly dependant upon memories which may fail over time. On the evidence I have, I find that the Commissioner was not aware that the assessment was disputed after judgement was entered in September 1991 until action was taken to have the judgement set aside in December 1991 but I am satisfied that he was not prejudiced in this regard or by subsequent delays.

73. There remains to consider the merits of the objection. Those depend upon the application of section 26AAA of the Act which applies to a sale of property or an interest in property that occurred after 25 May, 1988 (sub-section 26AAA(1)). Sub-section 26AAA(2) provides that

"Where -

(a) a taxpayer has purchased property after 21 August 1973 and before

the commencement of this section or purchases property after the

commencement of this section; and

(b) the taxpayer has, whether before or after the commencement of this

section, sold the property or an interest in the property before the

expiration of the period of 12 months from the date on which he

purchased the property,

then, subject to this section, the assessable income of the taxpayer

includes any profit arising from the sale of the property or

interest."

74. Sub-section 26AAA(1) has the following provisions relevant to this case. It provides that,

"(a) a reference to property generally or to a particular kind of

property includes a reference to an estate or interest in property or

in that kind of property, as the case may be;

(b) a reference to an agreement or option includes a reference to an

agreement or option that is not enforceable by legal proceedings

whether or not it was intended to be so enforceable;

(c) an arrangement or understanding, whether formal or informal and

whether express or implied, shall be deemed to be an agreement;

(d) the acquisition of a share by way of a subscription of capital,

with or without the payment of any other consideration, shall be deemed

to be the purchase of that share;

(e) ...

(f) if property is transferred from a person or persons to another

person or other persons in exchange for other property or without

consideration, the transfer shall be deemed to constitute the sale of

the property by the first-mentioned person or persons and the purchase

of the property by the second-mentioned person or persons;

(g) if land is sold to or purchased by a person in pursuance of a

contract, the date on which the contract was made shall be deemed to

have been the date on which the land was so sold or purchased;

(h) ...

(j) ...

(k)...

(m) ..."

75. Mr Laurie submitted that no beneficial interest had passed from the husband to the applicant by virtue of the contract dated 24 July, 1984 as the contract did not pass the beneficial interest. That beneficial interest was held for both of them regardless of the name in which the land was actually held. I do not agree. Even if the husband and wife held property on trust for themselves and their spouse, this does not mean that there is no transfer of interest when the land is sold from one spouse to the other. The legal interest must reasonably be transferred from husband to wife even if that legal interest is then held in trust for both of them. This is not a case, however, where the issue of who held the beneficial interest and at what time is particularly relevant. It is governed by the words of the Act and these are quite strict.

76. This is a different situation from that in cases such as Palmarc Investments Pty Ltd and FCT (85 ATC 4410) to which Mr Laurie referred me. Hunt J of the Supreme Court of New South Wales found that a taxpayer had assigned its present interest in a rights issue to shares and options in a new company to another party but not a future interest in the shares themselves. The deed of assignment had provided that the taxpayer would apply for the shares or options on behalf of the assignee and hold them as trustee. Hunt J found that the transaction did not involve the acquisition of the shares or of property by the taxpayer or the sale of property as he had never acquired any interest in the shares and options. The legal effect of the assignment was that the assignee became immediately obligated to pay the taxpayer $341,746.60 and the taxpayer immediately obligated to the assignee to apply for the shares and options. Once the shares were allotted to the taxpayer the effect of the deed of assignment was that there was immediately a trust in favour of the assignee. The transaction, Hunt J held, involved no acquisition of property by the taxpayer at all and no sale of property which he had acquired.

77. The contracts signed on 13 June, 1984 and 24 July, 1984 do not leave themselves open to a construction of the type adopted in Palmarc. After examining each of the contracts exhibited to the affidavit of the applicant sworn on 15 July, 1992, I am satisfied that each purports to be for the sale of vacant land and not with shares which are subject to different provisions and particularly those in paragraph 26AAA(1)(d). In view of that, it would be very difficult to counter an argument that paragraph 26AAA(1)(g) which applies specifically to the sale of land would lead to the conclusion that the applicant purchased the land as a result of the contract dated 24 July, 1984.

78. For similar reasons, I also consider that this case differs from that considered by Senior Member Roach in AAT Case 4697 [1988] AATA 308; (19 ATR 4011) to which Mr Laurie referred me. That case concerned the sale of shares which the Commissioner considered had been "acquired" and so "purchased" by virtue of the deeming provision of paragraph 26AAA(1)(d). It was found as a matter of fact that the taxpayer's mother had intended to be the beneficial owner of the shares, and had always acted in that manner, long before he made arrangements with the company to have the shares transferred to his name. Senior Member Roach decided that the "acquisition of property" did not equate with the "passing of property" as a matter of substantive law (page 4016). AAT Case 4697 differs from the present case in that they concern different provisions of the Act. This case is not governed by the concepts of "acquisition" and the particular meaning given to that concept by the Act in relation to shares but to the provisions dealing with land.

79. This brings me to Mr Laurie's submission that the date of 13 June, 1984 should be regarded as the relevant date on which the applicant acquired the interest in land as it was the latest date on which the agreement to transfer the land into her name was unconditionally agreed and execution of the formal document came later. This, however, seems to be at odds with the words at paragraph 26AAA(1)(g) which provides that the date on which the contract was made is deemed to be the date on which the land was purchased.

80. It would also seem to run counter to Case T51 (1986 ATC 407, Mr Beddoe, Chairman, Dr Gerber and Dr Beck, Members) to which Mr Laurie referred. In that case it was decided that the date on which the formal document was signed was to be taken as the date on which the taxpayer acquired the property. This conclusion was consistent with that reached in Case 62 (1986) 29 CTBR (NS) 446 (Mr Beddoe, Chairman, Dr Gerber and Dr Beck, Members).

81. That brings me to Tribunal Case 95 ((1987) [1987] AATA 234; 18 ATR 3663, Deputy President McMahon, then Senior Member). That case was not concerned with the sale and purchase of land as in this case but with the sale and purchase of a unit. Consequently, the provisions of paragraph 26AAA(1)(d) were not considered at all but those of sub-section 26AAA(2) and paragraphs 26AAA(1)(b) and (c) were considered relevant. It was found that there was no "arrangement" within the meaning of paragraph 26AAA(1)(c). It would be difficult to maintain an argument that the provisions of paragraph 26AAA(1)(c) are applicable to the sale of land.

82. With regard to the date on which the land was sold, Mr Laurie has submitted that the date of 4 August should be chosen for it is the date on which the contract of 15 July, 1985 became unconditional. Mr Laurie relied upon Case 18 ((1946) 12 CTBR 316, Mr Gibson, Chairman, Mr Hannan and Mr Hamilton, Members). The issue in that case was whether certain assets had been "disposed of" within the meaning of sections 36 and 59 of the Act at that time. A number of conditions were specified in the contract for the sale of cane and it was also subject to a number of consents being obtained from specified boards and bodies. It was decided that the assets had not actually been "disposed of" until all of the conditions had been fulfilled. It was explained in the following passage from the reasons for decision of the Chairman:

"As to the transfer of the property in the land the general rule is that an agreement for the sale of land (or any estate in land), provided it is capable of being the subject of a decree for specific performance, operates as an alienation by the vendor of his beneficial proprietary interest in the property. In view of that rule it is sufficient for present purposes to state that a contract which is conditional upon the happening of subsequent events is not capable of being the subject of a decree of specific performance (Ibid. at pp 384-90, and, particularly, the English and Empire Digest, Vol.40, pp 180, 1500 and 1501). It follows that the (beneficial) ownership of the land included in the property which was the subject of the contract under consideration was not transferred until the last of the necessary consents was obtained. I do not think it matters that when the contract was completed the ownership of the purchasers related back to the date of possession. In deciding whether the property was sold in the year of income the Commissioner must surely be required to look at the position which existed at the end of that year. The position at that time was that here was merely an agreement to sell subject to the obtaining of the consents." (page 320)

83. On reading the provisions of section 26AAA, I do not consider that this case assists the applicant's case. It was concerned with specific sections of the Act and particular wording which is not the wording of section 26AAA in relation to land. Paragraph 26AAA(2)(b) speaks of the property having been sold and paragraph 26AAA(1)(g) deems the date on which the property has been sold. It does not allow for the fulfilment of conditions to be taken into account.

84. Finally, Mr Laurie has submitted that there was no identity between what was acquired and that which was disposed of. He relied upon Case 96 ((1980) 23 CTBR (NS), 885, Mr Donovan, Chairman, Mr Voumard and Mr Thompson, Members). The facts and decision in that case are summarised in the headnote:

"The two taxpayer companies formed a partnership in 1964 and

entered into a partnership with a third company on 1 November 1975. On

the same day this second partnership acquired a 50% interest in a

liquor store for $30,000. The goodwill and liquor licence were given a

value for the purposes of this purchase. On 3 July 1976 the business

of the second partnership (the liquor store) was sold and a profit made

on the goodwill and the liquor licence. The Commissioner assessed the

taxpayers on their share of the profit under s26AAA.

It was submitted for the taxpayers that what was purchased was a share

in a partnership and what was sold was a business, and therefore the

property purchased was not the property sold within the meaning of

s26AAA.

Held, disallowing the objections and confirming the assessments; the

evidence was insufficient to establish the taxpayers' contentions and

accordingly they had not discharged the onus of proof; even if this

were not the case, what was bought and sold was an interest in a

partnership and under s26AAA the profit was assessable." (page

885)

85. In this case, I do not consider that there are very solid grounds upon which the point could be argued. The contract for the sale of the business was expressed to be subject to the contemporaneous sale of the land but each contract was for clearly different items. The contract for the sale of the land was concerned with exactly the same property as that purchased by the applicant for her husband. Again, I consider that the applicant would be able to maintain this argument only with great difficulty.

86. Overall then, I consider that the case which the applicant proposes to make out would have doubtful chances of success. I was referred to Spender J's judgement in granting the adjournment in the bankruptcy proceedings. He referred to a judgement of Toohey J in Re Briggs v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (WA) ((1986) 18 ATR 663) when he said at 668

"I must approach this matter on the basis there is an arguable case

before the Federal Court in Sydney to have the assessments

quashed. Whether that case succeeds is of course a quite different

matter."

and later:

"While that proceeding continues, it would not be appropriate

within the framework of an application to set aside the bankruptcy

notice to litigate, even on a prima facie basis, the very issues before

the Court in that proceeding."

This case differs from that before Toohey J for I must now consider this case in the context of the taxation jurisdiction. In that regard, I do not consider that I have quite the same constraints as faced His Honour and can conduct a broader examination, as I have done, of the merits of the case.

87. Taking into account all of the matters under consideration, I do not consider that it would be fair and equitable to allow an extension of time in which the applicant might lodge an objection. I have already indicated that I do not consider that there has been an acceptable explanation of the delay which has occurred and that the delay is not insubstantial. While the Commissioner is not prejudiced and I do not think that this is a case in which there is likely to be any great unsettling of the public or of settled practices, I think that the lack of a satisfactory explanation for the delay together with doubtful merits of the case lead me to conclude that I should not grant the extension of time as sought by the applicant.

88. For these reasons, I affirm the decision under review.
