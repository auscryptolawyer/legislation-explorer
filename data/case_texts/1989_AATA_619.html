Construction Contract Services (Southern) Pty Ltd and Commissioner for ACT Revenue Collections [1989] AATA 619 (19 October 1989)

Last Updated: 11 April 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Payroll Tax - jurisdiction of Tribunal where no extension of time granted before commencement of ACT Self-Government - whether right to apply - effects of defective Notice

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, ss.25, 28, 29

Payroll Tax (Territories) Assessment Act 1971 (Cth.)

Taxation (Administration) Act 1987 (ACT), ss.89(4), 91, 92(1.)

ACT Self-Government (Consequential Provisions) Act 1988 (Cth.), s.27, Schedule 5

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. AT89/30-31

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: CONSTRUCTION CONTRACT SERVICES (SOUTHERN) PTY LTD

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER FOR A.C.T. REVENUE COLLECTIONS

Respondent

DIRECTION

Decision No 5454

Tribunal : Deputy President R.K. Todd

Date : 19 October 1989

Place : Canberra

Direction : The Tribunal directs that there is no reviewable decision before it herein and that it will proceed no further in the matter.

(sgd.) R.K. Todd

Deputy President Presidential Member

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. AT89/30-31

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: CONSTRUCTION CONTRACT SERVICES (SOUTHERN) PTY LTD

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER FOR A.C.T. REVENUE COLLECTIONS

Respondent

RULING

19 October 1989 Deputy President R.K. Todd

  1. Two issues arose in this matter, one in relation to a request for a reference under s.39 of the Payroll Tax (Territories) Assessment Act 1971 (Commonwealth) and one in relation to a purported application for review under the Taxation (Administration) Act 1987 ("the TAA") which was, prior to self-government of the Australian Capital Territory ("the ACT"), an Ordinance. No issue remains as to the former, but the respondent has raised for the consideration of the Tribunal a question of jurisdiction in relation to the latter.
  2. The respondent, by a letter dated 2 March 1989, issued a notice advising of disallowance of objection. The notice did not comply with s.92(1) of the TAA in that (as the applicant contended in its written submission) although it informed the applicant that it could refer the decision to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal ("the AAT"):

"(a) it did not include a statement to the effect that the Applicant had to apply to the Tribunal for a Review within sixty (60) days after service of the Notice as required by paragraph 92(1)(a);

(b) It did not include a statement to the effect that the Applicant could request a statement pursuant to section 28 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act as required by paragraph 92(1)(b) (the decision of the Commissioner was not a decision to which sub-section 28(4) of that Act applies)."

3. Section 92(2) of the TAA provides that the validity of a decision referred to in s.91(1) shall not be taken to have been affected by a failure to comply with s.92(1). But the applicant contends that this is not the end of the matter. It contends:

(i) That on Self-Government Day (11 May 1989) the applicant's right of review had accrued and had not been lost by effluxion of time, and had been preserved by s.27(2) of the A.C.T. Self-Government (Consequential Provisions) Act 1988 ("the Consequential Provisions Act").

(ii) Alternatively, the defective notice has not been rectified and the time for lodging the application cannot have expired because no valid notice has ever been served pursuant to s.89(4) of the TAA.

(iii) In the further alternative, if the notice of 2 March 1989 (which was served on the applicant on 6 March 1989) was a valid notice pursuant to s.89(4), then as at 11 May 1989 it was conceded that the applicant was required to apply for an extension of time in which to lodge the application. This would involve the exercise of discretion by the Tribunal, which discretion might be exercised notwithstanding that time for bringing the substantive application had expired. The applicant therefore had a "substantive right" to apply for an extension of time in which to apply for a review. The "right to a review" had not therefore been irretrievably lost although it was contingent upon the favourable exercise by the Tribunal of its discretion.

  1. I will deal with point (iii) first. I am clearly of opinion that the applicant was not, on the footing under consideration, a person which "immediately before Self-Government Day, had a right to apply to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for a review of a decision". On the footing that the applicant required, at the relevant time, the favourable exercise by the AAT of a discretion to extend the time in which to apply, the only right which the applicant had was to apply for that favourable exercise of discretion. That did not constitute a right to apply to the AAT for a review: see J R Exports Pty Ltd v Australian Prade Commission 71 ALR 717.
  2. It follows that the applicant has lost its right of review unless it can be said that on some other basis it still had immediately before 11 May 1989, and on 20 June 1989 when an application for review was in fact claimed to have been lodged, a right to apply for review. In fact what appears to have occurred was that a purported application for review had been lodged with the office of the respondent on 15 June, which had then been forwarded to the Tribunal Registry on the same day. An application for an extension of time was received by the Tribunal on 19 June. In any event, the right had, on the face of it, expired 60 days after 6 March 1989, that is to say before 11 May 1989. But what is the effect of the failure of the notice to comply fully with s.92(1) of the TAA?
  3. The requirements of s.92(1) may be said to go to the heart of that part of Australian systems of administrative law that require that persons in respect of whom administrative decisions have been made be informed that they have a right of review, and that they are entitled to reasons for the decision in question. In this case, the Notice did advise that the applicant had the right to apply to the AAT for review, but there was silence as to the fact that that right was circumscribed in point of time. Further, it failed altogether to inform the applicant that it could request a statement pursuant to s.28 of the AAT Act. There is the further fact that the Notice referred not to a right to apply to the AAT for review, but rather to a right to "refer the matter" to the AAT for review. As the procedure under the other legislation, the Payroll Tax (Territories) Assessment Act 1971, which applied in respect of a previous period, was to request that the respondent refer the matter to the AAT for review, it is not surprising that confusion existed. The applicant, by letter dated 5 May 1989, asked the respondent to "forward" both matters to the AAT for review. The letter said that the "letters advising of the disallowance of the objections" were received by the applicant's accountants in Queensland on 6 March 1989. The letter of 5 May was thus said to be in time. But this approach cannot answer the present problem, because on no footing was an application for review made to the AAT within 60 days after 6 March 1989.

7. The applicant must, to be able to succeed on this hypothesis, be able to show that the defects in the Notice were such as to cause time not to run for the making of the application to the AAT pendente the curing of the defects in the Notice. The respondent says -

(i) that the Notice was a valid notice, relying on s.92(2);

(ii) that alternatively there has been a notice issued "pursuant to" s.89(4).

8. There is no doubt in my mind that the notice was a valid notice, having regard to the provisions of s.92(2). But that does not put to an end the possibility that there could be other legal consequences of the failure to comply with s.92(1). Could it be said that while the decision the subject of the notice remained valid, the notice was defective to the extent that it prevented that very time running that was the time the subject of the requirement of notice contained in s.92(1)(a)? I have already spoken of the fundamental importance of the need to inform a prospective applicant of the ability to seek review of a decision. But notwithstanding that principle I do not see how I could find that failure to comply with s.92(1)(a) can have such an effect. This particular defect did not of course extend so far as not to inform the applicant at all about a right of review. It simply did not inform the applicant of the time limit, although I doubt that that would make any difference.

  1. I have tried very hard to find support for the argument that the applicant had a continuing right to apply for review having regard to the defects in the Notice. It does not however seem to me that that argument can be sustained. The no doubt unintentionally misleading defects in the notice were defects which would have given rise to an ironclad case in support of an application for an extension of time. Had it been simply such a case, I would have granted that application for that reason, and also for the reason that the respondent made it clear that no opposition would have been offered to the Tribunal taking such a course. The position is however otherwise.
  2. Had such application been made between 6 May and 11 May, being the commencement of self-government, all would have been well. But the fatal flaw is that, as the respondent drew to the Tribunal's attention, from the commencement of the Consequential Provisions Act on 11 May 1989, Schedule 5 of that Act removed ACT enactments from the definition of enactment in the AAT Act of the Commonwealth, and the TAA is an ACT enactment. To this the applicant responds that it is saved by the provisions of s.27(2) of the Consequential Provisions Act, but I have already found, when discussing the broader question of entitlement, that the defects in the Notice did not invalidate the Notice and did not prevent time running. I also found that this being so the applicant did not immediately before Self-Government Day have a right to apply to the AAT for a review of the decision. I repeat this finding, with the addition that once 11 May had come no foundation whatsoever remained for an application to the Commonwealth AAT of any kind.
  3. The applicant further submitted that although the TAA ceased on 11 May 1989 to be an enactment within the meaning of the AAT Act that cessation was, it was said, not retrospective. The decision of the respondent was made at a time when the TAA was an enactment for the purposes of the AAT Act, and the decision was therefore a decision within the meaning of the AAT Act. The fact that the enactment subsequently ceased to be an enactment did not, it was said, affect the application of s.29 of the AAT Act to a decision under legislation which was an enactment at the time when the decision was made. The difficulty with this argument, as it seems to me, is that at the most it could be sound until 11 May only. Further, I agree with the respondent's submission (sub-paragraph (ii) on p.7) that the amendments effected by Schedule 5 of the AAT Act are not retrospective. To be retrospective the amendments would have to deem ACT enactments not to be enactments some time prior to the commencement of the Consequential Provisions Act. It is enactments which are the subject of the amendments, not applications. When the purported application was made the TAA was not an enactment and therefore did not fall within ss.25 or 29 of the AAT Act.
  4. I am aware, as the applicant emphasises in its submission, that the decision seems unfair and that rights are lost as a result of my construction (see paragraphs 21 and 22 of the applicant's submissions). But this Tribunal is a statutory tribunal with jurisdictions grounded on statute. If I come to a clear conclusion about the matter there is nothing that the Tribunal can do. The arrival of self-government heralded a sharp cut-off point that is the real source of the problem. There is some indication in the material that the respondent, if a conclusion favourable to the applicant is arrived at in the matter under the other legislation, and that an analogy can be drawn for that with this matter, an ameliorative amendment might be made. I would commend that course if that point is in fact reached.

13. In the circumstances, and for the reasons set out above, the Tribunal will direct that there is no reviewable decision before it herein and that it will proceed no further in the matter.
