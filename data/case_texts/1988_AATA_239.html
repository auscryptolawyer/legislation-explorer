INCOME TAX - assessable income - company deriving profit upon sale of residential real estate previously purchased, renovated and let - whether sales "mere realization" of invested capital.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss. 25(1), 26 (a).

REASONS FOR DECISION

29 July 1988 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. The Applicant is a company which I shall refer to as "Homeco", although its actual name was something more nearly like "Heritage Land Development Co Pty Limited": the difference being that, in place of the word "Heritage" there stood, in the name of the company, a non-English word for "land". Homeco was at all material times controlled by a married couple. The husband alone gave evidence but his evidence was that he and his wife acted jointly in all decision-making. She did not give evidence because it would have been inconvenient for her to have travelled interstate for the purpose and because it would have been particularly inconvenient to have travelled at the time appointed for the hearing because of commitments to the children of the family. I merely observe that those difficulties could have been avoided had application been made at a suitably early time for a more convenient hearing date to be fixed or for a more convenient venue to be appointed.

2. Homeco was incorporated in 1973. The unchallenged evidence of the husband was that it was incorporated as a shelf company and that it was merely coincidence that it bore the name it did. For the same reason no significance is to be attached to the circumstance that two of the first three of the named objects of Homeco were as follows:

"2.(a) To carry on the business of real estate agency or personnel selection or advisory service or any related or ancillary business.

(b)

(c) To purchase or otherwise acquire for investment resale or otherwise and to deal in land buildings and other property of any tenure and any interest therein and to create sell and deal in freehold and leasehold estates and make advances on the security of land buildings or property or any interest therein and generally to deal in by way of sale lease licence exchange or otherwise all kinds of property whether real or personal."

3. The unchallenged evidence of the Applicant was that the company was established by the couple with a view to providing for their retirement. To serve that purpose it was necessary that the company should pursue the accumulation of wealth. If it should succeed in that then, to the extent to which that activity brought with it the derivation of taxable income, the company would be liable to pay income tax. However, to the extent to which it could acquire wealth without deriving taxable income it would not be so liable to pay tax. With that object in view the Applicants subscribed $2 by way of capital. By 30 June 1986 the last date to which any information was provided shareholders funds had increased to $235,215 despite having suffered accumulated trading losses as recorded by the profit and loss accounts of Homeco at $126,604.

4. The issues for determination arise particularly out of events of the years of income ended 30 June, 1979 to 1982 (inclusive) but they only involve assessments as to the years of income ended 30 June 1980 to 1982 (inclusive). In short form the difference between the parties may be said to be whether profits derived by Homeco upon the sale of ten parcels of real estate in those four years should be considered as assessable income to be offset by a loss suffered on one sale in the year of income ended 30 June 1982.

5. At some time shortly after the couple acquired Homeco, and probably during the year of income ended 30 June 1973, the company acquired two properties. One was a modest residential property in an older area (No. 1); the other was a commercial property (No. 2) used for some time by a company associated with Homeco. Both properties were held long term. The commercial property was sold during the year of income ended 30 June 1985 at a profit of $198,081 (sale price $250,000 less cost $61,919). The property first acquired was sold in September 1985 at a profit of $51,476 (sale price $79,208 less cost $27,732). As the sales were effected in the years of income ended 30 June 1985 and 1986 - years not in issue before me - it is not necessary that I should express any view as to the taxability of those profits. However, it is proper to have regard to those two transactions in shedding light on the activities, purposes and objectives of Homeco and in assessing the submissions made on behalf of the parties. Those considerations tend to favour the Applicant.

6. The first year for which a Balance Sheet and Profit and Loss Account was placed in evidence was the year ended 30 June 1976. It showed comparative figures for the previous year, but not for earlier years. That being so, I am not able to reconcile what appears on the Balance Sheets for 30 June 1975 and 1976 with the other information placed in evidence as to the history of the company's dealings in real estate. However, I doubt that that circumstance is to the detriment of Homeco. If anything it seems likely that Homeco might have engaged in more real estate transactions than appeared from the schedule to be presented later. The accounts to 30 June 1976 would have it that the only fixed assets of Homeco comprised land and buildings: $165,646 in 1975, $135,791 in 1976. That suggests that there had already been at least one sale. The same accounts suggest there may have been 5 or 6 distinct properties owned at 30 June 1975, but only four at 30 June 1976.

  1. The figures to 30 June 1977 show that the value of land and buildings had fallen from $135,791 to $91,760. However, two new items were added to the balance sheet: "investments" at cost $78,326 and as a liability: "Investment Mortgages $75,800". The investments were identified as five residential properties (Nos. 3, 4, 5, 6, 7). Properties 3 and 4 were sold during the year of income ended 30 June 1978 at profits of $4,225 ($25,000 less $20,775) and $5,685 ($32,500 less $26,842). No issue arises before me as to whether the profits so derived constituted assessable income.
  2. The accounts to 30 June 1978 disclosed that three more similar properties had been purchased (Nos. 8, 9 and 10); that the cost of "investments" had increased to $141,860; and that "investment mortgages" had increased to $117,300. The newly acquired properties included the former family home of the married couple (number 8).
  3. The accounts to 30 June 1979 followed substantially the same format and indicated that two properties number 5 and 10 had been sold and 11, 12 and 13 and 14 purchased; that cost of investments had increased to $254,504; and "investment mortgages" to $159,983.
  4. For the year of income ended 30 June 1980, and each year thereafter, the format of the annual accounts changed in that there was no longer a distinction drawn between "investments" and other real estate. Similarly all mortgage borrowings were consolidated under an item "investments mortgages".

  1. The essential argument for the Applicant is that the sales constitute nothing more than "mere realisations" of invested capital. On the other hand the representative of the Commissioner contended that the profits on sale constituted assessable income by reason of s.25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") and, alternatively, by reason of the first limb of s.26(a) of the Act.
  2. The argument for the Applicant is essentially founded upon propositions that initial sales were prompted by a need to refinance the properties; and later sales by the need to consolidate the portfolio to limit it within the bounds of the capacity of the Directors to service the mortgages and the increasing interest burdens arising out of sharp rises in interest rates. I am not persuaded that that explanation is accurate. The first sales were in the 1978 year and the "profit" was returned as income. The next sale - the first of the sales now in question - was to a tenant of a property. The sale was initially simply explained on the basis that the tenant made an offer which the witness considered to be fair and that, accordingly, Homeco sold the property. It did so on the basis of a no-deposit terms contract under which within two years the tenant was able to raise long-term finance and complete the purchase. As to sales of other properties, it is notable that the profits derived upon sale were reinvested in the Sydney real estate market without there being any evidence that the newly acquired investments were financed on any easier terras or at lower rates of interest. 19. There was no detailed evidence presented as to the terms of any of the mortgages referred to; or as to interest rates payable under any those mortgages; or as to the need to re-finance any of the properties by reason of pending expiry dates for subsisting mortgages. Further, it is a matter of significance that throughout the years ended 30 June 1976 to 1981 the cost of real estate which was held rose constantly despite the sales which commenced prior to July 1978.

20. Having regard to those circumstances I am persuaded that more was involved than "mere realisation". I have reached that conclusion despite the long-term holding of the first two properties. I am satisfied that the company did not seek to "profit" on what it represented as revenue account, but rather that it sought to make each enterprise viable by reason of increases in the value of its investments. Had it done no more than maintain that policy, even to the point of adding to its investments and thereby incurring sufficient additional expenses to absorb what might otherwise have been any profit on rental account, the matter may have had no tax significance. But the company did do more than that. Having renovated properties and let them for relatively short terms, it realized the available profit by sale. Having sold, it then reinvested in other properties with a view to renovating them, and letting them. In due course it sold them and thereby realized a profit in all instances but one. In all the circumstances I am satisfied that profit-taking by way of sale of properties which had been purchased and renovated was an integral part of the business operations of the Applicant.

21. Counsel for the Applicant presented a second argument based on the decision of a Full Bench of the Federal Court in F.C.T. v. Cyclone Scaffolding Pty Limited 87 A.T.C. 5083. His argument was that that decision is authority for the proposition that, where the Commissioner over many years has accepted the taxpayer's accounting procedures as a valid approach in accounting terms, it is not open to the Commissioner to thereafter elect to apply a different accounting approach. I do not find the decision to be one supportive of the Applicant. Nor do I find that the circumstances in that decision were in any way analagous to those in the present instance. All that happened in the present instance is that over many years the company lodged returns of income with the Commissioner asserting that it had not derived a taxable income. That assertion was based upon the representations made in its accounts which represented each sale in the following terms:

"This property was purchased more than 12 months ago for investment purposes and has been used for purposes of rental only. The profit on sale was transferred to capital profits reserve."

22. Further, in that period the methodology used by the Applicant in the presentation of its accounts did change (cf para 10). Then the Commissioner examined the affairs of the company and issued the assessments which have been under review before me. In those circumstances there can be no basis for suggesting that the Commissioner has changed his methodology. Nor is there any basis for proposing that anything done by the Commissioner should provide a foundation for denying the Commissioner the right to assess the taxable income which I have found to be derived by the Applicant.

23. The determination of the Commissioner upon each of the objections under review shall be affirmed.
