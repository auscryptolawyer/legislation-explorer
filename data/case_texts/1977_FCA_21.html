Mccormack v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1977] FCA 21; (1977) 33 FLR 53 (8 December 1977)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

McCORMACK v. FEDERAL COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION (1977) 33 FLR 53

Income Tax

COURT

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Bowen C.J(1), Brennan(1) and Deane(2) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Assessable income - Profit from sale of property acquired for purpose of profit-making by sale - Jurisdiction of Supreme Court on appeal from board of review - Question of law - Evidence of profit-making purpose - Onus of proof - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss. 26(a), 190(b), 196 - Federal Court of Australia Act 1976, ss. 24(1)(c), 27, 28(1). The appellant had purchased two acres of land in Macey Street, East Perth in December 1963 and resold the property in early 1970 at a substantial profit. The purchase price was paid over two years and the profit was the subject of assessment in the years ending 30th June, 1970, and 30th June, 1971. The appellant alleged that she had purchased the property to use the house erected upon it, after repairs and renovations, as a family home. The appellant denied having a purpose of profit-making by sale and alleged in the alternative that the property was to be retained as a capital investment.

The appellant was a partner with her husband in a real estate agency business. In the month preceding the purchase of this property, she had purchased and resold in short succession, two other properties. This property which was acquired and financed in an identical manner to the two earlier purchases had been retained for six years.

The subject property was affected by zoning by-laws, city council changes in requirements as to the erection of "high-rise flats" and finally, acquisition by the Metropolitan Region Planning Authority as land reserved for public purposes, which accounted for its dramatic increase in value.

After purchasing the property, minor work was carried out on the building and it was rented for a period of approximately three years. No endeavour was made to use the house as a family home and the idea was subsequently abandoned due to the intervening changes and the discovery that the land would be acquired by the Authority.

The objections against the assessments were disallowed and the matter was referred to a board of review. The board confirmed the assessments and the appellant appealed to the Supreme Court of Western Australia. The appeal was dismissed and the appellant, having obtained leave to appeal, appealed to the Federal Court.

Held: (1) Where the decision of the board of review involves a question of law, a right of appeal to the Supreme Court is conferred by s. 196(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

(2) In such a case, the whole decision of the board is open to review. If the issue before the court includes questions of fact, the court will come to its own conclusions on the facts.

Ruhamah Property Co. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1928] HCA 22; (1928), 41 CLR 148, and Krew v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 45 ALJR 324, referred to.

(3) The parties are not restricted to the evidence before the board of review, since the proceedings before the Supreme Court are in its original and not its appellate jurisdiction.

Watson v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1953] HCA 98; (1953), 87 CLR 353, referred to.

(4) The board of review was wrong in that it approached the case on the basis that there was a presumption that the property was acquired for resale at a profit, having relied on the decision in Gauci v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1975), 6 ALR 183, which was subsequently reversed by the High Court in Gauci v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 54; (1976), 50 ALJR 358.

(5) As the reasoning of the members of the board was vitiated by error and misunderstanding, its conclusion lost substance and could not be taken as a rejection of evidence given by the witnesses. The court hearing the appeal had to assess the evidence itself and reach its own conclusion on the questions of fact involved.

(6) Per Bowen C.J. and Brennan J. - Section 190(b) is expressed to apply to the "reference or appeal" provided for in ss. 187 and 188 and not to the "appeal" from a board to the Supreme Court provided for in s. 196(1).

(7) Per Deane J. - It was erroneous, having rejected the appellant's evidence, to find that there was material upon which it could be concluded that the property was acquired with the relevant purpose and to treat s. 190 as requiring the confirmation of the assessment in the absence of satisfactory evidence to the contrary. The correct approach is, whether on the evidence, the conclusion that the property was acquired with the relevant purpose, should and not could, be drawn. If on weighing the evidence a conclusion on the essential question could not be reached, then the question of onus of proof and the effect of s. 190(b) become critical.

(8) On a proper assessment and weighing of all the evidence before the Supreme Court, on the balance of probabilities the appellant's dominant purpose in acquiring the property was profit-making by sale.

DECISION

December 8.

The following judgments were delivered.

BOWEN C.J. AND BRENNAN J. This is an appeal pursuant to leave given by this
Court from the judgment and order of Wickham J., a judge of the Supreme Court of Western Australia, given on 22nd April, 1977. By his judgment and order, the learned judge upheld an assessment of the appellant to tax in respect of income derived by her during the year ended 30th June, 1970, and an amended assessment of the appellant to tax in respect of income derived by her during the year ended 30th June, 1971. By the same judgment and order, the learned judge dismissed appeals by the taxpayer against the decisions of the board of review which had rejected her appeals against those assessments. (at p55)

2. The appellant purchased a property, 8 Macey Street, East Perth, in December, 1963, for 2,000 pounds ($4,000). She sold it in the year ended 30th June, 1970, for $260,000 and received one-half of the purchase price in each of the income years ended 30th June, 1970, and 30th June, 1971. The respondent Commissioner of Taxation assessed her upon a profit of $249,636. He included in her assessable income for each of the income years ended 30th June, 1970, and 30th June, 1971, an amount of $124,818. (at p55)

3. The taxpayer's objections against the assessments were disallowed, and the commissioner's decisions to disallow the objections were referred to a board of review. (at p55)

4. When the matter came before the board of review, the two members who delivered reasons for decision (the chairman being ill) held that the profit arising from the sale of the property was assessable income under s. 26(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 as a profit arising from the sale by the taxpayer of property acquired by her for the purpose of profit-making by sale. The board members appear to have taken the view that there was a presumption that the property was acquired for the purpose specified in s. 26(a), that the appellant's evidence that she acquired the property as a family home was not acceptable, and that she had not rebutted the presumption. They had before them and relied upon the decision of Lavan J. in Gauci v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1975) 6 ALR 183 , a decision which was subsequently reversed by the High Court on appeal (Gauci v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 54; (1976) 50 ALJR 358 ). (at p55)

5. Against the board's decision upholding the assessments, the taxpayer appealed to the Supreme Court. She appealed on grounds which raised the question whether the board members' decision was right in law in according to ss. 26(a) and 190 the effects which they respectively expressed in their reasons for decision. The decision of the board clearly involved a question of law and the notices of appeal sought to challenge the conclusions of law which the members respectively reached. Where the decision of a board involves a question of law, a right of appeal to the Supreme Court is conferred (s. 196 (1975) 6 ALR 183 ). (at p55)

6. The court, once seized of jurisdiction, may hear the whole case again (Ruhamah Property Co. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1928] HCA 22; (1928) 41 CLR 148, at p 155 ; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Sagar [1946] HCA 6; (1946) 71 CLR 421, at p 423 ). If the issues in the case before the court include issues of fact, the court comes to its own conclusions on the facts and gives effect to its findings in the order which it makes. In such a case, the court is not limited to deciding whether the board was, on the material before it, either entitled to make or right in making the findings which it made. The court reaches its own conclusions on the evidence presented to it (Rowdell Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1963] HCA 61; (1963) 111 CLR 106, at p 119 ; Krew v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 45 ALJR 324 ). That evidence may be more extensive than the evidence before the board for the parties are not restricted to the evidence which they led before the board of review (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Lewis Berger & Sons (Australia) Ltd. (1927) 39 CLR 468 ). However, the court is bound (as the board is not) by the rules of evidence (Commissioner of Taxation v. Miller [1946] HCA 23; (1946) 73 CLR 93, at p 104 and Mobil Oil Australia Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1963] HCA 41; (1963) 113 CLR 475, at p 491 ), and the evidence before the court does not necessarily correspond with the material before the board. (at p56)

7. The parties may, of course, accept the board's findings on some issues, and thus limit the issues to be determined (cf. Caltex Ltd. v. Commissioner of Taxation (1953) 10 ATD 301 ) but where, as in the present case, they do not do so, the court must determine all the matters in issue upon the evidence presented to it. (at p56)

8. As a matter of convenience, it is not uncommon for the transcript of the evidence before the board of review to be tendered by agreement as evidence in the proceedings before the court. In the present case this was done. Also tendered by agreement were the exhibits which had been in evidence before the board. The procedure of tendering the transcript of evidence before the board is often a useful method of saving time and expense. However, this procedure contains some dangers where the intention of the taxpayer is in issue, or where the credibility of witnesses is at stake. The present case unhappily illustrates that danger. The reasons for decision of the two board members who made the decision were tendered by agreement. Since an appeal lies from the board only if its decision involves a question of law, it would seem that the reasons for decision were a necessary part of the record to establish the condition expressed in s. 196(1). The marking of them as exhibits no doubt provided a convenient means of identifying them, but it did not change the effect of them. (at p56)

9. The learned Supreme Court judge did not have the benefit of seeing the appellant or Mr. McCormack, her husband, in the witness box although the questions in issue concerned the intention held by the appellant at the time when she acquired the Macey Street property and the circumstances of its acquisition after negotiations by Mr. McCormack. He did receive oral evidence from a Mr. Mickle who was an estate agent and valuer. This evidence explained the effect of Metropolitan Region Planning Authority Scheme and the importance in relation to values of the adoption in 1966 of the Clarke-Gazzard Code. One result of this was to lead his Honour to conclude that in commenting adversely on the evidence of the appellant and her husband, the board members had, to some extent, proceeded upon a wrong basis. Even if it be assumed that he would have been entitled to determine the credibility of the critical witnesses by giving weight to the board's view of them, the board's misconception of factors affecting their credibility deprived their view of any weight it might otherwise have had. The learned judge, however, appears to have disregarded that circumstance by adopting, as the starting point in his assessment of the evidence, the board's decision not to accept the evidence of the appellant as to her purpose in acquiring the property. Having found no sufficient reason to depart from the board's decision in this regard, he concluded that the position was the same as if she had not given evidence in the material respects at all. (at p57)

10. Proceeding from that conclusion, his Honour said: "The question now is whether leaving aside the evidence of the taxpayer there was any material upon which it could properly be concluded that the property was acquired with the relevant purpose. If there is, then there being no satisfactory evidence to the contrary, the taxpayer has not overcome the prima facie inference, and by virtue of the provisions of s. 190 the assessments must be confirmed". He considered the evidence and propounded for himself a test with reference to the existence of the purpose specified in s. 26(a). He said: "It is sufficient that there be evidence from which the conclusion" (scil. the conclusion as to purpose) "may be properly drawn. Although views may differ on a question of 'sufficient evidence' my opinion is that there was in this case sufficient evidence to form a basis for the assessments. This being the case and there being no sufficient acceptable evidence to the contrary, s. 190 requires the confirmation of the assessments". Thus his Honour stopped short of making his own findings. His task was to make the necessary findings on the evidence before him, and that task was not performed by saying there was sufficient evidence to form a basis for the assessments and then by relying on s. 190. (at p57)

11. Section 190(b) is expressed to apply to the "reference or appeal" provided for in ss. 187 and 188, not to the "appeal" from a board to the Supreme Court provided for in s. 196(1). A judge who is finding the facts of the evidence before him in an appeal from a board of review may not rely upon s. 190(b) for that section has no application to the issues before him (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Finn [1960] HCA 69; (1960) 103 CLR 165, at p 170 ). His Honour, holding only that there was sufficient evidence to support the assessments, and that there was no acceptable evidence to the contrary, erroneously conceived that s. 190(b) made it either unnecessary for him to find, or mandatory upon him to find, that the statutory purpose existed. He did not make the finding which was required pursuant to s. 196(1) to determine judicially the question in issue between the parties. (at p57)

12. How then does the matter stand before this Court? It has not been suggested that the matter should or could properly be remitted to the Supreme Court. It appears to us that this Court should now endeavour to discharge the task of finding the facts on the evidence which was before the Supreme Court and determining the appeal accordingly. (See Federal Court of Australia Act 1976, s. 24(1)(c), s. 27 and s. 28(1).) (at p58)

13. The matter is one of some difficulty due to the complexity of the circumstances and the nature of the issue to be determined - the intention of the appellant. The difficulty is compounded by the procedure adopted before the Supreme Court of tendering the transcript of the evidence before the board without calling the appellant or her husband to give any oral evidence, thus depriving the learned judge of the usual opportunity of evaluating the appellant's credibility upon the critical issue in the case. This Court is thus constrained to make findings upon the printed record without the benefit of a judicial appreciation of the appellant's credibility based upon observation of her as a witness. (at p58)

14. The acquisition of the Macey Street property was the third consecutive acquisition of a house property by the appellant and her husband. Notwithstanding the evidence to the contrary of the appellant and her husband, we are of the view that the earlier two acquisitions were acquisitions for the purpose of profit-making by sale. The appellant sought to distinguish the acquisition of the Macey Street property from the earlier acquisitions by asserting an intention to use the Macey Street property as the family home, and by contrasting the quick sale of the properties earlier acquired with the retention and management of the Macey Street property over a longer period. But on the whole of the evidence, the probabilities are against the truth of the appellant's evidence as to the intention to use the Macey Street property as a family home. On the probabilities we conclude that the appellant did purchase the property for the purpose of profit-making by sale. In our view, the retention and management of that property is not to be accounted for by an intention, held contemporaneously with the acquisition, to acquire it as a capital investment or for a purpose not specified in s. 26(a). (at p58)

15. Deane J. has analysed the evidence bearing upon these issues and upon the connection between the three acquisitions. We agree with his analysis and in the findings which he makes. (at p58)

16. In the result, by following a somewhat different path, we arrive at the same conclusion as the learned judge. The appeal should be dismissed with costs. (at p58)

DEANE J. By a document described as an "Offer and Acceptance" and dated 3rd December, 1963, the appellant agreed to purchase an area of almost two acres of land in Macey Street, East Perth upon which there stood a vacant dwelling house and some stables. Shortly after that date, settlement of the purchase was effected and the transfer of the land to the appellant was registered. The purchase price was 2,000 pounds. In early 1970, the appellant sold the land for $260,000 payable, as to one-half, in the taxation year ended 30th June, 1970, and, as to the balance of $130,000, in the taxation year ended 30th June, 1971. The proceeds of sale were duly received. (at p59)

2. The Commissioner of Taxation assessed the appellant to tax under the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 on the basis that the net profit actually realized on the sale of the land, namely $124,818 in each of the two taxation years, was assessable income in her hands. The appellant objected to the assessments. The objections were disallowed by the commissioner and, at the request of the appellant, referred to a board of review. The chairman being ill, only two members of the board of review participated in the decision on the reference. They confirmed the assessments. (at p59)

3. The appellant appealed from the decision of the board of review to the Supreme Court of Western Australia (Wickham J.). That appeal was dismissed but the learned judge found that the reasons for decision published by each of the two members of the board of review were marred by misunderstanding of "the significance of the zoning of the property and of the impact of the Metropolitan Region Town Planning Scheme . . . at the time when the property was acquired" and by a fundamentally incorrect approach to their task in that they approached the case on the basis "that there was a presumption that the property was acquired for resale at a profit". The appellant appeals, by leave, to this Court from his Honour's decision. (at p59)

4. The commissioner initially claimed that the relevant amounts were assessable income by virtue of either s. 25(1) or s. 26(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). The basis upon which the assessments were confirmed by the board of review was that the profit in question came within the first limb of s. 26(a) of the Act, that is to say, that it was profit arising from the sale by the appellant of property acquired by her for the purpose of profit-making by sale. The commissioner did not contend, either before the learned judge at first instance or before this Court, that the assessment could be maintained on any basis other than by reference to the first limb of the provisions of s. 26(a) of the Act. (at p59)

5. The appellant denies that the property was acquired by her for the purpose of profit-making by sale. Her purpose in acquiring the land was, she maintains, to use the house erected upon it, after repairs and renovations, as a family home. On the hearing of the appeal before us, it was submitted on behalf of the appellant that, even if we rejected the appellant's evidence and primary case as to her purpose in acquiring the property, we should, nonetheless, conclude that the appellant's purpose in acquiring the property was not profit-making by sale but was to retain it as a capital investment. (at p60)

6. At the time of purchase, the Macey Street land was within an area classified in Zone 2 (Residential flats, etc.) under Zoning By-law No. 65 of the City of perth which had been gazetted on 24th May, 1962. The zoning of the land effected by the by-law was not a re-zoning: it was the first zoning of the land and was therefore restrictive in effect. The "Zone 2" classification allowed development of the land for residential flats. It also permitted the erection of single tenements. Owing to the plot ratio then observed by the city council, "high-rise flats" could not have been erected. This position changed in 1966 when the council, as a matter of policy, adopted what has been referred to as "the Clarke-Gazzard Code". The result of that adoption was that the number of flats which could be erected on the land increased and the erection of "high-rise flats" upon it became feasible. The overall evidence supports Wickham J.'s conclusion that it was this change in the policy of the council which "played a very big part in the spectacular rise in the value" of the land. (at p60)

7. Under the Metropolitan Region Scheme which became operative on 30th October, 1963, the Macey Street land was reserved for public purposes being land which the Metropolitan Regional Planning Authority ("the authority") intended eventually to acquire (by negotiation or resumption) in connection with the construction of a proposed bridge. The result of that reservation was that any new development of the relevant land was prohibited unless the consent of the authority, which was the resuming authority under the scheme, had been obtained. Compensation for any injurious affection due to or arising out of such reservation was, under s. 36 of the Metropolitan Region Town Planning Scheme Act 1959 (W.A.), payable if and when either the land was first sold following the date of reservation or an application made under the scheme for permission to carry out development on the land was refused altogether or granted subject to unacceptable conditions. (at p60)

8. The first sale after the reservation was the sale to the appellant. The vendor (a Mrs. Hamilton) was entitled to claim compensation provided she complied with the procedural requirements laid down by the section. Apparently, she did not exercise any right to claim compensation. In these circumstances, there is much to be said for the view that any right to claim compensation in respect of injurious affection due to or arising out of the reservation had been exhausted. This was not, however, the approach which, in fact, prevailed in that it was apparently assumed by all concerned that the appellant enjoyed full rights of compensation under s. 36 even though she had purchased the land after it had become affected by the reservation under the scheme. The land was, in 1970, purchased from the appellant, after negotiation, by the authority. The negotiations leading up to that purchase were conducted, and the amount of the ultimate purchase price was agreed, on the basis that any diminution in value of the property resulting from the reservation should be ignored. (at p61)

9. Before the board of review, the appellant gave evidence that the property had been acquired as a family home. The appellant's husband (to whom I shall refer as "Mr. McCormack") gave evidence to the like effect. Her daughter (Mrs. Dodd) and a Mrs. Schwind who, with her husband, had become the appellant's tenant of the property were also called to give evidence on her behalf. The only other oral evidence before the board of review was formal evidence from a bank manager who identified certain bank records and expert evidence from two valuers. (at p61)

10. The evidence before the Supreme Court was confined to the transcript of evidence before the board of review, the exhibits before the board of review, the reasons for decision of the two members of the board of review who participated in the decision (all of which were admitted, without objection, into evidence) and the oral evidence of an experienced estate agent and valuer (Mr. Mickle) who was called to give evidence on behalf of the appellant. This oral evidence was important in that it placed in perspective certain newspaper cuttings which had been admitted into evidence before the board of review and which were also in evidence before the Supreme Court, by clarifying the extent of the public knowledg, in December 1963, of the details and effect of the Metropolitan Region Scheme. It also helped to explain the effect of both scheme and zoning and the importance of the adoption by the council of the City of Perth of the "Clarke-Gazzard Code". (at p61)

11. Plainly, the decision of the board involved a question of law. In the forefront of the appellant's attack upon the decision of the board before both the Supreme Court and this Court, was a submission which found favour with his Honour that the board "did approach the case on the basis that there was a presumption that the property was acquired for resale at a profit" and that such an approach was inconsistent with the law as stated by Barwick C.J., and Jacobs J., in Gauci v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1976) 50 ALJR at p 360 . In the circumstances, the whole decision of the board was open to review before his Honour (Ruhamah Property Co. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1928) 41 CLR, at p 151 ) regardless of whether the appellant was ultimately successful in her attack on the decision of the board in relation to any question of law which it involved (Krew v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 45 ALJR, at p 325 ). The proceedings before his Honour were not "an appeal" in the strict sense since the board of review was exercising an administrative function and the proceedings before his Honour were in the original, and not the appellate, jurisdiction of the Supreme Court (see Watson v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1953] HCA 98; (1953) 87 CLR 353, at p 373 ). It is, however, convenient to refer to them as an "appeal". (at p62)

12. It may be that, in a case where the only evidence before the Supreme Court is the transcript of the evidence before the board of review and the only question of law involved is whether the decision of the board was properly open to it on the evidence, the task of a State Supreme Court on an appeal from a board of review under s. 196 of the Act is restricted to examining the decision of the board to determine whether that decision was open to the board on the material before it (see Buckland v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1960) 34 ALJR 60, at p 62 ). This was not, however, such a case and it is unnecessary to express any view on that question. The present matter undoubtedly involved questions of law which made the appeal competent. Additional evidence to that which was before the board had been adduced. The task of the learned judge of the Supreme Court in these circumstances was to assess the evidence for himself and reach his own conclusions on the questions of fact involved. That task was defined by Walsh J., in the following passage from his judgment in Krew v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation: "In Commissioner of Taxation v. Miller [1946] HCA 23; (1946) 73 CLR 93, at p 98 Latham C.J. indicated that the Court should give 'due weight' to the Board's decisions on questions of fact. But I am of opinion that I am not restricted in hearing this appeal in the way in which appellate courts are restricted, according to established principles, when hearing appeals (by way of rehearing) from a lower court. I have a duty to reach my own conclusions on the questions of fact which have to be decided and to give effect to those conclusions. I am not limited to asking myself whether the findings of the Board were based on a misapprehension of the evidence or of the questions which had to be decided or were manifestly wrong. I must make my own decisions as to the facts. This view is, I think, in accordance with the observations of Dixon C.J. in Rowdell Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1963) 111 CLR, at p 119 " (1971) 45 ALJR, at p 326 . (at p62)

13. In Krew v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation Walsh J., on the appeal from the board of review, had the transcript of evidence given before the board in evidence before him and also had the benefit of seeing and hearing the appellant and the main witnesses, to whom reference was made in his judgment, give evidence in that they were called as witnesses before him on the hearing of the appeal. In the present matter, the transcript of all oral evidence given before the board of review was tendered by the appellant on the hearing of the appeal and, without objection, admitted into evidence. The reasons for decision of the two members of the board who participated in the decision were, likewise without objection, received into evidence. None of the witnesses who gave evidence before the board of review was, however, called to give evidence on the hearing of the appeal. The question therefore arises whether it is possible to extract from the reasons of either of the members of the board any finding, which is unaffected by error or misunderstanding, as to the weight to be given to the evidence of any of the witnesses and, if so, what weight should be given by a court, in an appeal pursuant to s. 196, to such a finding in a situation where the members of the board had, and the court has not, the benefit of seeing and hearing the witness give evidence. (at p63)

14. The reasons for decision of each of the two members of the board of review proceed, as his Honour found, upon misunderstanding of "the significance of the zoning of the property and of the impact of the Metropolitan Region Town Planning Scheme". The explanation of this is to be found in a degree of confusion which existed in the evidence before the board and which was, to some extent, resolved by the evidence of Mr. Mickle as to the operation, in relation to the subject land, of both zoning provisions and the metropolitan scheme and as to the effect of the adoption, in 1966, of the Clarke-Gazzard Code. It is, for example, unlikely that if he had had the benefit of Mr. Mickle's evidence, Mr. Dempsey would have approached the matter on the basis that "(at) the time of purchase of the . . . property, it was zoned for high rise development and had been so zoned for some time" (italics added). (at p63)

15. In an essential part of his reasoning, Mr. Dempsey expressed the view that the Macey Street property was, at the time of purchase by the appellant, "in bad repair and could only be repaired to a limited degree and then only with approval of the resuming authority". This confuses new development which did require approval and the continuation of existing use which did not. Any necessary repairs to the house on the property would not have involved development and would not have required the consent of the resuming authority. This misunderstanding was obviously of relevance to the question whether the appellant's evidence that she purchased the property with the intention of effecting extensive repairs to the house and making it her family home should be accepted. It clearly played a significant part in the reasoning of the member of the board which led him to conclude that it should not. (at p63)

16. The finding by the other member of the board, Mr. Thompson, that the appellant, either by herself or through Mr. McCormack or other professional advisers or associates, knew of the "relatively recent rezoning" and of the effect of the Metropolitan Region Scheme, played a critical part in the reasoning which led to his inability to be "satisfied on the balance of probabilities" that the appellant acquired the relevant land as a family home. As his Honour pointed out, Mr. Thompson's decision to "fix" the appellant "with knowledge of the planned re-zoning and road development at the time of the acquisition of the subject property" (italics added) was not, once the position as to zoning, the Metropolitan Region Scheme and the part played by the adoption, in 1966, of the Clarke-Gazzard Code, had been clarified, a proper conclusion on the evidence. The misunderstanding as to the effect of zoning and scheme and the unjustified fixing of the appellant with "knowledge" of what was not the fact played a significant part in the inability of the learned member of the board to be satisfied that the appellant's purpose was that which she pro-pounded and in his consequential imputation to the appellant of "an intention to acquire the subject property for future resale at a profit". (at p64)

17. There is nothing in the reasons for decision of either of the two members of the board of review which can properly be construed as indicating complete rejection of the evidence of either the appellant or Mr. McCormack. Indeed, the inclusion of the second paragraph in the extract from the judgment of Fullagar J., in Pascoe v. Commissioner of Taxation (1956) 30 ALJR 402 which was quoted by Mr. Dempsey in par. 42 of his reasons may well indicate an acceptance by him of much of their evidence. Each member of the board of review reached the conclusion that he was not satisfied that the appellant purchased the property for the purpose alleged by her by a process of reasoning which did not involve the outright rejection of either the appellant or Mr. McCormack as witnesses. Once that process of reasoning is seen to be vitiated by the errors and misunderstandings to which reference has been made, the conclusion itself loses substance. Neither member of the board expressed any view as to whether the evidence of the other non-expert witnesses, should be accepted. It is, therefore, unnecessary, for the purposes of this appeal, to consider what weight should be given on an appeal from a decision of a board of review pursuant to s. 196 of the Act, to the evaluation by members of the board of the evidence of witnesses before it where the evidence of those witnesses before the board has been received into evidence on the hearing of the appeal without the witnesses being recalled and where additional evidence has been adduced on the hearing of the appeal to that which was before the board. Once the reasoning of the members of the board is seen, in the present case, to be vitiated by error and misunderstanding, no relevant evaluation by either member of the board of the evidence of any non-expert witness remained. (at p64)

18. As Wickham J. pointed out, the members of the board of review did not have the benefit of the decision of the Full High Court of Australia in Gauci v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 54; (1976) 50 ALJR 358 at the time they published their reasons. Mr. Dempsey understandably placed considerable value on the judgment, in that case, of Lavan J. in the Supreme Court of Western Australia which was subsequently overruled by the Full High Court. The members of the board of review approached the matter on the basis that there was a presumption that the property was acquired for resale at a profit. In the light of what was said in the judgments of Barwick C.J., and Jacobs J., in Gauci's case [1975] HCA 54; (1976) 50 ALJR 358 , they were in error in adopting that approach. I also consider that Mr. Dempsey was in error in his view that some remarks made by Lord Sumner in the course of his dissenting speech in Inland Revenue Commissioners v. Blott (1921) 2 AC 171, at p 218 can properly be regarded as applicable to a case involving the first limb of s. 26(a). The relevant comments of his Lordship were: "The intention, which the final decision assumed, was one of those so-called intentions which the law imputes, it is the legal construction put on something done in fact". Those remarks of Lord Sumner are, in my view, quite inappropriate to the purpose to which s. 26(a) refers. Indeed, reference to their context in Lord Sumner's speech would indicate that they could only be regarded as offering guidance to the correct approach to a s. 26(a) case if the position were finally reached where the common tendency to approach the question whether property was acquired for the purpose of profit-making by sale through a maze of extracts culled, often without regard to the need to read them secundum subjectam materiam, from other cases had finally reached the stage where it was an imputed and not the actual purpose of the taxpayer which was seen as relevant. (at p65)

19. Having found that "the approach of the board was incorrect" and that the reasoning of its members was marred, in important respects, by error and misunderstanding, Wickham J. rightly concluded that the appropriate course was that he should "determine the matter" himself. His Honour appears, however, to have accepted as his point of embarkation on that task the board's conclusion not to accept the evidence of the appellant as to her purpose in acquiring the property and to have disregarded the fact that the result of the errors and misunderstanding which were involved in the reasoning which led to that conclusion was that the conclusion itself was without substance and irrelevant to his task. (at p65)

20. The importance of the part which this irrelevant conclusion of the board played in the learned judge's ultimate decision is not completely clear. Parts of the judgment tend to indicate that it was the critical factor. For example, his Honour stated, in a context where he made reference to the fact that the board had seen and heard the appellant and that he had not, that he did not find any sufficient reason to depart from the board's decision not to accept the appellant's evidence and that he was not in a position to say that the board was wrong. On the other hand, his Honour concluded the paragraph of his judgment in which he considered whether the appellant's evidence should be accepted with the comment that he was "not in a position to say" - that left to himself he accepted her evidence as to her dominant purpose. Notwithstanding this comment, the overall effect of the references in the judgment to that irrelevant conclusion of the board is to convince me that his Honour regarded it as, at the least, relevant to his own approach to that evidence and that he permitted the fact that the board reached that conclusion to play a significant part in his own decision not to accept the appellant's evidence as to her purpose in acquiring the property. In this, in my view, his Honour was in error. His decision not to accept the appellant's evidence was plainly an integral factor in the process of reasoning which resulted in his dismissing the appeal. (at p66)

21. Having decided not to accept the appellant's evidence, the learned judge was of the view that the question which then arose for consideration was "whether leaving aside the evidence of the taxpayer there was any material upon which it could properly be concluded that the property was acquired with the relevant purpose" (italics added). He answered that question in the affirmative. In the light of that answer he treated the provisions of s. 190 of the Act as requiring that the assessment be confirmed in the absence of "satisfactory evidence" (or "sufficiently acceptable evidence") that the property had not been purchased for the relevant purpose. Involved in this approach, as his Honour expressly recognized, was the view that there was no occasion for considering whether, on consideration of all the evidence, the conclusion that the property had been purchased for the purpose of profit-making by sale should be drawn. "It is not necessary", his Honour wrote, "that it must be drawn or even should be drawn. It is sufficient that there be evidence from which the conclusion may be properly drawn". In my view, the question whether, on the evidence, the conclusion that the property had been purchased for the purpose of profit-making by sale should be drawn was not only relevant: it was the critical question which arose for decision by the learned judge. As a result of the approach which he adopted, he did not give direct consideration to that question at all. (at p66)

22. It is apparent that the learned judge took the view that, in adopting the approach which he did, he was acting in accordance with the law as stated by Barwick C.J., and Jacobs J., in Gauci v. Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 54; (1976) 50 ALJR 358 . I am unable to agree with that view. In Gauci's case, Lavan J. (at first instance (1975) 6 ALR 183 ) had held that an assessment had been properly made under the first limb of s. 26(a). The Chief Justice and Jacobs J. laid down that in such a case the assessment is shown to be excessive if, when all the facts (except the state of mind of the taxpayer at the time of acquisition) are known, there is no material upon which it may properly be concluded that the property was acquired for the relevant purpose (1976) 50 ALJR, at pp 360, 362 . Upon the facts of that case, they reached the conclusion that there was no such evidence and that Lavan J.'s decision could not stand. I do not read anything which either of their Honours said as indicating that in a case where there is material in the evidence upon which such a conclusion could properly be drawn, it is (in the absence of "satisfactory evidence" (or "sufficiently acceptable evidence") that the property was not acquired for the relevant purpose) necessary that a court charged with determining the facts for itself affirm the assessment regardless of whether, on consideration of all the evidence, it is of the view that the finding of the relevant purpose should, as distinct from could, be made. (at p67)

23. It is, of course, possible to envisage a case where there is material in the evidence upon which a finding of the relevant purpose could be made and where there is no evidence at all either tending to nullify the effect of that material or tending to show that the property had not been purchased for the relevant purpose. In such a case, it is, no doubt, likely, in the light of the provisions of s. 190(b) of the Act, that a court or board of review would have little real alternative to finding that the property had been purchased for the relevant purpose. This was not however, on any approach, such a case. The material in the evidence which his Honour identified as the material upon which it could properly be concluded that the property had been purchased for the relevant purpose was that the property was in an area where prices were much more likely to increase than fall, that the taxpayer was probably aware of this and the fact of the ultimate resale. There was in the evidence, other matter which at least weakened (and could conceivably be thought to remove) the keystone of that material (the ultimate resale) as a proper basis for such a conclusion in that the ultimate resale was made to the authority under threat of resumption and after a dramatic rise in rates and land tax. More important, there was also, quite apart from the evidence of the appellant, material which supported a conclusion that the property had been purchased for the purpose of being retained as a family home. For example, Mrs. Schwind gave evidence (which was not challenged in cross-examination) that the appellant had, about the time of the purchase, told her that that was her intention in relation to the property. There was also material in the evidence (e.g., the fact that the property was put in tenantable order and let) from which a conclusion that the property was purchased for investment purposes could conceivably be drawn. It is apparent that the references by the learned judge to "no satisfactory evidence" and "no sufficient acceptable evidence" could not properly have been intended to indicate that there was no evidence at all. (at p67)

24. In these circumstances, the essential issue of fact for his Honour was whether, on a proper assessment and weighing of all the evidence, the appellant's dominant purpose in acquiring the property was, on the balance of probabilities, profit-making by sale. If it was, she was destined to fail in her appeal. If it was not, she was entitled to succeed and have the decision of the board confirming the assessments set aside. In the event that, after assessing and weighing all the evidence, his Honour found that he was not independently able to reach a conclusion on that essential question, questions of onus of proof, including the effect of s. 190 (b) of the Act, would become critical. It was obviously necessary that his Honour give careful consideration to the question whether the appellant's evidence as to her purpose should be accepted. It was, however, equally necessary to take care to avoid both the danger of equating inability fully to accept the appellant as a witness with a positive finding that the purpose of which she gave evidence did not exist and, in a case where there was some evidence of more than one non-relevant purpose, the danger of ignoring the fact that absence of a positive finding of a non-relevant purpose is different from a finding of a relevant purpose. In a case where there are three competing purposes, of which only one is a s. 26(a) purpose, a positive finding that the property had, on the balance of probabilities, not been acquired for the dominant purpose of profit-making by sale is obviously possible even though a positive finding that the property had, on the balance of probabilities, been acquired for a specific purpose other than profit-making by sale could not be made. (at p68)

25. In a case such as the present where there is clearly material in the evidence supporting and material in the evidence militating against the conclusion that property has been acquired for the purpose of profit-making by sale, a court charged with determining the facts for itself, as distinct from a superior court charged with reviewing the decision reached by the court below, will derive little assistance from the affirmative answer to the question whether there is any material evidence upon which such a finding could properly be made. The question will not however be an inappropriate one at a preliminary stage of the path to decision. It is however quite inappropriate for such a court in such a case to treat the affirmative answer to the question as the basis for a process by which the essential question for consideration, namely, whether, on the evidence, the property was in truth acquired for the relevant purpose, is actually excluded from consideration. In my view, the approach which his Honour adopted involved such a process with the result that his Honour never, in truth, attempted to make a finding on the essential question which arose for consideration before him. Even if I be mistaken in that view, I consider that his Honour was in error in permitting the conclusion of the board of review not to accept the appellant's evidence of her purpose in acquiring the property to play a significant part in his ultimate decision. (at p68)

26. All the evidence before his Honour, apart from the evidence of an expert witness, was in written form. The credibility of that expert witness has not been attacked. This Court is at no greater disadvantage than was his Honour in examining and assessing the evidence to determine whether, on the probabilities, the appellant did or did not purchase the property for the purpose of profit-making by sale. In the result, I consider that it is incumbent upon this Court to assess the evidence unassisted and unrestricted by the principles which guide an appellate court in an appeal on questions of fact from a judge who has reached a decision on the relevant questions and who has had the benefit of seeing and hearing the witnesses give evidence (see, generally, Paterson v. Paterson [1953] HCA 74; (1953) 89 CLR 212, at pp 219 et seq ; Da Costa v. Cockburn Salvage and Trading Pty. Ltd. [1970] HCA 43; (1970) 124 CLR 192 ; and Edwards v. Noble [1971] HCA 54; (1971) 125 CLR 296 ). The matter is properly "at large" before us (Benmax v. Austin Motor Co. Ltd. (1955) AC 370, at p 376 ). (at p69)

27. On an appeal to the Supreme Court from a decision of a board of review, the Supreme Court must, provided the decision of the board of review involves a question of law, decide the matter on the material before the Supreme Court (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Lewis Berger (1927) 39 CLR 468, at p 469 ; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Sagar [1946] HCA 6; (1946) 71 CLR 421, at pp 423, 424 ; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Finn [1960] HCA 69; (1960) 103 CLR 165, at pp 167, 168 ; Krew v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 45 ALJR, at p 326 ). The onus of adducing evidence rests upon the appellant in the sense that, if no evidence at all is led before the Supreme Court, there will be no material before it upon which it could properly interfere with the decision of the board of review. The position is more complicated in so far as the substantive burden of establishing a case on the preponderance of the evidence is concerned. It would seem that the provisions of s. 190(b) ("the burden of proving an assessment is excessive rests on the taxpayer") do not apply directly to the proceedings before a Supreme Court on such an appeal (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Finn (1960) 103 CLR, at p 170 ; cf. Buckland v. Commissioner of Taxation (1960) 34 ALJR 60, at p 61 ). As I see the matter however, the ultimate question for the Supreme Court on such an appeal is whether the decision which the board of review reached on the material before it is the decision which a board of review should reach on the material before the Supreme Court or, to put the matter in another way, whether, regardless of whether the decision of the board of review was right on the material before it, the decision of the board of review is right on the material before the Supreme Court (Krew v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 45 ALJR, at p 326 and Ruhamah Property Co. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1928) 41 CLR, at p 151 ). As at present advised, I am of the view that that ultimate question can only properly be decided in the context of the provisions as to substantive onus of proof which would operate before a board of review and that the provisions of s. 190(b) were, for that reason, indirectly operative upon the hearing of the appeal to the Supreme Court of Western Australia. If this view be correct, the provisions of s. 190(b) are likewise indirectly operative in relation to our consideration of the evidence to determine whether, on the evidence before the Supreme Court, the decision of the board confirming the assessments should be allowed to stand. It is not necessary to express any concluded view on this question however for the reason that, even without the indirect operation of the provisions of s. 190(b), the onus of impugning the correctness of the assessments which were confirmed by the board of review would lie upon the appellant (see McAndrew v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1956] HCA 62; (1956) 98 CLR 263, at pp 271, 275 ; Krew v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 45 ALJR, at p 326 ). (at p70)

28. I do not, however, consider it helpful or correct to treat the ultimate substantive onus of proof as the appropriate starting point for the assessment and weighing of the evidence in a matter such as the present. As has been said, the essential question of fact is whether the appellant did or did not acquire the subject property for the purpose of profit-making by sale. We have before us, if only in written form, the evidence of the appellant, of Mr. McCormack, of Mrs. Dodd, of Mrs. Schwind, of two expert valuers and of an expert valuer and estate agent. We also have, before us, a considerable body of relevant documentary evidence. There is evidence which supports a finding that the purpose of acquisition was profit-making by sale. There is evidence which supports a contrary finding. All this evidence must be assessed and weighed. The fact that the ultimate onus of proof rests upon the appellant will, as I see the matter, only become relevant if the result of that assessment and weighing of all the evidence is judicial atrophy or, to put the matter perhaps more accurately, if the evidence, pro and con, is found to be so evenly balanced that the court can come to no definite conclusion (see Churchman v. Churchman (1945) P 44, at p 52 ). On the other hand, when such assessment and weighing of the evidence leads to a positive finding one way or the other, questions of onus of proof can legitimately be put to one side (see Elsey v. Commissioner of Taxation [1969] HCA 48; (1969) 121 CLR 99, at p 108 ). In the event, I find it unnecessary, in the present case, to rely on considerations relating to onus of proof. (at p70)

29. Some of the objective facts in the context of which the appellant's purpose in acquiring the Macey Street property must be examined are clear and not in dispute. The appellant and her husband carried on, in partnership, the business of real estate agents under the name J.M. McCormack & Co. The business was concerned mainly with the sale of lower-priced houses in the inner suburbs of Perth "to the south of the river". The appellant's husband was the active partner. The appellant's activities were restricted to attending, on occasions, at the partnership office to answer the telephone, take messages and do some typing. Apart from their home which was mortgaged, neither the appellant nor Mr. McCormack possessed any substantial assets. Mr. McCormack's assessable income for the year ended 30th June, 1964 (before concessional deductions) was approximately 2,500 pounds. The position throughout 1963 was that any acquisition of land by either the appellant or Mr. McCormack would need to be financed with the net proceeds of sale of their home or with borrowed money. (at p71)

30. In the calendar year 1963, the appellant acquired three separate properties of which the third was the Macey Street land. The first of these properties was in Perth suburb of Leederville. The second was in the Perth suburb of Ashfield. (at p71)

31. The purchase price of the Leederville land was 2,100 pounds. The transfer of the land to the appellant was dated 15th March, 1963, and was registered on 3rd April, 1963. Of the purchase price, 2,000 pounds was obtained by the appellant through a first mortgage over the subject land which was arranged by a Mr. P. Burridge of Messrs. Leslie Burridge & Son, a firm of mortgage brokers and real estate agents. On 2nd April, 1963 (the day before the transfer of the land to the appellant was registered), the property was advertised for sale on behalf of the appellant. The property was sold for 3,100 pounds. On 18th June, 1963, the property was transferred to the purchasers and the mortgage in respect of the 2,000 pounds which had been borrowed by the appellant was discharged. An amount of 400 pounds of the purchase price remained owing to the appellant on the security of a second mortgage over the property. (at p71)

32. On 18th June, 1963 (the day on which the sale of the Leederville land was settled), the appellant purchased the Ashfield property. The purchase price was 2,400 pounds. Of this purchase price, 2,000 pounds was obtained as the proceeds of a first mortgage over the Ashfield land. This mortgage was likewise arranged by Mr. Burridge. The mortgagor (a Mr. Sugden) was, however, different. In November 1963 the appellant agreed to sell the Ashfield property for 3,100 pounds and on 17th December, 1963, a transfer to the purchaser was registered and the mortgage discharged. (at p71)

33. Settlement of the purchase of the Macey Street property was apparently effected on or about 24th December, 1963. The whole of the purchase price (2,000 pounds) was provided by Mr. Burridge arranging for the 2,000 pounds, which had been borrowed from Mr. Sugden on the security of a first mortgage over the Ashfield property, to be again lent, this time on the security of a first mortgage over the Macey Street land. The transfer of the land to the appellant was not registered until 7th January, 1964, when the mortgage was also registered. In the context of the common (at least in 1963) Western Australian practice of settling such purchases at the Titles Office, it may be that the actual settlement was not effected until 7th January, 1964, even though the mortgage of the land by the appellant was dated 24th December, 1963. (at p72)

34. There were a number of features common to each of the three acquisitions of property by the appellant. In each case, the property came to the attention of Mr. McCormack in the course of his activities as a real estate agent. In each case, there was erected on the land purchased a dwelling house. In each case, the purchase was of the type of property with which the real estate business was mainly concerned. In each case, the bulk of the purchase price was borrowed upon a fixed three-year mortgage. It is apparent from the evidence that, in each case, neither the appellant nor Mr. McCormack believed that, if the relevant property were not sold, there was any real likelihood of their being in a position to discharge the mortgage at the end of its term otherwise than from the proceeds of further borrowing. In each case, the mortgage to enable the purchase was arranged by Mr. Burridge. In each case, it was 2,000 pounds. The 2,000 pounds borrowed in respect of the Leederville property was repaid on the day on which the 2,000 pounds borrowed on the Ashfield property was advanced. The 2,000 pounds borrowed on the Ashfield property was repaid a few days before the 2,000 pounds advanced on the Macey Street property was advanced. (at p72)

35. On the other hand, the facts surrounding the acquisition of the Macey Street property differed, in a number of important respects, from those surrounding the acquisition of the other two properties. The Macey Street property was not sold within months of its acquisition. It was held by the appellant for over six years and there is nothing in the evidence to suggest that any negotiation or active step towards its sale occurred until almost three years after its acquisition. The house erected on the Macey Street property had not been lived in for some time and was not in a fit condition for immediate occupation. At about the time the appellant agreed to purchase the property, Mr. McCormack had some essential plumbing and electrical work carried out. The house was immediately let by the appellant to Mr. and Mrs. Schwind who, apparently, went into occupation before the purchase by the appellant had been completed and remained in occupation, as tenants, for the ensuing three years. (at p72)

36. The appellant's evidence as to her purpose in acquiring the property is obviously of critical importance in the case. She alone can give direct evidence of what that purpose was. She swore that her purpose in acquiring the property was to retain it and use it as a family home. In the circumstances of this case, I am of the view that acceptance of the appellant as a completely truthful and reliable witness would undoubtedly mean that she is entitled to succeed in her appeal. On the other hand, inability to accept the appellant as a completely truthful or a completely reliable witness does not mean that she must fail in her appeal or that the matter should be approached on the basis that Wickham J. seems to have approached it, namely, that the position was the same as if she had never given evidence in the material respects at all. Even though it is impossible to place complete reliance on the word of a witness, his or her evidence as to essential facts may be true in whole or in part. The approach of isolating all the evidence of the principal witness and, if it is found unacceptable, deciding the issues on the basis that the evidence in the material respects which that witness has given should be simply disregarded is not, in my view, appropriate to this case. (at p73)

37. The importance of the evidence of the appellant and Mr. McCormack in the present matter obviously makes it essential that their evidence be carefully examined and tested so that an assessment can be made of their truthfulness and reliability as witnesses. The approach adopted by the members of the board of review was, as I read their reasons, not so much to examine and test their evidence but to discount, and to some extent, disregard its importance. In a case where a number of purposes have concurrently operated in a witness' mind his evidence as to which was the dominant purpose is obviously likely, however honest he may be as a witness, to be affected by subconscious considerations when, often years later, he seeks for the first time to ascribe pre-eminence to one of the operative purposes. It was to such a case that Cussen J., was referring in the words from his judgment in Cox v. Smail [1912] VicLawRp 50; (1912) VLR 274, at p 283 which are quoted in the well-known passage from the judgment of Fullagar J. in Pascoe v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1956) 30 ALJR 402, at p 403 which is set out in the reasons of Mr. Dempsey. There is nothing however in the judgment of Cussen J., or the judgment of Fullagar J., which justifies seeing the purpose to which s. 26(a) refers as being a purpose which can be imputed to the taxpayer in the way in which, in some cases, the law imputes purpose or intention regardless of whether it was, in fact, present or operative. The purpose to which s. 26(a) refers is the subjective purpose of the taxpayer which was in fact operative. In this regard, the words cited by Fullagar J., from the judgment of Cussen J., may convey a less enthusiastic approach to the importance of the evidence of a witness as to his purpose or intention in the ordinary case than a reading of the whole of Cussen J.'s judgment would indicate to be appropriate. The question before Cussen J., in Cox v. Smail [1912] VicLawRp 50; (1912) VLR 274 , was whether a debtor had made a transfer "with a view to" giving a particular creditor a preference over the other creditors within the meaning of s. 73 of the Victorian Insolvency Act 1890. Cussen J.'s approach to the importance of the direct evidence of a witness as to what his intention or purpose was appears from the following extract from his judgment: "But the legislature did not intend, I think, that there should be any mystery about s. 73. It was meant as a plain guide for ordinary people, and if a debtor is intelligent, honest, and desirous of telling the truth, his statement, which, apart from form, is undoubtedly admissible (on the question, usually a difficult one, of his state of mind), should be treated at least as of great weight. I think in most cases a debtor knows perfectly well whether or not he does an act with the view of giving a preference" (1912) VLR, at p 278 . (at p74)

38. In approaching the overall assessment of both the appellant and Mr. McCormack as witnesses, it is important to be conscious of the fact that their evidence was given more than eleven years after the acquisition of the Macey Street property. This is particularly so when one is attempting to assess the reliability or truthfulness of a witness from the written record of his or her evidence. Confusion, uncertainty and, to some extent, contradictions in evidence which can readily be explained by lapse of time in the assessment of a witness giving oral evidence tend to take on a more intractable aspect when the assessment of evidence is restricted to examination and analysis of the written record. (at p74)

39. In assessing the appellant and Mr. McCormack as witnesses, I have endeavoured to make every allowance that can properly be made by reason of the period of years between the acquisition of the three properties and their being called upon to give evidence in relation to those acquisitions. In the event, I find myself unable, in the context of the overall evidence, to accept either of them as a reliable or completely truthful witness. It is necessary that I examine the evidence of each in some detail. (at p74)

40. Mr. and Mrs. Schwind went into occupation of the Macey Street property before settlement of the appellant's purchase of it. It is clear from the overall evidence that the electrical and plumbing work (of a total cost of approximately $350) which Mr. McCormack arranged to be effected before the Schwinds went into possession of the property was effected to make the house tenantable for their purposes. Indeed, in his questioning of Mrs. Schwind, senior counsel for the appellant clearly accepted that this was so. The appellant, in her evidence, stated that this electrical and plumbing work was carried out, "so that I could get into it and have proper living facilities" and denied that the work had been effected so that a tenant could be put in. In the overall context of the evidence, I do not accept the appellant's version that the necessary work was carried out so that she could move into the house or that, at the time the work was carried out, there was "no intention of putting a tenant in". This evidence was close to the heart of the appellant's case. (at p75)

41. The appellant's evidence was that she was led to acquire the property by reason of the complete unsuitability for her family's needs of the home which she and her husband owned and occupied in South Perth. It was "a misery" living in that house. It was a misery "always as long as I lived there". "There was no room." "It was most inconvenient." When she acquired the place, she wanted to get into it "as soon as I possibly could. I was very excited about it". In fact, the Schwinds were allowed to stay there, as tenants, for three years without any efforts being made to expedite their departure. When they left, the house was re-let. It was not suggested that any effort at all was made, until years afterwards, to sell the unsatisfactory South Perth house. (at p75)

42. The appellant's evidence as to when she first realized that her plans to use the house as a family home had been rendered impracticable by reason of the reservation is confused and difficult to follow. In a signed joint statement which was forwarded to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation in May 1973, the appellant and Mr. McCormack had said that they became "aware of the zoning of the land and of the Metropolitan Region Planning Authority plans for the area in approximately 1967". It is possible, from part of her evidence, to place the time at which the appellant claims she first acquired the relevant information in either 1969 or 1968. Overall, however, her evidence is to the effect that she first realized that her plans were no longer feasible in 1967. On the other hand, Mr. McCormack said that he found out that the "place was going to be resumed" in 1964 and that thereafter he realized that "it was not much good persisting with" the plans to use the property as a family home. If the appellant's evidence is accepted on the point, the fact that between 1963 and 1967 she took no step to move into the house does not lie well with her evidence of consistent "misery" in living in the South Perth house and determination to move as soon as she could. If Mr. McCormack's evidence is accepted, the appellant's evidence is obviously unreliable and, indeed, quite untrustworthy in respect of matters which again lie close to the heart of her case. (at p75)

43. An important factor in the overall assessment of the evidence is the time between acquisition of the property and the opening of negotiations to dispose of it. There are critical internal inconsistencies in the appellant's evidence. In her evidence-in-chief she indicated that negotiations with the Region Town Planning Board did not commence until after the receipt of a land tax assessment dated 22nd October, 1969. In cross-examination, it emerged, that on 2nd October, 1967, the appellant had written to the secretary of the Region Town Planning Board asking whether there was a chance that Macey Street would not be required by them and expressly stating that she had been advised to write to him "and perhaps start negotiations for a settlement". It further emerged in cross-examination that, by July 1968, the appellant was writing to the Under-Secretary for Works in the following terms:

"In answer to your letter regarding the purchase of my property No. 8 Macey St. East Perth. "I am asking $120,000 per acre for it. This property 1 1/2 miles from the City G.P.O. (walking distance) river frontage, has unlimited potential for flat accommodation with ample parking space, it (sic) it were not to be resumed. "I feel this price is in keeping with current values and I hope will meet with your approval". (at p76)

44. The cross-examination also disclosed that, in August 1968, the appellant wrote to the Under-Secretary of Works rejecting an offer of $75,000 and indicating that her price had risen to $200,000. The appellant's evidence indicated that she played an active part in steps leading up to the ultimate sale of the property. In view of the appellant's and Mr. McCormack's general financial situation and the very substantial amounts of money which were being sought by the appellant in 1967 and 1968, it is somewhat difficult to attribute the misleading character of the appellant's evidence-in-chief to mere confusion. In this regard, it is relevant to note that the appellant had, apparently and as one would expect, prepared herself to give her evidence. When questioned about the age of her children she remarked: "I haven't sort of got exactly the dates. I didn't expect to have to look up these sorts of things". (at p76)

45. The appellant's evidence in relation to the Leederville and Ashfield properties is, upon analysis, equally unconvincing. (at p76)

46. It will be recalled that Leederville was purchased for 2,100 pounds and sold, within months, for 3,100 pounds and that Ashfield was purchased for 2,400 pounds and, likewise within months, sold for 3,100 pounds. True it is, interest on the borrowed moneys had to be paid during the period each property was held. On the other hand, the commission which the appellant and Mr. McCormack obtained from the vendors on the sale of each property counter-balanced the amount of such interest. Each purchase and subsequent resale turned out to be highly successful from the financial point of view. The appellant, when asked in cross-examination whether she had ever had any property in her own sole name other than the Macey Street property, sought to give a contrary impression. Her reply was: "Yes. We bought a couple of old places for investment for the children. One was in Leederville, in Byron Street - which I am very sorry we ever bought because I don't think we were in a position to do it. One was very hard to let, and we sold it". (at p76)

47. It has been seen that the transfer of the Leederville land to the appellant was registered on 3rd April, 1963, and that, on the day before such registration, the property was advertised for sale on behalf of the appellant and that settlement of the subsequent sale took place on 18th June, 1963. The appellant was uncertain whether, in the very short interval between purchase and resale, she had let Leederville. Her explanation of the sale of Leederville, namely, that it was sold because "it was very hard to let", seems, to put the matter mildly, somewhat unsatisfactory. (at p77)

48. The appellant's evidence as to Ashfield was scarcely more satisfactory. Ashfield was owned by the appellant for at most, a period of five months before she agreed to sell it. The reason for the sale, according to the appellant, was that the tenant defaulted. "They never paid any rent." "They only paid a little bit of rent." "They just didn't pay the rent." In his evidence, Mr. McCormack supplied some relevant figures. The weekly rent was 6 pounds.10.0 and the tenant in fact paid approximately 90 pounds. According to the appellant, the property was vacant when she purchased it and she agreed to sell it, on 18th November, 1963, because of the default in rent. Treating the tenants as having been in occupation for every day of the period in which the appellant owned the property up until the day she entered into the contract to sell it (and ignoring the period between purchase and letting and any period between eviction and her agreement to sell), the total rental, on Mr. McCormack's evidence as to the weekly rental, was 143 pounds and the arrears, on his evidence that 90 pounds was paid, were, at most 53 pounds. (at p77)

49. The appellant's evidence, when asked if she made a profit on the sale of the Ashfield land, was: "I suppose so". The impression which she endeavoured to convey of these two highly successful investments appears again from the following extract from her evidence:

"Q. You have got no idea of that? A. We sold it. We were just so fed up with the people, trying to catch up with them and trying to get the rent from them, that we felt we would never buy another place as an investment because we had had such bad luck." "Q. In those two? A. Yes". (at p77)

50. I find the conclusion inescapable that the appellant sought to conceal, in her evidence, the importance and the relevance of her purchase and subsequent sale of the Leederville and Ashfield properties. (at p77)

51. My inability to accept the appellant as either a reliable or completely truthful witness does not necessarily lead to the conclusion that her evidence as to her purpose in acquiring the property should itself be rejected. As regards that purpose, she remained adamant and unshaken. Her stated purpose, namely, to retain and use the property as a family home, emerged as a constant refrain. Indeed, her overall evidence conveys the impression that she was determined that anything she might say in relation to related matters must give way to the maintenance of that refrain. One example of this was when, near the end of her evidence, she was shown a previous statement she had signed in which she had referred to possible demolition of the house erected on the land: "Q. Do you say that that statement forwarded by your agents to the commissioner was in fact wrong - that you had no intention of demolishing the house? A. When I bought it, I just had intentions - but you wouldn't know as time went on what you would do with it, would you? You might. Is that an alright answer? I mean, I just bought it. I was going to do it up and live in it, but maybe I might have later on if I could have". As I see the matter, the result of my assessment of the appellant as a witness is not that her direct evidence as to her purpose in acquiring the property and as to other matters which are relevant to ascertaining that purpose disappear from the case. The result of that assessment is that I do not attribute to her evidence the weight which must, at the least, be accorded the evidence of an honest and reliable witness as to the purpose which motivated him (see per Cussen J., Cox v. Smail (1912) VLR, at p 278 ). (at p78)

52. The evidence of Mr. McCormack is even less calculated to inspire confidence than that of the appellant. His evidence in relation to the acquisition of the Leederville land, the acquisition of the Ashfield land and the acquisition of the subject land is, in my view, unsatisfactory in many respects. I find his evidence when read as a whole quite unconvincing. Examples are his evidence as to when the appellant and he first decided that they wished the appellant to purchase the property, his evidence as to the time when he told Mr. Burridge that he wished to purchase it, his evidence as to the dealings between himself and the vendor of the property who became his client and his evidence as to the circumstances which led to the commission on sale being shared by Mr. Burridge and J.M. McCormack & Co. (at p78)

53. In some places, Mr. McCormack's evidence conveys the impression that he was concerned with the effect of his evidence as a whole rather than with the accuracy of his answer to the question which he had been asked. An example is his evidence on the question whether he had read certain articles in the newspaper, The West Australian, in 1962 or 1963. Mr. McCormack was a regular subscriber to that newspaper in which, in those years, a number of articles appeared relating to planned road and bridge construction in areas of Perth including areas in which the appellant's and Mr. McCormack's real estate business carried on its activities. Those plans included a plan to erect a bridge in the vicinity of the Macey Street property. The articles included discussion on the effect the planned construction would have on land values. Mr. McCormack denied that he had ever seen any of these articles. His whole approach to questions in relation to particular articles was obviously to deny that he had seen them if their publication preceded the date of the purchase of the Macey Street property without any real attempt to consider whether the contents of a particular newspaper article might assist or alter his recollection. Indeed, at one stage, the chairman of the board of review commented that he had denied having seen a particular newspaper article before counsel "had specifically asked you what it was". (at p79)

54. One thing that does, however, emerge quite clearly from Mr. McCormack's evidence, despite confusion as to detail, is the anomalous role he played in relation to the sale of each of the three properties to the appellant. It is no exaggeration to say that Mr. McCormack's activities in relation to each acquisition provide the context in which the acquisition itself must be viewed. (at p79)

55. The Ashfield land and the Leederville land were each acquired by the appellant from a client of the real estate business carried on by the partnership between Mr. McCormack and the appellant. Mr. McCormack was the active partner in the business and may well have given the appearance of carrying on business solely on his own account. The evidence of the subsequent resale of them indicates that the appellant purchased the Leederville and Ashfield properties at an advantageous price. (at p79)

56. It is not surprising that, in the case of both Ashfield and Leederville, the suggestion emerged that the appellant was purchasing the land to hold as an investment. One assumes that in each case the vendor, at some stage, became aware that he was selling to his "agent's" wife. It is scarcely likely that the vendor in either case would have been receptive to the proposition that the property which he was selling was being purchased by the wife of his estate agent for the reason that the purchase price was so low that she would, within months, be able to resell it at a handsome profit. If the vendor, in either case, had been aware in advance of what the ultimate outcome of the appellant's purchase would be (i.e. resale within months at a substantial profit), he may well have been constrained to seek from his agent on the sale (to whom he was paying commission) the exercise, on his behalf, of the selling efforts which were shortly to be exercised on behalf of that agent's wife. The probability of such an approach would obviously be increased if the vendor, in such circumstances, also knew that the purchaser was the partner as well as the wife of his "agent" and that both "agent" and wife regarded the wife as holding the property for them both. (at p79)

57. The appellant and Mr. McCormack gave evidence that the Leederville property and the Ashfield property had each been purchased as "an investment" for their children. Their evidence does not, however, lie well with the objective facts. They retained neither property as an investment. In the case of each property, they set out to resell it at a profit within a short period from its acquisition. In each case, they so sold it. Their explanation for resale is, in each case, unsatisfactory. There is nothing additional in the evidence to support their assertion that they either purchased or held either property as an investment for their children. There is no suggestion that the substantial profits arising, in each case, on resale were treated as being held on account of their children. (at p80)

58. There is some uncertainty in the evidence as to the precise role of Mr. McCormack in the sale of the Macey Street property to the appellant. His evidence-in-chief conveyed the impression that his initial contact with the property was when he was, possibly at the same time as other estate agents, approached to act as agent in conjunction with Burridge & Son. In cross-examination, the impression emerged that the approach by Burridge & Son to Mr. McCormack was in a context where Mr. McCormack was seen, not so much as an agent to act jointly on the sale of the property, but as a potential purchaser. What then occurred is not really consistent with either view. Mr. McCormack (according to his evidence, in the presence of Mr. Burridge) saw the very old lady who was selling the property and obtained a sole agency agreement in respect of the property which was for a period of eight weeks and which expressly provided that, in the event of a sale, full commission would be paid to J.M. McCormack & Co. It is doubtful whether even this written sole agency agreement correctly represented the true position since, when the property was ultimately sold to the appellant, the commission was, according to Mr. McCormack, received by Burridge & Son and shared by that firm with J.M. McCormack & Co. in the proportion of three-quarters (retained by Burridge & Son) and one-quarter (paid to J.M. McCormack & Co.). Whatever may have been the relationship and arrangements between Messrs. McCormack and Burridge, it does however seem clear that, so far as the vendor was concerned, the firm of J.M. McCormack & Co. was acting as her agent and was entitled to receive commission from her on any sale. (at p80)

59. An examination of the original written "Offer and Acceptance" dated 3rd December, 1963, shows that the words "purchaser is wife of agent" appear in a section of the document which would, prima facie, be cancelled by a diagonal scratch line. The bottom of the "p" in "purchaser" is below the ruled horizontal line and is in complete alignment with the starting and ending point of the scratch line. It is apparent that the words "purchaser is wife of agent" were inserted after the scratch line was drawn. This could well be the subject of a completely innocent explanation. Mr. McCormack was, however, emphatic that the words in question were not added after the diagonal line had been drawn. (at p80)

60. It appears probable, from the answers given by Mr. McCormack under cross-examination, that the appellant and he may well have decided that they wished to acquire the property before Mr. McCormack first saw the vendor and obtained the authority to act as her sole agent on sale. It was suggested to him, in cross-examination, that he had procured the execution by the vendor of the blank form of offer and acceptance at the same time (the visit to her on 29th November, 1963) that he procured the signature of the authority to sell (incorporating the sole agency agreement). He denied that this was the case. It was also suggested to him, in cross-examination, that the words "purchaser is wife of agent" which, as I have said, were, in my view, patently inserted after the scratch line had been drawn, were added after the vendor had actually signed the document. To this suggestion he replied: "You are trying to insinuate that that has been altered, are you, and Mrs. Hamilton didn't know that my wife was the wife of an estate agent? She knew - Burridge knew - so what is the point of that?" In the face of Mr. McCormack's denials, I consider that a finding that he procured the execution by the vendor of a blank form of offer and acceptance or that the vendor was unaware that the purchaser of the property was the wife of Mr. McCormack would consist of speculation for which no sufficient basis exists in the evidence. The cross-examination to which reference has been made did, however, highlight the existence of a clear conflict of interest and duty. Mr. McCormack's answers clearly indicate that he at no time attempted expressly to explain that conflict of interest and duty to the old lady for whom he was acting as agent. (at p81)

61. The position as to commission, the sole agency agreement and the apparent alteration to the offer and acceptance may well be, in themselves, unimportant for the purposes of this appeal. They are, however, symptomatic of a fundamental malaise lying at the heart of the appellant's acquisition of the Macey Street property. It is, one would hope, unusual for a real estate agent acting for a vendor on a sale to be procuring the sale of his client's property to his (the agent's) wife. The difficulties involved in such a situation are compounded when the client is a very aged lady from whom her agent is endeavouring to obtain, for his wife, a reduction in the purchase price. The conflict of interest and duty becomes formidable indeed when it appears that the agent (who is acting for his client vendor) claims to be interested with his wife in the property being acquired and where the wife, who is purchasing, is an equal partner with her husband in the firm which is acting as agent on sale and which is entitled, by the express terms of both the written authority and the contract of sale, to a commission on sale. (at p81)

62. Assuming that the statement "purchaser is wife of agent" was in the document at the time the vendor signed it, Mr. McCormack's position was scarcely improved by its inclusion since that statement did not constitute any adequate disclosure to the vendor of the impossible conflict of interest and duty to which Mr. McCormack had subjected himself. Indeed, the statement was itself misleading in that Mr. McCormack subsequently claimed to be jointly interested in the property with the appellant and, in any event, the agent specified in both sole agency agreement and contract was J.M. McCormack & Co., in which the appellant was interested as an equal partner. The fact that the vendor may have had available to her advice from a daughter and a son does little to relieve the gravity of Mr. McCormack's situation. (at p82)

63. It may well be that neither the appellant nor Mr. McCormack fully appreciated the impossibility of the situation which Mr. McCormack had brought about. One must assume however, that there was at least a feeling of unease as to Mr. McCormack's part in the transaction. The addition of the statement that "purchaser is wife of agent" to the written "Offer and Acceptance" supplies some confirmation of that assumption. The explanation advanced in the notices of objection as to the reason why the Macey Street property was put in the name of the appellant supplies further confirmation. Clause (f) of each notice of objection states that it "was because of James Malcolm McCormack's association with the transaction that it was thought desirable that the purchase by the McCormack family should be in the name of the taxpayer". (at p82)

64. As was the case with the acquisition of the Leederville property and the Ashfield property, it was scarcely to be anticipated that, if the Macey Street property was being purchased for the purpose of profit-making by sale, the appellant or Mr. McCormack would divulge that purpose to the vendor. Assuming that Mr. McCormack did disclose to the vendor that the purchaser was his wife, it is unlikely in the extreme that he would have disclosed to the vendor that his wife was purchasing because of a belief that it would be possible to dispose of the property at a handsome profit. Such disclosure would, one would think, inevitably have led to the inquiry why Mr. McCormack could not, as the paid agent of the vendor, procure such a sale on her behalf or, at the least, wait to assess the response produced by the advertisement which he had placed for sale of the property at 2,500 pounds and which appeared on the morning it was sold to his wife, before arranging a sale to his wife at 2,000 pounds. (at p82)

65. There is conflict in the evidence as to the value of the Macey Street property at the time of purchase. Mr. Wilkins, an expert valuer, stated that the value at the time of purchase would have been about "Two thousand something pounds" which he believed was the price paid for it. This view was expressed in answer to a question in cross-examination. It was not suggested that he had attempted to go through the exercise of preparing a considered valuation. Mr. Riches, also a qualified valuer, produced a detailed valuation which he had prepared in which he reached the conclusion that, as at December 1963, the land had a minimum value of 10,000 pounds. The valuation of the land for land tax purposes, as at 30th June, 1963, was 3,000 pounds. That valuation was an old one which had been made in 1956. A new valuation of the land for land tax purposes was made during the months prior to 30th June, 1964. The new valuation, which applied in respect of the 1964-65 year, was 9,500 pounds. Mr. Riches gave evidence that a valuation for land tax purposes "must contain an element of conservatism". (at p83)

66. It is unnecessary, for the purposes of the present matter, to attempt to determine a precise valuation of the land as at the time of purchase. It suffices to say that, the evidence convinces me that, if one ignores the effect of the reservation under the Metropolitan Region Town Planning Scheme, the value of the land, at the time of purchase, was substantially more than the 2,000 pounds which the appellant paid for it. (at p83)

67. When questioned about whether Mr. McCormack had told her, at the time the decision to purchase was made, that the purchase of the Macey Street property would represent good value, the appellant said: "He never said I couldn't go wrong if I bought it. He never said one thing about value . . .". When it is remembered that, according to Mr. McCormack, the vendor initially wanted 2,500 pounds for the property and subsequently agreed to accept the appellant's offer of 2,000 pounds, I simply do not believe that the appellant's real estate agent husband did not indicate to her any view on the value of the property which she was, as a result of his activities, acquiring. The appellant's participation in the activities of the real estate firm was very limited. Presumably her knowledge of values would not be equal to that of her husband. I think it must, however, be inferred that both the appellant and Mr. McCormack believed in December 1963, that the price they were paying for the Macey Street property was below its true value. Their belief in that regard may well have been strengthened by the fact that Mr. Burridge, who was himself a real estate agent, was prepared to arrange that a client advance the whole of the purchase price which they were paying upon the security of the property which they were purchasing. (at p83)

68. My conclusion that both the appellant and Mr. McCormack believed that the purchase price which the appellant was paying for the Macey Street property was substantially less than its true value does not, of itself, necessarily support the inference that the property was acquired for the purpose of profit-making by sale. The home buyer, no less than the land trader, is likely to be anxious to acquire his property at the most advantageous price. Nor does that conclusion involve a finding that, at the time of the purchase, either the appellant or Mr. McCormack was aware that the land was reserved for public purposes under the Metropolitan Region Town Planning Scheme or that either Mr. McCormack or the appellant anticipated the remarkable increase in the value of the property which would occur. The 1963 value which Mr. Riches placed upon the property was unrelated to either reservation for public purposes (which would not be expected to increase the value) or suitability for "high rise flats" (which only arose upon the adoption, in 1966, of the Clarke-Gazzard Code). On balance, and taking into account the evidence of Mr. Mickle, I think it probable that, at the time of the purchase, the appellant and Mr. McCormack were unaware that the land was reserved for public purposes under the Metropolitan Region Town Planning Scheme. I accept the evidence of the appellant and Mr. McCormack that it was only after the property had been acquired that they first learned of the reservation. It is therefore unnecessary to speculate as to the effect, if any, which either the appellant or Mr. McCormack would have believed the reservation would be likely to have upon the value of the property. (at p84)

69. I have already referred to the evidence of Mrs. Schwind that, around the time of the purchase, the appellant indicated to her that the property was being purchased as a future family home. That evidence is relevant to the question of what the appellant's intention actually was (see per Windeyer J., Elsey v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1969) 121 CLR, at p 108 ). Mrs. Schwind was purporting to give detailed evidence of a casual conversation which she had had more than eleven years previously. She was not, however, cross-examined as to when and in what circumstances she had first been asked to recall the terms of the conversation. Nor was she asked, in cross-examination, any questions which were calculated to raise doubt as to her ability to remember the conversation or to challenge her veracity in recounting it. Mrs. Schwind's evidence of that conversation constitutes, in my view, the most telling piece of evidence in the appellant's favour in the whole of the material before us. It must however be seen in the context where, as has been said, it was not to be expected that, assuming that the appellant had purchased the property for the purpose of profit-making by sale, either the appellant or Mr. McCormack would acknowledge that purpose. (at p84)

70. Mrs. Schwind's evidence is, in other respects, inconsistent with other evidence. She swore that the first payment of rent in respect of the Macey Street property was made on 7th December, 1963, and that she "had been in there that week". She also swore that the arrangements in relation to her tenancy were made with Mr. McCormack two weeks before she moved in. On the other hand, Mr. McCormack was quite specific that he did not obtain even an authority to sell the property prior to 29th November, 1963. His evidence to that effect is supported by the written sole agency agreement which is dated (in two different places) 29th November, 1963, and which did not, by its terms, commence to operate until that date. Acceptance of Mrs. Schwind's evidence as to when Mr. McCormack arranged for her husband and herself to rent the Macey Street property would lend much support to what was, in effect, put to Mr. McCormack in cross-examination, namely, that the whole of his evidence as to the circumstances surrounding the execution of the offer and acceptance should be rejected. On the whole, the evidence of the written sole agency agreement has led me to conclude that Mrs. Schwind's evidence as to when the tenancy of the Macey Street property was arranged should not be accepted. (at p85)

71. Mrs. Dodd, the appellant's daughter, gave evidence that she returned to Perth for a holiday at Christmas time in 1964 and that she went, with her parents, to see the house in Macey Street. Her parents told her "that was where we were going to live". Her evidence was given after both her mother and father had given evidence. The appellant's evidence, in relation to this alleged incident, was that she and her husband told Mrs. Dodd that they had bought the Macey Street property "and we took her up to show her where we were going to live". In the context of the appellant's evidence, it is clear that the alleged statement was meant to convey a continuing intention. On the other hand, as has been mentioned, Mr. McCormack's evidence was to the effect that, by Christmas 1964, the idea of using the Macey Street property as a family home had been abandoned. When Mrs. Dodd was cross-examined as to the incident, it emerged that the reference to "where we were going to live" had suffered, as it were, a sea change. It was no longer a statement of continuing intention. It was a statement of an intention that no longer existed. Mrs. Dodd's evidence of this conversation does, in my view, lend some support for the appellant's case that her purpose at the time she acquired the property was to retain it as the family home. On the other hand, her evidence can be seen as highlighting the inconsistency between the evidence of the appellant and the evidence of Mr. McCormack. (at p85)

72. Consideration and assessment of all the evidence has led me to the conclusion that the Leederville land, the Ashfield land and the Macey Street land were all acquired by the appellant for the purpose of profit-making by sale. The appellant's and Mr. McCormack's evidence that the Leederville and Ashfield properties were purchased as an investment for their children is, as I see the evidence, quite unconvincing and, in important respects, inconsistent with the objective facts. I find myself compelled to reject it. The clear inference from all the evidence is that each property was purchased for the purpose of profit-making by sale. The position as regards the Macey Street property is not, as I see the matter, quite as clear cut as is the case with the other two properties. There is evidence supporting the appellant's contention that it was acquired to be retained as a family home. It is also possible to speculate, on the evidence, that it may have been purchased simply to take advantage of the circumstances surrounding its acquisition at the price at which it was acquired leaving considerations of what would be done with it to the future. On an assessment of all the evidence however, I am satisfied that the Macey Street property was purchased for the purpose of profit-making by sale. Its purchase was the third in a connected series of purchases and was, on the conclusion which I have reached, made for the same purpose as that for which the two previous purchases had been made, namely, profit-making by sale. The explanation of any mention of the Macey Street property being acquired for an ultimate family home (if mention there was) is to be found, in the view I take, in the circumstances in which the purchase was made rather than by reference to the real purpose motivating the purchase. (at p86)

73. In reaching this ultimate conclusion I have taken into account the evidence supporting the alternative basis upon which the appeal was argued on behalf of the appellant, namely, that the appellant's purpose in purchasing the property was to retain the property as an investment. The appellant, in her evidence, denied that she had purchased the property for any such purpose or, indeed, that she had ever entertained any such purpose in relation to it. In her letter of 2nd October, 1967, to the secretary of the Town Planning Board in which the question of resumption was raised, she had, however, stated that the proposed resumption had "put a stop to a personal project for flats and ample parking" which she had in mind. The fact that the appellant and Mr. McCormack spent between $250 and $350 in plumbing and electrical repairs and that the property was, in fact, let for a period of slightly more than six years during which the appellant retained the property, lend some support for the inference of such a purpose. On the overall view I take of the evidence however, the statement of intention in the letter was not an accurate one and the other matters do no more than indicate a willingness of the appellant and Mr. McCormack to hold the property for a period before realizing the profit for the making of which, it had been acquired. (at p86)

ORDER

The appeal should be dismissed with costs.

Order accordingly.
