Re Taxation Appeals [1991] AATA 169; 22 ATR 3322 (12 July 1991)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. QT90/147

AAT No. 6878A

Income Tax

[1991] AATA 464; 22 ATR 3322

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

Dr P. Gerber(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Share value enhancement scheme - Whether partnership engaged in share trading - Practice and procedure - Whether grounds of objection sufficiently explicit to raise the issue that the return should have been assessed on a receipts rather than an accruals basis - Whether due to alteration of assessment from accruals to receipts basis in a previous year has the automatic effect of enabling a loss in that year to be carried forward - Limits to the power to amend grounds of objection.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1980, secs 51(1), 52A(2A), 52(2B), 80, 82KH, 82KL,170(1), 170(7), 185, 190(a), 260.

F.C. of T. v Executor Trustee and Agency Co. of South Australia (Carden's case) [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108

HR Lancey Shipping Co Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. (1951) 5 AITR 135

Archer Brothers v F.C. of T. [1953] HCA 23; (1953-54) 90 CLR 140

F.C. of T. v Firstenberg 76 ATC 4141

Deane and Croker v F.C. of T. 82 ATC 4112

F.C. of T. v Dunn 89 ATC 4141

Case W65 89 ATC 590

Case X88 90 ATC 640

HEARING

BRISBANE

12:7:1991

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the objection decision for the year of income ending 30 June 1980.

DECISION

In this case, I have been asked to determine a preliminary point - is the Tribunal able to authorise what is said to constitute an amendment to the objection, which would have the effect of dealing with the taxpayer's 1980 tax return on a "receipts" rather than an "accruals" basis.

2. In the year now under review, the taxpayer lodged a Form B tax return (Business and Professional) in which he described himself as a chartered accountant. Attached to the return was a note:

"NOTE: DURING THE YEAR OF INCOME ENDED 30 JUNE 1980, THE

TAXPAYER WAS A MEMBER OF THE UNDERMENTIONED PARTNERSHIP AND THE

COMMISSIONER IS REQUESTED TO ASSESS THIS INCOME TAX RETURN AFTER

DUE REGARD FOR THE TAX CONSEQUENCES OF THE TRANSACTIONS ENTERED

INTO BY THE SAID PARTNERSHIP.

DITON TRADING COMPANY

FILE NO: NOT KNOWN

PARTNERSHIP TAX RETURN LODGED AT MELBOURNE"

3. Notwithstanding that the taxpayer is a single practitioner, a chartered accountant and was presumably aware of the decision in F.C. of T. v Executor Trustee and Agency Co of South Australia (Carden's case) [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108, he returned his income on an accruals basis. In his return (dated 31 January 1980), the taxpayer returned as taxable income from the practice an amount of $33,837. The Commissioner reduced the distributed loss from the Diton Trading Company partnership ("Diton") from $29,816 to a loss of $23 in an assessment dated 2 June 1981, which, after a further adjustment of an amount of $66 qua distribution from the partnership Mortland Trading Company, left the taxpayer with a taxable income of $33,880.

4. The taxpayer objected against the disallowance of the "Diton" loss in an objection which pleaded everything except that he should have been assessed on a "receipts" rather than on the "accruals" basis as returned.

5. It is common ground that if the assessment were to be dealt with on a "cash receipts" basis, the issue involving the partnership loss in Diton would become academic since there would be no assessable income. The Commissioner did amend the 1979, 1982, 1983, 1984, 1985, 1986, 1987 and 1988 assessments from an "accrual" to a "cash receipts" basis, on the basis set out in paragraph 18 below. However, he asserts that, given the nature of the 1980 objection, he has no power to make the same consequential amendment for that year.

6. At the hearing, Mr P.K. Searle, of learned counsel for the applicant, submitted that the objection was wide enough to enable the taxpayer to raise the issue that he should have been assessed on a receipts basis.

7. I treated the width of the objection as a threshold question and, after due consideration, I advised both counsel that I was satisfied that the objection was not sufficiently explicit to direct the attention of the Commissioner to the fact that the return, although lodged on an "accruals" basis, should have been assessed on a "receipts" basis. Vague grounds, such as that the "amount of taxable income was wrong" and/or that "any judgment of the Commissioner was wrong ...based upon irrelevant matters ..." are not in compliance with the Act; cf HR Lancey Shipping Co Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. (1951) 5 AITR 135. Nor are such general "grounds", such as "the purported assessment is invalid, void, and of no effect" or "the income shown in the assessment is excessive". Yet, it is submitted that these "grounds" are sufficiently wide to support a submission that the income of the 1980 years should have been assessed on a receipts basis and that the Commissioner should have been alerted to that fact by the grounds as pleaded. I disagree.

8. After a short adjournment I delivered my decision on this point adverse to the taxpayer.

9. In summary, sec 185 provides that a taxpayer who is dissatisfied with the assessment may within 60 days after service of the notice of assessment post or lodge with the Commissioner an objection in writing against the assessment, stating fully and in detail the grounds on which he relies.

10. Sec 190 provides that "... on a review before the Tribunal or on appeal to a court, the taxpayer shall, unless the Tribunal or court otherwise orders, be limited to the grounds stated in his objection". Similar wording to sec 190 has been held to be a positive statutory provision that upon appeal or review, the taxpayer is limited to the grounds set out in his objection. This has been interpreted to be an imperative direction to the courts, rather than merely a provision for the benefit of the Commissioner which he is in a position to waive. The provision is made for the purpose of protecting public revenue, and the court (or tribunal) is bound to give effect to it. Whilst it may be unfortunate that one must look at grounds of objection as though they constitute strict pleadings, nevertheless the full High Court in Archer Brothers v F.C. of T. [1953] HCA 23; (1953-1954) 90 CLR 140, citing the decision of Williams J in the H.R. Lancey Shipping Case (supra) with approval, held that the grounds of objection need not be stated in legal form - they can be expressed in ordinary language - but they should be sufficiently explicit to direct the attention of the Commissioner to the particular respects in which the taxpayer contends that the assessment is erroneous and his reasons for this contention.

11. Whether interpreted technically, narrowly and with rigidity, or whether approached liberally, this case is not one where there could be some doubt whether the relevant grounds of objection have been included or not, so that the taxpayer could be given the benefit of any ambiguity; the particular point now relied upon has simply not been raised at all. One must therefore ask oneself whether, in the circumstances, such an omission is fatal.

12. The first thing to note is that the amendment to sec 190, giving the Tribunal wider power to amend grounds of objection, although retrospective in its operation, has been held not wide enough to permit a taxpayer to raise a wholly new ground of relief; cf Case W65 89 ATC 590, Case X88 90 ATC 640. The cases on the adequacy or otherwise of objections are too numerous to recite here. In the main, they deal with the question whether the arguments advanced on appeal can, somehow, be fitted into the stated grounds, and this becomes a matter of interpretation; that is, whether the grounds as pleaded are sufficient in their particularity to enable the Commissioner to gather from the written objection that he was being asked to apply his mind to any such contention.

13. On the whole of the documentary evidence and the argument of counsel, I am satisfied that the mere fact that, on one view, a specific point can be said to fall within a wider ground; that is, "that the assessment is excessive, and/or void, and/or invalid", cannot be prayed to aid since "it is not sufficiently explicit to direct the attention of the Commissioner to the particular respect in which the taxpayer contends that the assessment is erroneous, and his reason for this contention". I am therefore unable to conclude that the objection is wide enough to embrace the ground on which the applicant now seeks to rely.

14. It follows that the further and better particulars sought and settled by counsel in February 1991 cannot be prayed in aid to enlarge the grounds as raised in the objection. To the extent that they set out that the respondent has purported to assess the taxpayer on a false basis, namely by assessing the taxpayer - a sole practitioner - on the basis of "accruals" rather than on "cash basis", this should have been set out in the original objection; it is too late to seek to do it now. To try and achieve this by calling this process "further and better particulars" is innovative but, alas, ineffective to amend grounds of objection.

15. In an alternative submission, it was sought to be argued that the Commissioner himself had the power to amend and had misapplied or ignored the provisions of sub-sec 170(1) and (7), which state:

"(1) The Commissioner may, subject to this section, at any time

amend any assessment by making such alterations therein or

additions thereto as he thinks necessary, notwithstanding that

tax may have been paid in respect of the assessment.

...

(7) Nothing contained in this section shall prevent the

amendment of any assessment in order to give effect to the

decision upon any appeal or review, or its amendment by way of

reduction in any particular in pursuance of an objection made by

the taxpayer or pending any appeal or review."

16. I am not persuaded that the argument based on sub-sec 170(1) has any substance. The wording of the sub-section makes it clear that the exercise of the general power of amendment is subject to the operation of the other sub-sections which govern the circumstances in which amended assessments may issue. I am furthermore satisfied that sub-section 170(7) does not permit a reduction of the assessment upon a ground not stated in the notice of objection, if only because such reduction would not have been made "in pursuance" of that objection.

17. Undaunted, Mr Searle approached the issue from another angle. Thus, he submitted that because the assessment issued on an improper basis, it was equivalent to "non-assessment", hence "it isn't necessary for there being a ground of objection referable to a matter where there isn't an assessment". This submission lacks neither boldness nor imagination; unfortunately I do not believe it has any foundation in law.

18. Mr Searle's final submission relies on the amendment to the taxpayer's 1979 assessment, effected in August 1990. What happened can be set out in short compass. The taxpayer wrote to the Commissioner some time in 1990, enclosing a further return for the 1979 tax year in which he claimed a net loss as a result of converting his accounting basis from "accruals" to "cash receipts". The Commissioner accepted this consequential amendment and, in a letter to the taxpayer, dated 2 August 1990, stated:

"The loss for the 1979 year is accepted. This is in keeping

with the decision to allow you to alter the basis of accounting

in those years to which the statutory limitation period for

amendment does not apply. The 1979 year is one such year. No

amended assessment notice is required because the 1979 (year)

was originally non-taxable.

However, your request to amend the 1980 and 1981 assessments

cannot be met."

19. Thus Mr Searle submits that the Commissioner having, in 1990, allowed the taxpayer to alter his basis of accounting in 1979, this has the consequence of allowing him to carry forward into the 1980 tax year the loss which has been accepted for 1979; cf sec 80 of the Tax Act. To the question: "Why wasn't the loss carried forward in the 1980 return and/or why wasn't it raised in the objection?", Mr Searle has a simple answer: The Commissioner had originally wrongly assessed the return, hence there was no sec 80 loss to be carried forward. Counsel puts it movingly: "That is a totally new consideration; a new factor which couldn't have been included when the grounds of objection were originally drawn. So it's only now that it is possible to make application to amend the grounds of objection by now including a section 80 deduction in respect of the Commissioner's decision. Alternatively, it is submitted that the Commissioner has finally come round to accepting the "cash receipts" basis as the correct method of calculating the assessable income of a "one man practice", having previously adopted the accruals method." This argument is, in my opinion, entirely without merit. Carden's Case was decided in 1938 and was re-affirmed in F.C. of T. v Firstenberg 76 ATC 4141. It is thus not open to a party to say, as does Mr Searle: "The Commissioner having proceeded on a false basis for a number of years, and the taxpayer following him ... and (the Commissioner) literally used his armory to force taxpayers to join with him - 'you are all on an accruals basis' when in 1976 the High Court said: 'No, that's not right for sole practitioners' but nevertheless the Commissioner already had this taxpayer, along with many others, returning their incomes on a false basis. Now, on 2 August 1990 the Commissioner finally reversed his own ruling with reference to the applicant." It is a brave - if unprecedented - submission, but it must fail.

20. Ms Holmes, of learned counsel for the Crown, gave an effective answer to the argument. Thus she submitted with considerable force that the 1980 return did not, on its face, reveal that it was based on an accruals method; that was peculiarly in the knowledge of the taxpayer, so that the Commissioner had no means of knowing that there was any reason to do other than assess as returned.

21. More formidably, she points to the decision of Davies J in F.C. of T. v Dunn 89 ATC 4141 where his Honour stated:

"There is no principle of law or binding authority to the effect

that a sole practitioner is to be assessed on a cash receipts

basis. The matter depends upon the nature and incidents of the

income earning enterprise, upon current accounting principles

and practice and upon current perceptions in the field in which

the taxpayer gains his income. These may differ from time to

time. There may be relevant differences between different

occupations, between different periods and between different

taxpayers in the same field of occupation." (p 4151).

(The taxpayer in Dunn was a chartered accountant)

22. It was not sought to be argued that sec 80(2) operates of its own force in the sense that the loss in any one year is automatically carried forward by operation of law. The relevant provision states that:

"... so much of the losses incurred by a taxpayer in any of the

seven years next preceding the year of income as has not been

allowed as a deduction from his income of any of those years

shall be allowable as a deduction in accordance with the

following provisions:

(a) where he has not in the year of income derived exempt

income, the deduction shall be made from the assessable income;"

I take that provision to mean that a loss incurred in a previous year is an allowable deduction against the assessable income of subsequent years (if not otherwise disqualified from deduction), but it must be claimed; the loss is not an automatic deduction. In other words, if a carried forward loss is not claimed in a subsequent year or years, the only appropriate method thereafter of obtaining the deduction is to raise it by way of objection.

23. In summary, I am unable to find any principle of law or practice which would enable me to permit the taxpayer to raise, at this late stage, that the 1980 assessment should be treated on a "cash receipts" basis. The taxpayer lodged his return on an "accruals" basis and failed to object against the manner of assessment which he himself had nominated. I accept that it is an eccentric situation where the Commissioner is able, in 1990, to determine that a loss of some $24,238 is an allowable loss in 1979, and yet neither he nor this Tribunal is able to give effect to the provision of the Tax Act which would enable that loss to be carried forward into the next year.

24. Having determined the preliminary point against the taxpayer, the matter was adjourned for further hearing on the substantive issue. When the hearing resumed, there was no appearance on behalf of the taxpayer, who had previously notified the Tribunal that (i) although the taxpayer had ample notice of the hearing date, his office had arranged "commitments" for him for the whole day, so that he was thus unable to fit in an appearance before the Tribunal, (ii) Mr Searle would not be appearing, and (iii) acting on counsel's advice, he was prepared for the matter to proceed on those facts which appear as an unsigned document headed "Statement of Agreed Facts" (with provision for signature by both counsel, although at the commencement of the hearing, the document remained unsigned. It was tendered by Ms Holmes).

25. When apprised of these developments at the resumed hearing, Ms Holmes sought to have the matter dismissed for non-appearance. Despite the gross discourtesy to the Tribunal, I rejected Ms Holmes' application since the taxpayer had not been put on notice that his failure to appear could lead to such an application, preferring to see whether the facts which were agreed between the parties were such as to reduce the issue to a pure question of law.

26. The dispute involves a claimed deduction of the taxpayer's share of a partnership loss accruing from Diton in the 1980 tax year. The Commissioner reduced the distributed loss from $29,816 to a loss of $23. The taxpayer objected to the disallowance of the difference ($29,793).

27. Findings in Respect of Share Cost Conversion Expenditure

(XXX) Representatives of the shareholders, Diton Trading

Co (partnership) and Warneton Pty Ltd met to make the

resolutions which follow. (Warneton Pty Ltd was represented by

Graham H. Simmons as director and shareholder - holding 99 "B"

class shares. The only other director was Beryl Sinclair and

she held the only other share, one "A" class share.) Simon L.

Brown appointed Beryl Sinclair to be his proxy. The

shareholders of Hetel Pty Ltd decided:

(15 August 1979 Decision)

1. To alter the Articles and give the "A" class and "C" class shares the right to a return of capital paid up thereon on winding-up of the company.

29. The first thing to observe is that the arrangement Diton entered into lacks any of the indicia of "normal" share trading, by which I mean an activity in which traders buy and sell shares like fruiterers buy and sell fruit, hopefully to make a profit. Looking at the Statement of Share Trading for the 1980 tax year, one is tempted to apply the felicitous word "warpaint" to these transactions first used by Rogers J in Deane and Croker v F.C. of T. 82 ATC 4112, 4120 ("I can only say that the application of warpaint to a person does not make one into a warrior"). That case, like this one, depended for its successful implementation on the commercial trading in shares.

30. When this arrangement is looked at in slow motion (and confessing freely that I do not fully understand it), dealing only with the Hetel and Wirralton transactions, step 1 involves buying the "A" class shares in Wirralton and Hetel after substantial share premium reserves had been created in those companies. (These shares are subsequently sold at a loss). Step 2 involves an agreement between Diton and the holders of the "B" class shareholders in Wirralton and Hetel to acquire their shares for a substantial premium, paid, so it is said, in order to obtain the consent of these shareholders to vary the Articles of Association of the two companies. One effect of the alteration of the Articles is to severely restrict the "B" class shareholders' right to vote - in effect to convert them into fixed dividend preference shares. Why Diton saw the acquisition of 99 "B" class shares in these two private companies so attractive so as to persuade it to acquire them at this inflated price ($197,730 in the case of Hetel and (semble) $494,630 in the case of Wirralton - an amount just short of 100 times the par value and premium for the "A" class shares) is not readily explicable in commercial terms and, on any view, calls for an explanation when the reality of the sharetrading activities of the partnership is called into question before this Tribunal, an issue disclosed to the applicant in the decision on objection. No such explanation was forthcoming. Step 3 involves the merry-go-round with "C" class shares set out in para XXX of the Agreed Facts. These "C" class shares in the two companies were issued after the alteration of the Articles and were offered and allotted to the partners in Diton on an individual basis at a premium of $2 per share, having been first refused on behalf of the "A" and "B" class shareholders.

31. A taxation investigator who looked at this arrangement made a number of findings as to the effect of the payment by Diton to obtain the agreement to alter the Articles of Association of the two companies. These findings were disclosed to the applicant in the "T" documents.

32. In summary, they come to this: In Diton's 1980 return of income, the "A" class shares in Hetel and Wirralton was shown as purchased for $1962.00 and $4818.80 respectively. The shares were sold in the same financial year to non-arm's length purchasers. By that time the "A" class shares included a right to 300 "D" class bonus shares. However, the "A" and "D" class shares were sold for a mere $210 (Hetel) and $205 (Wirralton); i.e. the at a value considerably less than their purchase price. The sale price neither reflected the consideration paid by Diton nor the increased value in the "A" class shares after enhancement. Nor did the sale price reflect the change in value of "B" class shares when they became non-voting preference shares. The funds for Diton's payments to Warneton in respect of the latter's changed shareholding ("B" class to preference shares) were sourced in loans from Weisbrod. The total required for the 1980 payments was $692,360. The Balance Sheet (as at 30 June 1980) for Diton showed an unsecured loan of $698,920, but it did not identify the source. Cheques made out by Weisbrod evidenced this loan. In the 1981 return of income, the unsecured loan was shown as $701,520.

33. The loan from Weisbrod left the partners in Diton with personal liabilities to Weisbrod. These liabilities were negatived by the partners' individual entitlement to a distribution of the assets of Hetel and Wirralton. This entitlement was set up when the partners individually subscribed for and were allotted "C" class shares in each company. The assets of each company were the loans which each had made to Weisbrod. The total amount of the loans was $700,200 (Hetel $200,100; Wirralton $500,100).

34. The payments made to Warneton ($692,360) in the 1980 year contributed to the company's ability to subscribe for 99 "B" class shares in private companies in the stable of 42 non-trading private companies (set out in XII of Agreed Facts above). Alternatively, the share cost conversion payment was pooled by Warneton and loaned back to Weisbrod.

43. Against the whole of the (unchallenged) background set out above, a strong inference arises that the loss claimed by this taxpayer was not incidental and relevant to the gaining or producing the assessable income of the partnership, but was incidental to and relevant solely to the purpose of obtaining a tax loss. On that view, the loss is not deductible under the first limb of sec 51(1). Again, there is no evidence to support the assertion that the partnership was engaged in the business of share trading. In the absence of such evidence, I cannot be satisfied that the loss is deductible under the second limb of sec 51(1), if for no better reason than that I am unable to discern any nexus between the outgoing and any income derived by the partnership.

44. Another way of looking at this scheme is to regard the excessive payment incurred by Diton in acquiring the "A" class shares in Hetel ($197,730) and Wirralton ($494,630) as nothing more than an artificial enhancement of their true value. On that view, the outgoing is thus classically an affair of capital and excluded from deduction under sec 51(1), even on the assumption that the partnership was in the business of share trading.

45. Again, looking at these exotic transactions can reasonably lead to the presumption that the two private companies - Hetel and Wirralton - were in reality treated by their shareholders in a way which, in the absence of any contrary explanation, is explicable only in terms of deliberately creating a loss for the partnership. This presumption becomes overwhelming when it is demonstrated by the Commissioner that at about the same time as these impugned transactions took place with respect to Hetel and Wirralton, other "share trading" partnerships were participating in a scheme identical with the one now before me. While one must approach "similar facts" in civil proceedings with some caution, it behoves a taxpayer (an accountant to boot), even one with urgent commitments for the whole day, to come forward and offer some explanation of transactions which, unexplained, make no commercial sense, the more so when the Act places the onus to show that the assessment is excessive squarely on his shoulders. What such taxpayer cannot afford is to leave unexplained the respondent's assertion that he was part of a vast tax avoidance scheme, involving a pair of private companies, selected from the group of companies (cf XII in Agreed Facts above), which, in each case, were treated by their shareholders in the same senseless manner as occurred in the instant case. If such exotic happenings remain unexplained, such shareholder/taxpayer takes the risk that these "unusual" transactions will be interpreted as explicable only in terms of a tax minimisation scheme with the consequence that the scheme may be held void as against the Commissioner.

46. In this case, the Commissioner relied on a battery of defences, including, secs 51(1), 52A(2A), 52A(2B), 82KH, 82KL and 260 of the Act, and, of course "sham". It is sufficient for my purposes to find that the evidence - such as it was - has not persuaded me that the partnership was engaged in the business of share trading. The consequence of such a finding is that the shares which were purchased do not constitute the trading stock of the partnership. It follows that the cost of the shares (including the price paid for the "enhanced" value) is not an allowable deduction under the second limb of sec 51(1). That is sufficient to dispose of this case. I therefore do not propose to deal with alternative reasons which would lead to the same consequence, viz that this claim cannot stand on its own feet unaided. A taxpayer who fails to appear takes the risk that eccentric facts on which he relies and which make no commercial sense will be interpreted contra proferentem.

47. For the above reasons, the objection decision for the 1980 year is affirmed.
