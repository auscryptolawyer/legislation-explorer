HEARING

SYDNEY

7:7:1987

ORDER

Affirms the objection decision in relation to the claims for an undeducted portion of the purchase price of an annuity, which the applicant claims to have received.

Sets aside the objection decision in relation to the aggregate sums paid to the applicant by the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund and remits the matter to the respondent with the direction that $1,995 being 5% of $39,892 paid by the Fund was properly included in the assessable income of the applicant in the year of income ended 30 June 1981.

Sets aside the objection decision in relation to the aggregate amounts paid to the applicant by his employer in respect of other leave entitlements, and remits the matter to the respondent with the direction that the whole of the amount in question forms part of the assessable income of the applicant in the respective years of income in which each aggregate sum was received.

2. From these facts two principal issues arise. The first relates to the treatment for taxation purposes of the superannuation benefits paid to the applicant. The relevant legislation at the time was contained in section 26AA sub-sections (1) and (2) which were in the following terms:

"26AA. (1) The assessable income of a taxpayer

shall include the amount of any annuity, excluding,

in the case of an annuity which has been purchased,

that part of the amount of the annuity which

represents the undeducted purchase price.

(2) Subject to the next succeeding sub-section,

the amount to be excluded under the last preceding

sub-section from the amount of an annuity derived

by a taxpayer during a year ofincome -

(a) in the case of an annuity payable until

the death of the taxpayer or for a term

that will not end before his death - is

an amount ascertained by dividing the

undeducted purchase price of the annuity

by the number of years in the complete

expectation of life of the taxpayer, as

ascertained by reference to the

prescribed Life Tables, at the time when

the annuity first commenced to be

derived; and

(b) in the case of an annuity payable for a

term of years certain - is an amount

ascertained by dividing the undeducted

purchase price of the annuity by the

number of years in the term."

3. In order to determine whether the applicant is entitled to the exclusion referred to in sub-section (2) above, three questions have to be decided. It must be determined firstly, whether the monies he received can properly be described as an annuity. Secondly, it must be shown that if so, the applicant purchased that annuity. Thirdly, the applicant must show what amount, if any, represents the undeducted purchase price referred to in sub-section (2).

4. The second issue relates to the lump sum payment or payments made to the applicant on or after his retirement. The terms of the relevant sections of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") that were appropriate at the relevant time are paragraphs (d) and (e) of section 26 which were in the following terms:

"26. The assessable income of a taxpayer shall

include -

. . . . .

(d) 5% of the capital amount of any allowance,

gratuity or compensation where that amount is

paid (whether voluntarily, by agreement or by

compulsion of law) in a lump sum in consequence of

retirement from, or the termination of,

any office or employment, not being -

(i) an amount that, under any provision of

this Act, is deemed to be a dividend

paid to the recipient; or

(ii) an amount to which section 26AC or 26AD

applies;

(e) the value to the taxpayer of all

allowances, gratuities, compensations,

benefits, bonuses and premiums allowed,

given or granted to him in respect of, or

for or in relation directly or indirectly

to, any employment of or services

rendered by him, whether so allowed,

given or granted in money, goods, land,

meals, sustenance, the use of premises or

quarters or otherwise:

Provided that this paragraph shall not

apply to any allowance, gratuity or

compensation which is included in the

last preceding paragraph, which is an

amount to which section 26AC or 26AD

applies or which under any provision of

this Act is deemed to be a dividend paid

to the recipient;"

5. Section 26AD of the Act dealt with the taxability of amounts received on retirement or termination of employment in lieu of long service. For present purposes, it will not be necessary to reproduce that section. For reasons which will later appear, the relevant test to be satisfied in the present case is the same whether the amounts are considered in relation to section 26(d) or to section 26AD.

6. The issues here are whether the series of payments referred to in the statement of facts amounts to "a lump sum", and thus whether 5% of the total is taxable under paragraph (d) or secondly, whether any part of that series would be taxable under paragraph (e) or thirdly, whether the whole amount should be treated as a capital payment and therefore be not subject to taxation under the Act at all.

7. As to both issues, the applicant relied heavily upon the decision of the ACT Supreme Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Knight 83 ATC 4096. As to the second issue, the applicant relied not only upon that decision at first instance but also upon the decision of the majority in the appeal to a Full Bench of the Federal Court reported at 83 ATC 4789.

8. The superannuation monies payable to the present applicant are similar in character to those paid to Mr Knight. The fact that Knight received his benefits by way of a pension payable on premature retirement on the grounds of invalidity instead of a pension paid on retirement on the grounds of age, as is the case with the present applicant, in no way detracts from the parallel considerations in both cases.

9. It appears from page 4107 of the report that the Commissioner did not contend that the pension payable to Mr Knight was not an annuity. From this it may be assumed that the question was not argued. Nevertheless, His Honour did examine some authority on the subject and came to the conclusion that the pension was in fact an annuity. At page 4106 he said:

"In D.F.C. of Land Tax, Sydney v. Hindmarsh (1912)

[1912] HCA 27; 14 CLR 334 at p 338, Barton J. said:

"What then is the meaning of "annuity" as

a legal or technical term? According to

Co. Litt., 144b, an annuity is 'a yearly

payment of a certain sum of money granted

to another in fee for life or years,

charging the person of the grantor only'.

Viner's Abridgment, vol. II., p.504,

repeats the definition, with further

passages showing that the sum need not be

payable each year if only it is a yearly

sum. Bacon's Abridgment, vol. 1., p.233,

says that 'an annuity, strictly taken, is

a yearly payment of a certain sum of

money granted to another in fee simple,

fee tail, or life or years, charging the

person of the grantor only: if payable

out of lands, it is properly called a

rent-charge; but if both the person and

estate be made liable, as they most

commonly are, then it is generally called

an annuity.'

The text books generally adopt the

definition in Co. Litt.; no case was

found in which any other definition was

offered; nor any case in which an

indeterminate sum was held to be an

annuity.

It is therefore a characteristic of an

annuity that it be of a sum certain."

Isaacs J. also adopted the definition in Co. Litt.,

144b, and went on to say at p. 340:

". . . in saying an annuity charges the

person only, that is merely as to its

primary effect. It may also be charged

on the land, and . . . an action for debt

will in certain cases lie for a rent

charge."

It follows, I think, that payment of the sum agreed

to be paid as an annuity need not in all cases be

charged upon land or personalty of the grantor for

an action may lie upon the personal convenant of

the grantor. This accords with the definition of

the word "annuity" in Jowitt's Dictionary of

English Law, 2nd ed., where it is defined to mean

"a yearly payment of a certain sum of money granted

to another in fee, or for life, or for a term of

years, either payable under a personal obligation

of the grantor or charged upon his pure personalty,

although it may be a charge upon his freehold or

leasehold land . . ."

The element of certainty in the sum payable is

sufficiently satisfied, I think, because by

calculation and upon the happening of certain

events it becomes certain even though it may vary

from year to year. The basic figure is certain.

It represents 51.75% of Mr. Knight's final salary.

The provisions of Pt. X of the 1976 Act ensure that

Mr Knight's entitlement to a pension may be

calculated with certainty upon the publication of

the relevant all groups consumer price index number

and the application of the provisions of Pt. X.

The amount of the pension is no more uncertain than

was the rent in Ex parte Voisey; In re Knight

(1882) 21 Ch D 442 at p 458, per Brett L.J. (as he

then was)."

10. His Honour considered that payment of a lump sum benefit representing a refund of the amount of the applicant's contributions, together with a reduced pension, did not take away from the pension the character of an annuity. In doing so, he did not appear to draw any distinction between a lump sum that represented these contributions and a lump sum that represented a partial commutation of future rights. At page 4107 he said:

"Because of Mr. Knight's circumstances and the

election made by him, a question arises as to

whether his right to receive a lump sum benefit

together with a reduced pension under sec. 66(1) of

the 1976 Act takes away from his pension the

character of an annuity. It is to be noted that

para. (a) of sec. 66(1) is the only paragraph which

provides for what may be described as a pure

pension even though it makes provisions also for a

lump sum benefit in certain circumstances while

para. (b) and (c) both make provision for payment

of a lump sum benefit in addition to the

appropriate pension. I think the question is

resolved by Atkinson v. F.C. of T. (1951) 84 CLR

298 where, under the terms of an insurance

policy, a lump sum of 100 pounds proved payable on death,

quarterly payments of 39 pounds were to continue for a

fixed period and a further lump sum of 900 pounds was

payable on the last day of that fixed period. The

High Court held that the quarterly payments

constituted an annuity notwithstanding the fact

that lump sum payments were payable in addition.

It follows, in my opinion, that in Mr. Knight's

case the payment of the lump sum benefit to him

does not take away the character of annuity from

his pension.

I am satisfied, therefore, that in all the

circumstances the pension payable to Mr. Knight is

an annuity."

11. Kelly J. then turned briefly to the question whether the appellant had purchased his annuity. In holding that contributions were in fact purchase instalments, His Honour did not advert to the character of those instalments but relied on a decision concerning what he regarded as an analogous scheme. He continued:

"The next question is whether Mr. Knight purchased

the annuity. I think that on its true construction

sec. 26AA(1) of the Act is concerned with the

purchase price in money of the annuity and is not

concerned with any other consideration such as

service which might have been paid for the annuity.

I am satisfied that Mr Knight purchased his

pension. I adopt, with respect, the reasoning of

Jacobs J.A (as he then was) in Wayne v. Commr. of

Stamp Duties (1966) 85 WN (Pt. 1) (N.S.W.) 301 at

pp 311-312, particularly at p 312 where he said:

". . . where the scheme is a contributing

scheme even though the contributions are

compulsory, I think that the interest

created must be regarded as one which is

purchased or provided by the employee."

Cf. Just v. F.C. of T. (1949) 8 ATD 419; (1949)

Argus LR 438. It is to be noted that counsel for

the Commissioner did not contend that the pension

payable to Mr. Knight was not an annuity."

12. Kelly J. found, therefore, that the superannuation benefit was an annuity and that the appellant had purchased that annuity. He went on to hold, however, that it was impossible to determine what the undeducted purchase price was after examining the various formulae that had been submitted to him to enable him to resolve this issue. At 4108 he said:

"Since I am concerned only with the pension or

annuity actually payable, I am concerned with the

purchase price said to have been paid for that

pension. Accepting all that counsel for Mr. Knight

has said, it would still be the case that it would

be impossible to determine what was the precise

purchase price paid for the annuity in question.

The formula he suggested, 51.75 parts of 71.75

parts seems to me to be an arbitrary formula

without any real basis in the facts of the case or

in the legislation. Mr. Knight's position then is

very similar to that of the father in

Egerton-Warburton v. D.F.C. of T. (1934) 51 CLR

568. At p 575, the Court said of a provision

expressly excluding so much of the annual payments

as represented the purchase price of an annuity

that was purchased:

"The statutory provision gives an

advantage in cases which conform to

conditions established positivi juris.

One of the conditions is that there must

be an ascertained or ascertainable

price."

I find myself in a similar position in respect of

Mr. Knight's claim under sec. 26AA of the Act.

Since the onus is upon him to establish what

represents the undeducted purchase price, and

since, in my opinion, he has failed to establish it

the cross-appeal must be dismissed."

13. I find myself unable to agree with His Honour, with the greatest respect, on either question. It seems to me that a pension paid under the terms of the Superannuation Act 1976, or rather the congeries of rights to which a superannuant becomes entitled, cannot be properly described as an annuity. Furthermore, it seems to me that contributions made to the Superannuation Scheme cannot properly be regarded as instalments of purchase price of annuity.

14. I can only respectfully endorse what was said by Dixon, Webb and Fullagar JJ. in Atkinson v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1951] HCA 64; 84 CLR 298 at 304:

"The question what constitutes an "annuity" for the

purposes of income tax laws has been the subject of

much judical decision without any clear or

satisfactory test emerging."

15. In fact, most of the external judicial reviews that have concerned themselves with annuities have been directed towards the question whether monies bearing that character are taxable under certain circumstances (see for example Case D17 72 ATC 90). Until Knight's case, the question of identifying the characteristics of annuities, either in absolute terms or for the purposes of the Act, does not appear to have been addressed. There has been a long standing practice in the respondent's department which affirms the treatment of some pensions as annuities in certain circumstances. This practice, however, seems to have been originally based on questionable grounds and to have developed strength only through lack of challenge throughout the passage of years.

16. All definitions of annuities must start with the passage from Coke on Littleton referred to in Hindmarsh (supra). An annuity is a yearly payment of a certain sum of money paid either annually or fixed by reference to an annual amount. It is receivable until the death of the annuitant or until the expiration of a limited period. It may be purchased by the annuitant or may be given to him under a Will or a settlement.

17. A common example is an annuity that is left under a Will to give an income for life, for example, to an unmarried daughter before disposing of the balance of the testator's farming property to his sons. Annuities were commonly purchased from life assurance companies in order to provide an income for life for the purchaser. Such annuities were actuarily funded. The purchase price was calculated by reference to a rate of interest and the proposed annuitant's life expectation. The rate of the annuity was designed so as to exhaust the notional fund from which the annuity derived, upon the termination of the entitlement to payments. Thus, payments of an annuity would be partly of a capital nature and partly of an interest nature. For this reason, they have traditionally received special treatment for income tax purposes.

18. Simon's Taxes (Revised 3rd ed.) Vol. E at page 2106 offers these observations on the British experience:

"The present relief available for premiums paid in

respect of retirement annuity contracts takes its

origin from the relief given for life assurance

premiums, which was made available when income tax

was first introduced in 1799 and which, subject to

various changes, formed part of the income tax

structure between 1853 and 1984.

The rationale for providing such relief was that it

assisted taxpayers without capital resources to

make provision for themselves and their dependants

in old age, but because this tax privilege was only

given for one particular form of saving there was

always a school of thought that the relief was not

justified and was open to exploitation. For this

reason in the course of time the relief was made

subject to a number of restrictions.

FA 1916 significantly reduced the scope of the

relief by confining it to premiums on policies

which either (a) secured a capital sum on death

whether with or without other benefits, or (b) were

made in connection with a bona fide pension scheme

for the employees of one employer, or for persons

engaged in a particular profession, vocation, trade

or business. This is the first reference in the

tax legislation to pension schemes. FA 1920

extended the relief available for policies in

connection with pension schemes to those which

provided for the widows and dependants of members

of the scheme."

19. Although annuity is not defined under the Australian Act, it has been defined in other similar statutes. For example, the British 1940 Finance Act defines it as follows:

""Annuity" includes any series of payments, whether

inter-connected or not, whether of the same or of

varying amounts, and whether payable at regular

intervals or otherwise, and payments of dividends

or interest on shares in or debentures of a company

shall be treated for the purposes of this section

as a series of payments constituting an annuity

limited to cease on a death if the payments are

liable to cease on the death, or the amounts

thereof are liable to be reduced on the death, by

reason directly or indirectly of the extinguishment

or any alteration of rights attaching to, or of the

issue of, any shares in or debentures of a company

(Finance Act 1940, s. 44(3))."

There is of course great danger in taking one statutory definition and applying it in the interpretation of another statute. This is particularly so when one is dealing with so technical a field as taxation. I quote the definition for no other purpose than to illustrate an approach to the adaption of the traditional concept of annuities to modern financial arrangements.

20. Can the benefits to which a superannuant becomes entitled, either upon joining the Fund or upon retirement, be properly described as an annuity in traditional terms without the benefit of such a statutory extension of meaning? It is to be noted that the Superannuation Act itself nowhere uses the term annuity, nor (until recently) did the Act combine that term with the concept of a superannuation pension.

21. It is clear that a contributor to the Fund, whether or not a potential annuitant, does not have a settled interest in the Fund in the equity sense. The nature of the Fund was considered in Superannuation Fund Investment Trust v. Commissioner of Stamps (SA) 79 ATC 4429. At page 4440 Mason J. said:

"All the amounts payable for age retirement

benefits, both periodic and lump sum payments, are

payable out of the Consolidated Revenue Fund (sec.

55 to 57 inclusive). This result is achieved by

sec. 112(2) which provides that, except where other

provision is made, any payment of benefit is to be

made out of the Consolidated Revenue Fund, which is

appropriated accordingly. Every benefit to the

extent that it consists of periodic or pension

payment is payable out of the Consolidated Revenue

Fund and out of it only. Lump sum benefits payable

under sec. 62(2) in case of involuntary retirement

are also payable out of the Consolidated Revenue

Fund.

But a number of lump sum payments are made directly

out of the Superannuation Fund. They include

accumulated contributions payable out of that Fund

under sec. 68(5), 71(5) and 80(1) and certain lump

sum benefits equal in amount to the employee's

accumulated supplementary contributions. In each

case where a pension is payable additionally, it is

payable out of the Consolidated Revenue Fund. In

such case there is paid from the Superannuation

Fund to the Consolidated Revenue Fund an amount

equal to the employee's accumulated basic

contributions and the total benefit, with the

exception of the lump sum equal to accumulated

supplementary contributions, is payable out of the

Consolidated Revenue Fund (sec. 112(2)) . . .

The argument for the respondent rested chiefly on

the submission that the moneys standing to the

credit of the Fund belong in equity to the

contributors. I do not agree with this submission.

The Act prescribes the amounts of the benefits to

which contributors become entitled but it does not,

as I read its provisions, give them any property or

equitable interest in the Fund. To a very

substantial extent benefits payable under the Act

are payable out of the Consolidated Revenue Fund,

that Fund being reimbursed in appropriate cases by

the Superannuation Fund. The moneys standing to

the credit of the Superannuation Fund are

Commonwealth public moneys in an account within the

Treasury (sec. 60 of the Audit Act 1901 (as

amended)). To the extent to which the Trust is a

trustee of the moneys it is a trustee for the

Commonwealth, not for the contributors."

22. In the same case Aickin J. referred to features of the contributor's rights that would tend to detract from their characterisation as potential annuities. At page 4447 he said:

"Nor is the amount of the pension calculated by

reference, direct or indirect, to the amount of an

individual eligible employees' accumulated

contributions to the Fund, but is fixed by the Act

itself. Lump sums are, however, related to the

accumulated contributions, whether payable out of

the Fund or out of Consolidated Revenue. The

Consolidated Revenue Fund is from its very nature a

common fund in which are blended indistinguishably

all payments made to or moneys received by the

Commonwealth and payments are made out of the

general mass by appropriation. No statute provides

for the tracing of individual amounts that are paid

into the Consolidated Revenue Fund, for they are by

their very nature consolidated upon payment in.

. . . . That which reaches the Consolidated Revenue

Fund from the hands of the Trust thereby ceases to

be trust moneys under the Superannuation Act or at

all and becomes simply part of the general funds,

of the Commonwealth, consolidated together in a

single fund pursuant to sec. 81 of the Constitution

and the Audit Act 1901-1969 (Cth). In no sense,

therefore, can it be said that the pensions are

payable out of the Fund, though some lump sums are

payable directly out of it. In this sense the Fund

is held primarily, but not exclusively, for the

purpose of making payments to the Commonwealth on

the happening of the relevant events, i.e. the

cessation of an eligible employee to be such."

23. There are 54 sections comprised in two Parts, each divided into four Divisions in the Superannuation Act dealing with benefits payable under the scheme established by the Act. A member may not only contemplate benefits on reaching retirement age, he may also contemplate a range of benefits payable upon early retirement, whether voluntary or involuntary. The amount payable depends upon the circumstances leading to the retirement. Commutation is available in certain circumstances. Invalidity benefits are available where certain medical tests are met. Where they are not met, and where membership of the Fund is more or less than 8 years, further variations are introduced. Benefits are not confined to members. On the death of an eligible employee before obtaining his maximum retiring age, a spouse becomes entitled to certain benefits. These benefits are governed by certain medical considerations and by length of membership of the Fund. Within that sub-category, benefits vary depending upon whether membership is more or less than 8 years. Further benefits are payable to a spouse if the eligible employee dies after attaining maximum retiring age. Similar variations are to be found in those benefits. The range of potential beneficiaries does not stop there. Benefits are payable to a member's orphan at various rates, depending on whether the eligible employee dies after attaining maximum retiring age or before, depending on whether he had more or less than 8 years membership, depending on whether the member's spouse had died at certain times and depending on whether the events giving rise to any entitlement arose out of retirement, early retirement or invalidity.

24. It can be seen that there is a vast range of variations in benefits available to members and their existing or possible families, or survivors. It would be impossible to identify these benefits at the time the member entered the scheme. Most new entrants would be quite unable to say whether they would retire on grounds of age or invalidity, whether their spouse (if any) would survive them and what the position of their children would be in the events yet to befall them in their lives. The uncertainty of these benefits does not cease with retirement. Whether one analyses the nature of the benefits at the beginning of the membership period or upon retirement matters not. It is quite possible for a retired member to reorganise his personal life in such a way as to create new potential benefits in the future. For example, if a retired superannuant remarried a young wife and started a second family the same problems of ambiguity in entitlements would exist in the same way as they did at the time when he originally joined the Fund.

25. One may ask what have these rights to do with annuities as traditionally viewed and described in Snell's "Principles of Equity", 28th Edition at pages 363 and following?

26. Certainly any rights of commutation, when exercised, would not result in an annuity. This was recently decided by this Tribunal in Case U121, 87 ATC 726.

27. My view that benefits payable under the Superannuation Act cannot be regarded as annuities, seems to have been shared by the majority in Case M29, 80 ATC 191. At page 213 Mr C. F. Fairleigh QC said:

"The words "pension" and "annuity" are not

interchangeable. The Superannuation Act 1976 does

not grant any annuity. The "standard" pension,

particularly as to quantum, has too many variable

factors dependent upon imponderables (both prior to

first receipt and after first receipt) to be held

to be an annuity."

28. With respect, I adopt that expression of opinion. However, if I am wrong in this view I would also hold that payments of contributions could not properly be regarded as the purchase price for any supposed annuity.

29. This was also the view of the majority in Case M29. At page 213 Mr Fairleigh went on to say:

"(f) Even if "annuity" as in sec. 26AA of the

Income Tax Assessment Act could be held to

encompass the "standard" pension, there is no scope

for the application of the exclusion provisions of

sec. 26AA as to "purchase price" (regardless of

what had occurred pursuant to sec. 82H thereof or

its successors, i.e. sec. 159N and later sec.

159R). As at 1 July 1976 and thenceforth it could

not be said that any money paid by this taxpayer to

the new superannuation fund (a fortiori paid prior

thereto to the superseded fund cf. sec. 19A and 24

of the Act No. 32 of 1976) was for the purchase of

his "standard" pension, or was left unrecouped when

this taxpayer received his statutory benefits

pursuant to the Superannuation Act 1976."

30. In fact, the only external review in Australia that appears to have resulted in a decision that contributions to this fund did, in fact, constitute instalments of purchase price, is the decision at first instance in Knight (supra) referred to above. On the hearing of this application the respondent did not contest that the benefits payable to the applicant constituted an annuity. When I asked why such a concession was made by the Commissioner, I was told that the Commissioner relied on the decision in Knight. As I pointed out earlier, that decision seems to indicate that the question was not there argued. Accordingly, the respondent's position is that it makes the same concession in the present application because it made the same concession in an earlier case. I could not take a "default decision" even as persuasive authority, no matter how much deference I am bound to accord the reasoning in that decision. Furthermore, I do not accept that the Commissioner has the right to concede a matter of law. This would be a usurpation of the functions of this Tribunal. No such concession was made as to the second issue, namely whether payment of contributions amounted to instalments of the purchase price.

31. The question was considered in Case S63 85 ATC 454. At pages 459 and 460 Senior Member Roach (as he now is) said:

"The taxpayer contributed moneys to the Commonwealth

Superannuation Fund. Thereby he, and his

dependants, acquired potential entitlements which

could become actual entitlements depending upon the

happening of future events. Throughout the periods

of his contribution the identity of his

'dependants' might have varied. Some, who were

dependants initially, might have ceased to be so,

and others, who were not previously dependent,

might have come to be so. In the circumstances

which happened the first entitlement to receive

money from the fund vested in the taxpayer because

he survived a period of contribution as an

'eligible employee' to become a 'retirement

pensioner' of the Fund. Having so survived he was

entitled to amounts by way of pension. Further, in

relation to portion of his pension entitlements, he

was entitled to elect to commute that pension to a

lump sum. In fact he elected to do so. His

pension entitlement, whether commuted in part or

not, gave rise to an annuity. But the taxpayer's

entitlement to the annuity, even without

commutation, did not exhaust the rights acquired in

relation to the superannuation fund because upon

the death of the taxpayer further rights would, or

could, vest in others.

5. So if it be true (although I doubt it) that the

taxpayer's contributions constituted a 'purchase

price', they constituted the price paid by the

taxpayer to provide all of the benefits conferred

on himself and others by reason of his membership.

On that basis the question which would have arisen

pursuant to sec. 26AA would be one of apportionment.

It would then have been the responsibility of the

Board to 'make an apportionment which the facts of

the particular case may seem to make just . . . (to)

decide as a matter of fact what part or proportion

(of the amounts to be apportioned) is fairly and

properly attributable to gaining the assessable

income' (Ronpibon Tin N.L. v. F.C. of T. (1949)

[1949] HCA 15; 78 CLR 47 at p 60).

6. However, I am faced with the situation that in

F.C. of T. v. Knight 83 ATC 4096, on essentially

the same facts, his Honour, Kelly J., held that the

contributions did constitute a 'purchase price' in

the relevant sense, but that no allowance could be

made in respect of the 'undeducted purchase price'

because the taxpayer failed to establish just what

that was.

7. With all due respect to his Honour, I am not

persuaded that the contributions were aptly

described as a 'purchase price' of the annuity at

all. As each contribution was made by the

taxpayer, no entitlement to any annuity by way of

pension or otherwise arose or came into existence,

whether it be an annuity in favour of the taxpayer

or any other person. Further, there was no bargain

whereby the taxpayer would have been entitled to

recover his contributions as on a total failure of

consideration if no annuity came to be paid or

payable to him. Again, unless the taxpayer

survived to become a 'retirement pensioner' he

should never have been entitled to an annuity. In

my view the contributions made by the taxpayer were

no more the 'purchase price' of the benefits

actually paid to date and yet to become payable

(whether to the taxpayer or others) than the cost

of an art union ticket can be said to be 'the

purchase price' paid for the first prize by the

ticket buyer fortunate enough to win first prize.

8. Be that as it may, I must accept that my

reasoning is inconsistent with that in F.C. of T.

v. Knight (ante). I accept the authority of that

decision. Accordingly I hold on the evidence

before us, as I would have held but for that

decision, that the decision of the Commissioner on

the objection is in all respects to be upheld."

32. In Case U106 87 ATC 642 Deputy President Todd agreed with the reasoning of Senior Member Roach but unlike him, considered that he was not bound to follow the decision of Kelly J. and declined to do so. In paragraph 8 of his decision he said:

"8. I confess to having the most profound

difficulty, with the greatest respect, with

Knight's Case. It seems to me to be very difficult

indeed, accepting that that part of the benefits to

which the applicant became entitled represented by

pension constituted an annuity, to find that he as

a contributor to the Scheme purchased that annuity.

The 1976 Act, pursuant to which the pension/annuity

was received, stipulated for a complex of

entitlements, made up partly of pension, and partly

of commutable pension, with those entitlements

flowing because of the applicant's rights under a

statute which imposed upon him the duty to make

contributions but in turn conferred upon him those

and other possible forms of entitlement depending

upon what circumstances occurred."

33. This view was reiterated by Deputy President Todd in a later decision Case U114, 87 ATC 682. Both because I agree with the reasoning in all three decisions, and because it is desirable to preserve a consistency of approach from members of this Tribunal, I propose to follow the arguments and conclusions reached. I further agree with Deputy President Todd, with all respect to the reasoning of Kelly J., that I should not follow the decision at first instance in Knight.

34. Furthermore, I have independently arrived at the same conclusions as my colleagues, by a consideration of the nature of the payments and a consideration of the concept of a purchase price.

35. A public servant is obliged to become a member of the Fund and to contribute to it a fixed percentage of his salary. The benefits payable in respect of his membership will vary from one member to another. It is possible to visualise two persons joining the Fund on the same day, having vastly different benefits payable in respect of their membership when they retire some 40 years later. One may remain unmarried, the other may marry several times and have a large number of children. Benefits are not related to contributions.

36. Furthermore, contributions are not related to benefits. They are fixed by reference to a percentage of the member's salary. The provision of benefits is not "funded" in the actuarial sense. I can imagine that a Life Insurance Company, a vendor of annuities in the market, would not be easily persuaded to sell such a policy in consideration of a percentage of the buyer's salary. Benefits are simply paid by Consolidated Revenue except for those contributions made by the member which are represented by equivalent payments from a separate fund. A member is not given an opportunity of joining some other superannuation fund. There is the one fund, charging the one rate of contributions and providing the one range of benefits. There are, therefore, no market forces involved in determining what those benefits should be or what those rates should be. In these circumstances it seems to me impossible as a matter of commercial practice or ordinary usage to view instalments as other than statutory obligations ensuring the receipt of statutory rights.

37. It makes no difference, in my view, whether the contributions are viewed either at the commencement of the applicant's employment and membership, or at his retirement. They lack the quality of emption related payments whenever considered. It cannot be said that the applicant is purchasing a valuable chose in action which he can sell or mortgage or otherwise dispose of. Indeed, such transactions are prohibited by section 118 of the Superannuation Act. Neither is the applicant able to deal with the rights which he acquires by disposition under his Will. The basket of benefits is to be administered exclusively in accordance with the terms of the statute and not in accordance with any proprietary rights that the applicant might have acquired.

38. By Act No. 47 of 1984 a new code was introduced into the Act to deal with payments on termination of services. For the first time, the Act defined annuity. True it is that the definition is by way of inclusion rather than by specification. In section 27H(4) annuity is defined to include a superannuation pension. The fact that such a definition was thought to be necessary can be viewed in two ways. The applicant would say that it was simply declaratory of the previous law. In my view, the opposite is indicated. Because it has been held on so many occasions, after full argument that an annuity and a pension were mutually exclusive, it was necessary to amend the law so as to establish a legislative intention. Special provisions are included in the legislation to deal with the method of taxing annuities and superannuation pensions. It seems to me that prior to the introduction of this legislation, the weight of authority would appear to be in favour of the views to which I have referred. The old law continues to govern the taxability of annuities that commenced prior to the amendment.

39. The problem before the appellant in Knight was to demonstrate a credible method of calculating the undeducted purchase price of the annuity. In the present proceedings, a number of bases were put before me. Evidence was given of a most general kind by an actuary and an accountant. Neither put forward any particular detailed valuation of the applicant's undeducted purchase price. Both merely said that such a calculation was theoretically possible. In view of the conclusions that I have reached it is not necessary to examine their evidence or the submissions on this aspect put on behalf of the applicant. In so far as the objection decision refers to the taxability of the superannuation pension payments, it is confirmed.

40. I turn now to a consideration of the other aspect of the applicant's claim, namely the taxation treatment of the lump sums received by or in reference to his retirement.

41. By the conclusion of these proceedings, the taxability of the lump sum paid to the applicant by the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund had been settled. It was agreed that the amount was $39,892 (not the amount originally alleged in error by the respondent). It was further agreed that it had been paid in a lump sum and that consequently $1,995, being 5% of the amount paid, was properly included in the applicant's assessable income in the year ended 30th June 1981. Accordingly, in so far as the objection decision confirmed the correctness of this part of the assessment, it is affirmed.

42. The real dispute related to an amount of $21,262.64 net paid on 22 August 1980 and a further amount of $1,621.96 paid one year later on 5 August 1981. In both cases payment was made by the applicant's employer and represented monies in lieu of salary, annual leave and, principally, long service leave. The reason for the second payment arose because of the arbitration proceedings referred to in the above statement of facts and the retrospective operation of the Commission's decision.

43. It was the respondent's argument that the 1980 payment fulfilled the lump sum test and was, in fact, a final payment in respect of those entitlements at the time it was made. It was argued that the fact that there was an application before the Commission to consider an adjustment to the rates of pay of Commonwealth public servants did not detract from the finality of that payment at the time that it was made. A distinction was drawn between the payment made to the applicant in 1980 and the interim payment made to the appellant in Knight. The latter was only a partial payment of the capital value of an allowance paid under a specific statutory provision permitting an interim payment. It was argued that in contrast to the circumstances applying in Knight, the applicant's employer did not know and could not know at the date of the 1980 payment whether any of its employees would be entitled to any payments as a result of the Commission's decision. Even if a decision favourable to employees was anticipated, it was not possible at the date of the applicant's retirement to be certain that any adjustment would be retrospectively effective and that it would therefore apply to the applicant. Accordingly it was argued that the 1980 payment was, in itself, paid in a lump sum and should be considered separately from the 1981 payment. It is clear that both payments would fall for consideration under sections 26AC and 26AD. If not taxable under the provisions of those sections, they would fall for consideration under section 26(d). In the case of all three sections the same considerations apply. In order to come within the terms of the sections, or any of them, it must be established that the payments were made in a lump sum.

44. The respondent then went on to argue that the 1981 payment was merely an addition or enlargement of the 1980 payment and as such it had the same character as the amount to which it was added. For that reason it was argued that the second payment should be looked at as a second lump sum, and should be taxed accordingly.

45. It seems to me, however, that this is the very argument that was rejected in Knight. There is no doubt that I am bound by the decision of the majority of a Full Bench of the Federal Court. In that case their Honours appeared to have no difficulty in rejecting the argument that payment of an instalment was "not itself a separate and distinct allowance to which he was entitled or which was paid to him in consequence of retirement on termination of employment". At page 4791 they went on to say:

"The requirement that to fall within sec. 26(d), and

thereby qualify to be taxed on 5% only, the capital

amount of the allowance must be "paid in a lump

sum", is a requirement which has been in the income

tax legislation for a long time. The phrase "lump

sum" may be used to refer to a composite total sum

comprising different parts. In this sense "lump

sum" refers to the nature of the sum.

It may also be used in the phrase "paid in a lump

sum" in order to distinguish an amount paid by

instalments or successive payments. In this sense

"lump sum" refers not to the nature of the sum but

to the manner of payment.

Using the words in the former sense it would be

appropriate to speak of "a lump sum payable by

instalments"; using the words in the latter sense

it would be inappropriate to do so. In sec. 26(d)

the phrase is used in the latter sense as referring

to the manner of payment."

46. As counsel for the applicant pointed out, the payments in 1980 and 1981 were made in lieu of the same periods of accumulated annual leave and long service leave. The payments made in August 1980 were the amounts properly payable according to the law in force at the time of payment. The additional payments on 5 August 1981 were made on account of the same leave entitlements but gave effect to salary increases granted after August 1980 with retrospective affect.

47. In view of the reasoning of Bowen C.J. and Blackburn J. (the majority in Knight), it seems clear to me that I must hold that neither the first nor the second payment was paid in a lump sum and furthermore the two amounts cannot be notionally added together and treated as a lump sum paid in instalments.

48. How then are the payments to be treated for taxation purposes? The applicant argued that 5% of the two amounts could not be regarded as taxable under the terms of section 26(e) as it did not represent "the value to the taxpayer" of any allowance within the meaning of that section. I certainly agree with this proposition. I cannot agree, however, that it follows (as counsel for the applicant submitted) that the monies were therefore tax free receipts of a capital nature. They are clearly in the nature of income being related to rewards for services rendered, being calculated by reference to salary payable at different rates at the relevant time, and representing as they do industrial benefits payable to the applicant under an award governing terms and conditions of employment. If the monies were not taxable under any specific paragraph of section 26, I would consider them taxable in the ordinary way as monies in the nature of income and having all the characteristics of income. However, it seems to me that it is not necessary to resort to the general meaning of income when the terms of 26(e) are so specific.

49. The whole of the amounts paid in respect of leave in 1980 and 1981 seem to me to be benefits "given or granted to him in respect of, or for or in relation directly or indirectly to, any employment . . .". Such a possibility was adverted to by their Honours in Knight. As they were concerned only with the issues of law and the correctness, or otherwise from a legal point of view, of the decision then under appeal, it was not necessary to their decision to resolve that question. However, in my case I am reviewing the decision of a primary decision-maker and am obliged to give what I believe to be the correct or preferable decision, having regard to the facts and law as I understand them. In the present case it seems to me that the whole of the sums paid in 1980 and 1981 that are the subject of this part of the applicant's claim are taxable pursuant to the terms of section 26(e). To find otherwise would result in what their Honours referred to in Knight as "a capricious result so far as the Commissioner of Taxation is concerned". Although the Commissioner's case was restricted to the submission that both payments constituted separate lump sums and that, therefore, only 5% of the relevant amounts fell to tax, it seems to me that I must go further in rejecting that submission and hold that the whole amount, the subject of this part of the review, is subject to tax pursuant to the terms of section 26(e).

50. Accordingly in so far as the objection decision disallows a claim for an undeducted portion of the purchase price of an annuity, which the applicant claims to have received, it is affirmed. In so far as the objection decision relates to aggregate sums paid to the applicant by the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund, it is set aside and the matter is remitted to the respondent with the direction that $1,995 being 5% of $39,892 paid by that Fund was properly included in the assessable income of the applicant in the year of income ended 30th June 1981. In so far as the objection decision relates to the aggregate amounts paid to the applicant by his employer in respect of other leave entitlements, the decision is set aside and the matter is remitted to the respondent with the direction that the whole of the amount in question forms part of the assessable income of the applicant in the respective years of income in which each aggregate sum was received.
