REASONS FOR DECISION



22 June 1990 Deputy President R.K. Todd



1. These applications for review involved evidence, particularly documentary evidence, of enormous detail and complexity. Essentially, the case involved a re-evocation of F.C. of T. v. Westraders Pty Ltd, 80 ATC 4357 ("Westraders (3)"), although the events that gave rise to it predated that decision. The facts given in evidence are old, and so are the contested assessments; much of the legislation applicable to the case is no more; and the Commissioner seeks to treat the present applicant in a manner different from that which had been applied to a number of the other associated taxpayers involved in the tax enterprise that gave rise to the dispute. The evidence given before the Tribunal was that of the witnesses whose evidence appears in the transcript, which to the end of the evidence occupied 800 pages, and that contained in the documents which were received in evidence at the hearing and marked as exhibits. The exhibits fill a quite substantial cupboard. The applicant's representatives went to extraordinarily lengths to place all documentary evidence available before the Tribunal, although the

same could not necessarily be said in relation to viva voce evidence. As the matter will in all likelihood be taken on appeal far beyond this Tribunal, and as the Tribunal's views on the law are unlikely to have any influence at all, I shall

attempt to state those views briefly, and also to state my findings of fact as tersely as possible in accordance with the degree of detail indicated by written submissions of the parties as being necessary for the making of such findings.



2. I have been much assisted by the work done by Mr Searle of counsel who appeared for the applicant, and by Mr Maxwell of counsel who appeared for the respondent Commissioner. I note in particular Mr Maxwell's work in responding to the Tribunal's request that the parties indicate to the Tribunal the findings of fact that it was submitted should be made by the Tribunal, and Mr Searle's frank and constructive response (Ex.1C) to what Mr Maxwell submitted in writing as the findings that should be made. Such suggested findings were submitted by both before the hearing commenced (Ex.1B) and on the last day thereof (Ex.1E). I have relied on both. Especially in relation to the more basic facts contained in Ex.1B I have been able to rely on the fact that the applicant admitted those facts. In some cases the finding suggested in the material submitted has been omitted because it is dealt with elsewhere. I believe that the course which I have adopted is consistent with what was said in Ansett Transport Industries (Operations) Pty Ltd v Wraith [1983] FCA 179; (1983) 48 ALR 500 at 507. Despite the difficulties involved in so doing I have attempted to use anonyms to conceal the identity of the persons, companies and partnerships involved. A separate key to most of these anonyms will be supplied to the parties only. There are likewise two explanatory tables that will be supplied to the parties only. These Reasons for Decision will not be published

until the time for appeal has expired in order to give an opportunity for any objection to publication to be made.



3. The relevant provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the ITAA"), as applicable in the years of income in question, are ss.36 and 36A, and 260. I set out only as much of those provisions as is necessary:



"s.36(1). Subject to this section, where -



(a) a taxpayer disposes by sale, gift, or otherwise of property being trading stock,

standing or growing crops, cropstools, or trees which have been planted and tended for the purpose of sale;



(b) that property constitutes or constituted the whole or part of the assets of a business which is or was carried on by the taxpayer; and



(c) the disposal was not in the ordinary course of carrying on that business,



the value of that property shall be included in the assessable income of the taxpayer, and the person acquiring that property shall be deemed to have purchased it at a price equal to that value.



36AAA ...

36AA ...



36A(1) Where, for any reason, including -



(a) the formation or dissolution of a partnership; or



(b) a variation in the constitution of a partnership, or in the interests of the partners,



a change has occurred in the ownership of, or in the interests of persons in, property constituting the whole or part of the assets of a business, and being trading stock, standing or growing crops, crop-stools, or trees which have been planted and tended for the purpose of sale, and the person, or one or more of the persons, who owned the property before the change has or have an interest in the property after the change, section thirty-six of this Act applies as if the person or persons who owned the property before the change had, on the day on which the change occurred, disposed of the whole of the property to the person, or all the persons, by whom the property is owned after the change.



(2) Where -



(a) property in relation to which the last preceding sub-section applies has become, upon the change in ownership or interests, an asset of a business carried on by the person or persons by whom the property is owned after the change;



(b) the person or persons by whom the property was owned before the change holds or hold, after the change, an interest or interests in

the property of a value equal to not less than one-quarter of the value of the property; and



(c) the person or persons by whom the property was owned before the change together with the person or persons by whom the property is owned after the change give notice to the Commissioner, in accordance with this section, that they have agreed that this sub-section shall apply in respect of the property,



the value fo the property, for the purposes of section thirty-six of this Act, shall be, instead of the value specified in paragraph (a) of sub-section (8) of that section, the value (if any) that would have been taken into account at the end of the year of income if no disposal had taken place and the year of income had ended on the date of the change.



(3) A notice in pursuance of the last preceding sub-section shall be in writing, signed by all the persons giving it, and lodged with the Commissioner on or before the thirty-first day of August next succeeding the end of the financial year in which the change in ownership or interests occurred or on or before such later date as the Commissioner determines."



"s.260 Every contract, agreement, or arrangement made or entered into, orally or in writing, whether before or after the commencement of this Act, shall so far as it has or purports to have the purpose or effect of in any way, directly or indirectly -



(a) altering the incidence of any income tax;



(b) relieving any person from liability to pay any income tax or make any return;



(c) defeating, evading, or avoiding any duty or liability imposed on any person by this Act; or



(d) preventing the operation of this Act in any respect,



be absolutely void, as against the Commissioner, or in regard to any proceeding under this Act, but without prejudice to such validity as it may have in any other respect or for any other purpose."



4. There are two years of income in question, namely those ended 30 June 1976 and 30 June 1977. I shall call them the 1976 year and the 1977 year.



The 1976 Year



A. Background

(i) In his income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1976, the applicant disclosed a taxable income of $9,406.00.

(ii) The applicant stated his occupation as being public accountant, grazier and share trader.

(iii) In his 1976 income tax return, the applicant disclosed $46,965.00 net income from his business as a public accountant. In addition to this amount, he included as income an amount of $352.00 from rents received, $2,043.00 from dividends, $1,086.00 interest, $376.00 allowance and director's fees of $400.00.

(iv) The applicant's income was offset by an amount of $40,137.00 being a loss claimed in respect of the L1 Partnership in which the applicant was a partner.

(v) On 14 March 1977 the respondent issued a Notice of Assessment No. 203093/005 to the applicant based on a taxable income of $49,445.00.

(vi) The adjustment sheet accompanying the notice of assessment advised that the loss of $40,137.00 had been disallowed and the amount of $40,137.00 added to the taxable income as returned. There was also a deduction of $98.00 in respect of an arithmetical error.

(vii) By notice dated 17 March 1977, the applicant objected to the notice of assessment. The notice of objection claimed that the assessment was excessive and should be

reduced by the allowance of the deduction of $40,137.00 in respect of the applicant's share of the loss in the L1 partnership.

(viii) In September 1985 the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, Brisbane, advised the applicant that the objection had been disallowed.

(ix) In September 1985 the applicant requested that the decision on the objection be referred to a Board of Review for review.

(x) The delay in determining the applicant's objections was said by the Commissioner to be due to the Commissioner's investigation into sharetrading partnerships which followed from the decision in Westraders Pty Ltd v. F.C. of T. 77 ATC 4444 ("Westraders (1)").



I make no findings in relation to this assertion as I consider it to be of no relevance.



B. The L1 Partnership ("the partnership")



(xi) In the Commissioner's original suggested findings of fact it was asserted that the partnership was established by agreement dated 27 May 1976, and that the partnership commenced 35 days prior to the end of the 1976 financial year. Prior to the commencement of the hearing the applicant agreed with this assertion. On the second last day of a hearing that occupied almost a fortnight counsel for the Commissioner withdrew the assertion, one that had apparently been maintained or accepted through the long years of administration of this matter. The partnership agreements were tendered in evidence, there was evidence that the partnership was entered into and I reject the Commissioner's submission

that there was no partnership. I agree with and adopt the arguments of Mr Searle on p.1025 of the transcript.

(xii) The partnership was one of a number of partnerships of a similar kind promoted by G and C1. The respondent contended that the Tribunal should find that the partnership was formed with a view to generating a tax loss and providing tax deductions to the members of the partnership. The applicant contended that the allegation that the partnership was one of a number of partnerships of a similar kind was not relevant to the present issue. If such "similar" partnerships were relevant, a number of partners have had their losses allowed in accordance with the decision in Westraders (3). In my opinion the suggested finding of fact is not relevant to the issues before the Tribunal, and I make no finding in relation thereto.



(xiii) The respondent contended that the partnership aimed to incur a tax loss in excess of $1 million, having regard to the various tax deductions which each of the partners required to offset their income.



The G-C1 partnerships operated on the basis of taxpayers contributing in cash to the partnership an amount equal to 1 per cent of their required tax loss. The applicant denied this proposed finding in its entirety, but I find the facts to be as asserted by the respondent.



(xiv) In the income tax return for the partnership for the year ended 30 June 1976, the partnership returned a tax loss of $1,248,814.00.



(xv) The 1976 partnership return stated that the business of the partnership was share trading. The return was signed by C1 and the partnership address for service was shown as C/- C1, G.P.O. Box ..., Melbourne, Victoria, 3000.

(xvi) The management committee of the partnership comprised C1, T and the applicant.

(xvii) The partnership deed records that there were twenty partners, eighteen individuals and two companies, one of which was a company named L2 Pty Ltd ("L2").



(xviii) The second schedule to the partnership deed set out the respective interests of the individual members in the partnership and their capital contributions. The initial capital of the partnership was stated as being $873,726.00, contributed by L2 in the form of shares and by the other partners in various amounts of cash.

(xix) L2 contributed as capital to the partnership shares in 6 public companies and 18 private companies, equal to 26 per cent of the total capital of the partnership. The shares contributed to the partnership by L2 are set out in the second schedule to the partnership deed.

(xx) Of the capital to be provided by each partner (except L2) only an amount equal to 1 per cent of the required tax loss was paid in cash. The remaining cash capital was advanced by a company called P Pty Ltd by means of unsecured loans to each partner for a 6 months term at an interest rate of 10 per cent. P Pty Ltd in turn borrowed back from the partnership after its formation the total amount lent by it to the partners, for the same term and at the same interest rate.

These allegations were denied by the applicant, but I find the facts to be as asserted by the respondent.



(xxi) P Pty Ltd was a company controlled by the promoters of the partnership, G and C1. The company was incorporated as a "shelf company" in Victoria on 26 November 1975. On 6 April 1976, the original directors resigned and G

and his associate C2 were appointed directors. G and C1 each acquired 1 share in P Pty. Ltd.

(xxii) The Second Schedule to the partnership deed records that the applicant held a 3.214 per cent interest in the partnership and contributed $28,085.00 as capital.



(xxiii) The applicant paid in cash $400.00. The respondent contended that this indicated that a $40,000.00 tax loss was sought by the applicant, and I so find. The remaining cash capital being $27,685.00 ($28,085.00 less $400.00) was lent to the applicant by P Pty Ltd on 27 May 1976, the day on which the partnership was formed.



These contentions were admitted by the applicant, save that he did not admit that by paying $400 in cash any inference or indication could be drawn other than that the applicant paid in $400. I need make no finding of fact as to this suggested limitation on the Commissioner's assertions as it is argumentative in nature.



(xxiv) In its 1976 return, the partnership disclosed a profit on share trading activity of $13,566.35 and interest of $5,918.65 (a total of $19,485.00).

(xxv) The partners gave notice to the respondent that they had agreed that s.36A(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the ITAA") would apply in respect of the shares contributed by L2, and accordingly the deemed cost of those shares to the partnership was $1,495,529.00.

(xxvi) In reliance on s.36A the partnership claimed a tax loss of $1,248,814.00. The applicant's portion of the tax loss was $40,136.88, being 3.214 per cent of the partnership loss.

(c) The Purported Trading in Private Company Shares



(xxxv) The private company shares held by the partnership were all contributed by L2. The contribution by L2 and disposal by the partnership of the shares in one such company, UBH, was offered as a representative example of the use made of private company shares in the s.36A(2) scheme, and I accept that it is proper to proceed, for

(xliii) After acquiring the shares in UBH, L2 proceeded to "strip" UBH of its assets before introducing the UBH shares into the partnership.



(xliv) The steps in the "strip" are set out in Table B to these Reasons and were as follows:

(a) On 24 May 1976 S0O paid $700 to L2 (to enable L2 to purchase "B" class shares in each of the companies which it acquired).

The applicant agreed with this proposed finding, but said that it was unaware of the purpose of the payment of $700 to L2. I find that the purpose was as contended for by the respondent.



(b) On the same day the directors of UBH resolved that 50 "B" class shares of $2.00 each be allotted to L2. L2 was allotted the "B" class shares and paid $100.00 to UBH for the shares.



(c) UBH in turn "passed" $100.00 to S0O.



(d) S0O returned $20,228.42 to UBH.



(e) On 25 May 1976 the directors of UBH resolved that a dividend of $20,228.42 be paid to the holders of the "B" class shares and accordingly a dividend of $20,229.00 was paid to L2 being the sole "B" class shareholder.



(f) L2 repaid $20,229.00 to S0O.



The assets of UBH now consisted only of its paid up share capital of $6,926.00 (3413 ordinary shares of $2 each fully paid and 50 "B" class shares of $2 each).



(xlv) It is proper to state here that the applicant in answer contended that the sense in which the respondent used the expression "strip" was not understood. The applicant's contention was that L2 paid $28,754 for shares in UBH which in turn had a cash value of $27,054.42. The applicant agreed that after L2 acquired the shares in UBH the directors of UBH resolved that a dividend of $20,229 be paid to L2 being the sole B class

shareholder. Thus the holding company, L2, was able to ensure that it received dividends totalling $20,229 from its newly acquired subsidiary UBH. The applicant challenged the view that if a subsidiary company paid dividends to its holding company this was a "strip" of a subsidiary company. The applicant agreed that the subsidiary company, being UBH, paid to L2 dividends of $20,229. The applicant's contention was that the payment of dividends by a subsidiary company to L2 was part of the methodology adopted by L2 to ensure that it made a profit from the acquisition of the shares in the subsidiary. The applicant's contention was that at all relevant times it was the intention of L2 to derive a profit as a result of its acquisition of shares in a subsidiary such as UBH. The profit could be realized either by the sale of shares at a profit, in this case for an amount in excess of the purchase price of the shares being $26,754, or alternatively by the receipt of both dividends from the subsidiary company and sale proceeds from the sale of the shares in the subsidiary

company after the subsidiary being UBH had paid dividends to the holding company being L2.



(xlvi) The 3,413 ordinary shares held by L2 were contributed to the partnership on 27 May 1976 at a deemed cost of $26,754.00 and were sold by the partnership for $6,143.00. On 30 June 1976 the 3,413 ordinary shares in UBH were held by 15th A Pty Ltd.



The 1977 Year



A. Background



(i) In his income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1977, the applicant disclosed a taxable income of $10,518.00.

(ii) The applicant stated his occupation as being public accountant, grazier and share trader.

(iii) In his 1977 income tax return, the applicant disclosed $47,297.00 net income from his business as a public accountant. In addition to this amount, he included as income an amount of $697.00 from rents received, $214.00 interest, $1,945.00 from dividends, director's fees of $505.00 and an allowance of $384.00.

(iv) The applicant's income was offset by an amount of $40,038.00 being a loss claimed in respect of the Apart Trading Co. Partnership ("the Apart partnership"), in which the applicant was a partner.



(v) On 18 May 1978 the respondent issued a Notice of Assessment No. 247067/042 to the applicant based on a taxable income of $50,556.00.

(vi) The adjustment sheet accompanying the notice of assessment advised that the loss of $40,038.00 had been disallowed and the amount of $40,038.00 added to the taxable income as returned.

(vii) By notice dated 24 May 1978, the applicant objected to the notice of assessment. The notice of objection claimed that the assessment was excessive and should be reduced by allowance of a deduction of $40,038.00 in respect of the applicant's share of loss in the Apart partnership.



(viii) By letter dated 16 September 1985, the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, Brisbane, advised the applicant that the objection had been disallowed.

(ix) By letter dated 18 September 1985, the applicant requested that the decision on the objection be referred to the Board of Review for review.



B. The Apart Partnership



(x) The Apart partnership was established by agreement dated 24 November 1976.



(xi) The Apart partnership was one of a number of partnerships of a similar kind promoted by G and C1. The respondent contended that the Apart partnership was formed with a view to generating a tax loss and providing tax deductions to the members of the Apart partnership.



The statements and findings made in paragraph 4B(xi) above apply here, mutatis mutandis.



The applicant denied these contentions. I find the facts to be as claimed by the respondent. Their relevance and weight is left for discussion below.



(xii) The respondent contended that the partnership aimed to incur a loss in excess of $1 million, having regard to the various tax deductions which each of the partners required.



The applicant denied these contentions. I find the facts to be as claimed by the respondent. Their relevance and weight is again left for discussion below.

6. Although I have declined to make a number of findings as sought by the Commissioner in relation to particular matters, a further finding of fact of a broader kind should be made. It is in my opinion clear beyond measure that the primary and fundamental purpose of the enterprises before the Tribunal in this case was the tax effectiveness of the arrangements. The tax benefits sought were in my opinion not merely incidental to, but were at the heart of, the transactions described. If any doubt should be entertained about that it is necessary only to refer to Ex.S, which consisted of three Memoranda and an Advice that have been referred to above from eminent counsel in the field of taxation. It goes without saying, but nevertheless should be said, that counsel obviously considered that what they were advising was lawful and involved no impropriety. The Memoranda and the Advice however set out step by step, and in detail, the

steps that needed to be taken, being the steps that were in fact taken and have been referred to above. The first Memorandum commenced by stating that the proposal outlined in counsel's brief sought to take advantage of the provisions of ss.36(1) and 36A(1), (2) and (3) of the ITAA, and set out those provisions. It then continued:



"2. What is proposed is a scheme whereby my client will enter into a partnership with (L2), a company which on my instructions has a large parcel of public and private company shares whose market value is substantially less than the value which L2 has adopted for the purpose of the trading stock provisions of the Act. Section 31 provides that 'the value of each article of trading stock (not being livestock) to be taken into account at the end of the year of income shall be, at the option of the taxpayer, its cost price or market selling value or the price at which it can be replaced.' (L2) has valued these particular items at cost price. It is proposed that my client and others will enter into a partnership with (L2) in which (L2) will have a 25% share, that the partnership will purchase the portfolio of shares held by (L2), that a notice will be given pursuant to sub-section 36A(2) (so that the stock is to be deemed to be taken over not at market value but at the value which it would have had for the purposes of the trading stock provisions of the Act if it had remained in (L2's) sole ownership) that the partnership will re-sell the shares at market value and that the resultant loss for taxation purposes thrown up by the disposal of the shares at a figure less than their book value will be available to each of the partners as a taxation loss in proportion to their interests in the partnership. Section 92(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act provides that "The assessable income of a partner shall include his individual interest in the net income of the partnership for the year of income,

and his individual interest in a partnership loss incurred in the year of income shall be an allowable deduction".



3. I may say at once that I doubt that the transaction will be struck down by the provisions of section 260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act which permits the Commissioner to avoid transactions which are in substance transactions entered into for the purpose of avoiding income tax. The merit of the proposal is that it takes advantage of a particular provision of the Act and the application of the provisions of section 260 would have the effect of overriding the effect of that provision. In the past the courts have not

applied the provisions of section 260 in such a manner. I do not mean to say that the Commissioner may not seek to apply the provisions of section 260 in the first instance or that a Judge could not find substantial arguments for upholding the Commissioner's stand. However, I think that the High Court would have to undertake a new approach to the provisions of section 260 if it were to permit the scheme to be avoided on that ground."



7. The Memorandum, seemingly blessed with the gift of prophecy, then turned to a discussion of what were referred to as crucial elements of the scheme. All that followed in the Memoranda and Advice was virtually a brief for anyone arguing the case for the present applicant, save that being written in March, May and November 1976 it predated, amongst other authorities, Westraders (1) at first instance in the Supreme Court of New South Wales judgment in which was handed down 13 October 1977.



8. The other feature of the matter is that it is true that a number of persons who could have shed light on central issues were not called. Mr Maxwell's argument on this is set out on pp.955-960 of the transcript and I have taken his submission into account.



9. Taking into account the whole of the evidence before the Tribunal, including in particular the Memoranda and Advice, which were relied upon and the advice given therein accepted and acted upon, it is abundantly clear that I should find, as I do, that the scheme or schemes, for such they must inevitably be called, were put into effect for the purpose of minimising tax. To the extent that the applicant denied this in his evidence I reject it. I do not say that his evidence was not honestly given, but rather that accepting that he believed it to be true, I simply do not find it credible. After so many years he may well have persuaded himself of the truth of his evidence as to the commercial intent of what was done, but he was well aware and in detail of counsel's advice. It is quite another question however whether the findings set out in paragraph 6 above can have any operative effect in law.

10. Having effectively made findings of fact that are in large measure those which I was asked to make by the Commissioner, where does that leave the matter? I shall attempt to summarise Mr Maxwell's argument.



11. Broadly speaking, Mr Maxwell's address dealt, apart from the question of the findings of fact that he contended should be made, with the applicability of s.36A and s.260 of the ITAA as those provisions then stood, and with the applicant's claim of inconsistency of administration in the matter. The latter was the subject of evidence, and the Tribunal noted the very careful and highly competent treatment of the matter in evidence given by an officer of the Australian Taxation Office that dealt with the end-points of that administration. Her evidence is set out on pp.539-562 and 576-608 of the transcript. Having regard however to the conclusions to which I have come in relation to s.36A and s.260 it proves unnecessary to canvass that issue.



12. In his final submission Mr Maxwell for the Commissioner fleshed out submissions that had been made in writing prior to the commencement of the hearing. Those submisisons were:



(i) The establishment of the partnership, the contribution of shares to the partnership by L2 ("the scheme shares"), the purported share trading, the stripping of value from the scheme shares, and the disposal of the scheme shares, constituted a tax avoidance scheme designed to exploit the provisions of s.36 and s.36A of the Act to obtain tax benefits for the taxpayer - members of the partnerships. It was said to be one of many similar schemes, but I consider that particular assertion to be of no relevance.



(ii) The scheme in the present case was structured similarly to, and for the same purpose as, the scheme upheld by the High Court in Westraders (3). The scheme in the present case was however

distinguishable, it was contended, from the Westraders scheme on the facts. Specifically, the partnership in the present case was not entitled to make an election under s.36A(2) of the Act because:-



(a) the scheme shares did not constitute trading stock or assets of a business of L2 at the time L2 disposed of them to the partnership, in particular:-

. L2 was not at the relevant time carrying on business as a share trader;

. the scheme shares were not trading stock;

. the scheme shares were not assets of a business of L2.



(b) the scheme shares did not become, after their disposal to the partnership, an asset of a business of share trading carried on, as claimed, by the partnership. The partnership was not at the relevant time carrying on any such business, or, if it was, the scheme shares were not an asset of that business.



(iii) Further or alternatively amounts returned by the partnership as the proceeds of the sales of shares (including the scheme shares) were not assessable income in accordance with sub-section 25(1) of the ITAA. The amounts claimed in respect of purchases of shares by the partnership were not allowable deductions in accordance with sub-section 51(1) and (2) of the ITAA.



13. As to (i) above, it seems to me quite clear that the contentions therein contained beg the question that, in view of the authorities, and especially the authority of Westraders (3), the Tribunal has to decide. It seems to me to be nothing to the point to allege successfully, as the respondent has done,

that what occurred was "a tax avoidance scheme". If it were enough for the Commissioner to allege that, there would be little difficulty in deciding this or many other cases. Mr Searle's argument on this was that the Commissioner's allegations were totally contrary to the evidence of MJS and LH. Their evidence was that they were in the business of acquiring the kinds of companies that had been involved. The group which controlled both made profits from the acquisition of companies pregnant with profits, usually accumulated profits and they were able to take advantage of Investment and Merchant Finance Corporation Ltd v. F.C. of T. 71 ATC 4140 ("IMFC") and Patcorp Investments Ltd and Ors. v. F.C. of T. 76 ATC 4225 ("Patcorp") in being able to declare dividends out of those companies. But it is enough in my opinion to say that the allegations in contention (i), even if accepted, do not answer any question that I have to answer in the resolution of the case.



14. As to the contention set out in paragraph 12(ii) above, I deal with its opening passage first. The fact is, as Mr Searle pointed out, that Westraders (3) was handed down four years after the transactions presently under consideration. The provisions of s.36A of the ITAA were available in the year ended 30 June 1976 to all taxpayers. The scheme in the present case was structured similarly to that in Westraders (3) and was, proleptically, entered into for the same purpose. The harder question is whether Westraders (3) is distinguishable and this involves, in the first instance, the question whether the Commissioner's contentions in (a) and (b) are correct. I find that they are not, and I so find having regard to the evidence as a whole and to the very large share acquisitions involved.



15. As to the contentions in paragraph 12(ii)(a) above, which involve questions of law or of mixed fact and law, I say first that it is not in my opinion open to me on the whole of the evidence to find that L2 was not at the relevant time carrying on business as a share trader. At all events I do so find that it was. It is noteworthy that the Commissioner had always so treated it. I agree with Mr Searle, as I understand his

argument, that in truth the contention is based essentially upon the respondent's regret for what was decided in IMFC and in Patcorp.



16. Further, as to the contentions of the respondent in paragraph 12(ii)(a) above, they again constitute questions of law or of mixed fact and law. I find that the scheme shares did constitute trading stock and assets of a business of share trading carried on by L2, and I so find in particular having regard to the very large share acquisitions involved. This effectively answers also the proposition contained in paragraph 12(ii)(b) above, for I consider that it must follow that amounts returned by the partnership as the proceeds of the sales of shares are assessable income in accordance with s.25(1) of the ITAA. The amounts claimed in respect of purchases of shares by the partnership are allowable deductions in accordance with s.51(1) and (2) of the ITAA.



17. It follows that I consider that the Commissioner's case fails in respect of the contentions advanced in respect of s.36A. This brings me to what must be seen as the real point in the case, that is to say the applicability or otherwise in the present case of the decision of the High Court in Westraders (3). This point was elaborately and ably argued before the Tribunal. In his address in reply Mr Searle put the point very directly, contending that the Commissioner was asking the Tribunal to act as if it were the Full High Court, asking that the Tribunal find that Westraders (3) was wrongly decided, or was

not binding because the High Court had not considered the applicability of s.260 in Westraders (3) and that if it had so considered that provision the case would have been decided differently. I consider that this was an accurate summary of what the respondent was asking the Tribunal to do.



18. I shall pause at this point to deal with the question of the argument surrounding the fact that s.260 was not dealt with in Westraders (3). I have already noted that the remarks of senior counsel in paragraph 3 of the first Memorandum contained

in Ex.S were prophetic as to the outcome of an attempt to have s.260 applied in what later came to be a Westraders' type of case. But what has to be emphasised is that in Westraders' the Commissioner had abandoned any reliance on s.260, that abandonment having occurred not before the High Court, but before the Federal Court (see F.C. of T. v. Westraders Pty Ltd 79 ATC 4089 ("Westraders (2)")) on the hearing of the appeal from Rath J at first instance in the Supreme Court of New South Wales. A reading of Rath J's judgment after the hearing of the present case gives a strong sense of deja vu. Having referred on pp.4460-4461 to the written submission tendered by the Commissioner (some of which was said to be "in the language of denigration") his Honour said at p.4461 of the report in 77 ATC:



"Even accepting the correctness of the presentation of the facts in this submission, it cannot prevail. Stripped to its essentials, the submission is that the appellant availed itself of a deduction permissible under the Act, and that sec.260 applied for no other reason than the appellant's intention of so organizing its affairs as to avoid the incidence of tax. It is true that in doing so, the appellant had to enter into an arrangement with Jensen; but that in itself does not constitute an arrangement within the ambit of sec.260. Nor is the arrangement brought within sec.260 by reason of the fact that it was Jensen's business to provide for reward to itself means of tax avoidance. Jensen's role was similar to that of Cadiz Corporation Pty. Ltd. in Slutzkin v. F.C. of T. (77 ATC 4076)."



Earlier on p.4460 his Honour had said:



"The Commissioner also relies upon sec.260. It was contended for the appellant that the principles stated in Mullens v. F.C. of T. (76 ATC 4288) applied to the circumstances of the present case, with the consequence that sec.260 had no application. This was, it was said, a case where there was a provision in the Act entitling the taxpayer to a deduction, and sec.260 could not be applied to strike down such a deduction. No argument was presented to the Court on behalf of the Commissioner that Mullen's case (above) was in any relevant sense distinguishable."



19. In these circumstances I consider it impossible for this Tribunal to come to a conclusion that had s.260 been argued in the High Court it would have been found to be applicable. Am I to suppose, with respect, that counsel in that case had somehow forgotten to argue the point in the High Court after it had been expressly abandoned below? It is not for me to say whether the point could be taken up in the Federal Court, but I can say, as I do, that I regard Westraders (3) as being, before this Tribunal, flat against the applicability of s.260.



20. Should I come to any other conclusion in the light of authorities that were referred to me that had been decided after Westraders (3)? As I see it, this effectively raises the question whether the "choice" principle has been so eroded that I should venture to find that, so far as s.260 is concerned, Westraders (3) can be effectively put on one side. For this purpose, Mr Maxwell relied on: 86 ATC 4647 ("John (1)") Yeldham J, Supreme Court of New South Wales, at p.4671, John v F.C. of T. 87 ATC 4713 ("John (2)"); and on John v F.C. of T. 89 ATC 4101 ("John (3)"). Mr Maxwell submitted that -



(a) construing the Act as a whole no room was left for doubt that the incidence of tax was intended to fall on the applicant in respect of the income of his professional practice, subject to allowable deductions, and that it was not intended that he be able to reduce that income by entering into an artificial arrangement of the kind involved in the present case;



(b) the history of s.36A(1) showed clearly that it was designed to expand rather than restrict the operation of s.36(1) as an assessing provision, and that this necessarily coloured the interpretation of provisions such as s.36A(2) which are ancillary to ss.36(1) and 36A(1); and that

(c) it was never the intention of the legislature, by the enactment of s.36A(2), to confer tax benefits of the kind claimed by the applicant in the present case, so that s.36A(2) is not, in relation to the applicant, a "specific and particular provision".



21. The linchpin of the argument put for the respondent was the decision of the High Court in F.C. of T. v. Gulland 85 ATC 4765, the ratio decidendi of Gulland being said to be found in the judgment of Dawson J. This case was said to be a watershed in respect of s.260 and the "choice" principle, entitling the Tribunal to view what was said by the High Court in 1980 in Westraders (3) about s.260 in the light of Gulland.



22. Mr Maxwell further submitted that the analysis of the judgments in Gulland by Wilcox and Pincus JJ. in F & C Donebus Pty. Ltd. v. F.C. of T. 88 ATC 4582 was correct. In a later written submission it was further submitted that the Tribunal should, as did Peter Murphy J. in Pettigrew v. F.C. of T. 89 ATC 4475 at 4484-5, regard itself as bound by the interpretation of Gulland adopted in Donebus. For similar reasons to those set out by his Honour, the Tribunal should find that -



"the artificial and deliberately contrived arrangements in which the taxpayer participated for tax avoidance purposes and certainly not for purposes of any ordinary 'business or family dealing' is struck down and rendered void by s.260(c) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 as amended.' (89 ATC at 4485)"



23. Mr Maxwell, who said everything that could possibly be said in support of the respondent's case, drew attention to the following passage in the judgment of Hill J. in Davis & Anor v. F.C. of T. 89 ATC 4377 at 4406:



"The ultimate difficulty which has to be faced and which is acknowledged by Brennan J. [in Gulland] will lie in determining whether a taxpayer is really seeking to rely upon a specific provision in the relevant sense so that that provision will ultimately prevail over the general provisions of s.260. The question of whether the provision in the relevant sense is a specific provision will

often be a difficult one and will involve construing the Act as a whole to determine where the incidence of tax was intended to fall."



  1. The following passage in the judgment of Brennan J. in Gulland (160 CLR at 78) was cited:

"The intention of the legislature in enacting specific and particular provisions which affect assessable income or add to or increase allowable deductions is that those provisions should have effect according to their tenor, so that a taxpayer who brings himself within a specific and particular provision which purports to confer a tax benefit should be entitled to have that benefit. That intention would fail if s.260 were to operate according to its literal terms, for s.260 would avoid any arrangement which brought the taxpayer within a specific and particular provision of the Act which conferred a tax benefit. It could not have been intended by the legislature that s.260 should prevail over the specific and particular provisions and destroy the tax benefits they were intended to confer."



25. Reference was also made to Stamp v F.C. of T. 88 ATC 4803 which, it was correctly submitted, had no direct application in the present case. The following passage was however cited from the judgment of Pincus J at 4808:



"Where one has a basically circular movement of money making no commercial sense, prima facie s.260 applies in my opinion, subject to the possibility of (the) application of the choice principle."



Stamp was said to affirm that Gulland was the key decision on the "choice" principle, and that the key features for which to look in a s.260 case for the purpose of displacing the "choice" principle are those which on their face are stamped with a purpose or effect of tax avoidance; with contrivance, artificiality or circularity; with no commercial justification; or with pre arrangement. In my view however the argument of the Commissioner is hopeless before this Tribunal. In John (3) in the joint judgment of Mason CJ, Wilson, Dawson, Toohey and Gaudron JJ at 4107 Westraders (3) was referred to with evident approval, along with IMFC, and Patcorp. In Gulland, Westraders (3) was, as far as I can detect, not mentioned at all. Is this

Tribunal entitled to suppose that Westraders (3) is no longer binding on it? It is my opinion that it is so binding.



26. I was also referred to the decision of Tadgell J in McLean v F.C. of T. 86 ATC 4288. But as Mr Searle pointed out, no election under s.36A could there have been made because by the time at which the relevant events occurred in that case s.36A(1)(c) had been inserted.



27. The objection decisions under review must in my opinion be set aside and the matter remitted to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the objections of the applicant be allowed.
