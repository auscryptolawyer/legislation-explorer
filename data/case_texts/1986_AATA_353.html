5793/1986 and Commissioner of Taxation [1986] AATA 353; 86 ATC 1153 (7 November 1986)

Last Updated: 6 August 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax – Allowable Deductions - traveling expenses - School-Master – visits to schools overseas not allowed attendance at interstate conference allowed

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) – s 51

FCT v Hatchett [1971] HCA 47; (1971) 125 CLR 494

Ref. No. 173/1984 84 ATC 727

Re: 5793/1986

And: Commissioner of Taxation

AAT Decision No 3004

Mr. P.M. Roach (Senior Member)

7 November 1986

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. 5793/1986

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Respondent REASONS FOR DECISION

Mr. P.M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. The applicant is a school-teacher who claims to be entitled to an income tax deduction in relation to expenses incurred while travelling overseas during his long service leave. He is the Headmaster of the Junior School of a major private school which, in common with most schools, has a commitment to excellence. That commitment is personally shared by the applicant. It finds expression in several documents emanating from the school authorities, which were exhibited in evidence during the hearing. The school encouraged its teachers to participate in a teacher-exchange scheme with other States, New Zealand and the United Kingdom. In that and in other ways the school encouraged the professional development of its staff. Some years before the period in question, the applicant had studied at Oxford, United Kingdom, for 12 months.

2. When the applicant was able to take the long service leave to which he had become entitled, he travelled overseas with his wife. Their tour took them to New Zealand, the United States of America, Canada and the United Kingdom and throughout their tour (Canada excepted) they visited and inspected schools; met with and conferred with other teachers including retired teachers; and on occasion were accommodated at schools which they visited. Some of the teachers they conferred with had previously taught at the applicant's school; others had previously attended conferences of teachers in Australia; and others were friends whom the applicant had first met when he had been a student at Oxford.

3. At little additional cost in air fares the couple were able to visit Canada but while there did not visit any schools although the applicant, while flying from Canada to the United Kingdom, sat beside a Welshman who had been teaching in Canadian schools for 20 years. They discussed the Canadian education system.

4. The applicant and his wife returned to their home via Perth and there attended the Biennial Junior Schools' Headmasters' Association Conference. The school funded the applicant's accommodation costs in attending that conference and the evidence was - and I accept it - that the school expected that the applicant should attend that conference whenever it was held unless there was a good reason for not doing so. The school made no financial contribution to travel costs in that regard or in relation to the overseas tour. On the other hand, the Conference did make a grant of $700 towards the travel costs of the overseas journey undertaken by the applicant. In accordance with an undertaking to do so, the applicant reported verbally to that Conference in Perth and later a fuller report was published in the relevant journal. The applicant did not present any formal report to the school.

5. It was accepted that the $700 related only to the travelling expenses of the applicant and only to his travel overseas. It was also accepted that $250 of the total fare would have been the portion of the total fare appropriate to the costs of travel between his home airport and Perth.

6. At all material times the applicant was enrolled as, and studying as, a student for the University Degree of Master of Education. The learning experiences arising from his tour were relevant to those studies as they were to his responsibilities as a headmaster and teacher. His degree thesis related to the development of curricula in schools and that was an area of particular interest for him in discussions held in different countries. The applicant believed his experiences overseas were more beneficial to him as a teacher than as a student. He considered that his experiences changed his thinking in some areas and resulted in some changes within the school.

7. In all, the applicant and his wife were absent from their home for 95 days and it was agreed that on 27 1/2 of those days he had some involvement in school-related activities for either all or part of the day.

8. His claim was for a deduction pursuant to s 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 in relation to one-third travel cost ($1,046.00); one-half of campervan and accommodation costs ($340.00); meals (for the applicant only) on 7 days ($140.00): in all $1,526.00.

9. I accept that the applicant is a dedicated and enthusiastic teacher who, as headmaster, is responsible for the implementation of educational policy in the Junior School while being subject to the overriding control of the headmaster of the Senior School. I also find that he is a person who finds considerable personal satisfaction in his professional work. As commonly happens in such cases, his recreational interests are in part work-related and many personal friends are to be found among professional colleagues. In those circumstances it is not surprising that when the applicant was able to find an opportunity to take his long service leave he took the opportunity to travel and in doing so to visit places of interest (schools) and meet with people (teachers) in the company of his wife, as well as being - in the words of his own published Report - "two of the thousands of tourists (visiting) historic homes, castles, cathedrals and ruined abbeys".

10. But in doing what he did, I am not pursuaded that he was then about the task of deriving his assessable income. The only obligation he was discharging was one due to the Conference which had provided him with some financial assistance to enable him to undertake a private tour. Undoubtedly the school would be well pleased that in the person of the applicant it had a headmaster so dedicated to his professional responsibilities that even when on long service leave he would continue to maintain an interest in educational matters and that that interest would in due course work to the benefit of his school. But that is not enough.

11. As Menzies J. said of a school-teacher undergoing university studies (FCT v Hatchett [1971] HCA 47; (1971) 125 CLR 494 at 499):-

"... I am not able to find any connexion between the payment of fees [to the University] and the assessable income of the taxpayer beyond the circumstance, which I take to be self-evident, that a teacher who has pursued university studies is likely to be a better teacher than if he had not done so and is therefore more likely to obtain promotion within the department."

12. I adapt to this taxpayer words used in relation to another teacher in Ref.No. 173/1984 84 ATC 727 at 732-733 where I said:-

"The expenditure in question was incurred in relation to a period of time during which he was without obligation to render service to his employer. ... He utilised a period of time available to him for recreation and private pursuits. The type of recreation which he chose was to travel, albeit to places which were of interest to him as a teacher. Notwithstanding that his experience would be of value to him and to others when he resumed performing the duties of his employment, the essentially recreational nature of his journey did not alter. That being so I conclude, ... that the taxpayer fails because the expenditure was of a 'private' nature within the meaning of sec 51 of the Act."

Subject to one exception yet to be mentioned I reach the same conclusion upon this reference.

13. The exception relates to the costs of attending the Perth conference. I accept that it was expected of the taxpayer by the school that he should attend and, on the part of the taxpayer, that it was his responsibility to attend. That conclusion is supported by the circumstance that the school paid for his accommodation in Perth even though it did not contribute to his costs of travelling.

14. Accordingly, even though the applicant had to utilise a period of time available to him for his recreational and private purposes, I nonetheless think that the nexus is sufficient to entitle him to a deduction in the appropriate amount. It was agreed that the appropriate amount is $250.00. To that extent only I would allow the objection of the applicant.
