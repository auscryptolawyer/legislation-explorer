VT86/7157 and Commissioner of Taxation [1989] AATA 321 (3 March 1989)

Last Updated: 26 March 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - allowable deductions - travel and accommodation expenses - taxpayer diabetic - travelling overseas as employyee for work purposes - risk of hypoglycaemic episodes in sleep and consequent inability to fulfil work engagements - wife accompanying taxpayer - wife's travel and accommodation expenses private expenses, not deductible

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s. 51(1)

Ronpibon Tin N. L. and Tongkah Compound N. L. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47

C35 71 ATC 153

REASONS FOR DECISION

3 March 1989 Deputy President I. R. Thompson

  1. The objection decision under review is a decision to disallow an objection lodged by the taxpayer in respect of the assessment of income tax payable by him for the taxation year which ended on 30 June 1984. The amount in issue is $4,301. There is no dispute that that is the amount which the taxpayer paid in respect of air fares, rail fares, departure taxes, accommodation, travel insurance and food for his wife during an overseas trip on which she accompanied him during that taxation year.
  2. The applicant is employed by the Commonwealth. He undertook the overseas trip at the behest of his employer to visit a number of establishments in the United Kingdom, Canada and the United States of America. The purpose of his making those visits was to enable him to obtain knowledge of the scientific developments which had been made by scientists at those establishments in areas in which he was himself employed as an expert by the Commonwealth. The Commonwealth, as his employer, paid his own costs of the trip. It did not pay anything towards the cost of his wife's accompanying him on the trip. The circumstances in which the wife accompanied him are set out in documents which he supplied to the respondent together with his income return for the 1984 taxation year. He amplified that information by oral evidence at the hearing. At the hearing he was not represented; the respondent was represented by a departmental officer.
    1. The taxpayer suffers from diabetes. He gave evidence that at the time when he made the trip, although he was under medical treatment and in accordance with medical advice injecting himself with insulin daily, his diabetes was not very well under control. He was suffering episodes of hypoglycaemia from time to time. The trip was to last from 1 June 1984 to 2 July 1984. During that time he was to fly from Australia to London, from London to Ottawa and then to and within the United States of America and finally back to Australia.
    2. Within the United Kingdom the establishments which he was to visit were in London, Farnborough (Hampshire), Cambridge, Bristol, and Manchester. The establishment which he visited in Canada was in Ottawa. In the United States of America he visited establishments in Massachusetts, New York, New Jersey, Cincinnati, Dayton, Cleveland, Palo Alto, and Edwards (California). His schedule was therefore demanding. It meant moving between time zones. He envisaged that he would find it exhausting and that he would have problems maintaining the regular meals and the diet needed by him as a diabetic. He envisaged, apparently on the basis of medical advice and his own knowledge of his condition, that he would probably suffer hypoglycaemic episodes during the trip. None of that evidence was in dispute and I accept it as true.
  3. The applicant gave evidence that hypoglycaemic episodes which he might suffer while he was awake did not present him with any major problem. As soon as he was aware that an episode was starting he could quickly ingest something to increase the sugar level in his blood and so rapidly bring the hypoglycaemic episode to an end. What concerned him was that he had suffered hypoglycaemic episodes during his sleep. He regarded the risk of his doing so while on his trip as substantial. He said that, if a hypoglycaemic episode occurred while he was asleep, it might cause him to remain unconscious for up to six hours; then, when he woke, it would take him up to another two hours to get back to normal. If that occurred, he would be unable to keep the appointments which had been made for him for the following day; in other words, he would not be able to do the job for which he was being sent overseas.
  4. He decided that he needed to have present with him while he was asleep someone who, if a hypoglycaemic episode occurred, would be aware of it and wake him up so that he could promptly ingest something to bring his blood sugar level back to normal. He gave evidence that his wife recognised the signs of a hypoglycaemic episode and slept lightly enough to become aware quickly that such an episode had occurred during his sleep. He knew of no other way in which he could avoid the risk that a hypoglycaemic episode would occur during his sleep and not be detected quickly and that in consequence he would he unable to rise at the correct time on the following morning and attend to all his commitments for that day. His evidence that hypoglycaemic episodes might occur while he was asleep and that, if they did so without being quickly detected by someone, he would not be able to fulfil properly his commitments on the following day was not challenged. I am satisfied that he took his wife with him to obviate that risk.
    1. The taxpayer did not suggest that his wife was an expert in his scientific field or could assist him with his work in any way except by ensuring that he was not unfit to attend to it because of a hypoglycaemic episode occurring during his sleep. He gave evidence that she did not accompany him on his visits to the various establishments; she remained in the hotel or "wandered about" the town, presumably sightseeing or shopping.
    2. On the facts of this case, the only section of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") under which a deduction might be allowed in respect of the cost of the taxpayer's wife accompanying him on the trip is section 51(1). That reads as follows:-

"51. (1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

  1. As the taxpayer was an employee and not conducting his own business, for the outgoings to be allowable deductions they must have been incurred in gaining or producing his assessable income as an employee. Also they must not have been outgoings of a capital, private, or domestic nature. The meaning of the phrase "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income" has been considered by the High Court of Australia on a number of occasions, both as used in the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922-34 and in the present Act. In Ronpibon Tin N. L. and Tongkah Compound N. L. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at 57 the Court said:-

"Notwithstanding the differences in other respects in the present provision, the expression 'incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income' has been left unchanged and bears the same meaning. In brief substance, to come within the initial part of the sub-section it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income or, if none be produced, would be expected to produce assessable income. It is by this standard that the question raised by the present cases must be determined."

  1. In the present case what was productive of the taxpayer's assessable income was his employment by the Commonwealth and the work which he did under that contract of employment. Further, the extension of the scope of his expertise which resulted from his visits to the various establishments during the trip was described by a senior officer as "significantly beneficial to his chances of being successful in his application for promotion". Mr Fraser gave evidence that he had subsequently been promoted. I am satisfied that his own trip was undertaken not only in the course of his employment as a result of which he received assessable income in the 1984 taxation year but has contributed to his subsequent promotion and a greater amount of taxable income in later taxation years. The question which has to be answered is whether "the occasion of the outgoing" which is in issue in these proceedings, that is to say the cost of the taxpayer's wife accompanying him on his trip, is to "be found" in his employment by the Commonwealth and the performance of his work under that contract.
    1. I have come to the conclusion that the occasion of the cost of the taxpayer's wife accompanying him on his trip is not to be found in his employment or the performance of his work under his contract of employment but is rather of a private or domestic nature. Essentially, the purpose of her accompanying him was to enable him to maintain his health so that he could perform his work. The maintenance of health is generally a matter of a private nature. It may be otherwise where health is put at risk by the actual work, for instance where objects may fly up into an employee's eyes; if the employer does not provide equipment to protect the employee's eyes, the occasion out of which the cost of the employee providing himself with appropriate goggles to protect them is, I have no doubt, his employment and his work in performance of his contract of employment. In the taxpayer's case it was not his work which gave rise to the need for his health to be protected. Rather, it was that his health had to be maintained so that he could perform his work in the circumstances of the trip.
  2. In case C35 71 ATC 153 the taxpayer went overseas on a trip and took his wife with him partly because he had previously suffered a heart attack and it was undesirable for him to travel on his own. No. 2 Board of Review held that, so far as his wife accompanying him on the trip had the purpose of preventing his having to travel on his own, the cost of her doing so was not an allowable deduction because it related to a matter personal to him, notwithstanding that he was himself at that time engaged on business. It therefore bore a private character.
  3. In my view the cost of the taxpayer's wife accompanying him in the present case was also an outgoing of a private nature. For that reason I find that it was not an allowable deduction and that accordingly the objection decision under review must be affirmed.
