Aldred and Australian Taxation Office [1989] AATA 297 (6 February 1989)

Last Updated: 26 March 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

FREEDOM OF INFORMATION - exemption claimed under section 36 of the Freedom of Information Act, 1982 - whether disclosure would be contrary to the public interest.

And: AUSTRALIAN TAXATION OFFICE

Before: Hartigan J, (President)

REASONS FOR DECISION

On 30 March, 1988 the applicant, Kenneth James Aldred, wrote to the Treasurer, the Honourable P J Keating in the following terms:

"Re: (a) The Freedom of Information Act

(b) The Joint OECD and Council of Europe Draft Multilateral Convention on Mutual Administrative Assistance in Tax Matters.

I hereby make application pursuant to the Freedom of Information Act for all documents relating to Ref (b) above concerning the following:

(a) Any advice the Treasury and/or Tax Office has tended to the Treasurer or any other Minister on this matter.

(b) Other than the Convention document itself, any other OECD or EEC documents relating to the Convention.

(c) Any advice given or documents from any other source.

(d) Any advice or documents showing that the Convention is, or may be, in conflict with the domestic laws of the Commonwealth of Australia, its various States and Territories or any international laws, conventions or agreements.

(e) Any documents relating to discussion of the Convention by Ministers or Government officials.

I enclose my cheque for $30.00 being the requisite application fee.

I hereby also seek remission of all fees and charges relating to this application as I require the documents to assist me with my Parliamentary duties. Furthermore, I consider their release to be in the public interest as the proposed OECD Convention on Tax Matters will have a major impact upon Australian companies and individual Australians."

On 15 April 198B the Senior Private Secretary to the Treasurer, D E Russell, wrote to Mr Aldred and advised him that the Australian Taxation Office was the agency primarily responsible for the matter which had been the subject matter of his request of 30 March 1988. Mr Russell advised Mr Aldred that his request had been transferred in full to the Australian Taxation Office. On 18 May 1988 the Australian Taxation Office made available to the applicant a number of documents and, however, claimed that further documents were exempted for various reasons under the Freedom of Information Act 1982 (Cth) (the FOI Act). By letter dated 18 May 1988 the applicant then sought review of that decision by this Tribunal. In due course on 11 July 1988 the applicant was notified by the Australian Taxation Office of the result of an internal review of the decision of which he had been notified in the letter dated 18 May 1988.

2. At the hearing of this matter the respondent claimed exemptions in respect of a large number of documents under various sections of the FOI Act. Subsequently during the hearing of the matter the Treasurer released a Press Statement on 18 October 1388. It was as follows:

"STATEMENT BY THE TREASURER, THE HON P.J. KEATING MP

OECD CONVENTION ON MUTUAL ADMINISTRATIVE ASSISTANCE IN TAX MATTERS

The Government has decided not to become a signatory to the OECD/Council of Europe Convention on mutual administrative assistance in tax matters.

There have been a number of inaccurate claims recently that the Convention would lead to access by various countries to confidential tax file information through a central data bank. Those claims are in fact unfounded, and of course, the Government would not for a moment countenance such a possibility.

However, apart from those misrepresentations, the Government has reached the view that Australia is unlikely to gain significantly by becoming a signatory to the Convention.

This decision is consistent with the recommendation of the Australian Taxation Office.

CANBERRA

18 October 1988"

As a consequence of that statement most of the documents which had been in issue during the earlier part of the hearing were released to the applicant. There remained only four documents in respect of which exemption is claimed and in issue between the parties. They are documents which are numbered 30, 52, 55 and 57. Exemption is claimed to the whole of part or those documents on section 36 grounds. I turn now to consider whether that exemption has been properly claimed.

3. Section 36 so far as it is relevant to this matter provides as follows:

"36. (1) Subject to this section, a document is an exempt document if it is a document the disclosure of which under this Act -

(a) would disclose matter in the nature of, or relating to, opinion, advice or recommendation obtained, prepared or recorded, or consultation or deliberation that has taken place, in the course of, or for the purposes of, the deliberative processes involved in the functions of an agency or Minister or of the Government of the Commonwealth; and

(b) would be contrary to the public interest.

(2) ...

(3) ...

(4) ...
  • (5) this section does not apply to a document by reason only of purely factual material contained in the document.

... .

It is fair to say that section 36 basically requires that two matters be satisfied. First, that the documents are documents which come within 36(1)(a), namely that they are documents which relate to the deliberative processes; and which do not contain wholly or in part purely factual material, see section 36(5). Second, in order to be exempt their disclosure must be contrary to the public interest.

4. Senator Alston who appeared for the applicant conceded that all the documents met the description of deliberative process documents; see the transcript at pages 52 and 53. I would add that I have read those documents and that in respect of documents where exemption is still claimed I am satisfied that each such document does not contain wholly or in part purely factual material. It remains to be considered therefore whether the disclosure of those documents in whole or in part would be contrary to the public interest.

5. In argument, Mr Tracy for the respondent, referred me to Re Howard and Treasurer of the Commonwealth (1985) 3 AAR 169. I think it is useful to set out that part of Davies J.'s judgment at pages 175 to 178 which dealt with the public interest aspect.

"Section 36 is concerned with pre-decisional communications. Many of these are made within an agency or between an agency and a person outside the agency in the expectation that the communication will not be disclosed to the public. However, it is unfortunate that, because of the secrecy provisions in numerous Acts and regulations, including reg 35 of the Public Service Regulations, and the existence of a system of confidential markings, it was unnecessary for the Commonwealth Public Service prior to the passing of the FOI Act to formulate any clear criteria as to what documents ought not to be disclosed to a person outside an agency. Such criteria have still not been formulated.

In Re Murtagh and Commissioner of Taxation, supra, the Tribunal said (at 428):

'It is clear that the public interest is not to be limited by the prescription of categories or classes of documents the disclosure of which to the public would be contrary to the public interest. The public interest is not to be circumscribed. All documents must be examined to ascertain whether, having regard to the circumstances, their disclosure would be contrary to the public interest.'

The Tribunal went on to consider a number of American authorities on Exemption (b)(5), including (at 121-122) the following remarks of Wilkey J in Jordan v Department of Justice [1978] USCADC 317; 591 F (2d) 753 (1978):

'...The privilege attaches to inter- and intra-agency communications that are part of the deliberative process preceding the adoption and promulgation of an agency policy. There are essentially three policy bases for this privilege. First, it protects creative debate and candid consideration of alternatives within an agency, and, thereby, improves the quality of agency policy decisions. See NLRB v Sears Roebuck & Co 421 US at 151; [1975] USSC 81; 95 S Ct 1504; Montrose Chemical Corp v Train, [1974] USCADC 40; 160 US App DC 270, 273; [1974] USCADC 40; 491 F 2d 63, 66 (1974). Second, it protects the public from the confusion that would result from premature exposure to discussions occurring before the policies affecting it had actually been settled upon. See Grumman Aircraft Eng Corp v Renegatiations Board, note 77 supra, 157 US App DC at 129; 482 F 2d at 718; Sterling Drug Inc v FTC [1971] USCADC 275; 146 US App DC 237, 245-246; [1971] USCADC 275; 450 F 2d 698, 706-708 (1971). And third, it protects the integrity of the decision-making process itself by confirming that 'officials should be judged by what they decideC[,], not for matters they considered before making up their minds'. Grumman Aircraft Eng Corp v. Renegotiation Board, supra. See Boeing Airplane Co v. Coggeshall, 108 US App 106, 112; 280F 2d 654, 660 (1960); Carl Zeiss Stiftung v VEB Carl Zeiss Jena, note 77 supra, 40 FRD at 325-326."'

The Tribunal referred to the decisions of Conway v Rimmer [1968] UKHL 2; [1968] AC 910 and Sankey v Whitlam (1978) 142 CLR 1, both of which considered public interest in the context of Crown privilege. The Tribunal concluded (at 123):

' ... Broadly speaking, s 36 can be seen as an attempt by the legislature to protect the integrity and viability of the decision-making process. If the release of documents would impair this process to a significant or substantial degree and there is no countervailing benefit to the public which outweighs that impairment then it would be contrary to the public interest to grant access.'

The Tribunal also referred to the object of the Act expressed in s3(1)(b) and the reference in that paragraph to 'essential public interests'. A generally similar approach was taken by the Tribunal in Re Lianos and Secretary to the Department of Social Security (1985) 2 AAR 503, though the Tribunal there gave rather more attention to the Crown privilege cases.

Reference should also be made to the remarks of Lord Reid in Conway v Rimmer, supra, where his Lordship said (at 952):

'I do not doubt that there are certain classes of documents which ought not to be disclosed whatever their content may be. Virtually everyone agrees that Cabinet minutes and the like ought not to be disclosed until such time as they are only of historical interest. But I do not think that many people would give as the reason that premature disclosure would prevent candour in the Cabinet. To my mind the most important reason is that such disclosure would create or fan ill-informed or captious public or political criticism. The business of government is difficult enough as it is, and no government could contemplate with equanimity the inner workings of the government machine being exposed to the gaze of those ready to criticise without adequate knowledge of the background and perhaps with some axe to grind. And that must, in my view, also apply to all documents concerned with policy making with departments including, it may be, minutes and the like by quite junior officials and correspondence with outside bodies. Further it may be that deliberations about a particular case require protection as much as deliberations about policy. I do not think that it is possible to limit such documents by any definition. But there seems to me to be a wide difference between such documents and routine reports. There may be special reasons for withholding some kinds of routine documents, but I think that the proper test to be applied is to ask, in the language of Lord Simon in Duncan v Cammell, Laird and Co Ltd [1942] UKHL 3; [1942] AC 624, 642, whether the withholding of a document because it belongs to a particular class is really "necessary for the proper functioning of the public service"'.

These remarks were referred to and in general approved in the judgments delivered in Sankey v Whitlam, supra.

In considering the public interest element of s 36, the Tribunal may be called upon and in this review is called upon to have regard not only to the incidents of the disclosure of the particular documents but also to the effect which availability for disclosure to the public under the FOI Act of like documents would have upon the decision-making processes of the Minister and of his Department. The task reposed upon the Tribunal by s 36 is not that which arises in the Crown Privilege cases of which Conway v Rimmer and Sankey v Whitlam, supra, are pre-eminent examples, or that undertaken in Jordan v Department of Justice, supra, which considered US Exemption b(5). Yet those authorities throw light upon the elements of the public interest to be considered and the circumstances in which traditionally it has been thought that the reservation of documents from public view should be maintained. From such authorities and from decisions of Tribunals, including Re Peters and Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet (No 2) (1983) 5 ALN No 21B; Re Waterford and Department of Treasury, supra; Re Murtagh and Commissioner of Taxation, supra; Re Chandra and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (19B4) 6 ALN 257; Re Burns and Australian National University and Re Lianos and Secretary to the Department of Social Security it is possible to postulate that in each case the whole of the circumstances must be examined including any public benefit perceived in the disclosure of the documents sought but that:

  1. The higher the office of the persons between whom the communications pass and the more sensitive the issues involved in the communication, the more likely it will be that the communication should not be disclosed.
  2. Disclosure of communications made in the course of the development and subsequent promulgation of policy tends not to be in the public interest.
  3. Disclosure which will inhibit frankness and candour in future pre-decisional communications is likely to be contrary to the public interest.
  4. Disclosure, which will lead to confusion and unnecessary debate resulting from disclosure of possibilities considered, tends not to be in the public interest.
  5. Disclosure of documents which do not fairly disclose the reasons for a decision subsequently taken may be unfair to a decision-maker and may prejudice the integrity of the decision-making process.

The FOI Act has been in operation since 1 December 1982. As was said in Re Murtaqh and Commissioner of Taxation, supra, Re Chandra and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs, supra, and Re Lianos and Secretary to the Department of Social Security, the Tribunal has not yet received evidence that disclosure under the FOI Act has in fact led to a diminishment in appropriate candour and frankness between officers. As time goes by, experience will be gained of the operation of the Act. The extent to which disclosure of internal working documents is in the public interest will more clearly emerge. Presently, there must often be an element of conjecture in a decision as to the public interest. Weight must be given to the object of the FOI Act. "

6. It is necessary therefore to address each of the particular documents and the extent to which exemption is claimed for that document under section 36.

7. Document 30 is an internal report that was raised at the beginning of December 1987 and which brings up to date the situation in relation to the convention and summarizes the major parts of it that are likely to impact on Australia; see transcript at page 71. Exemption is now claimed only in relation to the final part of that document which deals with the recommended position that Australia should adopt so far as becoming signatory to the treaty is concerned. I have examined that part of the document and I am satisfied that its disclosure would impair the integrity and viability of the decision making process. I am conscious of the office of the person who created the document but I am equally conscious that it deals with a particularly sensitive issue. It is a document which forms part of the development and subsequent promulgation of a policy. Its disclosure would therefore lead to confusion and an unnecessary debate of the possibilities considered.

8. Document 52 contains departmental suggestions as to what Australia's position ought to be as of 15 April 1988. Exemption is claimed in relation to the second sub paragraph of the second paragraph of that document. I have examined the contents of that sub paragraph and I am satisfied that it concerns the current position as to negotiations with an Australian Government Department and a foreign Government. There is no doubt in my mind having regard to the level of that communication and its content that its disclosure would be contrary to the public interest.

9. Document 55 is a file note which records a follow up telephone conversation between the Australian Taxation Office and an Australian Government Department. During the course of that telephone conversation the views of that Department and of a Government Minister were expressed. Again I have no doubt that having regard to the nature of the subject matter of that conversation that its disclosure would be contrary to the public interest.

10. Document 57 is a hand written file note again involving discussions as to what Australia's attitude should be and contains views about the advice obtained from a particular Australian Government Department on that question. As with the preceding documents it relates to a sensitive issue because it involves communciation made in the course of the development and subsequent promulgation of Government policy and. its disclosure would lead to confusion and unnecessary debate.

11. I am satisfied that the exemption claimed in relation to the four documents pursuant to section 36 was properly made. Accordingly the decision under review is affirmed.
