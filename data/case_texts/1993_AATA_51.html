HEARING

PERTH, 16 November 1992

12:2:1993

ORDER

1. With respect to all but the following documents, the Tribunal

affirms the decision under review.

2. With respect to the name of the officer of the Director of Public

Prosecutions in document 35 of Exhibit 1 the Tribunal finds that it

is not exempt under the Freedom of Information Act 1982 ("the Act")

and, subject to an application to this Tribunal being made within

28 days to stay disclosure, the Tribunal directs that it should

thereafter be released to the applicant.

3. In the event of any application being made pursuant to the previous

Direction, the Tribunal shall make such further direction as it

sees fit.

4. With respect to those parts of document 48 of Exhibit 1 which

consist of information obtained from the Titles Office of Western

Australia, the claim for exemption on the basis of s.12(1)(b) of

the Act is not upheld.

DECISION

The applicant seeks review, under s.55 of the Freedom of Information Act 1982 ("the Act"), of a decision of the respondent refusing access to certain documents contained within what compendiously has been described as his "Audit File". The request which initiated the matter is contained in a letter of 26 November 1990 and was expressed as follows:

"I hereby request formally under the Freedom of Information Act

release of all my Audit File not previously released".

Background

2. By way of background it is necessary to appreciate that the applicant made a previous request on 20 May 1988 for disclosure of the materials comprising his Audit File. Upon the respondent denying access to a number of documents covered by that request, the applicant sought review before this Tribunal. On 16 June 1989 this Tribunal, constituted by Deputy President G L McDonald, upheld the respondent's objections to disclosure of the documents in contention (Decision No 5160). The Tribunal in that decision (which shall be referred to where appropriate as "the first proceedings") dealt with two categories of documents; the first comprising working papers generated by departmental officers in the course of the audit; the second being documents in which various departmental officers had been named.

3. In relation to the first category, the Tribunal upheld the claim for exemption under s.37(1)(a) and s.37(2)(b) of the Act on the basis that their disclosure, at that point in time, could reasonably be expected to lead to prejudice to the investigations whilst they were being pursued. So far as the documents in which departmental officers' names were disclosed were concerned, the Tribunal held that they fell within the exemptions in s.40(1)(c), (d) and (e) of the Act, based on his findings that disclosure would or could reasonably be expected to have the substantial adverse effects set forth in those paragraphs.

4. The audit of the applicant's tax affairs having been completed, the basis for exemption in relation to the first category of documents has fallen away since disclosure could not be said any longer to prejudice the outcome of those investigations. In consequence, the respondent has released over a period of time a large number of documents that had been comprised within that category. So far as documents containing the names of officers is concerned, a number of such documents have also been released to the applicant subject to the deletion of the names of particular officers.

5. Upon the applicant making the fresh request on 26 November 1990, the respondent has declined to release the residue of documents in the Audit File, except in some cases where deletions have been made, and has claimed them to be exempt on various bases explored in greater detail below. In the meantime, the applicant has sought to enlarge the ambit of his initial request in two respects. His first assertion is that the audit was in fact in progress from an earlier date (and perhaps from as early as July 1984) than the date that has been nominated by the respondent (6 August 1986) as the date on which the audit relating to the applicant had commenced. In that respect the applicant sought to have documents in the possession of the respondent prior to 6 August 1986 encompassed within his request for disclosure.

6. At the other end of the scale, he has sought access to documents which have come into existence as part of the audit but which were created subsequent to 26 November 1990, the date of his current request.

7. With respect to various documents where the basis for the claim for exemption was protection of the disclosure of the identity of particular officers, the respondent has objected to the competence of the Tribunal to review its refusal to disclose those documents. The respondent's contention was that Deputy President McDonald had already upheld the claim for exemption by in his decision and that aspect of the matter could not be re-agitated before this Tribunal.

8. In order to clarify the ambit of these present proceedings the Tribunal held two Directions Hearings on 19 May 1992 and 25 June 1992. In the result the Tribunal made the following ruling on 9 July 1992:

"1. All documents sought by the applicant as part of his "Audit

File", (being documents generated in relation to or as a

result of the decision to conduct an audit into the

applicant's tax affairs) that have come into existence up to

the date of the hearing of this matter, shall be included in

this application.

2. To the extent that any of those documents were determined by

Deputy President McDonald in application W88/120 to be

exempt from disclosure by reason of s.40(1)(c) of the

Freedom of Information Act 1982, they shall be excluded from

further consideration by the Tribunal."

9. The reasons supporting that ruling, as delivered on 9 July 1992 (Decision No 8092), should be read as incorporated with this decision. Shortly summarised, the second ruling was based on the view that either, because of the doctrine of issue estoppel, or as a matter of practical policy, the Tribunal should not review an earlier decision of the Tribunal dealing with an identical issue in relation to an identical set of facts.

The Evidence and Materials concerning the Present Application

10. Upon the matter proceeding to hearing, the applicant appeared for himself, and the respondent was represented by Mr E Carlose, instructed by the Australian Government Solicitor's Office. The applicant gave evidence on his own behalf. The respondent filed an affidavit of Mr D Parker, a senior officer of the Perth Tax Office of the respondent's department who is in charge of freedom of information matters in that office. Mr Parker gave evidence in support of the respondent's objections to disclosure. In addition to the T documents filed with the Tribunal pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and Mr Parker's affidavit, the Tribunal also accepted into evidence the respondent's Freedom of Information Reference Handbook Exemption Guidelines ("FOI Guidelines") (exhibit 3), a copy of a Resolution of the Tax Branch of the Federated Clerks Union ("FCU") dated 17 October 1988 (exhibit 4) and part of the transcript (pages 28-31) of the first proceedings on 14 April 1989 (exhibit 5).

11. Exhibit 5 was released to the applicant under an Order made by the President of the Tribunal, Justice D F O'Connor, and related to evidence given before Deputy President McDonald.

12. Subsequent to the hearing the Tribunal received an affidavit of Mr Carlose dated 25 November 1992 to which reference is made below. This relates to enquiries which the Tribunal had directed him to undertake about whether certain governmental agencies charged fees under legislative authority.

The scope of this Application

13. The first problem the Tribunal must address is the ambit of review in this matter. The applicant, relying on the extract from the transcript of the proceedings before Deputy President McDonald (exhibit 5), points to evidence given by a senior officer in the Tax Office in that case that suggests that in July 1984 a decision was made to refer his tax return for further investigation. It is therefore his contention that all documents held by the respondent which relate to his tax affairs and have come into existence between that date and the present fall within the scope of the present review.

14. It should be said that the applicant's approach to this matter is shaped by the purpose he seeks to achieve through these proceedings. His essential object, as he explained it to the Tribunal, is to lay a foundation so that he may take further proceedings under the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 to have the decision to subject him to audit set aside as being invalid. His approach, reflected in his various contentions to the Tribunal and in his own evidence and cross-examination of the respondent's witness, was therefore directed in part to questioning the validity of various actions of the Tax Office as well as challenging the claims for exemption relating to the documents before the Tribunal. At the other end of the scale, he sought to establish by his own evidence that from conversations he had had with officers of the Tax Office in early 1988, no formal decision had been taken up to that time to submit his affairs to audit. As a third contention, he submitted that if the audit had commenced in August 1986 he was unaware of that fact until early 1988 and therefore there must be some doubt as to whether an official audit was under way prior to the latter date. This was reinforced, in his view, by the fact that the audit was not completed till October 1991, ie. more than 3 years after the date when it was said to have started.

15. Mr Carlose on the other hand sought a ruling from the Tribunal that documents in the applicant's Audit File prior to 15 April 1988, though included in those provided to the Tribunal, should be excluded from this application given that they have already been the subject of review in the first proceedings.

Evidence about the Audit Process

16. Mr Parker gave evidence as to the relevant procedures of the Tax Office. He is an experienced senior officer and the Tribunal accepts his evidence as truthful and reliable. As he explains it, once a tax return had been lodged, there may be many reasons why an assessor might have referred it to the Audit Branch or its predecessor for further consideration. There may be inconsistencies in detail; there may be material suggesting omissions in disclosure; or there may be other reasons why verification is called for. In this respect, a distinction should be drawn between investigations carried out at the stage prior to audit, and the audit process itself. In fact, many files referred for audit are, after a preliminary scrutiny returned for assessment with no further action undertaken. In other cases a determination is made that the matter is one appropriate for further investigation and at that stage, the matter is listed for audit. It becomes part of the audit process with the allocation of an auditor. Until that point of time, a particular file might sit for some time until a specialised auditor became available. In the case of the applicant, from his examination of the documents contained in the Audit File (which actually comprises three lever arch files) Mr Parker stated that the decision to undertake the audit in question appears to have been taken on 6 August 1986.

17. Mr Parker's evidence is in fact consistent with that given in the first proceedings. According to the latter, the applicant's 1984 return was first referred to the Investigation Research and Training Section of the Tax Office in July 1984 (p 29 exhibit 5). But the evidence given on that occasion confirms 6 August 1986 as the date when a decision was taken authorising the audit. The Tribunal is therefore satisfied that that is the date which marks the terminal point in time with which this review is concerned.

18. The Tribunal appreciates that persons seeking access to government documents are rarely able to identify with precision the boundaries of their claim for access. In the present application, the applicant has chosen to define it in terms which strictly are confined to the audit process and the matter has been understandably addressed by the respondent in those terms. The applicant in this instance has acquired considerable experience of the operations of the Tax Office and should therefore be taken to have properly indicated the intended ambit of his request. In the circumstances, concerning the first ruling of the Tribunal on 9 July 1992, the Tribunal is not prepared to enlarge the scope of this application to cover documents that arose in the pre-audit process.

19. In so defining the scope of this application the Tribunal is satisfied, from the evidence of Mr Parker, that other than the documents which are the subject of the present claim for exemption (those collectively comprised in exhibit 1) all other documents in the applicant's Audit File have been released to him. The applicant did debate whether some material relating to enquiries made of the applicant's bank had been disclosed to him. When cross-examined by Mr Carlose about the nature of documents that might not have been disclosed the applicant was not able to be more specific nor assist the Tribunal further with the matter. The Tribunal therefore finds that all documents in the Audit File other than those presented to the Tribunal as exhibit 1 have been released to the applicant.

20. Concerning whether an audit may be characterised officially or in substance as an audit if a taxpayer is unaware that it is in progress, the Tribunal is of the opinion that the knowledge or ignorance of the taxpayer is not material to its existence. The question is simply one of fact. What is relevant here is that a certain process was administratively under way in the Tax Office as from August 1986. It is the documents generated or acquired during that process to which the applicant has sought access, and accordingly, it is in terms of that process that the applicant has chosen to institute proceedings for review before this Tribunal.

21. The Tribunal does not, however, accede to the respondent's request to exclude all documents prior to 15 April 1988 from this application. Though as discussed in the following section, some parts of the documents in contention should be excluded consequent upon the second ruling made on 9 July 1993, that was a limited ruling and was not intended to comprehend all other documents in issue.

Whether Documents containing Officers' Names held to be Exempt in the First Proceedings may be Reassessed in these proceedings

22. The second ruling given above was directed to precluding review of documents where exemption had been claimed in the first proceedings solely on the basis that the information therein related to names of officers of the Taxation Department or to identifying marks or signatures. As explained above, Deputy President McDonald in the first proceedings upheld the claim for exemption under s.40(1) of the Act. In the present proceedings the Tribunal permitted evidence to be led from Mr Parker in order to ensure that the materials before the Tribunal in the present claim in respect of which exemption under s.40(1) was claimed were precisely covered by the earlier decision of Deputy President McDonald. The Tribunal also allowed, out of an abundance of caution, evidence to be submitted in support of the claim for exemption. This included extracts from the respondent's FOI Guidelines and a resolution of the Tax Office branch of the FCU.

23. It was submitted by the respondent that, as Mr Parker deposes in paragraph 4 of his affidavit, disclosure of the relevant officers' names would be detrimental to the efficacy of the respondent's operations as it could give rise to industrial relations problems or create problems for promotions and transfer if officers were reluctant to move to sections of the Tax Office where unnecessary disclosure of their names to the public might attract harassment.

24. In the event, the Tribunal is satisfied, with the exception of one particular document, that the issue whether exemption could be supported on those grounds is identical with the issue determined by Deputy President McDonald in the first proceedings. Consistent with the second ruling previously made on 9 July 1992 (see above) this Tribunal does not regard itself as competent to re-open the issue in these proceedings. Consequently, to the extent that disclosure of officers' names is the only matter in issue, the claim for exemption in that respect is upheld. Without dealing with each individual instance, the Tribunal decides that the names, marks or signatures of officers of the Tax Office contained in the following documents, if not already released to the applicant, are exempt from disclosure:

Documents 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 18, 21, 22, 23, 25, 26, 27,

28, 29, 31, 32, 33, 37, 38, 41, 42, 43, and 44.

25. The one exception relates to the disclosure of the name of the officer recorded in document 35. The particular officer holds an appointment in the office of the Director of Public Prosecutions ("DPP"), Perth. The claims based on disclosure of officers associated with the Tax Office who have had no direct dealing with the applicant, and the likelihood of resulting disruption to its operations, cannot therefore apply in this case. The only basis that the Tribunal sees as relevant in this respect are sections 37(1)(a) of the Act where disclosure would, or could reasonably be expected to prejudice the enforcement or proper administration of the law in a particular instance, or s.40(1)(c), (d) or (e). No specific evidence was put before the Tribunal to substantiate any of these grounds and the Tribunal is therefore not satisfied that the claim for exemption should be upheld in respect of disclosure of this name. Nevertheless, since the matter has not been properly canvassed, nor representations been made on behalf of the particular officer of the DPP, the Tribunal is reluctant to overrule the claim for exemption without providing some safeguard. Accordingly, the Tribunal proposes to make certain directions (see the formal decision herein) relating to the implementation of this decision to cover the point.

Exemptions Based on Statutory Secrecy in respect of Information about Personal Affairs of other Persons and Taxpayers

26. Exemption is claimed under s.38 and s.41(1) of the Act in relation to documents 3, 6, 7, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 19, 20, 24, 36, 45 and 47. So far as relevant those provisions read:

"38 Subject to subsection (1A), a document is an exempt

document if:

disclosure of the document, or information contained in

the document, is prohibited under a provision of an

enactment; and

either:

that provision is specified in

Schedule 3; or

this section is expressly applied to

the document, or information, by that provision, or by

another provision of that or any other enactment.

(1A) A person's right of access to a document under section 11

or 22 is not affected merely because the document is an exempt

document under subsection (1) of this section if disclosure of

the document, or information contained in the document, to that

person is not prohibited by the enactment concerned or any other

enactment.

41 (1) A document is an exempt document if its disclosure under

this Act would involve the unreasonable disclosure of personal

information about any person (including a deceased person).

Subject to subsection (3), the provisions of subsection (1) do

not have effect in relation to a request by a person for access

to a document by reason only of the inclusion in the document of

matter relating to that person.

..."

27. So far as s.38 is concerned, it should be noted that Schedule 3 to the Act lists certain provisions of s.16 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the ITA Act"). So far as relevant that section provides:

"16 (2) Subject to this section, an officer shall not either

directly or indirectly, except in the performance of any duty as

an officer, and either while he is, or after he ceases to be an

officer, make a record of, or divulge or communicate to any

person any such information so acquired by him."

28. Regarding the claim based on s.38, the Tribunal notes that in earlier decisions of the Tribunal a somewhat strict view has been taken about the ambit of s.38. In Re Mann and Australian Taxation Office (1985) 3 AAR 261 the Tribunal held that s.16 of the Income Tax Assessment Act did not contain a provision of the kind referred to in s.38 (see also Re Z and Australian Taxation Office (1984) 2 AAR 190). On the other hand, audit reports themselves have been held to be non-discloseable by virtue of s.38 and s.16(2) in combination (Re Dyki and Commissioner of Taxation (1990) 12 AAR 544). In this instance, the Tribunal is not persuaded that discharge of the relevant tax officer's duty demands non-disclosure in the face of an FOI application and therefore declines to uphold exemption on this ground.

29. Regarding the claim under s.41(1), what constitutes "personal affairs" has been broadly construed by the Tribunal in previous decisions to mean matters of private concern to a person (see Re Resch and Department of Veterans' Affairs (1986) 9 ALD 380, at 385 and Re Williams and Registrar, Family Court of Australia [1985] AATA 226; (1985) 8 ALD 219; 3 AAR 529 at 531). In Re Murtagh and the Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1984] AATA 249; (1984) 54 ALR 313; 1 AAR 419 the Tribunal commented that disclosure of the tax affairs of another individual would fall within s.41(1). As to what is "unreasonable" disclosure, the Tribunal in Re Chandra and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs [1984] AATA 437; (1984) 6 ALN N257 (in part) at N259, has observed:

"Exemption is only attracted if the disclosure would involve the

unreasonable disclosure of information relating to (personal)

affairs. Whether a disclosure is "unreasonable" requires, in my

view, a consideration of all the circumstances, including the

nature of the information that would be disclosed, the

circumstances in which the information was obtained, the

likelihood of the information being information that the person

concerned would not wish to have disclosed without consent, and

whether the information has any current relevance. Plainly

enough what s.41 seeks to do is to provide a ground for

preventing unreasonable invasion of the privacy of third parties.

However, consistently with the stated object of the Act (see

s.3), it is also necessary in my view to take into consideration

the public interest recognised by the Act in the disclosure of

information in documentary form in the possession of an agency

and to weigh that interest in the balance against the public

interest in protecting the personal privacy of a third party

whose personal affairs may be unreasonably disclosed by granting

access to the document."

30. The documents mentioned above in whole or in part relate to the personal or business affairs of various individuals, partnerships or trusts or to property held by the same. Where they relate to business affairs they are, nevertheless, of an essentially private nature; ie. relating to the facts of an individual's own participation in such matters and the derivation of their own benefits from the same, rather than matters they would regard as appropriate for public disclosure. In some instances information about tax file numbers is included. The applicant sought to meet the objection that the persons concerned would be concerned about public divulgence of their affairs by seeking an order providing for disclosure limited to himself. The Tribunal is of the opinion that even if disclosure were not to the world at large but only to the applicant, the persons involved would in all probability be concerned and agitated, with no demonstrable public benefit in return. In this regard, the Tribunal agrees with Heerey J in Colakovski v Australian Telecommunications Corp [1991] FCA 152; (1991) 100 ALR 111, at 120, that it is not necessary:

"... in order to make out the s.41(1) exclusion that there is

some particular unfairness, embarrassment or hardship which would

ensue to a person by reason of the disclosure. Such matters, if

present, would doubtless weigh in favour of exclusion. But if

the information disclosed were of no demonstrable relevance to

the affairs of government and was likely to do no more than

excite or satisfy the curiosity of people about the person whose

personal affairs were disclosed, I think disclosure would be

unreasonable (1991) 100 ALR at 123."

31. Upon a perusal of the documents here in issue, and having regard to the oral evidence of Mr Parker and to paragraphs 5, 6 and 7 of his affidavit, the Tribunal is satisfied that the claims for exemption in respect of each of these documents is sustained on the basis of s.41(1).

Exemption Claimed on Basis of Confidential Sources of Information a nd Information Disclosure of which would enable Applicant to Identify Sources 32. In respect of documents 19, 24, 34, 39, 40, 45 and 46 exemption is claimed on the basis of s.37(1)(b) and s.45(1) of the Act. Those provisions read:

"37 (1) A document is an exempt document if its disclosure under

this Act would, or could reasonably be expected to:

...

disclose, or enable a person to ascertain, the existence

or identity of a confidential source of information, or the

non-existence of a confidential source of information, in

relation to the enforcement or administration of the law; or

...

45 (1) A document is an exempt document if its disclosure under

this Act would found an action, by a person other than the

Commonwealth, for breach of confidence."

33. Where a document, if disclosed, would reveal the identity of a person who has confidentially provided information in relation to the observance of the ITA Act, it is exempt under s.37(1)(b) (Re Letts and the Director-General of Social Security (1984) 6 ALN N176). In such a case it is not appropriate to balance disclosure against public interest considerations (Department of Health v Jephcott [1985] FCA 370; (1985) 8 FCR 85; 62 ALR 421).

34. Under s.45, the tests to be applied are:

"(i) whether the information is confidential;

(ii) whether it was communicated in circumstances of confidence;

and

(iii) whether disclosure by the confidant is unauthorised."

(see Re Maher and Attorney-General's Department (1986) 4 AAR 266, 286).

Section 45 is not limited to situations where an action in equity could

actually be maintained for breach (Corrs Pavey Whiting and Byrne v

Collector of Customs [1987] FCA 266; (1987) 14 FCR 434; 7 AAR 187). The key issue is

whether an obligation of confidentiality can be implied.

35. The Tribunal makes the following comments on the individual documents in these categories. Documents 19 and 24 (which are related) are records of an interview and conversation with a person who has apparently reluctantly furnished information to the respondent's officers in the course of the audit.

36. Documents 34, 36 and 39 also constitute a family of related documents and records of conversations which if disclosed would enable an informed person in the applicant's position to identify the source of information. Information therein disclosed also was evidently given in circumstances of confidentiality and was not intended by the provider to be disclosed.

37. Documents 45 and 46 are directed to different individuals but again contain information from which it could be ascertained who was providing the information contained therein. Furthermore, it is quite evident that the contents relate to conversations which were clearly not intended by the persons from whom information was elicited to be disclosed.

38. The Tribunal has scrutinised each of the documents for which exemption is claimed in respect of each of the documents in these categories the Tribunal is satisfied that the elements required to be established under s.37(1)(b) and s.45 have been made out. In each case having regard to s.37(1)(b) identification of the person would be immediate and would be unjustifiably disturbing to the person concerned. Though no express undertaking not to disclose was ever given, it can be inferred from the records in issue that the information was given in reliance on an understanding that it was received in confidence. This is sufficient in the view of the Tribunal to establish a basis for exemption under s.45. On these bases the claims for exemption of the documents dealt with in this section should be upheld.

Remaining Claims for Exemption

39. Having dealt with the claims for exemption in respect of the above 46 documents, the Tribunal turns to the remaining two documents, each of which involves special or unique considerations.

(a) Document 47

40. Document 47 consists of a number of pages being photocopies of a microfiche record listing various taxpayers and certain information of a financial kind in relation to their affairs. The information was lawfully obtained. It concerns a large number of people in relation to their financial affairs. To that extent the respondent claims exemption relying on s.37(2)(b) of the Act which provides as follows:

"(a) ...

(b)disclose lawful methods or procedures for preventing,

detecting, investigating, or dealing with matters arising

out of, breaches or evasions of the law the disclosure of

which would, or would be reasonably likely to prejudice the

effectiveness of those methods or procedures; or

(c) ..."

41. Although sub-section (2)(b) of s.37 does not refer explicitly to breaches of a law relating to taxation, it is clear that it extends, among other things, to protecting disclosure of Tax Office procedures directed to detecting breaches of taxation legislation (Re Z and Australian Taxation Office (1984) 2 AAR 190; Re Arnold Bloch Leibler and Co and Australian Taxation Office (No 2) (1985) 4 AAR 178; 9 ALD 7). There is no reason to confine detection of breaches or evasions of the law in this instance to procedures of investigation which lead to prosecutorial action, ie. criminal enforcement. It is equally as applicable to methods of detection in aid of civil compliance.

42. The Tribunal accepts the evidence of Mr Parker that the document is one that assists the Tax Office in verifying whether complete disclosure is made by particular taxpayers. With respect to the document in question, it is clear that even if members of the public could speculate and deduce that a method of this kind is probably resorted to by the Tax Office, the Tribunal is satisfied that explicit public revelation of the particulars and incidents of that method could reasonably be expected to prejudice the effectiveness of the investigative procedures of that office. The Tribunal is therefore satisfied that the claim for exemption under s.37(2)(b) has been sustained.

43. Even if that were not the case, given that the document contains names and details of individual taxpayers specifically in relation to their private tax affairs, the alternate claim for exemption under s.41(1) are also upheld by the Tribunal. It is therefore unnecessary to determine whether the claim made under s.38 can be maintained in so far as the details are prohibited from disclosure under s.16(2) of the ITA Act.

(b) Document 48

44. This document contains information of two kinds: firstly, that which the respondent has obtained from the Corporate Affairs Office, Perth, and secondly, that from the Office of Titles. It includes, respectively, various details of particular companies and of land owned in Western Australia by particular persons. In each case the information is actually available to the public. In this instance the respondent relies on s.12(1)(b) of the Act which relevantly reads:

"12(1) A person is not entitled to obtain access under this

Part to:

(a) ...

(b) a document that is open to public access, as part of a public

register or otherwise, in accordance with another enactment,

where that access is subject to a fee or other charge; or

..."

45. The applicant makes the point that if he can simply go to the relevant offices and obtain the same information as any other member of the public, the respondent should spare him that effort simply by releasing the documents to him now. Section 12(1) is not, however, a provision that can be waived or just put aside by consent (Re Wilberforce and Department of Foreign Affairs (1983) 5 ALD 534).

46. Mr Carlose in his affidavit of 25 November 1992 has provided copies of relevant fees charged by the Australian Securities Commission as determined under the Corporations (Fees) Regulations made under s.26(1) of the Corporations Act 1989 (Cth) and also fees charged by the Titles Office of WA for searches by way of regulations made under Transfer of Land Act (WA) 1893.

47. "Enactment" is defined in s.4 of the Act, to include relevantly "an Act" or "an Instrument, including regulations, made under an Act". "Act" means, relevantly, an Act of the Commonwealth Parliament (s.38(1) of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901). It does not, however, extend to Acts and subordinate legislation of State Parliaments. In consequence the Tribunal is satisfied that the claimed exemption should be upheld in relation to the documents provided by the ASC (since access is available under Commonwealth legislation) but not those which are accessible from the West Australian Titles Office.

48. Since the details obtained from the Titles Office are not exempt under this heading, the Tribunal is bound nevertheless, even if the respondent makes no other claims in respect of the document, to consider whether it is exempt under some other heading. It is apparent on the face of the document that it is a listing of various names in the sequence they appear in the land records with reference to property holdings. These are in alphabetical order and appear to be comprehensive to the extent they have been extracted. There is no reason therefore for treating them as in any way touching upon the personal tax affairs of the people so listed, nor as disclosing a method of detection of breaches of the law, since the method would be self-evident in any event. However, the provision of a discrete list of persons, one of whom might well have some association with the taxation affairs under investigation, might well lead to the person's detection by the applicant because of the information he otherwise has. The Tribunal must therefore consider whether there would be an unreasonable disclosure of the personal affairs of any such person. The Tribunal has no evidence before it on this account and must bear in mind s.3 of the Act which states that the object of the Act is to extend as far as possible the right of the Australian community to access information in the possession of the Commonwealth Government. Accordingly the Tribunal is not satisfied that there would be an unreasonable disclosure of personal affairs under s.41(1) of the Act and finds that the document, to the extent it contains details of a Titles Office search, should be disclosed.

Conclusion

(1) With respect to all but the following documents, the Tribunal

affirms the decision under review.

(2) With respect to the name of the officer of the Director of Public

Prosecutions in document 35 the Tribunal finds that it is not

exempt under the Act and, subject to an application to this

Tribunal being made within 28 days to stay disclosure, should

thereafter be released to the applicant.

(3) In the event of any application being made pursuant to the previous

Direction, the Tribunal shall make such further direction as it

sees fit.

(4) With respect to those parts of document 48 which consist of

information obtained from the Titles Office of Western Australia,

the claim for exemption on the basis of s.12(1)(b) of the Act is

not upheld.
