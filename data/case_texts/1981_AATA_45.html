Re Norma Jean Richardson and the Commissioner of Superannuation [1981] AATA 45 (5 June 1981)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: NORMA JEAN RICHARDSON

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR

SUPERANNUATION

No. V80/62

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mr R.K. Todd (Senior Member)

Mr V.J. Skermer (Member)

Dr M. Glick (Member)

CATCHWORDS

superannuation _ determination of retirement benefits _ issue of benefit classification certificate _ whether conditions specified were 'relevant for the superseded Act' _ physical and mental condition specified _ whether incapacity caused by or connected with a condition specified in certificate

Superannuation Act 1922 s.5, s.75

Superannuation Act 1976 s.66(2)(c), s.184(2), s.184(7)

HEARING

CANBERRA

5:6:1981

ORDER

The Tribunal decides to affirm the decision of the Acting Commissioner for Superannuation as varied on reconsideration:

(a) to issue pursuant to sub-section 184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 a benefit classification certificate in respect of the applicant specifying the conditions 'sinusitis, raised blood pressure, psoriasis, recurring upper and respiratory tract infection'.

(b) that under paragraph (c) of sub-section 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 the incapacity that was the ground for the applicant's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by a physical condition specified in the benefit classification certificate.

DECISION

1. This is an application for review of a decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation, confirmed upon reconsideration pursuant to s.154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act"). That decision had been to confirm as varied on reconsideration a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner:

(i) to issue pursuant to s.184(2) of the 1976 Act a Benefit Classification Certificate ("BCC") in respect of the applicant upon her ceasing to be an eligible employee on 8 January 1980 by reason of retirement on the grounds of invalidity before attaining a maximum retiring age of 65 years and having completed less than 20 years contributory service; and

(ii)that, pursuant to s.66(2)(c) of the 1976 Act, the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by a physical or mental condition specified in the BCC.

2. The applicant, who was born in 1939, had previously been an employee of the Commonwealth and a contributor to the Superannuation fund established pursuant to the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act"), but upon her marriage she had had, in terms of the then existing law, to resign and thus to cease contributing to the fund. Later, however, having commenced service as a temporary employee on 1 July 1972, she applied again for permanent appointment to the Public Service, and in March 1973 she was examined in that behalf by a Dr Burne, who held an appointment as a Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO").

3. For the purposes of the examination, the applicant answered a number of questions in a form furnished to her prior to the examination. The relevant questions, the applicant's answers thereto and Dr Burne's written comments in relation to them are as follows:

'(i) Bronchitis Yes cough with change of weather _ not requiring treatment

(ii) Nasal Antrum or Yes operation on sinus trouble nose/sinus 10 years ago. No troublesince that time

(iii)Details of any Psorisis head only illness or injury (scil not mentioned above Psoriasis)

(iv) When did you last Ear abscessdischarging ear,

receive medical or 1971 treated with anti-surgical

treatment biotics

and for what

condition?

(v) In the section headed Medical Officer's Report against the question 'Is there any skin disease?', Dr Burne wrote _ 'Rash, small patch above right ear only' which was plainly a reference to the Psoriasis.

(vi) In the section headed reasons for recommendation and remarks, Dr Burne wrote _ 'Accepted because psoriasis is confined to very small area about 2cm diameter hidden in the hairline above the right ear. I am not sure it is psoriasis, except stated by the patient, and it is quite invisible.'

4. This report came under the attention of the Senior Medical Officer, Department of Health, Melbourne, a Dr Sullivan, who is no longer alive. On 14 May 1973, Dr Sullivan wrote on the report completed by Dr Burne, under the concluding remarks set out in paragraph 2.(vi) above, the following comment:

'Re sinus trouble, re 'cough with change of weather' and 'psoriasis'. Consider unfit for Super Fund. However, as it seems likely that she will provide 3 years of useful service, recommended to accept to Provident Account.'

5. Permanent appointment of the applicant was, by a decision of the Acting Commonwealth Public Service Inspector, deferred until February 1974.

6. On 25 May 1973, an officer of the Australian Post Office sent to the Commonwealth Public Service Inspector (Victoria) a 'Recommendation Following Medical Fitness Report' which contained inter alia the following statements:

'In view of the disclosure/s at the medical examination which is/are as follows:-

(1) history of sinus trouble

(2) cough with change of weather

(3) psoriasis

it is recommended that:

...

He/She be accepted as a Superannuation Fund contributor

...'

This document presents obvious difficulties. It is consistent with Dr Burne's recommendation, but looks odd because, unlike Dr Burne's 'Reasons for Recommendation' it mentions two other conditions beyond psoriasis, and the presence of all three makes it unlikely that the applicant would in all the circumstances be accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund. It is not consistent either with Dr Sullivan's recommendation that the applicant should be accepted for the Provident Account.

7. In these circumstances, it is hardly surprising that in June 1979 the Acting Commonwealth Public Service Inspector wrote to the Director, Posts and Telegraphs, Melbourne, as follows:

'Mrs Richardson's medical papers have been examined and I have decided that a decision on her medical fitness will be deferred until February 1974 when another examination should be done.'

The Superintendent, Personnel Section, Australian Post Office, then advised the applicant that her medical report indicated that 'a routine medical examination in 7 months time will be necessary'.

8. On 22 February 1974, the applicant was examined by Dr Sullivan. His clinical notes of this examination were read to the Tribunal in evidence by Dr McInnes, who is referred to later, in the following terms:

'Refer for review re sick leave; gets cough with change of weather. 1963 puncture of antrum. 1971 abscess in one ear or conceivably two abscesses in one ear. 1968 gets rash in scalp, psoriasis. She is apparently pre-disposed to upper respiratory tract infections. Then the words 'ears, heart, lungs, abdomen' appear with a blood pressure 155/105. Provident account only.'

9. From the applicant's evidence it appears that Dr Sullivan's examination paid little or perhaps no attention at all to her psoriasis. He was mainly concerned, she said, with her bronchitis. At all events, the medical report which he made read as follows:

'Mrs Richardson was examined on 22.2.74. She has a history of some years of sinusitis. In 1963 she had puncture and washing out of antra. She has obviously a predisposition to recurring upper respiratory tract infections. BP 155/100. Other systems are normal.

Because of recurring upper respiratory tract infections, she is considered an unfavourable risk for the Super Fund. It is anticipated that she will provide 3 years of satisfactory service. Hence it is recommended that she be accepted to the Provident Account.'

10. After this an important document (Exhibit T5,p25) was prepared by a person described as 'Delegate of the Superannuation Board and the Public Service Board'. It was addressed to the Secretary, Superannuation Board, and had the letterhead 'Australian Post Office'. It was a 'Determination of Medical Fitness on Appointment' and advised, inter alia, as follows:

'The abovenamed appointee has been accepted as meeting the requirements of section 34(b) of the Public Service Act and is also accepted as a contributor to either the Superannuation Fund or Provident Account as indicated above with effect from the date of this advice or, the date of taking up duty following appointment, whichever is the later. However, the appointee may still be subject to further medical review duuring the period of probation.'

The indication referred to was, by a crossing out of the words 'Superannuation Fund', an indication that acceptance was for the Provident Account.

11. In March 1974 the applicant received from the Superintendent, Personnel Branch, Australian Post Office, Notice of her 'Permanent Appointment' and advice of her 'Acceptance as Provident Account Contributor'. This document (Exhibit T5, p26) contained the following statement:

'Reference is made to your probationary appointment with this Department. When the offer of appointment was issued, you were notified that you would be required to contribute to the Superannuation Fund or Provident Account.

The reports of the medical examination which you have since undergone shows that your medical fitness is below the standard set down for admission to the Superannuation Fund. However, the Public Service Inspector has determined your appointment may proceed subject to you becoming a contributor to the Provident Account, the benefits and rates of contribution for which are described briefly below.

Should no advice be received from you within seven days from the date of this letter, it will be assumed you have no objections to appointment under Provident Account conditions.'

12. The applicant's employment was as a telephonist with the Australian Post Office as it was then known. This was the same work as that which she had performed in her previous employment with the Commonwealth. For a while all was well, but after working for a period of time at a telephone exchange she was sent to work at '1100' with the result that she spent all day listening to complaint after complaint. Her nervous state caused the psoriasis to flare up. It is enough to say that the condition was undoubtedly distressing and serious. She was medically examined in November 1979 in relation to her continued employment or retirement on invalidity grounds by Dr McInnes. His conclusion at the time was that the applicant was unfit for continued employment and should be retired on the grounds of invalidity, a conclusion which on the evidence before us was unquestionably correct. The retirement conditions were psoriasis 70 per cent and anxiety neurosis 30 per cent.

13. The relevant Medical Standards in relation to skin diseases are stated as follows:

'136. Skin diseases such as scabies, generalized eczema, psoriasis, call for deferment or rejection of the candidate, according to the degrees of affection and amenability to treatment.

137. Apart from the general question, common to all disabilities, as to whether the disease is likely to prevent efficient service to retiring age, several special points need consideration in the case of candidates who give any history of skin diseases.

(a) Liability to Recurrence

Particularly in the various types of 'Dermatitis' there is great tendency to recurrence, sometimes in a very intractable form. It is necessary to inquire very closely into such cases and if the history shows this tendency, rejection or at least deferment for the maximum period of 11 months, for observation is indicated.

(b) ...

(c) Appearance

Where there is any disfiguring rash apparent on hands or face, even though the condition may not be of medical significance, consideration has to be given to the nature of the intended duties from the aspect of the reasons of either fellow employees or the public who are likely to fear infection from such case. Thus, it would be impossible to employ such persons, say on Post Office counter work or even as Telephonists.

A skin disease such as psoriasis warrants rejection if the lesions are easily visible, e.g. on the arms, as colleagues often object to working alongside anyone with an obvious skin disease. Acne should also be rejected if severe, mild cases should be deferred for treatment." (underlining supplied)

14. The applicant had been subjected to psoriasis from possibly as early as 1968, but certainly from 1971, and she had been treated for it. At the point when she was examined by Dr Burne, the psoriasis was so minor that Dr Burne was not even sure that it was psoriasis. Had the applicant said nothing about it it may never have come into question, although it is fair to say that if, when she indicated to Dr McInnes that she had had psoriasis for seven or eight years, he had discovered that she had never revealed it at her examinations, the matter would still have come up for determination because of the provisions of s.184 of the 1976 Act insofar as they deal with nondisclosure of conditions which existed at entry or of which there was then a history. But be that as it may, the applicant, who has displayed by her actions that she is a person of the highest integrity, made it quite clear at all examinations that she had had psoriasis and that she had been treated for it. We consider that there is no doubt that psoriasis has a direct connection with the anxiety state that was referred to by Dr McInnes. It is undoubtedly also the same condition for which she had attended for treatment prior to her examination by Dr Burne. It follows that, if the condition psoriasis should be seen as properly specified in the BCC, it would be then clear that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement (psoriasis as to 70 per cent and anxiety neurosis 30 per cent) was caused or was at least substantially contributed to by a physical condition specified in the BCC, namely psoriasis. In simple terms, the Tribunal would have no doubt on the evidence that there was a clear link between what was the dominant retirement condition and a condition specified in the benefit classification certificate. A much more difficult question, however, arises in relation to the question posed by s.184(2)(c) of the 1976 Act:

'(2) Subject to sub-section (4), where _

(a) ...

(b) ...

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the death of the person or the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the person was caused, or was substantially contributed to _

(i) by the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, by those conditions or one or more of those conditions; or

(ii) by a physical or mental condition or conditions of the person connected with the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, with those conditions or one or more of those conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the person a benefit classification certificate in which there is or are specified the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(4) and to have been in force in respect of the person immediately before his death or retirement.'

The question therefore is whether the condition psoriasis is properly to be seen as having been 'a condition relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act' having regard to the evidence stated above.

15. In terms of ordinary English language, it would be quite clear that psoriasis was 'a condition relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act', because what the provisions of the superseded Act, namely the 1922 Act, were concerned with was the question whether the health and physical fitness of the person were such as to justify that person being accepted as a contributor to the fund under Part 3 of that Act, to the Provident Account or, by inference, to neither (see s.5 of the 1922 Act). The proven condition of the applicant for which she had sought treatment is described above, and, in the light of the medical standards to which we have referred, must assuredly have been relevant for the purposes of the 1922 Act. However, the provisions of s.184(7) of the 1976 Act provided an exhaustive definition of the phrase 'condition ... relevant for the purpose of the superseded Act', in the following terms:

'(c) a reference to the physical or mental condition or conditions of a person that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act shall be read as a reference to the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, was or were the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person by reason of which _

(1) The Superannuation Board was not satisfied under sub-section 5(1) of the superseded Act that the health and physical fitness of the person were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the fund; ..."

C41G16. This definition thus requires a reference not to a condition of the person that is merely proved to have existed and to have been a condition to which reference might properly have been made, but rather to a condition that has actually been taken into account by the Superannuation Board for the purposes of coming to a conclusion as to the health and physical fitness of the person. In the case of this applicant, a strong case can be made, and was made for her at the hearing, that the condition psoriasis was not a condition which, although revealed and noted at her initial medical examination and referred to by Dr Sullivan when he considered the report, was taken into account when the decision was made in 1974 as to whether or not she should be accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund. It was said that after the initial medical examination, and Dr Sullivan's consideration thereof in March 1973, the only consideration given to psoriasis was contained in the reference thereto by Dr Sullivan made in his clinical notes, a reference which was not carried into the medical report which he made on 22 February 1974 which is set out above or into any other administrative consideration of the matter which was placed before the Tribunal. Cogent as this submission in many ways was we do not think that it should be accepted, for reasons which we now state.

17. The procedures to which the applicant became subjected in 1973 and 1974 involved both her appointment as a permanent officer of the Commonwealth Public Service and her acceptance as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund, noting that by reason of the provisions of s.75 of the 1922 Act the Provident Account formed part of the Superannuation Fund. The doctors who examined her did not decide anything. Dr Burne conducted an examination and made a recommendation, and Dr Sullivan qualified that recommendation. Neither was given effect, the Public Service Inspector having intervened. The applicant's re-examination on 22 February 1974 was 'a medical re-examination in connection with the permanent appointment of' the applicant. The doctor was asked to 'report on the appointee's physical condition with regard to fitness for appointment, with specific reference being made to the condition which necessitated re-examination'. He did not entirely comply with the latter request, for when he did report he did not mention psoriasis. And the Medical Standards which we have set out above indicate that existence of psoriasis would call for deferment or rejection, not simply for special attention as to whether an applicant should be permitted to contribute to the Provident Account rather than the Superannuation Fund. The final document in the chain (the notice of permanent appointment which is set out in paragraph 10 above) proceeds upon an assumption that the real question was one as between acceptance as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund on the one hand or to the Provident Account on the other. Once again, we see an uneasy relationship between the part of the administration that was relevant to appointment to the Public Service and the part that was relevant to superannuation conditions. But the striking facts here are:

(i) that the applicant had had, and had freely revealed, a history of psoriasis;

(ii) that psoriasis in terms of the Medical Standards is regarded as serious enough to warrant 'deferment or rejection';

(iii) that in his initial review Dr Sullivan included psoriasis in the group of conditions that led him to consider her as 'unfit for Superannuation Fund';

(iv) that the Public Inspector then required deferment and re-examination, seemingly for the purpose of considering whether she should be appointed at all. There was at that point, having regard to Dr Sullivan's comments, no question of her being accepted for the Superannuation Fund;

(v) that when the delegate of the Superannuation Board made his decision as referred to in paragraph 10 above, he had access to all of the medical reports that had been made.

18. In view of the matters set out in the preceding paragraph, the only factor tending against a conclusion that psoriasis was a condition that, in the defined sense, was 'relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act' is that Dr Sullivan did not mention psoriasis in his report of 22 February 1974. But it is to be remembered that at that time there was no question of a CMO having to, as it were, list conditions for the purpose of their being set out in a BCC. The provisions relating to BCCs did not come into existence until 1 July 1976, and there are many difficulties involved in drawing conclusions from actions that were taken in contemplation of a system not then involving the concept of a benefit classification certificate. He had simply to make a recommendation, presumably upon sufficient grounds, for the purpose of a decision as to acceptance for the Superannuation Fund, for the Provident Account, or, in the case of a person unfit for permanent appointment, for neither. Dr Sullivan's clear conclusion in February 1974 was, as it was on the earlier occasion, that the applicant should be accepted only for the Provident Account, a conclusion that in the end was, unfortunately, clearly vindicated. The fact that he did not in his final consideration of the matter refer specifically again to psoriasis does not in our view tend to a conclusion that he had decided that it was no longer relevant. We should add that we think it correct to say that, given the medical standards, the best possible decision that could have been made from the applicant's point of view was in fact made, namely acceptance for the Provident Account.

19. In view of the difficulties that were apparent in this matter, the applicant was fully justified in making the application for review, but in all the circumstances and despite the attractive and thoughtful manner of presentation of the case by the applicant and her husband, we feel compelled to affirm the decision the subject of the application for review.
