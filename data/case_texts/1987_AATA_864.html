ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - deductions - expenses of borrowing money – money borrowed at 13 per cent - on-lent at 1 per cent - application of Ure's Case

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss 51(1), 67

Ure v FC of T 81 ATC 4100

COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Nos AT86/325-328

AAT Decision No 3948

Deputy President RK Todd

27 November 1987

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) Nos AT86/325-328

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

DECISION

Tribunal : Deputy President RK Todd

Date : 27 November 1987

Place : Canberra

Decision : The Tribunal varies the objection decisions of the respondent by allowing further deductions of:

(a) $186 in respect of the year of income ended 30 June 1979

(b) $216 in respect of the year of income ended 30 June 1980

(c) $144 in respect of the year of income ended 30 June 1981; and

(d) $289 in respect of the year of income ended 30 June 1982.

2. The Tribunal otherwise affirms the objection decisions of the respondent.

R K TODD

Deputy President

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) Nos AT86/325-328

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

27 November 1987 Deputy President RK Todd

  1. The applicant in this reference is a medical practitioner who was dissatisfied with the Commissioner's decisions on objection relating to his taxation returns for the years of income ended 30 June 1979 to 1982.
  2. The Commissioner's representative argued that the facts proved in this reference fell squarely within the decision in Ure v FC of T 81 ATC 4100, and that the reasoning of the Federal Court in that case should be applied. The applicant's representative proposed to distinguish Ure's Case.
  3. In dispute are interest payments paid by the applicant in each of the above years, and also "borrowing expenses" which, pursuant to s67 of the Income Tax Assessment Act (1936) ("the ITAA"), have been apportioned over the life of the loan hereafter described for the purpose of the claimed deductions.
  4. An agreed Statement of Facts, which I now set out, was tendered at the hearing:

"1. On or about 9 October 1978 [the applicant M] borrowed $93,000 from Permanent Trustees Co. Ltd.

  1. The loan was for a period of 3 years with repayments of interest only at an interest rate of 13% if paid on the due date, otherwise an interest rate of 15% applied to the loan.
  2. Security for the loan was a mortgage over the taxpayer's own home.
  3. The proceeds of the loan funds were then lent to T Pty. Limited as trustee for the M Family Trust in the amount of $90,000. The loan was at an interest rate of 1% per annum.
  4. Interest was paid to the taxpayer in each of the 1979, 1980, 1981 and 1982 income years.
  5. T Pty Ltd as trustee for the M Family Trust then used those funds (i.e. the $90,000) to invest in a joint venture with M-L Pty Ltd.
  6. The joint venture used the funds along with other[s] for the development of a unit site in G, NSW.
  7. The terms of the joint venture agreement were that units would be constructed on the site and the M Family Trust would then take one unit in return for its investment.
  8. During the 1980 income year the joint venture terminated with a profit to the M Family Trust of $14,790 and rental being derived of $570 from the unit.
  9. During the 1981 and 1982 income years the M Family Trust derived rental income from the unit.
  10. At the termination of the loan period, the principal was repaid.
  11. During the 1979, 1980, 1981 and 1982 income years the taxpayer was not a beneficiary of the M Family Trust.
  12. On 28 June 1983 the applicant became a beneficiary of the M Family Trust and participated in the distribution of income for the 1983 income year in the amount of $16,230."

5. Oral evidence given by the applicant expanded on these facts and provided the necessary detail. I accept the evidence of the applicant and find the additional facts to be as follows:

(1) The loan (from Permanent Trustees) was made to the applicant, using his house as security, due to advice received by the applicant from a bank manager.

(2) The interest rate of 1 per cent in respect of the loan to the Trust was suggested by the applicant's accountant. The applicant merely followed that advice when setting the rate.

(3) The major source of income for the applicant's household at the time of the loan was his earnings from his medical practice. Apart from "a few dividends from shares" there was virtually no other income being received by the household.

(4) The trust was a discretionary trust. Until the applicant joined the trust in June 1983, the beneficiaries of the trust were his wife and his three children.

(5) Whilst the applicant did not enter into the arrangement as a tax avoidance scheme, the taxation implications of the arrangements were prominent considerations when the structuring of the loans took place.

(6) The applicant, in all aspects of the transactions, received and acted upon professional advice.

6. In Ure's Case a Full Court of the Federal Court was faced with a similar arrangement. In that case Brennan J stated at 4104:

"Section 51 requires that a deductible expenditure be incurred 'in' gaining assessable income, that is to say, it must be incidental and relevant to the gaining of that income (Ronpibon Tin NL & Tongkah Compound NL v FC of T [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at p56). An outgoing of interest may be incidental and relevant to the gaining of assessable income where the borrowed money is laid out for the purpose of gaining that income (FC of T v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153 at pp. 170, 171, 197; Texas Co (Australasia) Ltd v FC of T [1940] HCA 9; (1940) 63 CLR 382 at p468). The laying out of the borrowed money for the purpose of gaining assessable income furnishes the required connection between the interest paid upon it by the taxpayer and the income derived by him from its use.

In the present case a question arises as to whether it can truly be said that the incurring of interest charges at rates of 7.5%, 8.5%, 10% and 12.5% per annum is incidental to the gaining of income by way of interest at the rate of 1% per annum. The answer to that question does not turn directly upon the disparity in interest rates, but upon an examination of the purposes for which the money was laid out. The disparity in interest rates is itself eloquent to suggest the existence of purposes ulterior to the earning of interest at the rate of 1% per annum, and the evidence confirms the existence of further purposes. The loans of the borrowed moneys made by Mr Ure were calculated to achieve the further purposes of providing some income to Mrs Ure, of enhancing the profits of Listohan, of providing Mr Ure and his family with the Epping residence at a nominal rental, and of enabling the Joan Honeybourne Trust to take the benefit of any increment in the capital value of the Epping property and to enjoy the rents and profits of that property when it became available for letting at a commercial rental.

Of course, the achievement of these further purposes required the co-operative action of Mrs Ure, Listohan and Weston, but where a question arises as to the purpose for which money is laid out by a taxpayer, it is erroneous to exclude as irrelevant evidence of the use of that money, albeit by others, in conformity with arrangements made by the taxpayer. That is not to deny the separate identity of those who use the borrowed money, nor to attribute vicariously to the taxpayer the activities of others. It is simply to take some relevant factors into account in determining the purposes for which money is laid out. Those purposes do not depend upon the state of the taxpayer's mind but upon what the taxpayer in the circumstances of the case is ascertained to have done in using and arranging for the use of the borrowed moneys.

The purposes for which money is laid out is an issue of fact, turning upon the objective circumstances which human experience would judge to be relevant to the issue (cf Magna Alloys and Research Pty Ltd v FC of T 80 ATC 4542 at p4549). In the present case, there is an air of unreality about the proposition that the borrowed moneys were laid out wholly for the purpose of earning a return of 1% per annum. Rather is it right to say that the purpose for which the borrowed moneys were laid out included all the purposes earlier mentioned, only one of which was to earn a return of 1% per annum.

If the borrowed moneys had been laid out solely for the purpose of gaining assessable income, the interest would be wholly deductible; but as they were laid out in part for that purpose, and in part for other purposes, the interest charges must be apportioned."

  1. The applicant's representative attempted to distinguish this case. The basis of these attempts was largely the final use made of the borrowed money. In Ure's Case the money was used to purchase the home in which the taxpayer then resided, whereas in the present case the money was invested in a commercial enterprise which then returned profits to the trust. Therefore, it was submitted, whereas in Ure's Case there was the hidden benefit of the provision of housing to the taxpayer, there was no such hidden benefit in this case.
  2. While I accept that such was the fact, other benefits present in this case were provision of income to the applicant's wife and children in addition to a reduction in his personal taxation liability. I find that these are in fact the dominant reasons for entrance upon the transaction and I therefore find that Ure's Case is clearly applicable.
  3. Applying the decision in Ure's Case to the facts before the Tribunal in this review, I find that the interest incurred by the applicant is only deductible to the extent of the income produced. As this amount was allowed as a deduction on assessment, no variation need be made to that amount.
  4. Section 67 of the Act, as far as relevant, states:

"67.(1) Subject to this section, so much of the expenditure incurred by the taxpayer in borrowing money used by him for the purpose of producing assessable income as bears to the whole of that expenditure the same proportion as the part of the period for which the money was borrowed that is in the year of income bears to the whole of that period shall be an allowable deduction."

The amounts incurred as borrowing expenses I find to be deductible in full under s67, although the deduction is to be spread over the life of the loan. I am clearly bound, in this respect, by the Federal Court decision in Ure's Case in which, at 4,113, Deane and Sheppard JJ stated:

"Section 67(1) does not refer to the use made by persons other than the taxpayer of the money borrowed by the taxpayer. It refers to the use made of that money by the taxpayer himself. In the present case, the only use which the taxpayer himself made of the moneys borrowed was to lend them either to his wife or to Listohan at a rate of interest of 1% per annum. In these circumstances, we consider that the moneys were used by the taxpayer for the purpose of earning assessable income. It follows that the taxpayer was entitled to a deduction, pursuant to sec 67(1) of the Act, in respect of the appropriate proportion of the guarantee fees, valuation fees and legal expenses.

Their Honours went on to make it clear that the apportionment was an apportionment over the period of the various loans, not an apportionment between private and business purposes. On the face of s67, as it then was, it can be seen that no apportionment between different objects was envisaged. An amendment, effected by the inclusion of s67(4), was made in 1984 to allow such apportionment.

  1. At the hearing the Commissioner's representative tendered an agreed table of the amounts that would be allowable for borrowing expenses if they were found to be deductible. These amounts are $186 in fiscal 1979, $216 in fiscal 1980, $144 in fiscal 1981 and $289 in fiscal 1982.
  2. The objection decisions of the respondent will be varied to allow the above borrowing expenses.
