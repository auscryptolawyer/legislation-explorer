Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Government Insurance Office of New South Wales and Hussein Daoui [1993] FCA 464; (1993) 117 ALR 61, (1993) 93 Atc 4901 (1993) 26 Atr 544 (20 September 1993)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION v. GOVERNMENT INSURANCE OFFICE OF NEW SOUTH

WALES and HUSSEIN DAOUI

No. G468 of 1992

FED No. 653

Number of pages - 18

Bankruptcy - Income Tax - Constitutional Law

[1993] FCA 464; (1993) 117 ALR 61, (1993) 93 ATC 4901

[1993] FCA 464; (1993) 26 ATR 544

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

JENKINSON(1), HILL(2) AND BEAZLEY(3) JJ

CATCHWORDS

Bankruptcy - Discharge of bankruptcy - whether Deputy Commissioner of Taxation a secured creditor where notice served under s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 for the payment of moneys to become due by GIO to bankrupt and judgment given in personal injuries case after bankrupt's discharge from bankruptcy.

Income Tax - Notice under s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - whether recipient of notice required to make payment to Deputy Commissioner under notice where tax debt released consequent upon a discharge of the bankruptcy of the taxpayer.

Constitutional Law - Income Tax - whether s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 invalid as infringing s.55 of the Constitution - whether a law imposing tax or a law facilitating the recovery of tax.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936: s.218.

Constitution 1901: s.55.

Bankruptcy Act 1966: ss.153, 5, 116.

Commissioner of Taxation v Donnelly (1989) 25 FCR 432; applied.

Clyne v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 40; (1981) 150 CLR 1; discussed.

Re Lind (1915) 2 Ch 345; discussed.

DECISION

JENKINSON J I have had the advantage of reading the reasons for judgment of Hill J.

2. I understand His Honour's conclusion, that when Mr. Daoui was discharged from his bankruptcy the Commonwealth was not "a secured creditor" within the meaning of that expression in s.153(3)(a) of the Bankruptcy Act 1966, to rest on two propositions: first, that until a debt is due by the person served with a notice pursuant to s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 there can be effected by the service of the notice no "mortgage, charge or lien on property of the debtor as a security for" the debt due in respect of tax, within the meaning of that phrase in the definition provided by s.5 of the Bankruptcy Act; and, second, that Mr. Daoui's cause of action for personal injury was not assignable and therefore could not be the subject of any such a mortgage, charge or lien.

3. In my opinion In re Lind (1915) 2 Ch 345 does not admit of a confident acceptance that the first proposition is correct or that it supports the conclusion. What that case decided, and its standing as an authority in this country, are in my opinion correctly stated by Meagher Gummow and Lehane in Equity Doctrines and Remedies (3rd. ed.) paras. (654) - (665). The assignments in question in that case were of a mere expectancy : the assignor's share of his living mother's estate if she were to die intestate. Each assignment was made as security for repayment of money lent to the assignor. His bankruptcy, and his discharge from that bankruptcy, followed the assignments and preceded his mother's death, intestate. The learned authors state (para. 664):

"The Court of Appeal had also to deal, in Re Lind, supra, with the

argument that because (the assignor's) personal obligation to

repay (the assignees) was released by his discharge (as it

undoubtedly was) the assignments, being merely ancillary to that

obligation, were released also. The argument was fairly easily

disposed of. The whole purpose of the assignment was to obtain a

security which was intended not to be merely ancillary to the

personal obligation to repay, but to be available in the event

that the personal obligation was of no - or at least insufficient

- avail: per Swinfen Eady LJ at 360-1 and Bankes LJ at 371;

Robinson v. Ommanney (1882) 21 ChD 780."

Notwithstanding the definition of the expression "secured creditor" (in the English Bankruptcy Act 1883, indistinguishable from the Australian Act), the assignees were held to be able, after the discharge of the assignor from bankruptcy and after his mother's death, to enforce the assignments as securities for the moneys lent.

4. I accept the statements by Hill J in his reasons for judgment as to what was decided by the majority of the Full Court in Commissioner of Taxation v. Donnelly (1989) 25 FCR 432. But what was decided in Donnelly's Case is not in my opinion inconsistent with the application of the reasoning in Re Lind to the circumstances of this case. To avoid the conclusion to which Re Lind points in this case - that when judgment was entered for Mr. Daoui in respect of his personal injury claim the effect of the service of the notice under s.218 on the Government Insurance Office of New South Wales was to charge the amount, for which judgment had been entered and which was payable by that insurer, in its hands with payment of the amount of tax specified in the notice - it is necessary to go beyond what was under consideration in Donnelly's Case.

5. It is to be observed that s.153(3) of the Bankruptcy Act does not preclude the operation of s.153(1). Mr. Daoui's discharge from bankruptcy did operate to release him from the tax debt, by force of s.153(1). The tax debt is by s.153(3) "deemed not to have been released by the discharge of" Mr. Daoui "for the purposes of enabling the secured creditor .... so to realize or deal with his security, but not otherwise". But, although one may think (as Blackburn J thought in Thomson v. Cohen 1872) LR 7 QB 527 at 533) that once the debt is gone a security for its payment should be gone, it is Re Lind which has prevailed. That case is cited by Deane J as one of the authorities supporting the propositions stated in the passage from his judgment in The Commissioner of Taxation v. Everett [1978] FCA 39; (1978) 38 FLR 26 at 50-51 which was cited by Hill J in Donnelly's Case 25 FCR at 459. And in Palette Shoes Pty. Ltd. v. Krohn 1937) 58 CLR 1 at 27 Sir Owen Dixon propounded the same propositions thus:

"As the subject to be made over does not exist, the matter

primarily rests in contract. Because value has been given on the

one side, the conscience of the other party is bound when the

subject comes into existence, that is, when, as is generally the

case, the legal property vests in him. Because his conscience is

bound in respect of a subject of property, equity fastens upon the

property itself and makes him a trustee of the legal rights or

ownership for the assignee. But, although the matter rests

primarily in contract, the prospective right in property which the

assignee obtains 'is a higher right than the right to have

specific performance of a contract,' and it may survive the

assignor's bankruptcy because it attaches without more eo instanti

when the property arises and gives the assignee an equitable

interest therein. (In re Lind; Industrials Finance Syndicate Ltd.

v. Lind (1915) 2 Ch, at pp 364, 365, 366). In that case Swinfen

Eady LJ describes the effect of the decisions thus: - 'It is

clear from these authorities that an assignment for value of

future property actually binds the property itself directly it is

acquired - automatically on the happening of the event, and

without any further act on the part of the assignor - and does not

merely rest in, and amount to, a right in contract, giving rise to

an action. The assignor, having received the consideration,

becomes in equity, on the happening of the event, trustee for the

assignee of the property devolving upon or acquired by him, and

which he had previously sold and been paid for' : (1915) 2 Ch, at

p 360."

The statutory provisions in s.218 must be regarded as overcoming the lack of consideration.

6. The second of the two grounds for the conclusion of Hill J that when Mr. Daoui was discharged from his bankruptcy the Commonwealth was not a secured creditor entitled to the benefit of s.153(3) of the Bankruptcy Act - that Mr. Daoui's cause of action could not be the subject of assignment or charge - should in my opinion be examined in the light of Glegg v. Bromley (1912) 3 KB 474. In that case Mrs. Glegg, being plaintiff in an action against H. for false representation and plaintiff in another action for slander against the defendant Bromley, assigned to her husband "all that interest, sum of money or premises to which she is or may become entitled under or by virtue of the said action of Glegg v. Bromley or under or by virtue of any verdict compromise or agreement which she may obtain or to which she may become party in or consequent upon the said action or otherwise howsoever under or by reason of the same To hold the same .... subject to redemption on payment of all moneys due to him". Her action against H was thereafter dismissed and she was ordered to pay H 218 pounds costs. Then in her action against Bromley she obtained a judgment for 200 pounds. H obtained a garnishee order to attach those damages in respect of the debt due to him for his costs. An interpleader issue was directed to try the validity of the assignment to Mr. Glegg. Parker J said ((1912) 3 KB at 489-491):

"I also am of opinion that this appeal ought to succeed. In the

first place, I think that according to the true construction of

the mortgage in question the subject-matter assigned is money or

other property which might thereafter be acquired by means of the

pending action for slander. It is not an existing chose in

action, but future property identified by reference to an existing

chose in action. It is, I think, necessary to bear this

distinction in mind, particularly in view of the course taken by

the argument of counsel for the respondent. Ordinary choses in

action were not assignable at law, but were, generally speaking,

assignable in equity whether themselves legal or equitable choses.

In the former case equity compelled the assignor to allow his name

to be used for their recovery in legal proceedings, in the latter

case the assignee could sue in equity in his own name. There was

one exception to this rule. Equity on the ground of public policy

did not give validity to the assignment of what is in the cases

referred to as a bare right of action, and this was so whether the

bare right were legal or equitable. I have looked at a good many

authorities on that point, and I am satisfied that the real reason

why equity did not allow the assignment of a bare right of action,

whether legal or equitable, was on the ground that it savoured of

or was likely to lead to maintenance. There is no doubt in the

cases about the rule, and there is no doubt in the cases with

regard to the exception, but difficulties often arose in deciding

whether a particular right was within the exception or was within

the rule. It is to be observed that an equitable assignee of a

chose in action, whether it is legal or equitable, could institute

proceedings and maintain proceedings for its recovery. The

question was whether the subject-matter of the assignment was, in

the view of the Court, property with an incidental remedy for its

recovery, or was a bare right to bring an action either at law or

in equity. With regard to the assignments of future property,

they stand, I think, on a totally different footing. Nothing

passes, even in equity, until the property comes into present

existence. Only when this happens can the assignment attach and

an interest pass. It is clear, therefore, that no question of

maintenance can ever arise. Thus, in the present case, as I

construe the mortgage, the mortgagor remains at liberty to proceed

or not with the slander action as she may think fit. The

mortgagee has no right and is under no duty to interfere. The

mortgage, therefore, is open to no objection on the ground of

maintenance, nor is it open to any objection on the ground of

champerty. Champerty, after all, is only a particular form of

maintenance. This appears to be reasonably clear from such cases

as Spry v. Porter (1856 7 E. and B. 58 and Rees v. De Bernardy

(1896) 2 Ch 437. Even a solicitor who is conducting an action or

suit may take a mortgage on the fruits for the purpose of securing

the payment of his proper costs. He may not be able to purchase

an interest in such fruits because of the doctrine of champerty.

This doctrine is well established by the case of Davis v. Freethy

(1890 24 QB 519. I can find no trace in the authorities of any

distinction having been drawn in this respect between actions

based on personal tort and other actions; nor do I see why on

principle there should be any such distinction. I am of opinion,

therefore, that there is nothing to avoid the mortgage on the

ground of public policy. For every equitable assignment, however,

there must be consideration. If there be no consideration, there

can be no equitable assignment."

7. If, as the language of s.218 would perhaps suggest, it is only the fruits of a cause of action which service of a notice under the section charges or assigns, there is in my opinion no reason, except one to be discussed hereafter, why the reasoning upon which Re Lind rests should not be applied to justify the conclusion that what Sir Owen Dixon called the prospective right in property is of that species of "higher right" which may survive the taxpayer's bankruptcy.

8. Hill J states in his reasons for judgment that "(i)t is unlikely that the Parliamentary intention was that the recipient of a notice should make payment to the Commissioner where tax is no longer payable in cases where it would then be incumbent upon the taxpayer to seek to recover from the Commissioner the amount wrongly paid. In my view the proper construction of s.218 is that the words "the amount due by the taxpayer in respect of tax" refers to so much of the tax due and owing by the taxpayer immediately upon the issue of the s.218 notice as remains due and owing from time to time. So construed the section can be given a sensible operation". I agree. If that be the proper construction of s.218, the application of the reasoning of Re Lind to the statutory charge should perhaps be abandoned in order to ensure conformity with the section. But,if that view were taken, the Commonwealth as a tax creditor would be, upon the debtor's discharge from bankruptcy, in worse case than all other secured creditors, including all other creditors whose securities derive from rights over property not in existence at the time of discharge. Those others' debts, like the Commonwealth's tax debt, will have been released upon discharge. But they, unlike the Commonwealth, will be able to enforce their securities when the property comes into existence. And before bankruptcy a debtor will be able effectively to charge and assign future property for valuable consideration in disregard of notices served under s.218, provided only that the future property comes into existence after his discharge from the bankruptcy impending when the notices are served and the assignments and charges are given. A Parliamentary intention to bring about that result is also unlikely.

9. On balance I prefer to allow in favour of the Commonwealth the application, to the charge effected by service of a notice under s.218, of the doctrine expounded in Re Lind, and to understand the expression "the amount due in respect of tax" in s.218(1) as comprehending a debt deemed by s.153(3) not to have been released by the discharge of the bankrupt. So to understand the expression can, I think, be said to be for the purpose of enabling the secured creditor to realize his security.

10. I agree with the reasons given by the learned judge against whose orders the appeal is brought and with the reasons given by Hill J concerning the solicitor's lien. In my opinion the orders made by the learned judge were correct. I would dismiss the appeal with costs. For the reasons given by Hill J, the cross-appeal should be dismissed with costs.

HILL J In this appeal, brought by the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation ("the Deputy Commissioner") against the judgment of a judge of this Court (Wilcox J), the chronology assures special significance.

2. On 4 December 1985 Mr Daoui, the second respondent in the appeal and cross-appellant, was injured in an accident which gave rise to a claim for damages. On 3 April 1986 the Deputy Commissioner, the appellant and cross-respondent, issued notices of assessment of income tax to Mr Daoui in respect of the 1980, 1981, 1982 and 1983 years of income. The total amount payable under those assessments was $48,742.13. That amount remains unpaid. On 1 July 1986 Mr Daoui commenced proceedings in the District Court in New South Wales against the Government Insurance Office of New South Wales, the first respondent ("GIO"), for damages arising out of a motor vehicle accident suffered by Mr Daoui in 1985. On 3 October 1986 the Deputy Commissioner forwarded to the GIO a notice under s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the ITA Act") indicating that Mr Daoui was indebted in the sum of $52,499.82 to the Commissioner and requiring the GIO to pay so much of any moneys which, inter alia, might thereafter become due by the GIO to Mr Daoui to the Deputy Commissioner up to the amount of $52,499.82.

3. On 14 July 1987 Mr Daoui became bankrupt upon the filing of his own petition. On 9 May 1989 the Deputy Commissioner lodged a proof of debt in the bankruptcy but that proof was not in fact dealt with by Mr Daoui's trustee in bankruptcy. On 15 July 1990 Mr Daoui was discharged from his bankruptcy. On 13 August 1991 Mr Daoui obtained judgment against the GIO for the sum of $10,793 plus costs.

4. The substantial question at issue between the parties in these circumstances was whether the GIO was obliged to pay to the Deputy Commissioner pursuant to the notice under s.218 of the ITA Act, the sum of $10,793 together with such amount as ultimately might be paid to him by way of indemnity for costs which he had incurred.

5. An additional complication that arose prior to the hearing of the case at first instance by Wilcox J was that it appeared that Mr Daoui owed the solicitor who had acted on his behalf in the District Court proceedings (Mr Dennis) an amount of money for the costs of acting in those proceedings and it was claimed by Mr Daoui that Mr Dennis had a special lien upon the moneys payable to Mr Daoui by GIO in respect of those costs. Unfortunately Mr Dennis never became a party to the present proceedings although on the appeal senior counsel for Mr Daoui indicated to the Court that the solicitor would abide by the decision of the Court so far as that related to the existence of his lien and whether that lien took priority over any security which the Deputy Commissioner may have arising out of the notice given by the Deputy Commissioner under s.218 of the ITA Act.

6. When the matter was litigated at first instance, there were a number of minor issues which were agitated but which no longer are relevant to the appeal. Accordingly I say nothing about them. Ultimately it was held that subject to the question of the lien of the solicitor, the Deputy Commissioner was entitled to payment of the moneys otherwise due by the GIO to Mr Daoui (the actual verdict money of $10,793 was paid by GIO into Court pursuant to an order made in the proceedings). However, his Honour held that the solicitor's lien took priority over the right of the Deputy Commissioner to be paid the money under the s.218 notice. In so holding his Honour rejected a submission, made on behalf of Mr Daoui, that s.218 of the ITA Act was invalid in so far as it created a charge because it imposed a tax contrary to the provisions of s.55 of the Constitution. In the result his Honour made declarations, effectively, that the GIO was bound by the s.218 notice, but that the right to payment of the Deputy Commissioner was subject to Mr Dennis's lien. His Honour made consequential orders relating to the taxation of the costs of the solicitor relevant to the equitable lien and a direction that the Registrar pay the moneys held in Court to the solicitor in payment of the taxed costs and subject thereto, pay the balance to the Deputy Commissioner. His Honour made no order as to the costs of the application.

7. The Deputy Commissioner appealed from that part of his Honour's judgment which held that the money held in Court was subject to an equitable lien to the solicitor and in respect of his Honour's failure to order costs. Mr Daoui cross-appealed in respect of that part of the judgment which found that the s.218 notice was operative and not invalid.

8. Ultimately on the appeal the issues were relatively confined. They may be stated as follows:

(1) Whether the fact that Mr Daoui's bankruptcy had been

discharged before moneys became payable by the GIO to him

under the judgment operated to exonerate the GIO from the

obligations otherwise imposed on it by the s.218 notice.

(2) If question (1) be answered in favour of the Deputy

Commissioner, whether Mr Dennis's lien took priority, as

Wilcox J held, over the charge or assignment worked by the

operation of the s.218 notice in favour of the Deputy

Commissioner.

(3) Further, if question (1) be answered in favour of the Deputy

Commissioner, whether s.218 of the ITA Act was invalid as

infringing s.55 of the Constitution.

9. The parties were in agreement that if the first question were decided in favour of Mr Daoui, then neither of the remaining questions arose. I turn therefore to deal with that question which was decided by Wilcox J in favour of the Deputy Commissioner. His Honour's reasons appear sufficiently from the following passage from the judgment appealed against.

"For there to be a valid s.218 notice, tax must

be due to the Commissioner. But once the notice

is served, it takes effect as a charge over any

monies then payable by the recipient to the

taxpayer or which thereafter become payable,

until such time (if ever) as notice of payment

is given by the Commissioner to the recipient

under s.218(5). The continuing efficacy of the

notice is not dependent upon the tax remaining

recoverable from the taxpayer. I apprehend, for

example, that if the outstanding tax was paid to

the Commissioner by the taxpayer, or by someone

else other than the recipient, and, being

unaware of this, the recipient made a payment to

the Commissioner pursuant to the notice, the

recipient would still be entitled to an

indemnity under s.218(4).

Once a charge comes into existence, it continues

to bind the charged monies until payment,

notwithstanding the supervening bankruptcy of

the debtor. The position is similar to that

discussed in a recent Full Court decision,

Puntoriero v Nickpack Pty Limited (Beaumont,

Wilcox and O'Loughlin JJ, 3 June 1992); except

that the Court was there concerned with an

equitable assignment rather than a charge. Nor

does it matter that the debtor, the person over

whose property the charge arises, has been

discharged from bankruptcy: see In re Lind.

Industrials Finance Syndicate, Lind v Lind

(1915) 2 Ch 345."

10. By force of s.153 of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 (Cth) ("the Act") the discharge of a bankrupt from a bankruptcy operates to release the bankrupt from all debts provable in the bankruptcy. However, that discharge does not affect the right of a secured creditor to realise or otherwise deal with his security if that secured creditor has not proved in the bankruptcy. In such circumstances the debt of the secured creditor is deemed not to have been released. It is agreed between the parties that the Deputy Commissioner did not ultimately prove in the bankruptcy.

11. It follows from s.153 that if the Deputy Commissioner was not a secured creditor at the time of the discharge the amount due to him will have been released.

12. Section 5 of the Act defines "secured creditor" as meaning:

"a person holding a mortgage, charge or lien on

property of the debtor as a security for a debt

due to him from the debtor."

13. Accordingly it is necessary to consider whether at the moment of discharge the Deputy Commissioner held a charge or lien on the "property" of Mr Daoui as a security for a debt due from Mr Daoui to the Deputy Commissioner.

14. Section 5 also defines "property" as meaning":

"real or personal property of every description,

whether situate in Australia or elsewhere, and

includes any estate, interest or profit, whether

present or future, vested or contingent, arising

out of or incident to any such real or personal property."

15. The definition of "property" contains two limbs. The first is concerned with real or personal property which has a situs either in Australia or elsewhere. Property not yet in existence would have no situs and thus fall outside the first limb. While the second limb includes, inter alia, future interests, such interests, estates, or profits must arise out of or be incident to "any such real or personal property", that is to say they must be incident to existing property having a situs either within or without Australia. While the chose in action which Mr Daoui had at the date of discharge may be seen to be property, the money which ultimately became payable to him was not at that date yet "property" as defined, because that money had not yet come into existence.

16. It then becomes relevant to consider the effect of s.218 of the ITA Act and in particular the questions whether it operates to create a security and if so over what property. In Clyne v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 40; (1981) 150 CLR 1, there was a difference of opinion as to the effect of s.218. Gibbs CJ expressed no opinion on the matter, but contented himself with saying (at 11) that once notice had been given under the section it operated:

"to prevent any subsequent dealing with the

money which will prevent compliance with the

notice when the time for compliance arrives."

17. Mason J, with whom Aickin and Wilson JJ agreed, on the other hand (at 18), expressed doubt whether a concession made by the Commissioner that the section did not operate as a charge had been correctly made. His Honour referred to Stapleton v FTS O'Donnell, Griffin and Co (Q) Pty Ltd [1961] HCA 70; (1961) 108 CLR 106 at 113, 117. Later in the judgment his Honour indicated that the effect of the notice was similar to that of a garnishee order, with the consequence that the assignee of a debt due from a bank to its customer took subject to equities which included the bank's obligation to pay under s.218 as creating a defence or a right of set-off by reason of the provisions of s.218(4).

18. Brennan J (at 26) spoke of s.218 as working:

"an assignment of the moneys to be paid to the

Commissioner as though the taxpayer had charged

the moneys otherwise payable to him with payment

of his tax liability."

19. His Honour referred also to Stapleton v FTS O'Donnell Griffin and Co (Q) Pty Ltd (supra) to which Mason J had also referred.

20. Speaking of the effect of s.218, Brennan J said (at 26-27):

"When a notice is given pursuant to the section,

it takes effect according to its tenor. The

third person is immediately bound to comply with

it, though his obligation is not to be

discharged until some time later. Between the

time when the notice is given and the time when

the obligation is to be discharged, the third

person is not at liberty to pay to the taxpayer

the money falling within the terms of the

notice; the third party is obliged to retain it

in order to discharge the obligation to pay the

money to the Commissioner in compliance with the

requirement expressed in the notice.

The notice precludes the taxpayer from receiving

money to the payment of which he would otherwise

be entitled or become entitled, and it requires

the third person in whose hands the money is or

into whose hands it comes to pay it to the

Commissioner at some time, but not earlier than

when it would otherwise be due and payable.

The giving of the notice thus affects the rights

of the taxpayer who, once the notice is given,

is statutorily divested of his right to payment

of the whole or a part of the money specified in

the notice. Whether the money is property in

existence when the notice is given or whether it

is property which comes into existence

thereafter, the third party is obliged to hold

and dispose of it in accordance with the notice...

The Commissioner's right to payment out of the

money in the hands of the third person

distinguishes him from a garnishor of a debt who

obtains no proprietary interest in the debt

owing to the judgment debtor, though he may

obtain execution against the garnishee."

21. Clyne's case differed from the present because at the time of the notice the bank owed to Mr Clyne an assignable chose in action, a debt, albeit that the monies payable to Mr Clyne under that chose in action were payable in the future. In the present case not only were, at the time of the discharge, monies only payable some time in the future, but they were payable pursuant to a chose in action sounding in tort, rather than in debt, and one that classically is regarded as non- assignable as being a bare right of litigation: Trendtex Trading Corporation v Credit Suisse (1982) AC 679 and cf Poulton v The Commonwealth (1953) 89 CLR 540 at 602.

22. It is no answer to say that an assignment of the fruits of an action as and when recovered may be effective, notwithstanding that an assignment of the bare right to litigate would not (cf Glegg v Bromley (1912) 3 KB 474). For the issue here is whether s.218 operates in respect of moneys payable (the fruits of the action) but only when, and so soon as monies become payable, or whether in a case such as the present it operates in respect of the bare right of litigation. If the fruits of the action, then its operation is deferred until those fruits are crystallised, but at which time there is no tax debt, for that has been discharged; if the bare right of litigation then the difficulty of the non-assignability of that bare right would have arisen for consideration.

23. In Commissioner of Taxation v Donnelly (1989) 25 FCR 432, a full court of this Court was required to consider whether the service of a notice under s.218 worked an "attachment" of a debt due to a debtor. It was a central issue of that case whether the issue of the s.218 notice created charges over debts due by the Health Insurance Commission to a debtor. Lockhart J expressed the view (at 436) that s.218 operated as a statutory assignment of the moneys payable by the recipient to the taxpayer in favour of the Commissioner in the nature of a charge over the moneys. Von Doussa J, who dissented in the result in that case, expressed the view (at 441-442) that s.218 operated only in a case where there was an identifiable debt owing to the taxpayer by the third party, whether payable forthwith or at a future time or on a contingency. Where the conditions for the operation of the section were not present at the time the notice was served the notice was, in his Honour's opinion, not binding when given, although it would take effect thereafter if the conditions were then fulfilled. On that view until an identifiable sum of money became owing to the taxpayer by the third party, the giving of the s.218 notice had no consequence.

24. After discussing in some detail the judgments in the High Court in Clyne and the judgments in other courts thereafter, I concluded that the effect of the s.218 notice was essentially the same as that of a garnishee order and accordingly constituted the Commissioner a secured creditor, with a lien or charge over the debt owed to the taxpayer. However, I expressed the view that the charge attached only where there was in fact a debt owing to the taxpayer. In a case such as Donnelly, where as soon as medical services were completed there arose a right to be paid in the medical practitioner, I expressed the view that the Commissioner's charge attached only when the debt came into existence, albeit eo instanti upon it coming into existence. I said also (at 459-60):

"An alternative and perhaps equally satisfactory

way of arriving at the same conclusion is to

merely give effect to the words of s.218

themselves. As and from the date of the s.218

notice the Health Commission is bound as and

when debts become due and payable by it to Dr

Edelsten to pay those moneys to the

Commissioner. That obligation arises by

statute. The fact that subsequently a petition

is presented and a sequestration order is made

does not alter the character of the moneys as

being moneys due or accruing or moneys which may

become due to Dr Edelsten at the time the notice

take effect."

25. I made reference to the provisions of s.218(4) as reinforcing my view. The trustee in bankruptcy was, it seemed to me an "other person" within the meaning of s.218(4).

26. Lockhart J expressed agreement with my reasons. The additional comments made by his Honour I take to be in amplification of his disagreement with the judgment of von Doussa J on the question whether s.218 created a charge.

27. It seems to me that the majority view in Donnelly is determinative of the present issue. Section 218 has no operative effect, whether that effect be described as creating a lien or charge, or as working an attachment, or as working an assignment or merely by reference to the words of the section itself unless and until there is actually a debt due from the addressee of the notice to the taxpayer. The analogy raised in Donnelly to a floating charge is apposite. Where at the time of the notice there is no debt due by the addressee to the taxpayer it will only be when there comes into existence a debt which either then is due to the taxpayer or which thereafter becomes due to the taxpayer that the section will operate to work an assignment or create a charge of that debt in the Commissioner.

28. This result does not, in a case such as the present, where at the time of the notice being given the taxpayer has a bare right of action for damages in tort for bodily injury, pose any danger to the Commissioner from an attempt to defeat his rights by assignment as occurred in Clyne. This is so because unless and until there is property over which the Commissioner's charge can operate there could be no assignment of the bare chose in action. At best there could only be an agreement for consideration to assign the proceeds of the action when a verdict has been obtained. In the event of such an assignment the assignee would take subject to the rights of the Commissioner under s.218, or if the view of Gibbs CJ in Clyne be preferred, the wording of the section itself would bring about the same result.

29. Accordingly I am of the view that the Commissioner was not a secured creditor at the time the bankruptcy was discharged, because at that time there was still no debt capable of being expressed in terms of money due to the taxpayer in respect of which the Commissioner's charge under s.218 could operate.

30. There arises, however, the question whether the decision of the Court of Appeal in Re Lind (1915) 2 Ch 345, upon which the learned Judge below relied, requires a different result. In that case a debtor assigned by way of mortgage to a mortgagee the share of the personal estate of his mother to which he was presumptively entitled. Later the debtor assigned the same share to another mortgagee subject to the first mortgage. Later he was adjudicated a bankrupt. Subsequently he was discharged from bankruptcy. Neither mortgagee proved in the bankruptcy. Subsequently the discharged bankrupt assigned the interest to another. Thereafter the mother died and the estate fell into possession. It was held that the assignments continued in force, notwithstanding the discharge.

31. The legislation at the time (Bankruptcy Act 1883 (Eng)), differed to some extent from the present legislation. First, s.30, broadly comparable to s.153 of the Australian Act, operated, subject to certain exceptions not presently relevant, to release the bankrupt from all debts. No reference appeared in the section to secured debts. Second, s.9 then provided:

"(1) On the making of a receiving order an

official receiver shall be thereby

constituted receiver of the property of

the debtor and thereafter, except as

directed by this Act, no creditor to whom

the debtor is indebted in respect of any

debt provable in bankruptcy shall have any

remedy against the property or person of

the debtor in respect of the debt, or

shall commence any action or other legal

proceedings unless with the leave of the

Court and on such terms as the Court may impose.

(2) But this section shall not affect the

power of any secured creditor to realize

or otherwise deal with his security in the

same manner as he would have been entitled

to realize or deal with it if this section

had not been passed."

32. The definition of secured creditor in s.168(1) was substantially similar to that contained in s.5(1) of the Australian Act. However, the definition of "property" in the English legislation (s.168 (1)) differed in that the definition was an inclusory definition only. It read:

"'Property' includes money, goods, things in

action, land, and every description of property,

whether real or personal and whether situate in

England or elsewhere; also, obligations,

easements, and every description of estate,

interest and profit, present or future, vested

or contingent, arising out of or incident to

property as above defined:".

33. The primary argument for the assignee last in point of time was that an assignment of an expectancy, no matter how certain, for consideration, operated in contract only so that when the bankruptcy and discharge intervened, the contract was discharged. I say "no matter how certain" because the mother had been found to be insane and had no power to dispose of her assets. This argument was rejected on the basis that the security of the mortgagee remained in force and became effective whenever the expectancy vested in the assignor. So the case became one of a number of leading authorities for the now well-established principle that where there is an assignment for value of property not in existence, the assignor becomes in equity, upon the property coming into existence, trustee for the assignee of that property. It is for this proposition that it is treated as authority by Dixon J in Palette Shoes Pty Limited v Krohn (1937) 58 CLR 1 at 27.

34. The case is not authority for the proposition that the debt of the secured creditor as such is not discharged. Nor is it authority for the proposition that the mortgagees in that case fell within the definition of "secured creditor" or that the claim to the mother's estate before her death was "property" within the definition of that expression in the bankruptcy legislation. That the first of these matters is so can be seen from the following passage from the judgment of Swinfen Eady LJ in Re Lind at 360-1, where his Lordship said:

"It was urged by the appellants that having

regard to the wide language of s37 of the

Bankruptcy Act, 1883, with regard to debts and

liabilities provable in bankruptcy, all

liability of the assignor of every kind, under

the mortgages of 1905 and 1908, was provable;

and that as the debts created by the mortgage

deeds were extinguished by the bankruptcy, the

contract relating to the future property, which

was merely ancillary to and for the purpose of

securing the debt, was also discharged. The

answer is that the mortgagees under the deeds of

1905 and 1908 elected to rely upon their

security, and not to prove, and therefore as

mortgagees they stand outside the bankruptcy;

and, moreover, that any contract contained in

those deeds for vesting the future property in

the mortgagees was ancillary not to the debt,

but to the mortgage by which the debt was

secured, in like manner as the covenant not to

revoke a will which had appointed a sum of money

to a mortgagee was so held in Robinson v

Ommanney 21 Ch D 780; 23 Ch D 285 both by Kay J

and the Court of Appeal....Kay J instanced the

case of an ordinary mortgage with the usual

covenant for further assurance, where it could

not be said that, because the covenantor became

bankrupt and was discharged from personal

liability for the debt, the consideration was

merely gone, and the covenant for further

assurance no longer enforceable." (emphasis added).

35. Similarly Bankes LJ (at 370-371) regarded the security as in addition to the personal covenant to pay so that the discharge of the debt did not affect the agreement to assign.

36. That the case is not authority for the second and third matter referred to can be seen also from the judgment of Bankes LJ. His Lordship expressed the view that the interest which the debtor had in the estate of his mother was not "property" within the meaning of the Bankruptcy Acts at any time during the assignor's bankruptcy. If this be accepted, then it must likewise follow that the mortgagee fell outside the definition of "secured creditor" in the Bankruptcy Act. His Lordship thus stated (at 371) the real question of the case to be:

"...as to the exact effect which equity gives to

the assignments in question and as to the

operation of the Bankruptcy Acts upon the

covenant which equity imports into them."

37. In reaching the same conclusion as Swinfen Eady LJ, Bankes LJ took the view (expressed at 372) that the principle to be applied was that expressed by Cozens-Hardy LJ in Re Reis (1904) 2 KB 769 at 777, namely:

"When once it has been decided that the covenant

is one of which specific performance can be

obtained, it follows that the right to specific

performance is not barred by the bankruptcy."

38. The differences in wording of the 1883 English Bankruptcy Act and the present legislation, if anything, reinforce these conclusions. Clearly the proceeds of the claim for damages are not "property" until such time as they come into existence. From this it follows that until they do, the Commissioner can not fall within the definition of "secured creditor". It follows also from the Act, and from Re Lind, that the debt of the Commissioner as such will have been discharged. The outcome of the present case must accordingly turn upon the proper construction of s.218, bearing these matters in mind.

39. The question which then arises is whether the consequence of the bankrupt being released from all unsecured debts or secured debts of creditors who had proved in the bankruptcy is to exonerate the GIO from any obligation to make payment to the Deputy Commissioner under the s.218 notice. Section 218 of the ITA Act provides relevantly as follows:

"(1) The Commissioner may at any time, or from

time to time, by notice in writing... require-

(a) any person by whom any money is due or

accruing or may become due to a taxpayer;

(b) any person who holds or may subsequently

hold money for or on account of a taxpayer; or

(c) any person who holds or may subsequently

hold money on account of some other person

for payment to a taxpayer; or

(d) any person having authority from some

other person to pay money to a taxpayer,

to pay to the Commissioner...

(e) so much of the money as is sufficient to

pay the amount due by the taxpayer in

respect of tax...

and may at any time, or from time to time, amend

or revoke any such notice, or extend the time

for making any payment in pursuance of the notice.

...

(4) Any person making any payment in pursuance

of this section shall be deemed to have been

acting under the authority of the taxpayer and

of all other person concerned and is hereby

indemnified in respect of such payment.

(5) If the Commissioner receives any payment

in respect of the amount due by the taxpayer

before payment is made by the person so notified

he shall forthwith give notice thereof to that person."

40. The question is whether the words "the amount due by the taxpayer in respect of tax" in s.218(1)(e) of the ITA Act refer to the amount actually due by the taxpayer for tax at the moment the notice was given, unaffected by subsequent events, or whether they mean the amount, if any, of tax due by the taxpayer at the time the notice becomes operative to require the person to whom it is given to pay moneys to the Commissioner.

41. It seems clear enough that the words in question could not be construed so as to extend to tax amounts which may become due by virtue of an assessment made after the giving of the s.218 notice, for the person to whom the notice was given would not know what amount he would have to pay to the Commissioner. The section, while not explicit on the matter, at least seems to contemplate that the notice itself will inform the recipient what the amount due by the taxpayer in respect of tax is, thereby ensuring that that person knows the extent of the obligation imposed upon him by s.218 to pay moneys otherwise due to the taxpayer to the Commissioner. Certainly the contemplation is that the maximum amount payable to the Commissioner will be ascertained at the date the notice is given. The purpose of sub-sec.(5) in requiring the Commissioner, after receiving payment of some or all of the tax due, to notify that fact to the recipient of the notice is, one may confidently assume, to inform the recipient of the notice that the amount he or she is required to pay to the Commissioner has been reduced or, in the event of total repayment, eliminated.

42. It is hardly conceivable that the Commissioner, once he had given a notice under s.218(5), could continue to require the recipient of the notice to pay moneys to him when the tax debt had otherwise been satisfied by payment. Nor might it be conceived that the legislative intention was to subject the recipient of a s.218 notice to a penalty for committing an offence on failing to comply with that notice where the Commissioner had failed to give a notice under s.218(5) although he had received payment of some or all of the amount due by the taxpayer on the assessments which formed the foundation for the giving of the notice.

43. Section 218 is silent as to what is to happen if the tax debt upon which the s.218 notice is based is discharged other than by way of payment. That could occur, for example, by reason of an objection being made to the assessment and that objection being allowed; by reason of an appeal against an objection decision, either to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal or to the Court, being decided favourably to the taxpayer or, as in the present case, by reason of the debt being released on a discharge from bankruptcy. The short question is whether s.218, in any of these circumstances, contemplates that the recipient of a notice must nevertheless make payment to the Commissioner of tax no longer payable, or whether it is to be construed as requiring the recipient only to make payment in a case where the tax is in fact properly payable. I should add that in the event the former construction were adopted, the Commissioner would presumably be required to return the money to the taxpayer as being money paid on a mistake of law: cf David Securities Pty Ltd v Commonwealth Bank of Australia [1992] HCA 48; (1992) 109 ALR 57.

44. In my view the latter construction should be adopted. It is unlikely that the Parliamentary intention was that the recipient of a notice should make payment to the Commissioner where tax is no longer payable in cases where it would then be incumbent upon the taxpayer to seek to recover from the Commissioner the amount wrongly paid. In my view the proper construction of s.218 is that the words "the amount due by the taxpayer in respect of tax" refers to so much of the tax due and owing by the taxpayer immediately upon the issue of the s.218 notice as remains due and owing from time to time. So construed the section can be given a sensible operation.

45. However it follows that in the present case, there being upon the discharge of Mr Daoui from bankruptcy no amount of tax thereafter due, the GIO was under no obligation to make payment to the Commissioner but rather was obliged to make payment to Mr Daoui of the amount of the judgment and costs due to him.

Whether the solicitor's lien had priority?

46. There seems to be little doubt that immediately moneys became payable by the GIO to Mr Daoui, those moneys were impressed by a lien in favour of the solicitor for the costs to which he was properly entitled in acting for Mr Daoui in the proceedings. Had the proper view been that the Commissioner had a charge which survived the discharge of bankruptcy because the s.218 notice was not spent at that time, the question would have arisen whether the Commissioner's charge took priority over the lien of the solicitor. It is, however, strictly unnecessary to resolve this matter having regard to the view I hold as to the consequence of Mr Daoui's discharge from bankruptcy upon the s.218 notice. In deference to the argument of counsel, I would comment shortly on the question.

47. The Commissioner submitted that s.218(4) had the consequence that the obligation of the GIO to make payment to the Commissioner prevailed over any lien to the solicitor, whether or not that lien took priority otherwise in accordance with the law, because the solicitor was an "other person concerned" within the meaning of the section. Sub-section (4) was, it was submitted, a clause which had the effect of ensuring that the right of the Commissioner prevailed over any other right which might exist, whether otherwise in priority to the Commissioner. If that view were not correct, it was conceded on behalf of the Deputy Commissioner that it would be unconscionable for the Deputy Commissioner to take the benefit of the judgment in favour of Mr Daoui without bearing the burden of the costs associated with it: cf Hewett v Court [1983] HCA 7; (1983) 149 CLR 639 at 668-9 per Deane J. Clearly it would be unconscientious and unfair for the Deputy Commissioner to be permitted to take the benefit of the judgment without being subject to the lien for the payment of the costs of obtaining that judgment.

48. The Commissioner's reliance upon s.218(4) appears, in my view, misconceived. If the argument were correct, it would follow that the whole of the discussion of the High Court in Clyne would have been otiose. All that their Honours would have had to do was to rely upon sub-sec.(4) as excluding any right which Ms Peacock, the assignee of Mr Clyne's interest bearing deposits, would have had. Sub-section (4) was certainly relied upon by Gibbs CJ (at 12) but the sub-section received but a passing mention in the judgment of Mason J, with whose judgment Aickin and Wilson JJ agreed. The sub-section is not mentioned at all in the judgment of Brennan J.

49. It follows that if the discharge from bankruptcy had had no effect upon the obligation of the GIO to make payment to the Deputy Commissioner under the notice, the charge or other security interest of the Deputy Commissioner would have been subject to a lien in favour of Mr Dennis to secure to him the recovery of his costs of acting for Mr Daoui in the proceedings against GIO.

The Constitutional attack

50. It was conceded by counsel for Mr Daoui that no Constitutional issue would arise unless the Deputy Commissioner were, as at the date of discharge of Mr Daoui from bankruptcy, a secured creditor by virtue of the giving of the s.218 notice. So far as I understood the Constitutional argument, it was that if s.218 created a security interest notwithstanding that the tax debt itself had been discharged, then s.55 was invalid as itself imposing a tax. The argument in my view is without substance.

51. It is immaterial, for the purposes of the argument, whether the notice under s.218 operated to work an assignment of the moneys referred to in the notice, or a charge upon them. It is also immaterial, for the purposes of the present argument, whether the discharge of Mr Daoui from bankruptcy operated to render the s.218 notice ineffective. Even if the s.218 notice continued to be effective, contrary to my view, s.218 could not operate to impose a tax. Section 218 is properly to be characterised as a law to facilitate the recovery of tax initially due and owing, rather than as a law imposing some new tax. This characterisation would not, in my view, change, if, contrary to my view, the obligations created by the notice survived a release of the debt by virtue of s.153 of the Act. In my opinion, Wilcox J was correct in rejecting this submission and in so doing in referring to the judgment of Fullagar J, with whom Dixon CJ, Kitto, Taylor and Windeyer JJ all agreed, in Re Dymond [1959] HCA 22; (1959) 101 CLR 11, a case later applied by Gibbs CJ, Wilson, Deane and Dawson JJ in MacCormick v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1984] HCA 20; (1984) 158 CLR 622 at 644.

52. It follows that the Deputy Commissioner's appeal must be dismissed, as must the cross-appeal relying upon s.55 of the Constitution. Neither side made any submission concerning the failure of his Honour to order costs and accordingly I do not propose to consider that matter further. It is clear that his Honour proposed that it be ordered that there be no order as to costs but that this order was omitted when the formal minute of orders was taken out. However, there is no reason now why costs should not follow the event.

53. In the circumstances the Deputy Commissioner must pay Mr Daoui's costs of the appeal and Mr Daoui must pay the Deputy Commissioner's costs of the cross-appeal. As the GIO did not participate in the appeal, it is unnecessary to make any order of costs in respect of that corporation. In the result it is unnecessary to deal with any matters which affect the rights of the solicitor in respect of any lien he may have for costs. Accordingly nothing turns upon the fact that the solicitor was not a party to the proceedings.

BEAZLEY J I agree with the reasons for judgment of Hill J.
