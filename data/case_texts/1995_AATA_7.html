QT95/4 and Commissioner of Taxation [1995] AATA 7; 95 ATC 152; (1994) 30 ATR 1066 (20 January 1995)

Last Updated: 29 January 2009



TAXATION - application made for review of decision to refuse to revoke departure prohibition order or to issue departure authorisation certificate - whether applications for decisions made before application for review lodged - whether the Tribunal may review an oral decision - whether Tribunal may review a decision to refuse to make a decision - whether the Commissioner did so refuse or purported to refuse - whether the Commissioner had made a decision on the merits of the applications and had not notified the applicant of it - held that Tribunal does not have jurisdiction.

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) QT95/4

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:



Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



Respondent



Tribunal: Miss S A Forgie (Deputy President)



Place: Brisbane



Date: 20 January, 1995



REASON FOR DECISION



On 17 January, 1995, the applicant lodged an application for review of:



“(a) the decision of the Australian Taxation Office to issue a departure prohibition order against the applicant;



(b) the decision of the Australian Taxation Office to refuse to revoke the order or to issue a departure authorisation.”



He also lodged an application under section 41 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the AAT Act”) for an order staying the operation of the decisions referred to in his application for review.



2. Before the application for the stay order could be heard, there arose a question whether the Tribunal has jurisdiction to consider the substantive application. As the applicant was booked to fly overseas on the evening of 19 January, 1995, a hearing was held on the morning of 19 January, 1995 to consider the jurisdictional question. Shortly after the conclusion of that hearing, I advised Mr Savage of Counsel, who represented the applicant, and Miss Gilligan, who represented the Commissioner of Taxation (“the Commissioner”), that I had decided that the Tribunal does not have jurisdiction to hear the application. These are the reasons for my decision.



Legislative framework



3. Part IVA of the Taxation Administration Act 1953,

relates to the departure from Australia of certain tax debtors. Section 14R provides that



“A person in respect of whom a departure prohibition order is in force, and who knows that such an order is in force in respect of him or her, shall not depart from Australia for a foreign country unless the departure is authorized by a departure authorisation certificate.” (sub-section 14R(1))



It goes on to provide the penalty for infringing that provision.



4. The Commissioner’s authority to issue a departure prohibition order (“a DPO”) is found in section 14S. That section provides that where



“(a) a person is subject to a tax liability; and



(b) the Commissioner believes on reasonable grounds that it is desirable to do so for the purpose of ensuring that the person does not depart from Australia for a foreign country without -



(i) wholly discharging the tax liability; or



(ii) making arrangements satisfactory to the Commissioner for the tax liability to be wholly discharged,



the Commissioner may, by order in accordance with the prescribed form, prohibit the departure of the person from Australia for a foreign country.” (sub-section 14S(1))



5. If a person against whom a DPO has been issued wishes to leave Australia, he or she has three options. First, he or she may apply to the Federal Court or the Supreme Court of a State or Territory to have it set aside (see sub-section 14V(1) read with section 14X). Second, he or she may apply to the Commissioner to vary or revoke the DPO under section 14T. Third, he or she may apply to the Commissioner for a departure authorisation certificate under section 14U.



6. In this case, the applicant has chosen the second and third options and I shall look at them more closely. Looking first at the option under section 14T, it has two aspects. The first aspect is found in sub-section 14T(1) which provides for the circumstances in which the Commissioner must revoke the DPO. It states:



“Where a departure prohibition order is in force in respect of a person, and -



(a) the tax liabilities to which the person is subject have been wholly discharged and the Commissioner is satisfied that it is likely that the tax liabilities to which the person may become subject in respect of, or arising out of, matters that have occurred will be -



(i) wholly discharged; or



(ii) completely irrecoverable; or



(b) the Commissioner is satisfied that the tax liabilities to which the person is subject are completely irrecoverable,



the Commissioner shall, on application being made to the Commissioner by the person to do so or on the Commissioner’s own motion, revoke the departure prohibition order.”



7. The second aspect is found in sub-section 14T(2) when it provides:



“Where a departure prohibition order is in force in respect of a person, the Commissioner may, in the Commissioner’s discretion and on application being made to the Commissioner to do so or on the Commissioner’s own motion, revoke or vary the departure prohibition order.”



8. Whether the Commissioner makes a decision under sub-section 14T(1) or (2), section 14T makes provision for the person who is the subject of the DPO to be notified of the decision. Where the Commissioner makes a decision to revoke or vary the DPO, he is required to



“(a) cause to be served, as prescribed, on the person; and



(b) cause to be given to each person to whom a copy of the departure prohibition order was given,



notification of the revocation or variation of the departure prohibition order.” (sub-section 14T(4))



9. Where the Commissioner decides to refuse to revoke a DPO, he is required “as soon as practicable after a decision is made under subsection (1) or (2) ... [to] cause to be served, as prescribed, on the person notification of the decision” (sub-section 14(5)).



10. Regulation 17 of the Taxation Administration Regulations (“the Regulations”) sets out the way in which notification of the revocation or variation of the DPO or of the refusal to revoke a DPO must be served on a person. Notification is served for the purposes of sub-section 14T(4) if “a document containing the particulars of the revocation or variation” (sub-regulation 17(1)) or for the purposes of sub-section 14T(5) if a “document containing the particulars of the decision” (sub-regulation 17(2)) is delivered personally to the person or left at, or sent by pre-paid post to the address of the place of residence or business of the person last known to the Commissioner.



11. The third option a person may choose is to apply for a departure authorisation certificate and this is provided for in section 14U of the Act. The Commissioner is required to issue such a certificate if he is satisfied of certain matters set out in sub-section 14U(1). As soon as practicable after he has issued a departure authorisation certificate, he is required to serve it on the person (paragraph 14U(3)(a)). Where he decides not to issue the certificate then



“As soon as practicable after a decision is made under subsection (1) refusing to issue a departure authorization certificate in respect of a person or a decision is made under subsection (2) refusing to substitute a later day in relation to the return of a person to Australia, the Commissioner shall cause to be served, as prescribed, on the person notification of the decision.” (sub-section 14U(4))



12. Regulation 18 provides for service under section 14U. It mirrors the provisions of Regulation 17 in providing when “a copy of a departure authorisation certificate is served, as prescribed, on a person” (sub-regulation 18(1)) and when “notification of a decision” under sub-section 14U(4) is served as prescribed (sub-regulation 18(2)). In relation to the latter, it is taken to be served when “a document containing particulars of the decision” is delivered personally to or left at, or sent by pre-paid post to, the address of the person’s residence or business last known to the Commissioner.



13. Where a person chooses the second or third options, he or she may apply for review of the Commissioner’s decisions under section 14Y. As this section was of some relevance in the proceedings, I will set it out in full:



“(1) Applications may be made to the Tribunal for review of decisions of the Commissioner under section 14T or 14U.



(2) In subsection (1), “decision” has the same meaning as in the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975.”



Background



14. No evidence was led at the hearing but there was some measure of agreement between the parties as to the facts. Where there was not any agreement, there were various assertions made about them by the representatives for both parties. In view of my reasons for making my decision, I do not consider that I need to explore those areas of disagreement by asking for evidence or to make findings of fact in relation to them. I will merely set them out. Where I have made findings of fact they are in the context and for the purposes of this hearing only.



15. In early January, 1995, a meeting of the applicant’s creditors was convened under Part X of the Bankruptcy Act 1966. The applicant stated in his affidavit sworn on 17 January, 1995 that he had, at that meeting, fully disclosed his intention to travel overseas on 12 January, 1995. Mr Savage was instructed that, at that meeting, the applicant had told his creditors at the meeting that he intended to travel overseas for business purposes and for the purpose of re-financing. Miss Gilligan was instructed that the applicant had told those at the meeting that he might have the opportunity of obtaining a position overseas but that he had made no mention of making re-financing arrangements.



16. I note that the Commissioner has presented a creditor’s petition against the applicant and that petition is listed for hearing in the Federal Court in the next few weeks.



17. On 6 January, 1995, an Authorised Officer of the Delegate of the Commissioner issued a DPO pursuant to section 14S of the Act. Mr Savage stated that the DPO was posted to the applicant. Although no evidence was led regarding this aspect, notification of the applicant by post is, in certain conditions, open under paragraph 14S(4)(a) of the Act when read with regulation 16 of the Regulations. Mr Savage stated that the applicant did not receive notice of the DPO until 12 January, 1995 when he was seated on a plane which was about to leave Australia. This was not challenged by Miss Gilligan.



18. On 17 January, 1995, the applicant’s solicitors wrote to the Commissioner asking him “... to review the prohibition order and to either revoke it or to issue a departure authorisation certificate.” (Exhibit A) It went on to say that:



“In the event that the Australian Taxation Office declines to revoke the order or issue a certificate we ask that you give reasons for the grant of the original order and reasons for the refusal to revoke that order or to issue a departure certificate.” (Exhibit A, page 2)



19. The solicitors’ letter was sent by facsimile message to the Commissioner. On the basis of the facsimile transmission report, I find that the letter was transmitted to the Commissioner’s office between 11.56am and 12.09pm on 17 January, 1995. No notification of a written decision has been given to the applicant.



20. The stamp which an officer in the Tribunal’s registry placed on the application for review shows the date on which the application was received and that information is encircled by a series of numbers starting at “00” and ending with “23”. The stamp has an arrow pointing to the number “11”. No evidence was led on this point but, on its face, the stamp would seem to indicate at least that the application was filed after 11 o’clock and before 12 noon on 17 January, 1995. It would seem to indicate that it is possible that the application to the Tribunal for review of the Commissioner’s decisions “to refuse to revoke the order or to issue a departure authorisation” certificate was made to the Tribunal before the applicant, through his solicitors, had asked the Commissioner to make decisions in relation to revocation or the issue of a departure authorisation certificate.



Consideration



21. If it is the case that the application for review was lodged before the application was made to the Commissioner and so before he could respond to the application at all, there is a question whether that by itself precludes the Tribunal from having jurisdiction. There are occasions on which the Tribunal does have jurisdiction in relation to a decision made after the application for review has been lodged but, in so far as I am aware, that jurisdiction only arises where it has been specifically provided for by an enactment. Even then, it only arises in cases which the decision-maker varies a decision already the subject of an application in the Tribunal and under review. The Tribunal’s jurisdiction is then in relation to the decision as varied. An example is to be found in section 1284 of the Social Security Act 1991. I am not aware of any instances in which the Tribunal may entertain an application for review lodged prior to there being any opportunity for a decision-maker to make a decision even if a decision is subsequently made.



22. Despite my doubts as to whether the Tribunal does have jurisdiction in light of the possibly premature filing of the application, I have decided not to express a concluded view upon it for it was not raised at the hearing and the parties have not had an opportunity to make any submissions upon it.



23. The application seeks review of the decision to issue a DPO. Clearly, and there was no disagreement on this aspect between the parties, this is not a decision which this Tribunal may review. Jurisdiction lies with the Federal Court or the Supreme Court of a State or Territory pursuant to section 14V.



24. The application also seeks review of a decision to refuse to revoke the DPO or to issue a departure authorisation certificate. Mr Savage submitted that a decision not to grant a revocation of the DPO and a decision not to issue a departure authorisation certificate were constituted by the delegate’s failure to revoke the DPO or to issue a departure authorisation certificate. Put another way, he submitted that failure to revoke the DPO and failure to issue the departure authorisation certificate were failures to exercise the power to revoke or to issue a certificate and were in themselves decisions that might be reviewed in this Tribunal.



25. Mr Savage relied upon sub-section 3(3) of the AAT Act in which the word “decision” is defined. He also argued that the AAT Act is remedial legislation and that to construe it as meaning that it does not apply to the situation in which a decision-maker refuses to exercise a power despite his or her having that power is not sustained by an analysis of the Act. He referred to the structure of the AAT Act and to case law to which I shall refer shortly.



26. Mr Savage also submitted that the Tribunal has jurisdiction on the basis that the delegate had actually made a decision but had not communicated it to the applicant. He based this argument on his statements that the Commissioner knew that the applicant intended to travel overseas in early January as one of his officers had attended the creditors’ meeting and that the Commissioner knew from that meeting that, in view of the very large amount owed to one of the creditors, he will receive neither payment of the tax debt nor a dividend. It follows that the taxation debt is irrecoverable. Mr Savage also asserted that the Commissioner has been given all of the relevant facts by the applicant.



27. Mr Savage submitted that the Commissioner has, for an improper purpose, failed to exercise his powers under sections 14T and 14U. That improper purpose is to ensure that the applicant cannot leave Australia, will consequently be unable to organise re-financing and will be forced into bankruptcy. The result will be, Mr Savage submitted, that the Commissioner can never recover the debt.



28. The first question raised by Mr Savage’s submissions is whether the Tribunal has jurisdiction to review a decision which has not been made in writing. Miss Gilligan submitted that it does not and referred in particular to the notification provisions of sections 14T and 14U and of regulations 17 and 18 of the Regulations.



29. The starting point in this consideration is sub-section 25(1) which provides that



“An enactment may provide that applications may be made to the Tribunal:



(a) for review of decisions made in the exercise of powers conferred by that enactment; or



(b) for the review of decisions made in the exercise of powers conferred, or that may be conferred, by another enactment having effect under that enactment.”



The corollary of this provision is found in sub-section 25(4) which provides that “the Tribunal has power to review any decision in respect of which application is made to it under any enactment.” There is nothing in section 25 which suggests that the decision must be in writing.



30. Is there anything in the definition of “decision” which appears in sub-section 3(3) of the AAT Act?. That is quite a wide definition and provides that



“A reference in this Act to a decision includes a reference to:



(a) making, suspending, revoking or refusing to make an order or determination;



(b) giving, suspending, revoking or refusing to give a certificate, direction, approval, consent or permission;



(c) issuing, suspending, revoking or refusing to issue a licence, authority or other instrument;



(d) imposing a condition or restriction;



(e) making a declaration, demand or requirement;



(f) retaining, or refusing to deliver up, an article; or



(g) doing or refusing to do any other act or thing.”



There is nothing in it which suggests that the decision need be in writing.



31. Is there anything in the Act itself or the Regulations that suggests the decision must be in writing? Section 14Y, which I have set out at paragraph 13 above, does not assist for it only authorises applications to be made to the Tribunal in respect of decisions made under sections 14T or 14U. That leaves sections 14T and U and the related regulations 17 and 18 which have been referred to in paragraphs 10 and 12 above.



32. I have concluded that none of these sections or regulations requires that a decision need be in writing before it may be reviewed by the Tribunal. Indeed, the sections contemplate that there will be a decision and that notification of that decision will follow according to the procedures prescribed in the Regulations. There are two distinct steps. So, for example, in relation to the revocation of a DPO, sub-section 14T(4) provides that “notification of the revocation” shall be given “as soon as practicable after a departure prohibition order made in respect of a person is revoked ...” (my emphasis). The revocation must be regarded as the decision for it comes within the meaning of the definition of “decision” in sub-section 3(3) of the AAT Act, which is specifically referred to in sub-section 14Y(2) of the Act. Notification of the revocation must, therefore, equate with notification of the decision.



33. The distinction between the decision and the notification of that decision is carried through in regulation 17 which again refers to service of the “notification of the revocation” and not to service of the revocation or the decision to revoke. The other provisions of sections 14T and 14U and of regulations 17 and 18 show a consistent approach and lead me to conclude that there is no requirement in the Act that a decision must be in writing before the Tribunal has jurisdiction to consider an application to review it.



34. In support of this conclusion, I refer also to sub-section 29(1) of the AAT Act. That sets out the manner in which an application may be made to the Tribunal for review of a decision. In part, it provides that the application must be made within the prescribed time “if the terms of the decision were recorded in writing and set out in a document furnished to the applicant ...” (paragraph 29(1)(d)). The whole basis of that paragraph is that an application may be made in relation to a decision that was not recorded in writing and was not furnished to the applicant but that there are no time limits within which an application for review of such a decision can be made. Once the decision has been furnished in writing there are time limits within which the applicant must act. Sub-section 29(1), however, does not place any restrictions upon the way in which a decision may be made.



35. Finally on this aspect, I have considered sub-section 25(3) of the AAT Act. Under that sub-section, it is open to Parliament to specify conditions subject to which an application may be made (paragraph 25(3)(c)). It is apparent from what I have already found regarding the Act and the Regulations that I do not consider that any such conditions have been specified in relation to the types of decisions with which I am concerned.



36. That brings me to the next question and that is whether the Commissioner’s failure to make a decision under either section 14T or section 14U constitutes a decision or decisions that may be reviewed by this Tribunal. To support his submission, Mr Savage relied on some practical considerations in addition to his other arguments. He suggested that, if the Tribunal does not have jurisdiction in this situation in which no time limit is prescribed within which the Commissioner must make his decision, then he could frustrate a person’s attempts to seek a decision and subsequent review by refusing ever to make a decision.



37. Superficially, this argument may have some attraction but it does not follow from the fact that there may be such situations that the Tribunal has jurisdiction to exercise its powers of review in those situations. There must be a grant of jurisdiction. It also needs to be remembered that the AAT Act and the Tribunal itself are only one part of the Commonwealth’s administrative law package. Two of the other parts of that package, all of which are intended to complement one another, are the Ombudsman Act 1976 and the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977. Arguably, there would seem to be remedies available under those Acts if a decision-maker were tardy in making a decision. The fact that there may be a remedy under section 7 of the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 or perhaps by applying for a prerogative writ as in the case of O’Reilly; Ex parte Australena Investments Pty Ltd and Others (1983) 50 ALR 577 does not assist me to decide whether or not this Tribunal has jurisdiction.



38. Miss Gilligan submitted that I must look to the words of sections 14T, 14U and 14Y. Section 14Y permits an application to be made for review of decisions made under either of the other two sections. When regard is had to the other two sections, the range of decisions that may be made under those sections is very limited. Under section 14T, he may revoke or vary the DPO or refuse to do so. Under section 14U, he may issue or vary a departure authorisation certificate or he may refuse to do so. They are the only decisions under review. If a refusal to make a decision were reviewable, there would have to be a specific provision made in relation to it just as there is in relation to a failure to make decisions within periods prescribed by an enactment. Specific provision is made in relation to decisions in the latter category in sub-section 25(5) of the AAT Act.



39. Sub-section 25(5) of the AAT Act provides:



“For the purposes of an enactment that makes provision in accordance with this section for the making of applications to the Tribunal for review of decisions, a failure by a person to do an act or thing within the period prescribed by that enactment, or by another enactment having effect under that enactment, as the period within which that person is required or permitted to do that act or thing shall be deemed to constitute the making of a decision by that person at the expiration of that period not to do that act or thing.”



40. Sub-section 25(5) expressly deals with the situation in which a decision must be made within a prescribed time limit. It would seem to follow from sub-section 25(5) of the AAT Act, that it is not contemplated that the Tribunal will have power to review a refusal to make a decision unless specific time limits are imposed for the making of a decision and sub-section 25(5) comes into operation or unless specific provision is made in the specific enactment conferring jurisdiction. There have been specific provisions made in other enactments giving the Tribunal jurisdiction to review decisions where there has been a failure to make a decision within specified time limits. I am unaware of any that have given jurisdiction where the decision has not been made within a reasonable time.



41. An enactment in which jurisdiction has been given where there has been a failure to make a decision is the Freedom of Information Act 1982. Sub-section 55(3) is framed from a different perspective from that adopted by sub-section 25(5) but is consistent with it. It provides that an application to the Tribunal for review of a decision is not prevented by its not having been internally reviewed under section 54 in cases in which the agency has not made a decision under section 54 within 30 days. Furthermore, the application is to be treated as having been made within the time limits prescribed if it appears that there has been no unreasonable time delay in making it. Jurisdiction has, therefore, been specifically conferred by the legislation on the Tribunal in a case in which there has been a failure to make a decision.



42. A different formulation appears in sub-section 56(1) of the Freedom of Information Act 1982. It provides that a decision refusing to grant access to documents is deemed to have been made where the decision has not been made within specified time limits. This is the formulation adopted in legislation such as the Nursing Homes Assistance Act 1974 (sub-section 11A(5)) and the Australian Meat and Live-stock Corporation Act 1977 (sub-section 16E(3)).



43. No specific provision has been made in the Act and Regulations with which I am currently concerned. That would lead me to conclude that there is no jurisdiction. Mr Savage has, however, also relied on the definition of “decision” in sub-section 3(3) of the AAT Act. In particular, he relied on its provision that a reference in the AAT Act includes a reference to “doing or refusing to do any other act or thing.” (paragraph 3(3)(g)) His submission was to the effect that the definition should be read to mean that a decision includes a reference to the making of a decision (i.e. “doing” an “act”) and to the refusal to make a decision (i.e. “refusing” to do an “act”).



44. I must look to the Act itself to see whether jurisdiction has been given to the Tribunal to entertain an application where there has been a refusal to make a decision. In doing so, I must look also at the definition of “decision” in the AAT Act for the word “decision” in sub-section 14Y(1) has the same meaning (sub-section 14Y(2)).



45. Sub-section 14Y(1) provides that an application may be made to the Tribunal for review of decisions of the Commissioner. Taking a very simple approach and ignoring the rest of the Act, it would follow that an application may be made for review, for example, of the Commissioner’s retaining, or refusing to deliver up, an article within the meaning of paragraph 3(3)(f) of the definition of “decision” in the AAT Act. If the Commissioner were asked for a document and he refused to deliver it, then an application could be brought under section 14Y for the review of his refusal to deliver it.



46. The rest of the Act, however, cannot be ignored. It is not the definition of “decision” which is enabling an application to be made to the Tribunal but section 14Y of the Act. An application cannot be made in respect of any decision but in respect of a decision “under section 14T or 14U” (my emphasis). Continuing with my example, apart from the notification provisions, there is no reference at all in either section 14T or section 14U to the Commissioner’s retaining, or refusing to deliver up, an article. It would seem to follow that a decision in relation to such a matter would not be a decision under section 14T or 14U even though it may be a decision or a step along the way in reaching a final decision.



47. That a step along the way does not ordinarily amount to a reviewable decision in the context of the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 unless specifically provided for in the Act is apparent from the following passage from the judgement of Mason CJ in Australian Broadcasting Tribunal v Bond [1990] HCA 33; (1990) 170 CLR 321 (Mason CJ, Brennan, Deane, Toohey and Gaudron JJ). The High Court considered the meaning of the word “decision”, which is defined in terms identical to those used in sub-section 3(3) of the AAT Act. Mason CJ said at pages 335-336:



“The word has a variety of potential meanings. As Deane J noted in Director-General of Social Services v Chaney [1980] FCA 87; (1980) 47 FLR 80 at 100, in the context of judicial or administrative proceedings it ordinarily refers to an announced or published ruling or adjudication. In such a context, the word may signify a determination of any question of substance or procedure or, more narrowly, a determination effectively resolving an actual substantive issue. Even if it has that more limited meaning, the word can refer to a determination whether final or intermediate or, more narrowly again, a determination which effectively disposes of the matter in hand: see Chaney (at 100).



...



The fact that the ADJR Act is a remedial statute providing for a review of administrative action rather than some form of appeal from final decisions disposing of issues between parties indicates that no narrow view should be taken of the word `decision’. In this respect it is significant that s5 does not speak of `final decision’. It is also significant that the jurisdiction of the Federal Court to grant declaratory relief is not confined to granting relief in respect of ultimate decisions. The jurisdiction extends to questions in issue in pending proceedings: cf Forster v Jododex Aust Pty Ltd [1972] HCA 61; (1972) 127 CLR 421, per Gibbs J at 438. The existence of this jurisdiction which antedated the ADJR Act, suggests that the concept of a reviewable decision is not limited to a final decision disposing of the controversy between the parties.”



Mason CJ went on to consider the context of section 3 of the ADJR Act in which the term "decision" is used and the policy considerations. He said at pages 10-11:



“The relevant policy considerations are competing. On the one hand, the purposes of the ADJR Act are to allow persons aggrieved by the administrative decision-making processes of government a convenient and effective means of redress and to enhance those processes. On the other hand, in so far as the ambit of the concept of `decision’ is extended, there is a greater risk that the efficient administration of government will be impaired. Although Bowen CJ and Lockhart J appeared to emphasise the first of these considerations in Australian National University v Burns [1982] FCA 191; (1982) 64 FLR 166 at 172; [1982] FCA 191; 43 ALR 25 at 30, there comes a point when the second must prevail, as their Honours implicitly acknowledged. To interpret `decision’ in a way that would involve a departure from the quality of finality would lead to a fragmentation of the processes of administrative decision-making and set at risk the efficiency of the administrative process.



The policy arguments do not, in my opinion, call for an answer different from that dictated by the textual and contextual considerations. That answer is that a reviewable `decision’ is one for which provision is made by or under a statute. That will generally, but not always, entail a decision which is final or operative and determinative, at least in a practical sense, of the issue of fact falling for consideration. A conclusion reached as a step along the way in a course of reasoning leading to an ultimate decision would not ordinarily amount to a reviewable decision, unless the statute provided for the making of a finding or ruling on that point so that the decision, though an intermediate decision, might accurately be described as a decision under an enactment.”



48. It seems to me that I must look for those decisions made under sections 14T and 14U. Section 14T gives the Commissioner power to revoke or refuse to revoke a DPO. This is a decision which is a decision within the meaning of sub-section 3(3) whether it comes within paragraphs 3(3)(b), (c) or (g) or under the ordinary meaning of decision which is also encompassed by the terms of the decision. The Commissioner is not given power to refuse to make a decision. Therefore, although I have no doubt that a decision to refuse to make a decision is itself a decision, it is not a decision made under section 14T or 14U. If it is made at all, it is a decision made outside it.



49. Mr Savage pointed to the cases such as Collector of Customs (New South Wales) v Brian Lawlor Automotive Pty Ltd [1979] FCA 21; (1979) 2 ALD 1 (Bowen CJ and Smithers J, Deane J dissenting) to support his further submission that the Tribunal has jurisdiction to review decisions made by an official where those decisions are purported to have been made in the exercise of powers under an enactment but the powers have not in fact been given. This supported, he said, his argument that the Tribunal has power to review a decision where the Commissioner has refused to exercise his power to make a decision.



50. The essence of the view expressed by the majority of the Full Court is found in the following passage from the judgement of Bowen CJ when he said at page 7:



“... As I have said, in my opinion an applicant to the Tribunal has standing and the Tribunal has jurisdiction provided there is a decision in fact and provided further that the decision purports to have been made in the exercise of powers conferred by an enactment whether or not it was validly made and whether or not action on the basis there was power to make the decision was right or wrong.”



51. This does not assist the applicant in this case. Even if the Commissioner has refused to make a decision under section 14T or section 14U, there is no evidence that he has purported to do so, albeit incorrectly, in the exercise of his powers under either section. Both of these are factual matters. As I have said, no evidence was presented in this case. On what I do have, I am unable to find that either is established in this case.



52. Whether or not there has been a “refusal” to do a certain thing or take a certain course has been considered in a number of cases in various jurisdictions. In the realm of breathalyser tests, for example, it was said by the Court of Criminal Appeal in Reg v Honan [1971] N.S.W.L.R. 697 that:



“No precise form of words or actions is required to constitute a refusal. It is a question of fact to be inferred from all the circumstances, and in this context the remarks of Geoffrey Lane J. in R v. Clarke (1969) 53 Cr.App.R. 438, at p.442 dealing it is true with differing words in an English Act of Parliament but one that was enacted in the same field, are pertinent: `Plainly no particular formula of words is necessary from a defendant to constitute a refusal by him. It is the police, and not defendants, who are required by this legislation to adhere to formulae. Any words, or indeed any actions, on the part of a defendant, which in the eyes of the jury make it clear that the defendant in all the circumstances of the particular case is declining the police officer’s proper invitation, amount to a refusal within the section’.”



53. In the context of the Trade Practices Act 1974, the Full Court of the Federal Court considered what is meant by “refusing” to do an act where the definition of “refusing to do an act” in sub-section 4(2) included “refraining” from doing the act. Bowen CJ said at page 489:



“Although s4(2) recognises that an omission to do an act may constitute `engaging in conduct’, that will only be so where there has been a refusal to do, or a deliberate refraining from doing, an act. The words `refuse’ and `refrain’ clearly connote that the omission to do an act must be deliberate.” (Rhone-Poulenc v Uim Chem Services (1986) 12 FCR 477 Bowen CJ, Lockhart and Jackson JJ)



54. A similar conclusion was reached in relation to the Pure Food Act 1908 (NSW) which created an offence where a person refused to supply or allow to be obtained a sample demanded in accordance with that legislation. Campbell J found in Boon v Maher (1986) 7 NSWLR 232 that the word “refuse” “... conveys more than a mere failure or inability to do something as is exemplified by its frequent use with other words such as `or fails’ ...” (page 237). He went on to endorse impliedly the proposition that its ordinary meaning involved the idea of some exercise of discretion or some exercise of will (page 237).



55. Finally, I shall refer to Keen Bros Pty Ltd v Young (1982) 44 ALR 519 to which Mr Savage referred. Wells J of the South Australian Supreme Court considered whether the appellant had refused to allow an inspector to inspect certain records under the Potato Marketing (Furnishing of Information) Regulations (S.A.). Wells J said at pages 523-524:



“Mr Debelle strongly challenged the use of the expressions `conditional refusal’ and `conditionally refused’. If (Mr Debelle contended) the learned special magistrate intended to hold that a conditional refusal amounted to a refusal within the meaning of the charge, he was wrong in law: `refusal’ means a final or unconditional refusal. With that I agree. Just as, in claims in detinue, the possessor is given a reasonable time to verify the basis of the claimant’s demand, so ought an employee, to whom a request is made, be given a reasonable opportunity to consult his employer before acceding to the request. To defer compliance with a request is not, of itself, a refusal. To defer for an unreasonable time may amount to a refusal. The matter becomes one of fact and degree.”



56. Returning to the present case, it seems to me that I should look at all of the circumstances to determine whether or not the Commissioner can be said to have refused to make a decision under either section 14T or section 14U. Given the circumstances of the case, he cannot be said to have made a decision to refuse to make a decision. At the time the application for review was lodged he had either had not had any opportunity for he had not been asked to make it or, if he had been so asked, he had been given only a few minutes in which to make it.



57. In those few minutes, the applicant appears to have expected the Commissioner to decide whether he is satisfied that the tax liabilities are irrecoverable or that he should exercise his discretion to revoke it under section 14T. These are different questions from those he considered under section 14S when he issued the DPO and I can understand if he needs more than a few minutes in which to make the decision. The issues raised for consideration under section 14T also differ from those under section 14S and again would require some time to determine. The time taken by the Commissioner has not been unreasonable.



58. Miss Gilligan has stated that the Commissioner has not made a decision refusing to make a decision. The material I have fully supports that position and I find that he has not made such a refusal. Furthermore, I find that he has not purported to make such a refusal whether under the Act or otherwise.



59. I turn now to Mr Savage’s final argument and that is whether a decision has actually been made by or on behalf of the Commissioner and not actually conveyed to the applicant. Miss Gilligan has stated that such a decision has not been made. That statement is fully supported by the material before me. The time which the Commissioner has had to consider the applicant’s applications under sections 14T and 14U has been extremely short and it is not, in my view, unreasonable that he has not made a decision. I note again that the issues raised by those applications require a consideration of matters different from those raised by the initial DPO although, there may of course, perhaps be some overlap of material. I find, therefore, that the Commissioner has not made a decision under either section 14T or section 14U.



60. For the reasons I have given, I decided yesterday that the Tribunal does not have jurisdiction to hear and determine the application lodged on 17 January, 1995.
