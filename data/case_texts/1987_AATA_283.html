Re Taxation Appeals [1987] AATA 283 (30 July 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

Nos. NT86/10166, NT86/10167, NT86/10168, NT86/10169, NT86/10170, NT86/10171,

NT86/10172 and NT86/10173

AAT No. 3669

Income Tax

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

Dr R.A. Hayes (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - undisclosed income - amended assessments - evidence of systematic deposits and withdrawals to secret building society accounts over an eight year period - applicant argued that deposits represented savings and other non-assessable funds - same funds being "recycled" between accounts - onus of proof on applicant - failure to offer plausible explanation of financial transactions - no corroboration from financial records.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss.170; 190; 226

HEARING

SYDNEY

30:7:1987

ORDER

The decisions under review are affirmed.

DECISION

The Review

This is an application for review of a decision by the respondent disallowing the applicant's objections against the assessments based on income for the years ended 30 June 1972 to 1979 inclusive. The applicant was unrepresented at the hearing of her application. However, she was assisted by her accountant.

2. The respondent's reasons for disallowing the applicant's claims were that the applicant's taxable income in each of the years ended 30 June 1972 to 1979 inclusive was not less than the following:

1972 - $20,069.00

1973 - $16,333.00

1974 - $22,372.00

1975 - $11,395.00

1976 - $15,268.00

1977 - $22,401.00

1978 - $38,545.00

1979 - $37,661.00

Further, the respondent asserted that the applicant had omitted to include income in her income tax returns in each of the relevant years, and became liable to pay as additional tax, under sub-section 226(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (the Act), an amount equal to double the difference between the tax properly payable by her and the tax that would have been payable on the basis of the returns furnished by her. The respondent remitted part of the amounts of additional tax under sub-section 226(3); and the circumstances were not regarded as warranting any further remissions.

The Evidence

3. The applicant admitted that over the relevant period she had kept various building society accounts, in her maiden name, and with fictitious addresses, and had regularly deposited into them various sums of money, also from time to time making certain withdrawals. Gradually, the balances in these accounts built up, so that at various times over the relevant period, the amounts in them were quite substantial.

4. These accounts were not disclosed in her various tax returns. Ultimately, a routine vouching operation revealed that there were various building society accounts operated in a name for which no income tax returns were lodged. The Department undertook a systematic investigation which revealed that the applicant was the person operating the accounts. The deposits into the accounts, and the interest derived therefrom, constitutes the income which the respondent asserts that the applicant omitted to include in her assessable income in each of the years over the relevant period, providing the basis for the assessments set out above.

5. There was no dispute about the existence of the accounts, the amounts of the deposits, or the amounts by way of interest derived therefrom. Essentially, the applicant's case was that the deposits represented savings, and capital sums which, for reasons which I shall go on to explain, she felt constrained to keep secret, primarily from her husband, and inevitably as a flow-on from this, from the respondent. She admits that the interest which she earned on these various deposits over the relevant period ought to have been declared, and concedes her liability to pay tax and additional tax on this.

6. The applicant's explanation for her conduct was as follows. Her marriage has always been difficult and she lives in fear of her husband. Accordingly, she has had to keep money constantly on hand in case she should need to make a sudden escape and set up house on her own.

7. The applicant said that she began saving small amounts of money in the mid 1960's. At this stage all the money was kept at home, hidden under the carpet. By 1972 she had accumulated a small amount of funds. She decided that it would be better to deposit the money in a building society account and keep only a small emergency fund at home. This was the initial deposit in the secret accounts. From this time onwards, the applicant continued to make deposits to the secret accounts.

8. In 1975 the applicant read newspaper reports of official investigations of undisclosed bank interest. To protect herself, she began to recycle deposits from building society to building society, closing accounts and opening others, believing that this course of conduct would make it more difficult to trace the accounts. In this way, she consolidated the official perception that she was attempting to deceive the respondent by hiding income in various secret accounts.

9. The applicant also gave evidence that in January 1971, immediately prior to her return to Australia from a holiday in Greece, while at the airport, her sister-in-law gave her an amount of money (some in Australian dollars, some in drachmas) to deposit in an Australian account. The applicant felt constrained to hold this on trust for her sister-in-law. The money was deposited into a building society account in the applicant's name, in July 1971. This was not in the applicant's maiden name as were the later building society accounts. The 6 month delay was explained by the applicant's apprehension about these arrangements.

10. The large sums which gradually built up in the various building society accounts were not explained solely by the applicant in terms of recycling of a much smaller deposit with the addition of savings from account to account, and her position as trustee for a relative. In addition, she pointed to various capital sums which she had received over the relevant period. She admits that she deceived the respondent in relation to the interest which accumulated on these capital amounts. But she asserts that, as capital sums, they should not be taken into account by the respondent in calculating her taxable income.

11. In attempting to make out this aspect of her case, the applicant pointed to a number of different kinds of capital and other non taxable sums. Essentially, they fall under the following headings:

. Sums derived from the sale in Australia of

inheritances and gifts from relatives in Greece.

. Amounts received in settlement of insurance claims

in respect of burglaries from premises owned and

occupied by the applicant and her husband.

. Income earned by her husband.

. After tax profits derived by the applicant and her

husband through their various ventures, joint and

sole.

. Capital amounts from the sale of the assets of a

business previously conducted by the applicant and

her husband.

. Interest earned by the applicant's husband.

. The proceeds from the maturity of a life assurance

policy held by the applicant's husband.

. Repayment of various loans made by the applicant to

other businesses or persons.

Thus, on the applicant's account of things, all of the deposits in the various building society accounts can be accounted for as non-taxable in her hands.

The Applicant

12. Some essential words of background about the applicant are necessary. She has been extremely hard working, enterprising, and industrious. With her husband, over the relevant period, she has acquired and developed property from which rental income is earned. For some time, she had an interest with her husband in a laundromat, which was ultimately sold. For many years, she worked as a journalist with various Greek newspapers. She is now a newspaper proprietor in her own right. Her husband was for some time engaged in operating the laundromat. He has also worked for one of the newspapers with which the applicant was engaged. The applicant has imported into Australia jewellery and other valuables which were inherited from relatives in Greece, and these have been sold, mainly through contacts made through the laundromat. The applicant has made loans to others and borrowed money herself. She has been a trustee for others who for their own purposes, wished to move currency out of Greece. In a small way, she has been a money changer, selling drachmas for Australian dollars. Her financial affairs, and no doubt those of her husband, have to say the least been complex.

13. It might be thought that, with the applicant's complex network of financial arrangements, some form of systematic accounting and bookkeeping might have been valuable. But she largely kept it all in her head. Part of the explanation for this, she says, was the need to keep details of some of her wealth hidden from her husband. But putting this aspect aside, the way in which the applicant and her husband ended up attending to the details of matters financial was to leave the whole of the burden of accounting to the applicant. She had total control of the family finances. In her mind, everything balanced out.

14. The applicant gave evidence that she received funds from a number of different sources in any period, including her own salary, her husband's wages, income from the business, the rents from the properties, and various capital sums as outlined. As expenses became due she would pay them with whatever funds were available. It did matter to her, for example, that money from the sale of a tapestry could have been paid to a tradesperson for repairs to one of the properties, or that the rent could have been used to pay expenses of the business.

15. Whatever funds remained were regarded as "savings", a proportion or possibly all of which, she could use for her own ends. Her husband only cared that expenses were met, and provision made for day to day needs. In this way the applicant maintains that the deposits to the building society accounts were a mixture of savings and capital amounts.

16. In advancing all of this, the applicant relied upon her reputation in the Greek community as an honourable, honest, hard working, and self sacrificing person. Character evidence was given on her behalf by highly placed and respected citizens. Furthermore, affidavits and declarations made by relatives and others that they had entrusted money to the applicant, purchased family inheritances, or whatever, was before me.

The Applicant's Burden of Proof

17. Section 190(b) of the Act places the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive upon the applicant. The respondent has come forward with evidence of systematic financial dealings through a number of secret building society accounts, involving large sums of money, and over a long period.

18. The applicant admits that she maintained these secret accounts. Her claim, that she was forced to engage in so complex a subterfuge because of the need to maintain a nest egg hidden from her husband, is implausible. She might well be the victim of domestic violence. She might well have needed to keep at hand a sum of money to which she could have ready recourse in the event of her suddenly being precipitated out onto the street. But the details of the accounts, as provided by the respondent, and uncontested by the applicant, show a course of conduct and series of financial arrangements much too extensive to be explicable in such terms.

19. In any event, while her husband might have been physically overbearing, it was part of the applicant's case that she was the financial mistress. He had virtually no say whatsoever over financial matters. The applicant is a resourceful person. She could have dealt far more efficiently, effectively, and simply with her need to maintain a secret sinking fund than through the network of building society accounts that she opened over the relevant period.

20. The very fact of the admitted maintenance of secret accounts, in which deposits of amounts, large and small, were constantly being made, is highly suggestive of a scheme to defraud the revenue. The applicant's case was essentially that, she was recycling various amounts accumulated by way of savings from account to account, in order to keep a step ahead of any possible official investigation into undisclosed "bank" interest.

21. When one inspects the detailed breakdown of deposits and withdrawals, which again, I emphasise, was not contested, one cannot see in it any sign of some recycling process. On the contrary, on its face the record speaks quite clearly of a systematic series of small deposits, redolent of ordinary business banking. As an example in the 1977/78 tax year, there were 119 deposits totalling $33,927 (including interest). There were only 3 withdrawals within a six day period, totalling $33,417.85. There was no systematic matching of withdrawals and deposits.

22. This impression, from the face of the material tendered by the applicant, is reinforced by what is notorious, namely, that the applicant, by dint of her various business activities, and indeed, in the course of her work as a salaried journalist, would constantly be receiving cash from clients, tenants, and others - cash which she might be tempted to deposit in secret accounts in order to avoid tax.

23. These are admittedly hypotheses. The applicant strongly denies them. She relies on her high public reputation, which I am sure is justified. But this is not enough to displace the onus which the Act places upon her. In the circumstances which I have detailed, one way through which she might have displaced the obvious inferences from the evidence adduced by the respondent was through the production of business records and books systematically detailing all aspects of the funds passing through her hands. But the applicant produced no bookkeeping records. She fell back on a picture of herself at the start of the relevant period as a naive and inexperienced young woman battling in a new country to make a go of things, to whom proper bookkeeping would be completely foreign. But again, an inference from the absence of books is that the keeping of them would not have suited the applicant in what on the face of it would seem to be a long term plan to avoid paying tax on a significant part of her income through the maintenance of secret accounts.

24. The applicant relied heavily upon the fact that she had indeed over the relevant period received various capital amounts, such as settlement of insurance claims in respect of burglaries, the sale of inheritances and gifts from relatives, repayments to her of loans, the proceeds from the sale of business assets, and so forth. The receipt of these various capital items was documented. But it is difficult to see how far this takes her. In relation to a number of them, it was clear from the evidence that the proceeds were paid into her non-secret bank accounts. For example, as part of her objection to the amended assessment for the year ended 30 June 1974, the applicant pointed to the receipt of $706 as an insurance payout. In relation to this item, Mr Neligan for the Commissioner submitted that:

"the objections would appear - and I think the evidence

that will come out will show that they were prepared by

reference to a number of accounts which were not

included in the amended assessments; the deposits to

those accounts were not included in the amended

assessments. This $706 is merely the first of quite a

few deposits made to a variety of cheque accounts,

either at Paddington or at the Commonwealth Trading Bank

at Broadway, which have been held in the objections to

be receipts of a capital nature or a non-assessable

nature."

This point was reinforced in submissions (transcript p.142), and sustained by the evidence.

25. Thus, the evidence of receipt and deposit of capital amounts proved little. The applicant of course then fell back on her system of mental bookkeeping, which I have described. She said that the deposit of an item of capital into a non-secret bank account would allow her to deposit other funds from a declared source, into the building society accounts. Essentially she was saying: "Trust me. On my system of accounting everying balanced out." But apart from anything else, s.190(b) of the Act influences me from this course.

26. The only evidence of recycling that the applicant could actually point to in the financial records was for a period in April 1979, where there were a small number of deposits into one account, and equivalent withdrawals from another account. Her explanation for the constant stream of deposits, matched against infrequent large withdrawals, was that when she made a withdrawal she would take the money home, and hide it in a cabinet. Over an extended period of time this money would be deposited in other accounts.

27. There were however a number of contradictions to this story. At one stage the applicant said that she made frequent withdrawals of up to $500, as this was the limit she could withdraw in cash. Most of the withdrawals were however for much larger amounts. Presumably these withdrawals were by cheque. Her evidence was that the cheques were then deposited into a bank account, and the corresponding amount of cash withdrawn, taken home and stored in a cabinet. Despite the fact that the applicant tendered detailed financial records relating to her bank account, she could not prove these transactions.

28. In light of all of the material before me, and my assessment of the applicant over the two days of the hearing, I find her assertion in this respect also to be implausible.

29. The final issue was that the amended assessments issues on 10 June 1980, in respect of the 1972 and 1973 tax years, were outside the six years allowed by s.170(2)(b) of the Act. However, there is nothing in the evidence before me which would make me doubt the assessment of the Commissioner, pursuant to s.170(2)(a) of the Act, that the avoidance of tax was due to fraud or evasion.

30. Accordingly, I have no alternative but to uphold the decisions under review.
