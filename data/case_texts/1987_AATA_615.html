Carr and Commissioner for Superannuation [1987] AATA 615 (6 May 1987)

Carr and Commissioner for Superannuation [1987] AATA 615 (6 May 1987)

Last Updated: 30 January 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

SUPERANNUATION - entitlement to invalidity benefits - applicant retired on ground of incapacity - issue of a Benefit Classification Certificate - certificate specified conditions of "epilepsy", "visual defect", "anxiety depression" - whether incapacity was caused by or was substantially contributed to by one or more of the conditions specified in the certificate.

Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth.) ss. 16, 66

Commonwealth Employees (Redeployment and Retirement) Act 1979 (Cth.) ss.,14, 16, 19.

Commissioner for Superannuation v Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 63 ALR 237

RICHARD JOHN JOSEPH CARR

Applicant

COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent No N84/485

AAT Decision No 3427

Deputy President C J Bannon QC

Dr D.J. Howell (Member)

Mr N.J. Attwood (Member)

Respondent REASONS FOR DECISION

6 May 1987 Deputy President C J Bannon QC

Dr D.J. Howell (Member)

Mr N.J. Attwood (Member)

Richard John Joseph Carr ("the applicant") was retired from the Public Service with effect from 25th January 1983, pursuant to section 19(1) of the Commonwealth Employees (Redeployment and Retirement) Act 1979 (Cth.) ("C.E.R.R. Act") following the issue of a certificate (T20/1) dated 28 January 1983 issued under section 14(1) of the said Act by a Delegate of the Public Service Board, certifying that the applicant:

"should, for the reason that he is, in consequence of physical or mental incapacity, incapable of performing his duties, be retired from his employment".

It was open to the applicant to appeal against the issue of the certificate to a Tribunal constituted pursuant to section 16 of the C.E.R.R. Act, in accordance with section 15 of that Act. However, no appeal was instituted and he was accordingly retired (T21/1). Further, pursuant to the provision in section 19(3)(a) of the C.E.R.R. Act, the applicant on 8th February 1983, signed a notice of waiver of his right of appeal to a Tribunal (Exhibit 7). There may be some doubt as to whether or not he fully understood the nature of that document, but the fact is that it is now over four years since the retirement and he has moved from Sydney and bought a home at Nambucca Heads.

The applicant was born on 10th June 1944 and was educated at a school for blind and partially blind boys. In about 1970 he became employed in a temporary capacity as an Orderly in the laundry at Concord Repatriation General Hospital. Despite an adverse medical report recommending against permanent employment of the applicant (T10/1) he was subsequently accepted as a permanent public servant and became an eligible employee under the provisions of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth.) ("the Act") on 21st March 1978, by virtue of his appointment as an Orderly within the Department of Veterans' Affairs. For the purposes of section 16 of the Act he underwent a medical examination on 15th May 1978. This resulted in the issue on 13th December 1978 of a Benefit Classification Certificate ("BCC") (T12/1) pursuant to section 16(4) of the Act, a copy of which was sent to the applicant on the same day (T13/1).

The BCC stated that in the opinion of the Delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation, the applicant was not likely by reason of or for a reason connected with the mentioned physical or mental conditions to continue to be an eligible employee until he attained his maximum retiring age. The mentioned conditions were "Epilepsy", "Visual Defect" and "Anxiety Depression".

In spite of these problems and in spite of alleged badinage and teasing by fellow employees, the applicant continued to work in the hospital laundry. There were a number of days lost in sick leave as set out in Exhibit 3. He stated his epilepsy was controlled by medication and his partial blindness did not affect his job. In 1979 he fell over at home and fractured the tibia in his left leg. After hospitalisation at Gosford he returned to work. On 17th April 1980 the applicant suffered a more serious injury. Crossing Concord Road, Concord, he was struck by a motor vehicle and sustained compound fractures of both his legs. As a result of that accident the applicant was away from his work until about February or March 1982. When he returned to work he was placed back on duty in the laundry but became afraid of further damage to his legs as the result of collision with laundry trolleys. After two weeks back in the laundry, at his own request, he was redeployed to work sweeping gutters and roads in the hospital grounds. He did this work for one or two months until a laundry truck nearly ran him down. He told his Superintendent of the problem and was then redeployed to work cleaning windows. He found this work worrying because of the risk of epileptic fits while working on ladders and because his partial blindness restricted him from seeing cobwebs clearly so as to do his work properly. Following the redeployments mentioned the applicant faced further medical examinations leading to the decision to retire him.

For the purpose of assessing the applicant's entitlement to invalidity benefits pursuant to section 66 of the Act a decision was made by a Delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation on 2nd May 1983 (T5/1). The decision was that the incapacity which was the ground for retirement of the applicant was caused by or substantially contributed to by the physical and mental conditions set out in the BCC. That decision was reconsidered by Mr Ronald Charles Withear, another Delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation pursuant to section 154(4) of the Act. On 24th August 1984, Mr Withear confirmed the original decision made pursuant to section 66(2)(c) of the Act. His findings and reasons are set out in Tribunal Document No. 4. The applicant seeks a review of that decision by this Tribunal.

Although the applicant's counsel, Mr G. Scragg, contended that the applicant was in fact capable of working again as an Orderly in a hospital laundry, a view asserted by his client, the Tribunal is of the opinion that it is precluded by reason of the applicant's retirement on the ground of invalidity pursuant to sections 16 and 19 of the C.E.R.R. Act from now finding that nevertheless he was capable of performing the duties of an Orderly. Incapacity must be taken to be established, the only matter open to be reviewed before us being the question whether or not that incapacity was caused by or was substantially contributed to by one or more of the conditions stated on his BCC, or by conditions connected with such conditions.

In spite of a life burdened with physical and psychiatric difficulties, the applicant presented himself to us as a rather cheerful person and undoubtedly deserves credit for his attempts to play a useful role in society. Obviously other persons have assisted in this respect and officers of the Department of Veterans' Affairs have shown him some sympathy and consideration over a number of years. The Tribunal was informed that as the result of legal proceedings, the applicant obtained a settlement of his claim for negligence in respect of the motor accident, resulting in a payment to him, no doubt without prejudice, of the sum of $120,000 gross and about $85,000 nett after repayment of wages or compensation of a sum in the vicinity of $13,000 and around $12,800 in medical expenses and other expenses (Exhibit G). He has used this money to purchase a home at Nambucca Heads, where he helps clean a block of flats on a voluntary basis.

Extensive medical evidence was adduced by the parties before the Tribunal. The applicant called two doctors and the respondent called five doctors in a hearing lasting over three days. It would be pointless to recite all their evidence or to make findings on all minutiae of fact and opinion involved in their evidence. Both psychiatrists called, Dr Michael Giuffrida for the applicant and Dr David S. Bell for the respondent, agreed that the applicant suffered a personality disorder. Dr Giuffrida took the view that the personality disorder showed "features of the so-called epileptoid type and the paranoid type and that....these both arise as the long terra consequence of temporal lobe epilepsy." (Exhibit C).

Dr Bell preferred the view that the applicant's psychiatric disorder could be described as anxiety/depression, these words being a reference to the main symptoms displayed (Exhibit 1). He said the notion of an epileptoid personality disorder was outmoded. He said the applicant's personality disorder had features of the paranoid type but the connection with epilepsy is highly speculative. Dr Bell ruled out a mental illness such as a paranoid state (Exhibit 1).

The disagreement between these highly qualified gentlemen stemmed in part from methods of classification of symptoms. With some diffidence, we prefer on the whole, the view of the respondent's psychiatric witness, Dr Bell, who said in part, (Exhibit 1, p. 4) in a passage we accept:-

"Certainly there is good evidence for believing that epilepsy arising in the left temporal lobe can bring about a paranoid schizophrenia, but the clinical features in this case point to a discharging focus in the right temporal lobe, which does not have this association at all."

These conclusions lead us to say also that the description of a condition in the BCC as "anxiety depression" should not be regarded as a precise technical description in accordance with the terminology of the American categorisation system known as D.S.M. In our view it sufficiently describes a condition with which the personality disorder existing at the time of the applicant's retirement was connected. See Commissioner for Superannuation v Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 63 ALR 237, 238 per Davies J.

Having said the above, we found the applicant to be a more cheerful and sensible person that the evidence suggested. We consider the condition loosely termed anxiety depression was in existence and played some part in the applicant's incapacity. Dr G. Quinn, the Acting Deputy Director of the Department of Health placed it at 10%. We would not disagree, if percentages are appropriate.

An acute controversy erupted as to the extent of the orthopaedic injuries suffered by the applicant as the result of the motor accident in April 1980. Reports and oral testimony from various doctors were received in evidence. We have weighed these up and have come to the conclusion that we prefer the views expressed by Dr Bernard Bloch in his report of 13th October 1981 (T16/1). The fractures of both legs were compound and reasonably serious. The bones were slow in reuniting, but we accept Dr Bloch's conclusion that physically the applicant recovered well from the accident and that there was no residual disability of merit in orthopaedic assessment. Dr Bloch's views received confirmation from a report by Dr Robin J.E.D. Higgs (Exhibit 6) and the evidence given by Dr Munro S. Alexander and his report (T30). We consider the applicant's medical evidence exaggerated the extent of the applicant's orthopaedic problems. That is not to say that the injuries did not play a substantial part in the incapacitating of the applicant from work. We believe they did.

Coupled with the applicant's anxiety disorder and his partial blindness, the orthopaedic injuries and the previous leg fracture suffered in 1979 all played a part in making the applicant fearful of further injuries to his legs whether from trolleys in the laundry, or a fall from a ladder due to an epileptic fit, or injury by a car when sweeping the hospital grounds. Indeed a man without a personality disorder may well have feared further injury after successive fractures to his legs.

On or about 3rd August 1982, a further medical examination of the applicant was made by Dr N.J. Burke, a Commonwealth Medical Officer. His report is Document T18. However the words and percentages at the top of page 2 of T18 and the recommendation at the foot of the page were inserted by Dr Quinn. Before adding those items to the report Dr Quinn conferred with Dr JH Voss of the QEII Rehabilitation Service Centre (T19/1) whose written report dated 26th January 1983 (T27/2) was later furnished to the Commissioner for Superannuation. Dr Voss saw the applicant at the suggestion of Dr Quinn and formed the view expressed as follows:-

"In summary his disabilities consist of sequelae of fractures of the lower legs, anxiety depression, blindness and poorly controlled epilepsy, rendering him unfit to work for the foreseeable future."

Although not a specialist Dr Voss had had considerable experience in rehabilitation work. It was not until after he had sought further advice from Dr Voss, that the Acting Deputy Director of Health, Dr Quinn, felt obliged to reject Dr Burke's view, expressed in T18/2, that the applicant was fit for employment in the position presently occupied.

The form, T18, required a medical officer to list the conditions from which the applicant suffered in order of severity and to place estimated percentages against each as to the degree that each contributed to incapacity. Dr Quinn set out the following:-

"1. Partial Blindness 50%

2. Epilepsy 20%

3. Depression 10%

4. Bilateral tibia and fibula fractures 10%"

As stated previously it is now too late to postulate that the applicant's retirement was not due to incapacity.

We agree with Dr Quinn that all four factors contributed substantially to causing the applicant's retirement. While percentages are not, in our opinion, a proper method for expressing a qualitative analysis of what is a question of mixed fact and law, we take the view that the major contributions arose from the applicant's blindness and the fractures. However we agree that the other conditions also made a not insignificant contribution to a cumulative situation in which the applicant was rendered incapable of carrying out his duties. As three of the four factors were stated on the applicant's BCC and substantially contributed to his incapacity, notwithstanding the earnest and careful submissions made on behalf of the applicant, we have come to the conclusion that the decision under review was correct and the decision is therefore affirmed.

The result is that the applicant's superannuation entitlement is to be calculated in accordance with section 66(2)(c) and (e) of the Act. Mr Carr receives a pension in respect to his condition of blindness. The Tribunal hopes that in spite of his problems he will continue to lead a useful life at Nambucca Heads.
