Prestige Motors Pty Limited v Commissioner of Taxation [1993] FCA 580; (1993) 93 Atc 5021 (1993) 27 Atr 160, (1993) 118 ALR 497 (1993) 47 FCR 138 (3 December 1993)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

PRESTIGE MOTORS PTY LIMITED v. COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. NG380 of 1993

FED No. 878

Number of pages - 14

Income Tax

(1993) 93 ATC 5021

[1993] FCA 580; (1993) 27 ATR 160, (1993) 118 ALR 497

[1993] FCA 580; (1993) 47 FCR 138

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

VON DOUSSA(1), HILL(2) AND BEAZLEY(3) JJ

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Notice of Assessment - whether notice addressed to a named "trust" was a valid notice for the purposes of s.174 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. Contents of a valid notice of assessment discussed.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936: ss.95, 97, 98, 99A, 99, 100A, 166, 169, 174, 177.

Batagol v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 CLR 243; applied.

Bloemen Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 27; (1980) 147 CLR 360; discussed.

Hickman v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1922] HCA 58; (1922) 31 CLR 232; applied.

HEARING

SYDNEY, 13 August 1993

3:12:1993

Counsel and Solicitors

for Appellant: DH Bloom QC and M Christie

instructed by CR Fieldhouse

Counsel and Solicitors

for Respondent: AH Slater QC and JW Durack

instructed by the Australian

Government Solicitor

ORDER

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

1. Appeal allowed.

2. Orders of Gummow J made on 2 June 1993 be set aside and in

lieu thereof it be declared that the notices referred to in

paragraph 4 of the statement of claim herein are not notices

of assessment for the purposes of s.174 of the Income Tax

Assessment Act 1936.

3. Respondent to pay appellant's costs of the appeal and of the

proceedings before Gummow J.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

von Doussa J I agree with the reasons for judgment prepared by Hill J and with the orders he proposes.

Introduction

HILL J The appellant, Prestige Motors Pty Limited ("the appellant") appeals against the judgment of a judge of this Court upholding the validity of certain notices, alleged to be notices of assessment of income tax in respect of the years of income ended 30 June 1979 to 30 June 1990, inclusive ("the notices"). Consequent upon the judgment declarations were made that the notices were notices of assessment served by the respondent, the Commissioner of Taxation ("the Commissioner"), upon the appellant and further that income tax assessed to the appellant was due and payable to the Commissioner pursuant to s.204 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) ("the Act"), on the 30th day after service of each of the notices.

2. The proceedings were commenced by the Commissioner in the original jurisdiction of the High Court of Australia and were remitted to the Court pursuant to s.44 of the Judiciary Act 1903. The case proceeded upon agreed facts which supplemented admissions made on the pleadings.

3. At all relevant times the appellant was the trustee of a trust estate known as the "Prestige Toyota Trust". In that capacity it caused tax returns to be lodged with the Commissioner detailing the income derived by it as trustee and the deductions claimed by it. In due course calculations were made by officers of the Australian Taxation Office resulting in the computation of tax payable and these calculations were reflected in the notices which were headed "Notice of Assessment".

4. Each of the notices showed a file number and was addressed to:

"Prestige Toyota Trust

c/- Price Waterhouse

13th Floor,

200 St Georges Terrace

PERTH WA 6000".

5. The tax file number is the same number as appeared upon the tax returns lodged by the appellant and the accountant's address is the address for service of notices shown on those returns. I should perhaps add that the file number shown on the notices was not the file number of the appellant itself, which it affixes upon returns of income derived by it beneficially. The tax returns completed by the appellant for the relevant years of income required the taxpayer completing the return, if a company, to indicate its name, file number, address of registered office and name of public officer. Each of these items of information was completed by the appellant in the relevant returns.

6. In the body of the notices appeared information giving a monetary figure for the "taxable income" and a monetary figure for the tax assessed on that taxable income and a figure said to be the balance of the assessment. Although immaterial to the present appeal, there were also shown on the notices, inter alia, other amounts payable together with a figure for the total amount payable by the appellant. In years where it was exigible, there also appeared on the notices details of the Medicare levy payable and on some of the notices there appeared a note of what was said to be "additional tax for incorrect return". In the final year there was shown additionally an amount of provisional tax.

7. The appellant objected to the purported assessments, without admission that they had been validly made, relying, inter alia, upon a ground that there had not been an assessment and that the notices were not notices of assessments validly made of the net income of the trust estate and the tax payable thereon. The objections were in due course disallowed and the appellant having appealed against the objection decisions, those proceedings were adjourned pending the outcome of the present appeal.

8. Before the learned judge below and before us, the appellant submitted that there could be no valid notice of assessment unless there appeared upon the face of that notice (where the assessment was made of a trustee):

(1) the name of the taxpayer;

(2) the portion of the net income of the trust estate upon which

the trustee was liable to pay tax; and

(3) the amount of tax payable.

9. As the present notices were, it was alleged, defective in failing properly to set out the first two of these matters, the notices were not, it was submitted, valid notices of assessment.

The Statutory Background

10. Before considering the competing submissions, it is convenient to set out the relevant statutory provisions as they stood in the Act as at 19 June 1991, the date appearing on the majority of notices. References to the substantive provisions concerning the assessability of the income of trust estates, however, are references to the Act as it stood in the relevant years of income.

11. The Act deals with the taxation of trust income in Division 6 of Part III of the Act. The form that that Division took, at relevant times, reflects a long history both of case law and of legislative intervention. The scheme of the Division, however, still remains much as it was in 1936.

12. At the heart of the provisions lies s.95(1) which defines the "net income" in relation to a trust estate. The "net income" of a trust estate is to be calculated on the assumption that the trustee is a taxpayer by taking the total assessable income (calculated assuming also the trustee to be a resident) and subtracting therefrom all allowable deductions other than those specifically referred to in the sub-section.

13. The general principle enshrined in Division 6 is that that Division is an exclusive code (unless the Act provides otherwise) for the liability of the trustee to pay income tax in respect of the income of a trust estate: cf Union Fidelity Trustee Co of Australia Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1969] HCA 36; (1969) 119 CLR 177 at 181 per Barwick CJ. Under the Division liability for tax in respect of trust law income may fall either upon the beneficiary or the trustee. The questions who is the relevant taxpayer and upon what share of the defined income that taxpayer will be liable to pay tax, depends upon whether there is (or are) a beneficiary (or beneficiaries) of the trust estate who is (or are) presently entitled to a share of the trust law income of that trust estate. Where there is a beneficiary (or there are beneficiaries) who alone or together is or are presently entitled to the whole of the trust law income, then subject to territorial matters not presently relevant, there will be no liability upon the trustee of that trust estate to pay tax and the beneficiary, or each of the beneficiaries as the case may be, who is presently entitled to a share of the trust law income, will have included in his or her assessable income his or her proportionate share of the net income: s.97.

14. Where, however, that beneficiary or beneficiaries are presently entitled, alone or together, to the whole of the trust law income, but the beneficiary, or one or more of the beneficiaries is under a legal disability, the trustee of the trust estate is to be assessed and liable to pay tax on a proportionate share of the net income correlative to that beneficiary's share of the trust law income: s.98. Where s.98 applies, the rate of tax is, in general terms, calculated as if the proportionate share of the net income, corresponding to the share of the trust law income to which the beneficiary under a disability was entitled, were income of that beneficiary, but not subject to any deductions.

15. Where there is a part of the trust law income to which no beneficiary is presently entitled, the trustee is to be assessed and is liable to pay tax in respect of a proportionate share of the net income corresponding to the share of the trust law income to which no person is presently entitled: s.99A or s.99. Where s.99A applies, the tax is payable at a special rate. Section 97, where it applies, requires the Commissioner to make an assessment in respect of the beneficiary or beneficiaries presently entitled, in respect of the whole or part of the net income of the trust estate of the year of income. In the case of ss.98, 99 and 99A, the Commissioner is directed to assess the trustee. In the present case, which is said to involve the application of s.99A through the deeming provisions of s.100A of the Act, that means that the Commissioner is directed to assess the appellant.

16. Section 254(1)(b) imposes upon the trustee, in respect of any income, or profits or gains of a capital nature derived by him in a representative capacity, the obligation to lodge returns. That section directs that an assessment be made of the trustee, but in a representative capacity only.

17. From the returns lodged by the trustee or any other information, the Commissioner is obliged to make an assessment. There may be a question as to whether that assessment is authorised under s.166 of the Act, or s.169. Nothing turns upon that question in the present case. If the assessment is authorised to be made under s.166, then it is an assessment of the "taxable income" of the taxpayer and the tax payable thereon. Since Division 6 does not as such require a trustee to be assessed for tax upon a "taxable income", it may be doubted whether s.166 is the appropriate assessing section. There may also be some doubt whether a trustee is properly within the definition of "taxpayer" in s.6(1), which definition may contemplate only the case of a person deriving income or profit and gains beneficially. But if s.166 be inapplicable, an assessment would nevertheless be authorised under s.169, where the Commissioner may make an assessment of the amount of tax a person is liable to pay. Clearly s.169 (like s.166) contemplates an assessment of a particular person's liability, in the present case, a liability arising under Division 6.

18. Section 174(1) pivotal to the present appeal provides relevantly:

"As soon as conveniently may be after any

assessment is made, the Commissioner shall serve

notice thereof in writing by post or otherwise

upon the person liable to pay the tax."

19. The notice of assessment required to be served upon the taxpayer by s.174(1) serves at least three functions. First, it delineates the time at which the income tax will become due and payable by the taxpayer. Thus s.204 provides that income tax assessed is to be due and payable "by the person liable to pay the tax" either on a date specified in the notice or, if no date is so specified, on the 30th day after the service of the notice. Secondly, it marks the commencement of time running for a taxpayer desirous of objecting to lodge with the Commissioner an objection against the assessment: s.185(1). The third role played by the notice of assessment is an evidentiary role in proceedings for the recovery of income tax or in proceedings disputing the assessment, either in the Administrative Appeals Tribunal or in this Court. Thus s.177(1) of the Act provided (until its amendment in 1991):

"The production of a notice of assessment, or of

a document under the hand of the Commissioner, a

Second Commissioner, or a Deputy Commissioner,

purporting to be a copy of a notice of

assessment, shall be conclusive evidence of the

due making of the assessment and, except in

proceedings under Part V on a review or appeal

relating to the assessment, that the amount and

all the particulars of the assessment are correct."

20. This section was subsequently amended in 1991 to substitute for the reference to Part V a reference to Part IVC of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 which replaced Part V. I shall return later to consider in detail this third role and the implications it has for the submissions of the Commissioner.

The Commissioner's Submission

21. The starting point of the Commissioner's submissions is the decision of the High Court in Batagol v The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 CLR 243. The actual issue in that case was quite remote from that in the present case. The Commissioner had notified the taxpayer, in respect of certain years of income, that no tax was payable because losses were available to be carried forward to offset any income derived. Subsequently the Commissioner sought to disallow some of the losses and to assess a positive amount of tax in respect of some of these years. The taxpayer sought to challenge the assessments on the basis that they were amended assessments and did not comply with the provisions of s.170(3) of the Act. It was held by the Court that the only time that there had been an assessment was when there had been a notification of a positive amount of tax payable.

22. In the course of his judgment, Kitto J, in a well-known passage, said (at 251-2):

"The word 'assessment' is defined in s.6 to

mean, unless the contrary intention appears, the

ascertainment of the amount of taxable income

and of the tax payable thereon. There is

nothing in s.170 to show the contrary intention.

But the definition is not sufficient by itself

to answer the question before us, because

'ascertainment' is a word the force of which

depends upon the context. It is here used in an

Act under which the service of a notice of

assessment is the levying of the tax.

Assessment in the sense of mere calculation

produces no legal effect. No step that the

Commissioner may take, even to the point of

satisfying himself of the amount of the taxable

income and of the tax thereon, has under the Act

any legal significance. But if the

Commissioner, having gone through the process of

calculation, serves on the taxpayer a notice

that he has assessed the taxable income and the

tax at specified amounts, the tax becomes by

force of the Act due and payable on the date

specified in the notice or (if no date is

specified) on the thirtieth day after the

service of the notice: s.204. Thus, and thus

only, there is brought about an 'ascertainment'

of the taxable income and of the tax, in the

sense that thereafter it is possible to say what

could not have been said before: that amounts

have been fixed so that they are to be taken for

all purposes (except those of appeal: see s.177)

to be the result flowing from the application of

the Act in the particular case. The respective

amounts of the taxable income and the tax have

been rendered certain. The word 'ascertainment'

being understood in this sense, the definition

of 'assessment' means, in my opinion, the

completion of the process by which the

provisions of the Act relating to liability to

tax are given concrete application in a

particular case with the consequence that a

specified amount of money will become due and

payable as the proper tax in that case."

23. Turning to s.174, his Honour expressed the view (at 253) that:

"...it should, I think, be understood to mean

that the Commissioner shall serve a notice of

assessment as soon as conveniently may be after

his work for the making of the assessment has

been done."

24. Counsel for the Commissioner submitted that the question before the Court involved an element of circularity. Thus it was said, if the assessment be treated as the whole process culminating with the service of a notice of assessment, then a conclusion that there has been an assessment must carry with it the consequence that the notice will have been a valid notice of it. Likewise, if there has not been an assessment in the relevant sense, that will be because the notice of assessment itself did not comply with the Act, with the consequence that there never was a valid assessment of which the taxpayer was given notice.

25. But whether or not there be in truth such circularity, it was the Commissioner's submission that once there had been a calculation or determination of taxable income and tax payable, all that remained to be done was to serve a notice of that taxable income and tax payable upon the person who was liable to pay the tax. It was unnecessary, so it was submitted, that any name at all appear on the notice of assessment and immaterial if the wrong name appeared upon it. If the document served satisfied the description of a notice of assessment and was served upon the taxpayer in circumstances making it clear that it was an assessment of his taxable income and tax payable, it was a valid notice of assessment, whether or not the taxpayer's name appeared on the face of it.

26. The real point of this submission was that completion of the process of assessment was brought about merely by service of a notice which advised the taxable income and tax payable. It was submitted that in the present case such service had been effected and in consequence the appellant became liable to pay the tax stated in the notices.

The Decision Appealed Against

27. The Commissioner succeeded below. The learned trial judge said (at 11):

"The returns had been furnished by the

defendant, as trustee of the trust estate, had

identified file No. 78 805 021 as the tax file

number, and had given 'c/- Price Waterhouse 200

St Georges Tce Perth' as the postal address for

service of notices upon the trustee. The

plaintiff had ascertained so much of the net

income of the trust estate as was net income in

respect of which the trustee was liable to pay

tax, the tax payable thereon, and certain

additional tax. The notices had identified the

same file number as 'Your file number', and had

given 'Details of your assessment', being 'your

taxable income' and the tax on taxable income.

The notices were addressed and sent to what the

defendant had given as the postal address for

services of notices upon it. There is no

dispute that the notices were received there and

came to the attention of the trustee which,

indeed, by its public officer, signed and lodged

Notices of Objection.

In those circumstances, the plaintiff had served

in writing by post upon the person liable to pay

the tax notices of the assessments he had made,

and thereby completed the assessment process.

The use of the file number, the address for

service nominated by the trustee, and the

reference to 'Your file number', 'Details of

your assessment' and 'your taxable income...'

made this clear."

28. His Honour acknowledged, and before us the Commissioner conceded, that the words "Prestige Toyota Trust" could not name a relevant legal entity. However, his Honour saw those words as identifying the relevant trust estate in respect of the net income of which trust the returns had been furnished and as confirming that the Prestige Toyota Trust was the trust estate in respect of which the trustee had been assessed. It was not necessary, in his Honour's view, for the name of the trustees to appear on the notices.

29. Notwithstanding what is said in Batagol, s.174 of the Act proceeds upon the basis that the word "assessment", where used in the section, can not have the meaning of a process culminating in the service of the notice itself. Section 174 contemplates, indeed, that there has been an assessment and thereafter service of a notice is required. In its context perhaps all that is meant by assessment in s.174 is that the Commissioner must bring to an end the administrative process of computation involved in the calculation of taxable income and tax payable (or in the case of an assessment made under s.99A, of determining the proportion of net income which is to be taxed to the trustee and determining the tax payable thereon) and must thereafter serve a notice under s.174 of those matters. No form is prescribed for the notice. Nor is there any regulation or other provision stipulating what is to be contained in it.

30. The difficulties with the Commissioner's submissions become apparent when two examples discussed during the course of argument are considered. The first, a somewhat extreme example, involves the service on taxpayer B by the Commissioner of a notice of assessment, on the face of which A is named as the taxpayer. At the time of service B is told that the document is a notice of his assessment. On the formulation by the Commissioner a valid notice of assessment would have been served so that taxpayer B would become liable to pay the tax referred to in the notice. If this be correct then that valid notice of assessment would additionally be a notice of assessment which could be used for the purposes of s.177 of the Act.

31. Section 177 is a critical section in the administration of the income tax laws. It ensures that proceedings to recover income tax are streamlined so that the Commissioner need do no more than produce a notice of assessment and prove its service to obtain judgment against a taxpayer. If taxpayer B, in the example given, were sued and the notice of assessment naming taxpayer A were tendered, a question would arise as to whether the assessment was an assessment of taxpayer B rather than taxpayer A. It would seem that the Commissioner would need to prove the liability of taxpayer B to the amount in order to recover judgment.

32. There is much to be said for the view that the words "the particulars of the assessment" in s.177(1) encompass not merely the figures of taxable income and tax payable shown in the notice of assessment, but also the name of the taxpayer to whom that notice is addressed. Were this not true, s.177 would fail in its evident purpose, that is to say, to avoid in recovery proceedings the need for the Commissioner to prove that the taxpayer did have a taxable income of a particular magnitude and to preclude the taxpayer from denying that the figure shown on the notice of assessment as the taxable income is indeed his taxable income or that the tax payable is properly payable thereon.

33. The same point can be made by considering a notice of assessment in respect of which no taxpayer at all is named. The Commissioner's submission was that provided it was made known, whether in writing or by oral communication, to the person whose assessment of taxable income and tax payable it was, that this was his or her assessment, then the document complied with the terms of s.174 and would be a valid notice of assessment. A fortiori again the document would be available for tender (and proof of service) under s.177, but such tender would not enable the Commissioner to proceed to judgment without further proof that the document was indeed a notice of assessment relating to the particular taxpayer showing his, her or its taxable income and the tax payable thereon.

34. Section 177 was authoritatively considered by the High Court in FJ Bloemen Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 27; (1980-1) 147 CLR 360. The issue in that case (and related cases heard at the same time) was whether in proceedings to recover income tax and following a tender of the notice of assessment under s.177, the taxpayer could challenge the assessments on the basis that they were made for an improper purpose or were tentative and thus void. Senior counsel for the Commissioner argued (as is apparent from the report of the argument at 363-4) that s.177 could not operate as conclusive evidence until there was in fact an assessment of which the notice gave notice. This being so it was submitted that the taxpayer could, in proceedings for recovery of the tax, challenge the actual existence of an assessment. This argument did not find favour with the Court. Mason and Wilson JJ, with whose judgment Stephen J agreed, (at 365) posed the question for decision. Their Honours said (at 373):

"What then is the effect of s.177(1)? Does

production of an appropriate notice of

assessment or document provide conclusive

evidence that an assessment was actually made,

or does it merely provide conclusive evidence

that an assessment, if made, was duly made?

Although it was said that the second alternative

reflected a more natural reading of the

provision, our view is that the language of the

section is equally susceptible of either reading

and that the answer is to be found, not so much

in the language, as in the context and in the

scope and purpose of the Act."

35. After discussing George v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 21; (1952) 86 CLR 183 and McAndrew v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1956] HCA 62; (1956) 98 CLR 263, their Honours (at 374) expressed the view that there was no reason to read s.177(1) so as to allow a taxpayer in recovery proceedings to contest the making of an original assessment when the taxpayer was entitled to contest his or her substantive liability to tax in proceedings under the then Part V of the Act. Having said this their Honours then expressed the policy underlying s.177(1) and manifest in it (at 375):

"The effect of this policy is that, once the

Commissioner takes advantage of s.177(1) by

producing an appropriate document, the taxpayer

is precluded from contesting that the

Commissioner has made an assessment or that in

making the assessment he has complied with the

statutory formalities. The taxpayer is entitled

to dispute his substantive liability to tax in

proceedings under Pt V."

36. Then (at 376) their Honours said:

"Novel consequences will arise if the

appellants' construction of s.177(1) be correct.

If the actual making of an assessment be not

comprehended by the expression 'due making' then

s.177(1) gives the Commissioner no evidentiary

advantage in proceedings for recovery of tax.

He would be obliged to prove that an assessment

was made by calling an assessor.... It is not

to be supposed that Parliament intended that

s.177(1) should have no application to the

actual making of the assessment, thereby

compelling proof by oral evidence in recovery

proceedings that an assessment had been made.

The consequence is so radical as to make the

argument quite unacceptable."

37. The Commissioner's argument before us suffers the same difficulty. If a notice of assessment containing no name of the taxpayer, or an incorrect name be tendered, the Commissioner would, if that document were a valid notice of assessment, obtain no evidentiary advantage in proceedings for recovery of tax. He would be obliged to prove that an assessment had been made of a particular taxpayer of which the notice was a notice and that the taxable income shown in the return was the taxable income assessed to the person being sued. It is hardly to be assumed that Parliament contemplated that such a course might occur. As the High Court observed, the effect of s.177 is to put the making of the assessment beyond challenge.

38. I would, however, not wish it to be thought that some accidental misspelling of the taxpayer's name would involve the same difficulty.

39. Counsel for the Commissioner submitted that in recovery proceedings evidence could be called, outside the matters appearing on the face of the assessment, to show that in fact there had been no assessment because, for example, the process of assessment had been tentative. In my view that does not accord with what is said by Mason and Wilson JJ in Bloemen. It is true that the judgment accepts the correctness of the previous decision of the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v S Hoffnung and Co Limited (1928) 42 CLR 39. However, it is also clear from what their Honours said in Bloemen (at 374) that their Honours saw that case, like Kellow-Falkiner v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1928) ALR 276, as proceeding upon the basis that the document said to be a notice of assessment bore evidence:

"...if not on its face then in the supporting

documentation, which denied it that character.

Visual inspection alone was sufficient to compel

the conclusion that the document was not a

notice of assessment for the purpose of the

relevant Act."

40. This passage makes it clear that the majority, in Bloemen at any rate, would treat an adjustment sheet which accompanied the assessment as part of the notice of assessment for this limited purpose. A contrary view was taken by Murphy J, in the same case (at 380), relying upon Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Reynolds (1981) 81 ATC 4131. I mention this because there was contained in the appeal papers an adjustment sheet which identified the appellant as the trustee of the trust and which, if read together with the notice of assessment, would have made it quite clear that the notice was addressed to the appellant. There was, however, no evidence before the judge below as to whether this notice of adjustment did in fact accompany the assessment and there was some indication that it probably did not. Given that the burden of proof lay upon the Commissioner in seeking the declaration, it is not proper for us to take into account that adjustment sheet as part of the notice of assessment, even if it were otherwise appropriate so to do.

41. Senior counsel for the Commissioner referred us to the recent decision of the High Court in McDonald v Commissioner of Business Franchises [1992] HCA 59; (1992) 175 CLR 472. That case, which considered s.19E(2) of the Business Franchise (Tobacco) Act 1974, is in terms similar to s.177 of the Act. The Court held that s.19E did not preclude the taxpayer from arguing, in recovery proceedings, that there was no power in the Commissioner under the Business Franchise Acts Amendment Act (1988) to assess tax. However, nothing said in that case supports the submissions of the Commissioner. Indeed the Court made it clear that the problem in McDonald did not arise under the Income Tax Assessment Act. The full court, comprising Mason CJ, Brennan, Deane, Dawson, Toohey, Gaudron and McHugh JJ, said (at 477-8):

"There, (under the Income Tax Assessment Act)

the power to make an assessment is continuing

and a document which purports to be a notice of

assessment, if in appropriate form, purports to

give notice of an exercise of the statutory

power. Such a document attracts the operation

of s.177(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act

even though a condition governing the exercise

of the power is disputed. A document which

purports to notify an exercise of a statutory

power to make the assessment can attract the

operation of provisions such as s.19E(2) of the

Act (the Business Franchise (Tobacco) Act) and

s.177(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, but a

document which purports to notify an

'assessment' made when the Act confers no power

to make it cannot attract the operation of such

protective provisions."

42. The two examples already given may be thought to be extreme. However, the point can be illustrated by a third example which might be thought to be not uncommon. Let it be assumed that after the end of the year of income there is a change of trustees so that when the time comes for an assessment to be made there is a new trustee. If the assessment (as in the present case) were directed merely to the "trust estate" at the last address for service shown on the tax return, to whom has that assessment been addressed? Is it addressed to the trustee at the time of the end of the year of income or the trustee at the time of the assessment?

43. It is not necessary to determine, for present purposes, which of the two trustees would be liable to pay tax, although the Act would appear to proceed upon the basis that the liability to tax is fixed as at year end with the consequence that the original trustee would be the person liable. The real problem is that because there is no addressee of the notice, the recipient of the notice in physical terms can not be sure whether it is addressed to him or her or to some other person.

44. In my view, it is critical for fixing the liability of a taxpayer at the time when that liability arises and, for the evidentiary consequences of the notice, that the notice, under s.174, make clear on its face the person whose assessment is reflected in the notice. Where no person is stipulated the consequence, in my view, is that there will not have been what the High Court referred to as "an appropriate notice of assessment". From this it follows either that the process of assessment is incomplete, or that there has not yet been imposed upon the taxpayer a liability to pay any amount of tax assessed. Both lead to the same result.

45. The second submission of the appellant, which appears to have been raised below, albeit rather faintly, was that the notices of assessment were invalid because they did not show on their face that part of the net income of the trust estate as is directed by Division 6 to be assessed to the trustee. As I understood the submission, a notice of assessment will be invalid unless it shows the net income of the trust estate as assessed in accordance with s.95(1) and the proportion of that net income (expressed as a percentage, fraction or absolute figure, as the case may be) which is taxable to the trustee under ss.98, 99 or 99A. Expressed in terms of the definition of "assessment" in s.6(1) of the Act relative to trustees, there must be shown, so the submission would have it, the portion of the net income of the trust estate as is net income in respect of which the trustee is liable to pay tax. Of course there would also need to be shown the tax payable by the trustee.

46. Clearly it would be desirable if this information were shown on the face of the notice of assessment. It would direct the mind of the trustee to whether the "net income" as assessed by the Commissioner accorded with that returned and by indicating the relevant proportion taxed to the trustee, it would enable the taxpayer to determine whether that proportion was agreed or disputed.

47. For the Commissioner it was said that all that was necessary to be included on the face of the notice of assessment was the "taxable income" and "tax payable". As already indicated the expression "taxable income" is totally inapt so far as an assessment under ss.98, 99 or 99A is concerned, but these could, for the purposes of the argument, be replaced by "share of net income to which the trustee is liable to pay tax" and "tax payable". In my view, as a very minimum, it would be necessary to ensure validity of the notice of assessment to state on the face of the notice the amount in monetary terms of the net income upon which the trustee is liable to pay tax and the tax payable thereon. That indeed appears to be what the Commissioner intended by the words "taxable income" and "tax payable" appearing on the notices of assessment in the present case.

48. In Hickman v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1922] HCA 58; (1922) 31 CLR 232, a case decided under the War-Time Profits Tax Assessment Act 1917-1918, Higgins J (at 245-6) said:

"I am informed by counsel that there is no form

of notice of assessment prescribed by any

regulations. If so, there is every reason for

the Commissioner to comply strictly with the

words of the Act (secs.22 and 26) and to show on

the face of his notice his 'assessment of the

amount upon which, in his judgment, war-time

profits tax ought to be levied.' The notice

before me is only a notice of the amount of tax

claimed, and the taxpayer is left to delve in

complicated figures in order to find the amount

on which the tax is to be levied."

49. The reference to the "amount upon which, in his judgment, war-time profits tax ought to be levied" is a reference to a provision corresponding to s.167 of the present Act, permitting the Commissioner to make a default assessment. What his Honour was saying, and, with respect, correctly, was that the notice of assessment must show the amount upon which the tax is calculated which, in the case of an assessment under s.167, would be the amount upon which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, tax ought to be levied, as well as showing the actual tax payable. But even if this be accepted, all that would be necessary for the Commissioner to show in the present case would be the proportionate amount taxed to the trustee (ie a figure being the proportion of the net income to which the trustee is liable to pay tax) and the tax payable upon that figure. It would be unnecessary for the notice of assessment to show the actual net income as determined.

50. It has always been a matter of some concern that the Commissioner is not bound to advise the taxpayer the detailed basis upon which an assessment is made at the time the assessment is notified to the taxpayer. There appears to be no obligation upon him to notify a taxpayer whether he has added income, reduced a deduction, disallowed a rebate or some combination of these. In McLelland v Commissioner of Taxation [1969] HCA 72; (1967) 118 CLR 353 at 361, at first instance Windeyer J noted that the Commissioner was not obliged to convey to the taxpayer the basis of the assessment. In that case there had been an addition to the amount of the taxable income returned to the taxpayer, which was unexplained. His Honour expressed the hope that this was unusual, adding that the failure to provide an explanation was certainly unfair. Failure to indicate how the taxable income was calculated did not, however, invalidate the assessment. Subsequently, in Giris Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1969] HCA 5; (1969) 119 CLR 365, Barwick CJ, discussing the discretion under s.99A, suggested that the Commissioner was under an obligation to inform the taxpayer of the reasons for the exercise of that discretion. However, his Honour gave no indication of how that duty could be enforced, or from whence it sprang. Unless proceedings have been commenced in the Court or the Tribunal there is little that can be done to require the Commissioner to provide reasons, either for an opinion which he has formed or by way of explanation of an assessment. Fortunately, however, the Commissioner will usually indicate to a taxpayer, and in sufficient time to allow the taxpayer to lodge an appropriate objection, the basis of his assessment. That may well be what happened in the present case and there was a suggestion from the bar table that the adjustment sheet may ultimately have been forwarded to the taxpayer after a request for information as to the manner in which the assessment had been calculated.

51. In my view, it would be sufficient to the validity of a notice of assessment made under s.99A if the notice set out the share of net income upon which the Commissioner assessed the trustee liable to tax and the tax payable by the trustee. Those are the relevant particulars of the assessment which, together with the name of the trustee, should appear upon the notice.

52. As the present notices were not appropriate "Notices of Assessment" because they did not contain the name of the person assessed, the process of assessment is presently incomplete and the appellant is entitled to succeed. The appellant is entitled to a declaration that for the purposes of the Act the notices are not "Notices of Assessment" served by the Commissioner upon the appellant. In consequence, the income tax shown on the face of the notices is not due and payable by the appellant. The Commissioner must pay the appellant's costs of the appeal.

Beazley J I agree with the reasons for judgment of Hill J.
