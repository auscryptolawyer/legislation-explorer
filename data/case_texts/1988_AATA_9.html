ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - allowable deductions - retiring allowance paid by private company to former employee - whether incurred in carrying on a business - whether a "retiring allowance".

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss.51(1); 78.(1)(c); & 109.

F.C.T. v Whitford's Beach Pty Ltd [1982] HCA 8; (1982) 150 C.L.R. 355;

Parke v Daily News Limited (1962).1 Ch. 927;

Union Trustee Co. of Australia Limited v STC [1935] HCA 51; 1935 53 CLR 263;

W. Nevill and Co. Limited v F.C.T. [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 C.L.R. 290;

REASONS FOR DECISION

15 January 1988 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. The Applicant in this reference (AUSCO) is an Australian subsidiary of a corporation based in the United States of America (NYCO). The NYCO group operates internationally and particularly deals in chemicals and raw materials. It trades in and around the Pacific Basin. Prior to 1979 AUSCO was essentially an exporter from Australia. Prior to that time it had many trade dealings with FICO, an Australian company controlled by the family of F. F was a man who in 1965 had established FICO to trade in raw materials. Up to 1979 it was principally concerned as an importer of raw materials from overseas. I accept that at all material times up to 30 June 1979 FICO was under the control of F and his family, and that F was "the directing mind and will of (FICO) and controlled what it did, and (his) state of mind was the state of mind of the taxpayer" (Gibbs, C.J. in F.C.T. v. Whitford's Beach Pty. Ltd. [1982] HCA 8; (1982) 150 C.L.R. 355 at 370).

2. During 1979 GM, the General Manager in Australia for AUSCO, heard that F was thinking of selling FICO. He learned of that possibility through an employee of FICO with whom AUSCO commonly dealt. GM thought that acquisition of FICO could usefully serve the interests of the NYCO group particularly as it would strengthen the Australian operations of AUSCO on the importing side. At the time F had been dealing with another prospective purchaser and, according to F, he had verbally agreed to sell. At that point he received a phone call from GM which led to discussions with a visiting Vice-President of NYCO and GM. That discussion in turn led to F calling on four of the principal officers of NYCO in New York. In the course of "about four minutes" he agreed that the NYCO group could buy all the shares in FICO.

  1. Although at that point of time nothing was formally documented, from that date forward F acted as if he were an officer of the NYCO group. He did business on behalf of the group before leaving the United States and only returned to Australia after visiting Japan where he attended a conference in which he met representatives of all the Far East offices of the NYCO group. Having returned to Australia he continued working as an officer of the group. Although for some time he worked from the old offices of FICO, he frequently visited the premises of AUSCO.
  2. In due course the formalities of the sale were completed. The sale agreement was not presented for evidence before me but there was produced in evidence an agreement made "as of 30 June 1979" between FICO and F providing for the employment of F by FICO for three years to 30 June 1982. By the time that agreement was executed FICO was already under the control of the NYCO group. Inter alia, the agreement provided for service by F for a term of three years at $A35,000 per annum "inclusive of payments by the company in respect of superannuation and any director's fees"; for obligations of confidentiality on the part of F; and for restrictive covenants for periods extending up to five years from cessation of employment under the agreement. In particular the agreement also provided by Clause 15 :-

"1) This Agreement and the benefit thereof to the Company may in the absolute discretion of the Company be assigned to any person or company.

  1. The Company shall have the further right to novate this agreement in favour of any person or company and whenever called upon by the Company so to do the Employee shall execute all documents (including a novation agreement) and do all things necessary to make such novation effective."

5. Following the expansion by NYCO of its Australian interests, it moved towards consolidating its old and new interests. By May 1980 the businesses formerly conducted from the separate premises of AUSCO and FICO were brought together in a single operation in new premises carried on by AUSCO. FICO was preserved as a dormant company, available for use should it be needed. On 1 July 1980, without there being any formal documentation to that effect, AUSCO implemented what was contemplated by Clause 15 (aforesaid) and F came on to the staff of AUSCO.

6. By mid-1982 F had decided to retire from the workforce and to take up residence on the North Coast of New South Wales. He took the view that by 30 June 1982 he would have fulfilled his contractual obligations to serve for the three years and to do so on a fixed salary. He considered that, having done that and having served for a further six months, he had fully performed his obligations to AUSCO. I find that during those three years being tied to a fixed salary had irked him to some extent; to the point where he thought of himself as being less well paid "than a footballer" - what code was not specified. Nonetheless, or rather perhaps because of that, he knew that his services were appreciated. On one occasion a visiting Vice-President had thanked him for his "150%" effort. The further evidence of F was that it was proposed that the appreciation should be marked by an increase in salary. It was not an increase he had sought while he had considered himself tied down by his contractual obligations, but his evidence was that "towards the end - for about the last year" his remuneration was increased from $A35,000 per annum to $A55,000 per annum. He acknowledged in his evidence that that was the one and only alteration in his rate of remuneration during this time with AUSCO and the NYCO group. In this respect, the evidence was unclear. First of all, F could not explain why his Return of Income to 30 June 1983 (not presented as evidence) recorded a salary income of only $12,499. GM could not explain that either. In addition, it was put to GM in cross-examination that F's services with AUSCO only commenced "1.7.81": as recorded in the Income Tax Return of AUSCO for year ended 30 June 1981. GM asserted that the date was wrong and that the reference was no more than a typographical error. I accept that it was so.

7. Although F was determined upon retirement, when GM was told of the proposal he was somewhat disbelieving. He did not believe that a man such as F would want to retire completely. GM tried "to talk (F) out of it" but he was unsuccessful. As the end of the 1982 calendar year approached, GM spoke with his superiors in New York and expressed the view that AUSCO should do something for F in the way of retirement benefits "both for past and present services" and because he hoped to keep on good terms with F. In his view F was not only an able operative, but he also carried substantial personal goodwill. GM proposed to his superiors that a payment of about "two years' salary" would be reasonable. He was authorised to act accordingly. In order to formalise the proposed action of AUSCO, GM conferred with his only fellow director in AUSCO, a Sydney solicitor. He told him of what had been proposed and that the proposal had the approval of New York. The response of the solicitor/director was that whatever New York wanted had his approval. GM proceeded to record that decision in the way of a Minute of a Meeting of Directors in mid-December 1982. The Minute read as follows -

" SPECIAL RESOLUTION : It was resolved that in recognition of the services rendered to (FICO Pty Ltd) and (AUSCO) Pty Ltd over the past 17 years by (F) that he be paid a retirement gratuity of $110,000 in addition to superannuation benefits and Long Service leave.

  1. F was farewelled socially at a Christmas function, but some days before that GB informally presented him with a retirement cheque effecting the payment to him of the proposed $110,000. In doing so GM expressed the hope that F would make his services available if AUSCO found itself in trouble and F gave his assurance that he would "only be too pleased" to assist.
  2. Having retired, F and his wife set about the building of their new home - and, from at least the date of its completion, left Sydney to enjoy the pleasures of retirement. However, GM's expectation of F's future was not entirely wrong. By the time of the hearing F was again rendering services to AUSCO as a consultant but on his terms: he was not to serve regularly or, under ordinary circumstances at least, to travel to Sydney to do so.
  3. In its Return of Income to 30 June 1983 AUSCO claimed the sum of $110,000 as a deduction, claiming it under the description "retiring allowances", a phrase used to describe a payment of $45,000 made in the previous year. By the same return, in "Schedule 26 : Remuneration of Directors and Shareholders and their Relatives", F was described as a Director of AUSCO - an office he had never occupied. It was also recorded there that, although he had not received Director's fees, he had been paid salary of $14,705; a retiring allowance of $110,000; and that $5,000 had been paid to a superannuation fund for him. The same table showed that GM as Managing Director had received $44,800 as salary but no Director's fees; and that $25,491 had been paid to a superannuation fund for him.

11. Upon his retirement from the service of AUSCO F, who had not been on the payroll of FICO since 30 June 1980, also received long service leave and holiday pay from FICO approximating $8,600, and $101,000 by way of superannuation benefits from a fund contributed to by FICO and later AUSCO.

  1. The Commissioner, by his representative, contended that ordinarily "retiring allowances" will only be deductible by force of s.78(1)(c) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"); and that whether or not the retiring allowance in question should be allowable as a deduction pursuant to that section depended upon the construction of the resolution of Directors authorising the payment. That resolution was seen as expressing precisely and wholly the reasons of AUSCO for making the payment. Counsel for the Applicant contends that the claim, and in particular the resolution, is not to be so narrowly considered and that in any event, to the extent to which the claim is not allowable pursuant to s.78(1)(c) of the Act, it is allowable pursuant to s.51(1) of the Act.
  2. S.78 (1) provides:

"The following shall be allowable deductions......

(c) Sums which are not allowable deductions and are paid by the taxpayer during the year of income as pensions, gratuities or retiring allowances to persons who are or have been employees or dependants of employers, to the extent to which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, those sums are paid in good faith in consideration of the past services of the employees in any business operations which were carried on by the taxpayer for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income........"

S.5(1) provides:

"All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

  1. I prefer to approach the problem by first considering the application by s.51(1) of the Act. One reason for doing so is to indicate that there is nothing in s.78(1)(c) which diminishes the operation of s.51 of the Act. Nor is there a presumption that "retiring allowances" are only allowable pursuant to s.78(1)(c) of the Act.
  2. AUSCO was a company engaged in an ongoing business. I find that its objective was profit; not benevolence. In the course of that business it obtained the services of F following on its purchase from F and his family of the business of FICO. It consolidated the business activities of FICO into its own operations. F played an important and influential role in that undertaking. When he proposed retirement, AUSCO was concerned to preserve his goodwill in a positive way. It sought to ensure that in the short term his services would be available in resolving any problems arising out of the work on hand when he departed. In the longer term AUSCO, through GM, expected that F would again become active in some business and wished to ensure that his endeavours would be in the interests of AUSCO. It had the benefit of restrictive covenants but GM hoped for a more positive relationship than would be obtained if AUSCO had to seek to rely on them. In my judgment it made a commercial assessment which was with a view to profit. It sought to preserve that ongoing goodwill and to ensure the availablity of future assistance from F by paying him a substantial sum of money - a sum sufficient to provide for any unfulfilled promises as to salary increases (cf. pa 6-ante). It represented the act as a gratuitous and generous one. It was gratuitous and it was, in at least large measure, generous. But the fact that it did not speak to F in terms of paying a price for future goodwill does not in my view alter the self-interested nature of the payment. Nor did the circumstance that the payment was made against a background which had involved the purchase of FICO and a term-contract for the services of F alter that fact. Nor did the fact that AUSCO was appreciative that it had acquired FICO and thereby successfully expanded its Australian operations. Nor did the fact that AUSCO was appreciative that F's action in building up FICO had made that possible. Nor did the circumstance that AUSCO had already paid F for his efforts prior to 1979 when it purchased the company.
  3. "A gift undirected to the interests of the shareholders (in a company) is invalid. This prevents the exercise of pure philanthropy which, by definition, is non-self-regarding. The law does not impose welfare responsibilities on trading companies and has not abandoned shareholder protection to the extent of sacrificing the interests of the minority to the liberal impulses of the majority. Gifts will accordingly be permitted only when they tend either directly or indirectly, to benefit the company in some genuine manner" (Parke v. Daily News Limited (1962) 1 Ch. 927 at 944).

In my view, the payment made by this company did not breach that standard. It was directed to the benefit of the company. It was not an act of philanthropy.

17. It is in no way to be likened to the circumstances in Union Trustee Co. of Australia Limited v. F.C.T. [1935] HCA 51; (1935) 53 C.L.R. 263. In that case an employee who, for some 32 years, had been business manager, secretary and accountant to an individual was retired by reason of old age and ill health when, at the age of 71, he had become incapacitated. Claims were made that the amounts so paid were deductible either pursuant to s.23(1)(a) of the 1922 Act, which allowed for the deductibility of "losses and outgoings actually incurred in gaining or producing assessable income"; or as allowable under paragraph (j) of the same section which provided that "so much of the assessable income as is set aside or paid by an employer of labour as or to a fund to provide individual personal benefits, pensions or retiring allowances for employees:....". The High Court held that both claims failed. As to the former claim, that was because, to quote Dixon J. (at page 271), -

"There is nothing to show that it was not a purely voluntary grant to Lecky made by Black out of proper feelings of gratitude for long service of a confidential nature."

There was no prospect of future benefit to the business of Lecky or of obviating detriment. As to the latter claim, it failed because there was no payment to a "fund".

18. The High Court faced the problem of payments with a view to obviating detriment in W. Nevill & Co. Limited v. F.C.T. [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 C.L.R. 290. The Court held, again pursuant to the 1922 Act, that a payment made by a company in order to secure the resignation of an officer from a term contract with a view to improving the performance of the business, was deductible.

  1. In my view there was no distinction in principle between a payment made by an organization to be peaceably rid of an unsatisfactory employee in order to resolve an unsatisfactory situation and a situation in which a company voluntarily pays a sum to an influential employee retiring of his own volition when that employee has a capacity, principally exercisable through his personal goodwill and contacts, to do damage to the business of his employer. That will particularly be so in the case of a person who may yet be persuaded to promote the interests of the employer by rendering future service. In my view, in the circumstances of this case, the deduction is wholly allowable pursuant to s.51(1) of the Act. None of the complications arising out of circumstances whereby the recipient controls the actions of the paying-entity arise (cf. s.109 and the decision this day in NT87/3043).
  2. However, lest I be incorrect to some extent as to that, I proceed to other questions. In my view, to the extent to which the payment was not directed towards securing future advantages for AUSCO, it was sufficiently directed to gratuitously expressing appreciation for the past services of F to AUSCO. I am satisfied that to some extent AUSCO was rewarding F "in consideration of" his services as an employee of and in the interests of AUSCO.

21. It is not appropriate for the purpose of s.78(1)(c) in these circumstances to construe and assess the Directors' resolution as if it had been drafted at least as precisely and skilfully as s.78(1)(c) itself. But even if that were appropriate I find that - as the Minute acknowledged - F had rendered substantial, valuable and much appreciated services to AUSCO. I find that, in at least substantial measure, the payment was "in consideration" of those services. In all the circumstances I do not consider it necessary or even desirable to consider to what extent, if any, there should be an "apportionment" distinguishing what is allowable under one section or under the other.

22. The order of the Tribunal will be that the objection decision be varied and that the taxable income of AUSCO for the year ended 30 June 1983 be reduced by $110,000.
