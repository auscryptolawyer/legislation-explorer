VT87/3976 and Commissioner of Taxation [1990] AATA 189; (1990) 21 ATR 3610 (17 August 1990)

Last Updated: 2 June 2009



Income tax - assessable income - applicant a finance company - applicant subscribed for shares in 1973 in order to provide company with capital base for borrowing - company to be wound up after 11 years and assets distributed to shareholders - applicant intended to derive profit from dividends, interest on loans to be made to company and on winding-up - shares never part of applicant's trading stock - no intention to sell shares - company not wound up because unable to liquidate assets - company taken over - price paid for shares value of assets - whether difference between sale price and purchase price of shares assessable income - not assessable income under section 25 or first limb of section 25A(1) - whether assessable income under second limb of section 25A(1) - Steinberg applied - Kratzmann distinguished - detail of profit-making scheme changed but achievement of object of scheme not affected - Whitfords Beach applied - profit assessable income under second limb of section 25A(1) - objection decision affirmed



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss. 25(1), 25A(1)



Kratzmann v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 70 ATC 4043

Steinberg v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 75 ATC 4221

The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v Whitfords Beach Proprietary Limited [1982] HCA 8; (1982) 150 CLR 355



AAT No 6140



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. VT87/3976

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:

Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal : Deputy President I. R. Thompson



Date : 17 August 1990



Place : Melbourne



Decision : The objection decision under review is affirmed.



(Sgd I. R. Thompson)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. VT87/3976

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



REASONS FOR DECISION



17 August 1990 Deputy President I. R. Thompson



1. The applicant in these proceedings is a company which is a subsidiary of another company, X Co.; X Co. is a merchant bank. In 1973 the applicant subscribed for a number of shares in a company, Y Co., to which it was also to lend a substantial sum of money. In 1976 it bought a number more shares from another company which had subscribed for them in 1973 in the same circumstances as the applicant. The total amount paid by the applicant for all the shares was $7,692. In March 1985 the applicant sold all the shares in Y Co. to an unrelated company for $488,865, that is to say for $481,173 more than it paid for them. When the respondent assessed the applicant's tax liability for the 1985 year of income, he treated that amount as assessable income. The applicant objected to the assessment on the ground that it was not assessable income. The respondent disallowed the objection and then at the applicant's request referred the matter to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal pursuant to section 189 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act").



2. At the hearing of the deemed application to the Tribunal the applicant was represented by Mr P. Searle, of Counsel, and the respondent by Mr E. Power, of Counsel. Numerous documents were tendered in evidence, including copies of the applicant's income tax returns for the taxation years from 1980 to 1985, a copy of the memorandum and articles of the applicant, copies of minutes addressed to directors of X Co. and to directors of the applicant, a file note made by Mr S, who was Secretary of the applicant, and copies of letters written to the applicant by a company with which the company which bought the applicant's shares in Y Co. was associated. Oral evidence was given by Mr S.



3. From the documentary evidence and the evidence of Mr S I find the following facts. The applicant was incorporated in 1962. In the Memorandum of Association its objects were stated as including the carrying-on of business as bankers, capitalists, financiers, concessionaires and merchants and undertaking all kinds of financial commercial trading. During the 1960s the applicant acquired shares in various companies and was holding those shares in 1972. It was a subsidiary of a company, Z Co., which was one of three companies which together incorporated X Co. in 1972. Upon X Co.'s incorporation Mr S was appointed its Secretary/Controller. The applicant became a subsidiary of X Co. and Mr S became its Secretary. X Co. decided to utilize the applicant company as a vehicle for its medium- to long-term investments.



4. In 1972 a proposal was put to X Co. that Y Co. be incorporated for the purpose of purchasing from another company, A Co., certain existing income-producing properties. The necessary finance was to be raised, as to 30% by the issue of 10,000 $1 shares to six proposed shareholders, of whom X Co. was one, and by way of subordinated loans totalling approximately $900,000 made by those shareholders and, as to 70%, by the issue of debenture stock to the public. The directors of X Co. decided in principle to accept the proposal and to participate in incorporating and financing Y Co. However, they decided that the applicant should become a shareholder and make the subordinated loan instead of X Co. itself doing so. The proposal for the incorporation of Y Co. and its financing included provision that at the end of 11 years Y Co. would be liquidated and the investment redeemed with, inter alia, on realization an anticipated 30% profit on the moneys outlaid originally, that profit resulting from appreciation of Y Co.'s properties. In respect of their shareholdings, the owners were to receive 70% of Y Co.'s income surplus. In respect of the subordinated loans they were to be paid interest at a fixed rate and in addition a percentage of the net surplus income.



5. The properties which it was decided that Y Co. should purchase from A Co. were three shopping centres. The total purchase price was $2,871,000. The amounts of the subordinated loans and of the debenture stock issue were calculated so as to provide, together with the $10,000 subscribed for shares, the amount of $2,871,000. It was agreed that A Co. should subscribe for 35% of the share capital and make a subordinated loan of 35% of the total amount required to be raised by the subordinated loans. The applicant and the other four companies were each to subscribe for 13% of the share capital and make a subordinated loan of the amount of 13% of the total amount of the subordinated loans required. It was a term of the agreement that a shareholder might sell all or part of its "participation" but only with the agreement of the other shareholders. On 27 April 1973 the applicant subscribed for 1,300 shares at a cost of $1,300 and made its subordinated loan in accordance with the agreement. The other five shareholders subscribed for shares and made their subordinated loans as had been agreed.



6. Because of the restriction on the sale of the shares in Y Co. Mr S caused a red label to be attached to the share certificate to distinguish those shares from other shares which the applicant was already holding and which could be freely traded. The shares had been acquired by the applicant and the other shareholders for the purpose of providing a capital base for Y Co., so that it could receive the subordinated loans and issue a prospectus in respect of the debenture stock. Mr S and the directors of the applicant, who together constituted the mind of the applicant, anticipated that at the end of 11 years Y Co. would be wound up and the amount of the value of its assets distributed to its shareholders. They expected that upon that distribution the applicant would receive approximately 30% more than it had originally outlaid in respect of the shares and the subordinated loan. They intended not to sell the shares but to retain them and to continue to maintain the subordinated loan for the whole of the anticipated life of Y Co. They expected the applicant to receive from Y Co. income by way of dividends paid to it as shareholder and, in respect of the subordinated loans, by way of both interest and a share of Y Co.'s net income.



7. In 1976 one of the other shareholders who had subscribed for 13% of the share capital and made a subordinated loan of 13% of the total amount of the subordinated loans wished to sell its "participation". Apparently there was a possibility that A Co. might be willing to purchase the whole of it; if it had done so, it would have increased its shareholding to 48% of Y Co.'s share capital, a situation which the applicant and some of the other shareholders wished to prevent. Two of the shareholders did not wish to buy any additional shares but A Co. and three others including the applicant decided to do so. As a result on 6 October 1976 the applicant bought 277 of the shares at a cost of $6,392, being their value as ascertained at that time. Those shares were then held by the applicant together with the original 1,300 shares. As in the case of the 1,300 shares for which the applicant originally subscribed, Mr S and the directors of the applicant intended not to sell them but to hold them until Y Co. was liquidated at the end of the 11 years.



8. However, in 1983 Y Co. was having a problem with the preparations for its liquidation. It was finding it difficult to sell one of the three shopping centres. That is recorded in a file note dated 12 December 1983 written by Mr S following a meeting of Y Co. on 7 December 1983. The other two shopping centres had already been sold but all three had to be sold before Y Co. could be wound up and its assets distributed to the shareholders. A proposal had been put to Y Co. that the company be sold to persons who wished to acquire a listed company for use as a vehicle for an entirely different type of business. At the meeting of Y. Co. Mr S opposed its sale to those persons; he still wished all the properties to be sold and Y Co. wound up. However, the third shopping centre was not sold and Y Co. could not be wound up in 1984 as had been anticipated when it was incorporated. By then the companies which had owned A Co. had split up. One of them had formed a new group of companies. It made an offer in March 1985 to all five remaining shareholders of Y Co. that one of the companies in the group should purchase all the shares in Y Co., which would continue to carry on business and to own the one remaining shopping centre. The net value of the assets of Y Co. was calculated as being $3,109,973; that was the amount offered for the shares. Four days after the original offer an amended offer was made which set the value of Y Co.'s capital as $3,099,973. That offer was accepted by all five shareholders of Y Co., including the applicant. The applicant's 1,577 shares were valued at $488,866 and that amount was paid to it for them.



9. From 1977 onwards dividends had been paid by Y Co. to its shareholders. Initially the rate was $1.50 per share. By 1984 it had increased to $8 per share. In 1985 the dividend was $56.3875 per share. Mr S gave evidence, which I accept, that that was the distribution of the balance of the accumulated income of Y Co. The offer which was made for the purchase of the shares and which was accepted by the shareholders included an amount in respect of distribution of further accumulated profits, the amount being approximately $3.50 per share. That amount was paid to the shareholders separately from the amount paid for purchase of the shares, which therefore reflected entirely the value of the remaining shopping centre and the proceeds of the sale of the other two.



10. The applicant's returns of income tax for the years before 1980 are no longer available. However, Schedule 1 of the return for the 1980 year of income included the following statement:-

"In past years the company's practice has been to describe as 'Investments' in the Balance Sheet shares acquired as trading stock and also shares in a non-quoted company not held as trading stock. To facilitate the recording of long term investments not acquired for trading purposes, share trading stock is now classified as 'Securities' (see note 7). The classification of 'Investments' (see note 8) is now restricted to special holdings of shares which do not form part of the share trading portfolio."



After Mr S had given his evidence, Mr Searle conceded on behalf of the applicant that in 1980 and in all the subsequent years up to 1985 the shares which it held in Y Co. were included under the heading "Securities" in its balance sheet. Mr S had been unable to say whether that was so or not but had said that, if it was, it had been a mistake as the shares had never been treated by the applicant as trading stock. That was, he said, because they had been acquired only as part of the process of providing a capital base for Y Co. to obtain the subordinated loan from the applicant and the other shareholders and to issue a prospectus in respect of its debenture stock. Mr S said that until late 1983 or early 1984 the applicant had anticipated that Y Co. would be wound up and its assets distributed to the shareholders at the end of 11 years, and that the applicant had intended to hold the shares until then. He referred also to the restriction on the sale of the shares which was imposed by the agreement which the applicant entered into when Y Co. was incorporated.



11. I accept Mr S's evidence that he and the directors of the applicant did not regard the shares in Y Co. in the same way as they regarded the shares which the applicant held in other companies, which over the years it retained or sold as was thought most conducive to obtaining the best financial results. The documentary evidence before the Tribunal establishes that, until 1985, when it was decided to change the previous policy, the applicant generally held shares for long periods and traded in them very little. Furthermore, the documents establish that the holding of shares was a minor part of the applicant's business, most of which was the making of loans.



12. Mr Power submitted that the difference between the amount received by the applicant for the shares in Y Co. in 1985 and the amount which it paid for them in 1973 and 1976 was assessable income either under section 25(1) of the Act or under section 25A(1). In 1985 those two sub-sections read as follows:-

"25. (1) The assessable income of a taxpayer shall include -



(a) where the taxpayer is a resident -



the gross income derived directly or indirectly from all sources whether in or out of Australia; and



(b) where the taxpayer is a non-resident -



the gross income derived directly or indirectly from all sources in Australia,



which is not exempt income, an amount to which section 26AC or 26AD applies or an eligible termination payment within the meaning of Subdivision AA.



25A. (1) The assessable income of a taxpayer shall include profit arising from the sale by the taxpayer of any property acquired by him for the purpose of profit-making by sale, or from the carrying on or carrying out of any profit-making undertaking or scheme."



13. Mr Power submitted that the sale of the shares was part of the applicant's general business as part of a group of finance companies owned or associated with X Co., so that the profit derived thereby was assessable income under section 25(1). He referred to cases in which the income of a subsidiary had been held to be the income of the parent company. But in these proceedings the taxpayer is not X Co. but its subsidiary, the applicant. It is the characterization of the profit in the hands of the applicant which will determine whether or not it is assessable income of the applicant for the purpose of assessment of the applicant's tax liability.



14. Having made the findings of fact which I have set out above, I am satisfied that the difference between the purchase price of the applicant's shares in Y Co. and the price at which they were sold was not assessable income under section 25(1) or the first limb of section 25A(1) of the Act. The shares were not acquired by the applicant as trading stock and, in spite of their inclusion among "Securities" in its income tax returns, they did not become part of its trading stock. Receipt by the applicant of a larger amount for the shares than it paid for them was not the result of trading in the shares. Furthermore, the shares were not acquired by the applicant for the purpose of profit-making by sale. The question which poses more difficulty is whether the profit was assessable income under the second limb of section 25A(1).



15. On the basis of the findings of fact which I have made I am satisfied that the applicant acquired the shares as part of a scheme for making a subordinated loan to Y Co. with a view to making a profit thereby; their acquisition cannot be viewed in isolation from that scheme. Is the applicant, therefore, to be held to have carried on a profit-making scheme, with the profit from the sale of the shares being held to have arisen from the carrying-out of that scheme?



16. In Kratzmann v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 70 ATC 4043 the taxpayer had purchased land in order to carry out a profit-making scheme of developing it. However, for financial reasons he abandoned that scheme and sold the land in the condition in which he had bought it. In the High Court of Australia Menzies J. held that the profit which the taxpayer had made on the sale of the land was not assessable income as it arose from the sale, and not the purchase, of the land and the sale was not part of the profit-making scheme. Consequently, the profit did not arise from the carrying-on or carrying-out of the scheme.



17. However, in Steinberg v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 75 ATC 4221 at pages 4234-4235 Gibbs J. said:-

"On the findings of Mason J. it must be concluded that it was part of Morris Steinberg's plan that the Rockingham land should appreciate in value yielding a profit for himself or his family, although how or when he may not have foreseen. I am in agreement with the view expressed by Mason J. that 'it is not an essential element of a profit-making scheme in sec. 26(a) that every step which culminates in the making of a profit should be planned or foreseen before the scheme is put into operation'. Schemes may be precise or vague; every detail may be arranged in advance, or the working out of the plan may be left for decision in the light of circumstances as they arise. It is no objection to a plan that it allows room for manoeuvre. When property is bought with the purpose of making a profit in the easiest or most advantageous way that may present itself, and the taxpayer adopts 'one of the many alternatives' that his plan leaves open, thereby returning himself a profit, he will rightly be said to be carrying out a profit-making scheme; cf. The Premier Automatic Ticket Issuers Ltd. v. F.C. of T. [1933] HCA 51; (1933) 50 C.L.R. 268, at p. 300; Buckland v. F.C. of T. (1960) 34 A.L.J.R. 60, at p. 62."



18. At page 4242 of Steinberg Stephen J. said:-

". . . I should state my understanding of certain characteristics of the concept of a scheme for the purposes of sec. 26(a).



Once it is determined that a scheme exists the effect of supervening events which cause a departure from the original scheme will vary depending upon the view taken of those events. If they be seen as involving an abandonment of the scheme as a whole, as in Kratzmann v. F.C. of T. 70 ATC 4043; (1970) 44 A.L.J.R. 293 at p. 294, without the substitution of some new scheme - Eisner v. F.C. of T. 71 ATC 4022; (1971) 45 A.L.J.R. 110 - profits made subsequent to its abandonment will not be assessable under sec. 26(a). If, instead, a scheme in an amended form is thereafter carried out which can be seen to be a different scheme from the original scheme the date of inception of the new scheme will be the starting point for the calculation of profit, so that if the acquisition and resale of land is in question the value of the land at the date of inception of the new scheme will be the relevant starting figure. Again not every change in the planned details will involve the adoption of a new scheme, this will always be a question of fact and will be much affected by the degree to which the original scheme was precise in its details. An imprecise scheme, the details of which have not been worked out in advance, will more readily take in its stride unexpected events without becoming a different scheme."



19. In that same case Barwick C.J. laid emphasis on the date at which it was necessary that the scheme should have existed. In the present case that would need to have been on each of the two dates on which the shares in Y Co. were acquired by the applicant. On the first of those dates the scheme undoubtedly existed and it included, in addition to the making of a profit by way of income to be derived from dividends and from making the subordinated loan, the making of a profit on liquidation of approximately 30% of the moneys outlaid. At the date in 1976 when the remaining shares were purchased the scheme remained in existence, although it is not clear what the expectations of the applicant were with regard to further appreciation occurring before the liquidation of Y Co.; however, the applicant has not established that it did not expect their value to rise further. Small though the profit which the applicant expected to obtain from the distribution of the assets of Y Co. on its liquidation might have been compared with the income which it anticipated it would derive during the 11 years of Y Co.'s existence, the applicant intended to make that profit; it was profit which the profit-making scheme, not simply the acquisition of the shares, was intended to produce.



20. The decision by Y Co.'s shareholders to sell the shares rather than have the company liquidated and its assets distributed as had been intended undoubtedly resulted in the scheme being changed. However, in my view, it was, in terms of the passage from the judgment of Stephens J. in Steinberg, a "change in the planned details" which did not "involve the adoption of a new scheme". The scheme was intended to result in the applicant receiving its pro rata share of the increased value of Y Co.'s assets upon its liquidation. Because Y Co. was sold by all the shareholders to a buyer who paid them for their shares the value of Y Co.'s net assets, the result which was achieved was effectively the same as that intended originally. I am satisfied, therefore, that the original scheme was not abandoned, and that the change to the planned details of it did not involve the adoption of a new scheme so as to make the starting point for the calculation of the profit a date later than that on which the scheme was originally entered.



21. Mr Searle drew to the Tribunal's attention the fact that the agreement which the applicant and the other shareholders other than A Co. had entered into with the purchaser of their shares provided for them to indemnify the purchaser against any liability to pay income tax on the increase in the value of Y Co.'s assets which it might be found to have. He submitted that, if Y Co. was held to be liable to pay any such tax, it would not be just for the applicant to have, in effect, to pay the tax a second time. However, he did not present any evidence tending to show that Y Co. had any such liability. It is the applicant's liability to taxation with which these proceedings are concerned and that must be decided by applying the law to the facts as found.



22. In The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v Whitfords Beach Proprietary Limited [1982] HCA 8; (1982) 150 CLR 355 Gibbs J. at page 367 and Mason J. at pages 382-383 held that profit to which the second limb of section 26(a) (since repealed and replaced by section 25A(1) in similar terms) applied included profit which would not otherwise have fallen within section 25, because it could not be described as profit in the ordinary sense. I have come to the conclusion, therefore, that the amount of $481,173, being the difference between the price at which the applicant purchased the shares in Y Co. and the amount which it received when it sold them was assessable income under the second limb of section 25A(1) and that accordingly the objection decision under review must be affirmed.
