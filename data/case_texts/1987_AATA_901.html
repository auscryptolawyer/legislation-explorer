QT85/1820 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 901; 87 ATC 186 (2 January 1987)

QT85/1820 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 901 (2 January 1987); 87 ATC 186

Last Updated: 8 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax : Allowable Deductions – whether carrying on business of primary production - fruit trees grown on trial basis to determine most suitable varieties - capital expenses.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 – ss 51, 54

Softwood Pulp and Paper Ltd v FC of T 76 ATC 4439

DECISION AND REASONS

Re: QT85/1820

APPLICANT

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

RESPONDENT

NO: QT85/1820

AAT Decision No: 3146

Date: 2 January 1987

Place: Brisbane

Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) QT85/1820

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re: QT85/1820

APPLICANT

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

RESPONDENT

REASONS FOR DECISION

The question at issue in this application is whether certain operations carried on by the applicant in respect of fruit trees and nut trees can be said to be the carrying on of the business and whether losses and outgoings incurred in those operations are allowable deductions.

  1. The relevant sections of the Income Tax Assessment Act are ss 51 and 54.
  2. The applicant taxpayer is a mechanical engineer employed by a public electrical utility. In the year ended 30 June 1984, which is the year before the Tribunal, the applicant's only declared source of income was his salary paid by the electrical utility and an amount of $3 in the nature of interest paid to the applicant by the Australian Taxation Office.
  3. The applicant and his wife reside on a property approximately 40 kilometres from a major city. The property is jointly owned by the applicant and his wife and has an area of 3.885 hectares. It is zoned rural residential A and under this zoning can apparently only be subdivided into allotments with a minimum area of two hectares. Prima facie therefor the subject land cannot be subdivided. Access to the property is by a bitumen and gravel road and electricity and telephone are installed. The land is undulating and was originally timber country which has been cleared except for a few shade trees. There is no natural water on the property but it does have two dams suitable for use for irrigation and water pumping equipment has been installed on one of those dams. This equipment is connected to underground piping which supplies both a sprinkler irrigation system and tap water to a section only of the property when appropriate.
  4. The relevant sections of the property which appear to cover less than one acre were planted out by the applicant during the year of income with 80 fruit trees. I will return to the fruit trees shortly. The property also contains a substantial dwelling which is used as a residence by the applicant and his wife, a shed, a fowl run and a calf shelter. Beehives are also to be found on the property and I understand the property to be currently carrying four cattle, some geese, a number of hens, two roosters and three ducks. Also planted on the property but away from the 80 trees which I mentioned earlier, are about a dozen fruit trees apparently grown for domestic use.
  5. The applicant and his wife purchased the property in 1978 and for a number of years up until the year of income had been clearing the land, installing the irrigation system, erecting and extending the dwelling and constructing boundary fences.
    1. Apparently the applicant consulted government authorities as to the suitability of the land for commercial production of a crop. The net result of that consultation was advice to the effect that lychees or possibly persimmons would give the best results in that particular area, bearing in mind that the government authority anticipated above average returns in respect of these particular fruits.
    2. It seems the applicant taxpayer did not wish to immediately adopt the advice of the government authorities and he proceeded to plant 80 assorted fruit and nut trees. These included four lychee trees and two persimmon trees but apparently the remainder were either entirely different to each other or at least different varieties of a particular fruit, eg the applicant planted a number of plum trees of different varieties.
  6. The applicant's planting was fairly successful notwithstanding adverse drought conditions which occurred shortly after the planting, because he only lost about 20-25% of the trees planted during August/September 1983. Those trees that were lost have been replaced although the applicant did not replace the paw paw trees after they died due to wind buffeting.
  7. In the early stages the applicant made enquiries as to the possibility of selling the produce from his plantation and ascertained that fruit and nuts could be sold through agents at the nearby markets and that at least some of the fruit could be sold to restaurants and/or roadside stalls. His queries extended to possible financial return on these sales but of course really served no purpose other than to establish that markets existed, because the trees were then at least five or six years away from production.
  8. An issue also arose before the Tribunal in relation to the quantum of the expenses claimed as outgoings in the applicant's return. The applicant was unable to prove that some of these amounts claimed had in fact been spent. The applicant's accountants seem to have resorted to estimates in relation to at least some of the outgoings claimed.
  9. It is clear enough that the applicant intends to set up an orchard which will be run as a business. He has expended considerable amounts of money bringing his property into a state where such an orchard has a reasonable prospect of success. The extraordinary factor in this case is the nature of the plantation as it presently exists on the property. There is not sufficient of any species to provide commercial quantities whether it be lychees or the various types of plums, orange trees etc. Commercial quantities are unlikely to be obtained from one or two trees of a particular variety although it might be possible to sell small quantities of the exotic fruits to roadside stalls or restaurants.
  10. However, it became clear during the course of the hearing that the applicant was in fact engaged upon a feasibility study designed to ascertain those fruit and nut trees which would be the best producers in that locality and would provide the applicant with an assured income after he retires from his employment. There was no evidence before the Tribunal as to when the applicant intended to plant the orchard or to work full time on the venture but I can infer from the evidence before the Tribunal that the contemplated full time occupation is some years into the future.
  11. The activities of the applicant displayed his intention to carry on an income producing activity in a businesslike manner and towards that end he was, during the year of income, conducting a feasibility study on the property with that end in mind.
  12. I am satisfied that this is a case which comes within the terms of the decision in the Supreme Court of Victoria in Softwood Pump and Paper Ltd v FC of T 76 ATC 4439. The Supreme Court held that feasibility and other primary expenses incurred in connection with a proposed paper mill fell outside both positive limbs of subsection 51(1) and were of a capital nature.
  13. I think the expenses incurred by the applicant in this case in relation to his 80 tree orchard also fall outside both limbs of subsection 51(1) and were expenses incurred in obtaining an enduring benefit ie the most suitable fruit and nut trees for that particular site. The expenses relate to the making of a decision (yet to be made) whether or not to establish an orchard and the nature of that orchard. It follows that the applicant also fails to meet the test in sub-section 54(1).

17. The respondent's decision on the objection will be affirmed.

This and the preceding six (6) pages are a true and correct copy of the Decision and Reasons of Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member) in the matter of QT85/1820.

Deputy Registrar
