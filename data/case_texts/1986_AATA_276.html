Re Cheryl May Benham and Commissioner of Superannuation [1986] AATA 276 (17 September 1986)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: CHERYL MAY BENHAM

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. S86/27

AAT No. 2886

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

E. Smith (Senior Member)

Dr D.B. Travers (Member)

N.J. Attwood (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - retirement on ground of invalidity - employee suffering from macular dystrophy when became eligible employee - specification of that condition in benefit classification certificate - aggravation of cervical spondylosis by posture adopted by employee to aid reading - retirement due to neck pain arising from that aggravation - whether condition of cervical spondylosis connected with macular dystrophy

Words and phrases: "connected with"

Superannuation Act 1976, ss. 16(4), 66(2)

Commissioner for Superannuation v. Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 63 ALR 237

Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 3 ALN No. 1

HEARING

CANBERRA

17:9:1986

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in substitution therefor decides that the applicant is entitled to invalidity benefits in accordance with sub-section 66(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

DECISION

The applicant has sought review by this Tribunal of the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation, made through his delegate, under s.154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Superannuation Act") confirming the decision by a delegate of the Commissioner made on 19 December 1983 that the applicant's entitlement to invalidity benefit was in accordance with s.66(2) of that Act.

2. The Tribunal heard evidence by the applicant and had before it the documents lodged in pursuance of s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 as Exhibits T.1 to T.30. It also received in evidence documents provided by the respondent relating to a Freedom of Information Act application and a compensation claim by the applicant and correspondence in relation to the applicant's requests for visual aids.

3. The applicant joined the Australian Public Service in a permanent capacity on 24 April 1980 under the Special Placements Scheme and was allotted to a position in the Department of Veterans Affairs. On 6 February 1981 a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation, pursuant to s.16(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976, issued in respect of the applicant a benefit classification certificate ("BCC") in which were specified the conditions of macular dystrophy, lumbar strain and anxiety state. Only the first-mentioned is relevant for present purposes. On 17 November 1983, the Permanent Head (now Secretary) of the Department of Veterans Affairs, pursuant to s.19 of the Commonwealth Employees (Redeployment and Retirement) Act 1979, retired the applicant from the Public Service with effect from 20 July 1983. A certificate had been issued under s.14 of that Act that she should be retired, because she was in consequence of physical or mental incapacity incapable of performing her duties. On 19 December 1983 a delegate of the Commissioner decided that the applicant's entitlement to invalidity benefit was under s.66(2). The applicant's response was to seek reconsideration of that decision. On reconsideration, the Commissioner's delegate confirmed the decision made on 19 December 1983 on the grounds that the adoption of an abnormal posture in her reading was the link connecting the relevant conditions and such a link was not itself required to be a medical condition but only a medically sustainable connection.

4. At the hearing, the applicant's counsel (Mr Walmsley) indicated no medical witnesses would be called because the facts had been agreed with the respondent. The following were stated as agreed:

(1) Because the applicant had been provided with

no reading aids and because of her macular

dystrophy, she had adopted an abnormal posture

to read in her employment.

(2) Because she adopted such a posture, she

aggravated a pre-existing condition of the

cervical spine which would not otherwise have

been aggravated to the extent of requiring

invalidity retirement.

The respondent did not, however, agree that while employed by the Department of Veterans Affairs, the applicant asked for and was not given reading aids. The agreed facts as stated in paragraph (2) above are supported, to varying degrees, by the medical evidence in the form of reports from Dr Alistair Robson, Dr Raymond L.G. Newcombe, Dr Nadana Chandran and Dr P. Vodicka (Commonwealth Medical Officer).

5. The applicant gave evidence as to her University career at Macquarie University before entering the Public Service. She said she had commenced a Bachelor of Arts course there in 1970 and, having taken a year off in 1972, returned in 1973 to complete that degree. In 1975 she had done the Honours part of that course and had started a Ph.D. course at Adelaide University, which she ceased after 2 years for reasons unconnected with her eyesight. She had married in late 1976 and had done some casual nursing until 1980 and had come to Canberra in February of that year because her husband had taken a position there.

6. She had been aware of her visual problem from primary school years and it seems to have been recognised that her vision would become worse in time. It is therefore clear enough that if her retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by her macular dystrophy then s.66(2)(c) would apply to her.

7. The applicant commenced her Public Service career as a Clerk Class 1 but was progressively promoted to Clerk Class 4. She found the work she was given, which included reading faint documents and small type, caused her difficulties, particularly because of the inadequate lighting that was available in the offices of the Department of Veterans Affairs. She had referred this difficulty, about the middle of 1980, to the Special Placements Officer - an officer of the Public Service Board who was appointed for the purpose of dealing with problems of persons under the Special Placements Scheme - but she said his response was "not helpful". She said that her own supervisor was at first most helpful but became less so as she progressed in her career. She had approached the Department of Social Security also about mid-1980 for a specific reading aid, but found that she was not eligible for one under the Social Security legislation. What she had sought was a closed circuit television which displayed an enlargement of the reading matter on a vertical screen and thus would avoid the need to bend over to get close to what she was reading. In December 1980 she had approached the Special Placements Officer again, without success, and also approached her supervisor who had made no change. She had managed to have two light fittings removed from the lighting panel above her desk to reduce the glare some months later. In early 1981 she had again spoken with another of her seniors and discussed the possibility of getting a closed circuit television but he had discouraged that because, he said, it would make her look different to others in the office. In February 1982 she had again contacted the Special Placements Officer, who was a different person to the officer she had seen before, and found the person very helpful. In consequence various meetings and assessments were planned and conducted during the early part of 1982. She had had her vision tested by the Royal Blind Society in Sydney in June 1982 and found a closed circuit television reading aid that suited her needs. However, an officer from the Training Section of her Department told her that they could take no action until a report came from the Royal Blind Society, which was some time in being sent to the Department.

8. She said that, after she had begun to do a lot of reading in her employment, she had found it to be embarrassing, tiring and uncomfortable, but not painful. She illustrated before the Tribunal the posture she adopted to read; it showed approximately 10 centimetres distance between the page and the eye. But she said of the pain in her neck on the right side -

"Well, it increased very slowly. I do not know - it

tended to come on during the day and then go away

at night. There was a gradual continual period.

It started earlier and earlier in the day as time

went on, and finally became constant."

In November 1982 she saw her general practitioner who gave her a certificate. She did not return to the Department after that. She said that after ceasing work, she found "life was a little easier, but my neck still hurt a lot".

9. She said in cross-examination that she did not have much difficulty with reading while at the University. She did most of her reading in bed which allowed for much more flexibility. The subjects she did were such that not a great deal of reading was required and what was required was able to be done in a much more comfortable posture. During this time she did not have any posture-related problems.

10. On the basis of the uncontradicted evidence of the applicant, we find that the applicant did seek visual aids during 1980 to 1982 and was not provided with any suitable aid. We accept also the agreed facts set out in paragraph 4 above.

11. The issue in the present case is whether the terms of s.66(2)(c) of the Superannuation Act apply to the applicant on the facts as we have found them or have accepted them. Section 66(2) of the Act, so far as relevant for present purposes, reads as follows:

66. (2) Subject to sub-sections (3), (3A) and (4)

of this section and section 79, where -

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible

employee by reason of retirement on the

ground of invalidity before attaining his

maximum retiring age;

(b) there was in force in respect of the

person, immediately before the person's

retirement, a benefit classification

certificate; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that

the incapacity which was the ground for

his retirement was caused, or was

substantially contributed to, by a

physical or mental condition or

conditions specified in the certificate

or by a physical or mental condition or

conditions connected with such a

condition or such conditions,

the person is entitled -

...........

Section 16(4) is also relevant in the construction of s.66(2); it reads as follows:

16. (4) The Commissioner shall consider the

report or reports, and such other matters (if any)

as the Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he

is of the opinion that the person is not likely, by

reason of or for a reason connected with a physical

or mental condition or conditions referred to in

the report or reports, to continue to be an

eligible employee until the person attains his

maximum retiring age, the Commissioner shall issue

a benefit classification certificate to that

effect, being a certificate in which the relevant

condition or conditions is or are specified.

12. The Commissioner's delegate in his reconsideration conceded that there was no substantial contribution to the incapacity that resulted in the applicant's retirement by the condition specified in the BCC, namely, macular dystrophy , nor was her incapacity caused by that condition. Although this was not accepted by Mr Anforth (for the respondent), we are of the opinion that the delegate's concession was proper and inevitable. The delegate then went on to consider whether the incapacity that was the ground for retirement, namely, the neck pain, was caused, or substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition connected with a physical or mental condition specified in the BCC: in other words, whether the physical condition cervical spondylosis which caused her neck pain was connected with the physical condition macular dystrophy. He concluded that the cervical spondylosis resulted from the abnormal posture adopted when reading at work and that the abnormal posture was adopted because she suffered from macular dystrophy and would not otherwise have been able to read adequately. The delegate conceded that had she worked under different conditions she may not have had to adopt a posture so abnormal as to disrupt the operation of the cervical discs sufficiently to cause incapacitating symptoms of neck pain. But he took the view that the adoption of the abnormal posture was the link connecting the relevant (medical) conditions and was not itself required to answer the description of a medical condition.

13. For the applicant, it was argued that the adoption of an abnormal reading position in the course of her employment was not a physical or mental condition within the meaning of that expression in the latter part of s.66(2)(c) and there was accordingly no physical or mental condition connected with macular dystrophy which caused or substantially contributed to her retirement.

14. As we see the matter, the incapacity that was the ground for the applicant's retirement ("neck pain") was caused, or substantially contributed to, by aggravation of her pre-existing cervical spondylosis (see Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 3 ALN No. 1). The connection that must be found for her to fall within the terms of s.66(2) is a relevant connection between the aggravation of the cervical spondylosis and the macular dystrophy. As mentioned above, this connection was found by the delegate in the abnormal posture the applicant adopted.

15. Whether that is a sufficient connection is, in our view a matter that has to be decided basically on the views expressed by the Federal Court in Commissioner for Superannuation v. Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 63 ALR 237, rather than by a consideration of compensation cases or the provisions in other statutes said to be in pari materia, such as the Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Act, as Mr Anforth urged. In Miller's case, the President of this Tribunal (Davies J.) expressed his views on the operation of the words "connected with" in s.16(4)(b) and s.66(2)(c) of the Superannuation Act. He said:

" The words "connected with" are not technical

words and in ordinary parlance permit of an

association which is not causative. In my opinion,

however, Parliament did not intend the words to be

given a wide interpretation unrelated to the

context in which they appear.

Section 16(4), 16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) are all,

in my opinion, concerned with questions of

causation. These provisions look primarily to the

circumstances where a medical or physical condition

existing at the time the employee first became

eligible is likely to lead to early retirement and

in fact caused or substantially contributed to

early retirement.

In the context, it appears to me that the

words "connected with" were introduced to deal with

the circumstance where the early retirement arises

not from the medical condition which existed at the

time the employee became an eligible employee but

as a result of another medical condition that arose

out of or developed from that condition. Thus, in

the present case, the immediate or proximate cause

of the retirement was not the employee's condition

of "hormonal mastopathy presenting as breast

lumps", which had ceased to exist, but from the

adhesions which had arisen as a result of the

operation on the employee's breasts.

In the context, I would read the words

"connected with" in ss.16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) as

dealing with the circumstance where the immediate

or proximate cause of the incapacity leading to the

retirement was a medical condition other than the

condition existing at the time the employee became

an eligible employee but where, nevertheless, there

is a chain of causation such that the medical

condition existing at the time when the employee

became an eligible employee can be said, through a

chain of events, to have caused or substantially

contributed to the incapacity causing the

retirement.

This reading gives due attention to the

causative elements prescribed by s.16(10(b) and

66(2)(c) and also to the context of the Act in

which those provisions function. It also gives due

weight to the crucial provision, s.16(4) of the

Act. That provision requires the Commissioner to

prognosticate as to whether, by reason of the

medical condition existing at the commencement of

eligibility or for a reason connected with that

condition, the employee would not be likely to

continue to be an eligible employee until maximum

retiring age. The provision requires the

Commissioner to be able to postulate a chain of

causation between a medical condition existing at

the time of the commencement of eligibility and an

incapacity leading to early retirement. Sections

16(1)(b) and 66(2)(c) of the Act look at the matter

after retirement to see whether such a chain of

causation actually existed."

16. Beaumont J. after referring to the facts of the case, stated the question shortly as whether the fibrous reaction was "connected with" the hormonal mastopathy. He said:

" In its ordinary meaning, "connected with"

suggests a link or an association. In the present

context, in my opinion, the phrase is intended to

have its ordinary meaning and thus to pick up those

cases where a link or an association exists between

two medical conditions. That is a question of

fact, to be determined in the light of the expert

evidence.

It may be accepted that, in some cases,

difficult questions of fact will arise for

resolution in determining whether the requisite

nexus between the two conditions has been

established. In this respect, since the

Commissioner bears the onus of proof (see Minister

for Health v. Thomson [1985] FCA 208; (1985) 60 ALR 701 at

p 712), if the expert evidence available does not

permit of a conclusion that the two conditions are

linked or associated in a relevant medical sense,

the provisions of s.16(10)(b) and s.66(2)(c) will

not apply. Further, a question of law, one of

construction which it is presently unnecessary to

resolve, could arise in some cases as to whether

the nexus required by these provisions is one of

causal connection or something short of that.

In the present case, the requisite link or

association has, in my view, been established, even

if it were necessary for the Commissioner to

establish a causal connection for this purpose.

The respondent's hormonal mastopathy required

surgery; as a result of the surgery, the

prosthesis was inserted; the prosthesis caused the

fibrous reaction. It follows, in my opinion, that

the reaction was "connected with" the earlier

hormonal disorder for the purposes of both

s.16(10(b) and s.66(2)(c).

In so concluding, I have not found it

necessary or even helpful to seek analogies in the

general law. In particular, I have not found it

useful to consider whether a tortfeasor may or may

not have been liable for certain loss or damage

because the consequences of medical treatment were

not reasonably foreseeable (see Minister of

Pensions v. Chennell (1947) KB 250; cf. Mahoney

v. J.K. Kruschich (Demolitions Pty. Ltd. 59 ALR

722 at p 725; Hart and Honore, Causation in the

Law, 2nd ed. at p 184)."

17. Pincus J. said:

" The expression "connected with" is not very

precise. It is my view that the sort of connection

which is meant is a causal one. For the purposes

of s.16(10)(b) and s.66(2)(c) of the Superannuation

Act 1976, two conditions are connected with one

another if there is a substantial causal link

between the two. That may be so because one is a

cause of the other, or because both have a common

cause. It is my opinion that unless there is a

substantial causal link, there is no connection. I

appreciate that the word "substantial" introduces

questions of degree, but they are neither questions

which need to be resolved for the purposes of this

case, nor questions as to which one can usefully

give any guidance. The view I have expressed on

this point accords, I think, with that set out in

the reasons of Davies J.

It is necessarily involved in what I have said

that, if there is a substantial causal link between

two conditions, they are "connected with" one

another whether or not the final condition was

foreseeable.

In arriving at my view as to the sort of

connection contemplated by the legislature, I have

been influenced by notions current in other areas

of the law in which this sort of problem arises.

It is a familiar one, for example, in the law of

workers' compensation where the question is whether

a condition appearing after treatment was one

"arising out of and in the course of the

employment". In Lindeman Ltd. v. Colvin (1946) 74

CLR 313 at 321, the High Court had to consider

such a case. Referring to a "line of decisions

dealing with further or aggravated injury ascribed

to medical treatment" Dixon J. said at p.321:

'These decision show that, if an injury

resulting from accident arising out of and in

the course of the employment is aggravated by

medical treatment or if the surgical

procedures adopted to remedy or alleviate the

injury cause secondary traumatic or

pathological condition or death, the total

condition is to be attributed to the accident,

that is so long as the workman acted

reasonably ... The surgical procedure must,

however, be directed to the original injury,

and death or disablement caused by additional

procedures to remedy other ills cannot be

attributed to the original accident or injury

...'

In that case, of course, the boot was on the

other foot; it was in the interests of the injured

worker to find a causal connection between the

initial condition and the final one. It may be

that a more substantial link will be thought

necessary under the provisions presently being

considered, in order to deny a retired worker

superannuation, than would have been enough for the

opposite purpose under workers' compensation

statutes. The significance of the dictum of Sir

Owen Dixon, however, is that it illustrates that

the finding of a connection, on a causal basis,

between a condition consequential upon treatment

and the condition treated is a familiar idea in a

cognate branch of the law. It is so, further, in

the law of negligence where injured plaintiffs

commonly recover in respect of conditions caused by

treatment designed to alleviate the injury brought

about by the defendant."

18. As already mentioned, the question in the present case comes down to whether the posture adopted by the applicant, the event that linked her cervical spondylosis with her macular dystrophy, is a sufficient connecting link between those two medical conditions for the purposes of s.66(2)(c) of the Superannuation Act. In order to fall within the terms of that provision, the medical condition that caused the neck pain must be connected by "a chain of events" with a medical condition that is specified in the relevant benefit classification certificate, in this case, macular dystrophy. We do not read the judgments in Miller's case as requiring that the chain of causation must be comprised only of what might be termed medical events - though that was no doubt the situation in Miller's case and has been so in numerous other cases that have come before this Tribunal. We are of the opinion that the comments in particular of Davies J. embraced a wider scope than purely medical conditions when he referred to a chain of events. We do not regard the adoption of an unusual posture for reading, such as the applicant was forced to adopt, as itself constituting a medical condition.

19. While we take the view that the connecting link or links need not in all cases consist of a medical condition or conditions, it does not follow that, so long as there is a traceable chain of events, that is sufficient to satisfy the terms of s.66(2)(c). Davies J. indicated that Parliament did not intend the words "connected with" to be "given a wide interpretation unrelated to the context in which they appear". He referred to "a chain of causation such that the medical condition existing at the time when the employee became an eligible employee can be said, through a chain of events, to have caused or substantially contributed to the incapacity causing the retirement". He pointed out that s.16(4) requires the Commissioner to be able to postulate a chain of causation between a medical condition existing when the employee commenced his eligibility and an incapacity leading to early retirement. Pincus J. agreed that "the sort of connection that is meant is a causal one" and said that "two conditions are connected with one another if there is a substantial causal link between the two". He went on to say that, "That may be so because one is a cause of the other, or because both have a common cause. It is my opinion that unless there is a substantial causal link, there is no connection". Beaumont J. was less emphatic on the requirement that there be a causal link, finding as he did that, if the Act required such a link, it had been established.

20. We have concluded that at least Davies J. and Pincus J. took the view that the required connection must consist of a substantial causal link. We respectfully adopt that view.

21. In Miller's case a direct medical link could be seen between the condition specified in the BCC and the condition that caused the incapacity that resulted in Mrs Miller's retirement. In the present case, we have a different situation. The applicant adopted a particular posture which resulted in at least the aggravation of her cervical spondylosis, and she would not have had to adopt that posture but for the fact that she suffered from her macular dystrophy, a condition that ordinarily has no relationship with cervical spondylosis. We have concluded on the facts that that link falls short of the substantial causal link that s.66(2)(c) requires. The abnormal posture she adopted was not a necessary consequence of her macular dystrophy, though it was not unrelated to it. But it appears to us that that posture was the product of her working conditions and in particular the failure of the Commonwealth to provide her with adequate facilities, in recognition of her Special Placement status, to enable her to cope with her duties as they became more demanding. We see the link in this case as something more than a merely casual link but it is not a substantial causal link.

22. A useful discussion of the matter of causation is to be found in Haber v. Walker [1963] VicRp 51; (1963) VR 339 at 349 (Lowe J.) and 358 (Smith J.). That case dealt with a death by suicide that was claimed to be the consequence of a motor car accident for the purpose of the Wrongs Act 1958 of Victoria. Lowe J. said:

" I have pointed out that what the Act requires

is that the death should be caused by the wrongful

act, etc. It is not that it should be the natural

and probable result of the act or the direct result

of the act or any similar phrase. The problem

which the Act poses is one of causation only. Nor

are we without help as to the meaning of such

language. For many years the courts have been

familiar with cases arising under the Workers

Compensation Acts and in some of these cases the

injury suffered by the workman has led to his

suicide. Dunham v. Clare, (1902) 2 KB 292, was a

case which arose under the Workmen's Compensation

Act 1897 (Eng.) and posed the question whether the

death of the workman was caused by (resulted from)

personal injury which arose out of and in the

course of his employment. The County Court judge

held that it did not, on the ground that it was not

the natural or probable consequence of the injury.

But in the Court of Appeal Collins, M.R., used

language which has been approved as indicating what

the real test is. He said: "... the only question

is whether death in fact resulted from the injury.

If death in fact resulted from the injury resolves

itself into an inquiry into the chain of causation.

If the chain of causation is broken by a novus

actus interveniens so that the old cause goes and a

new one is substituted for it, that is a new act

which gives a fresh origin to the after

consequences. In dealing with an obligation

created by the Act we are not dealing with a case

of contract or tort or with a liability of a

criminal nature" in which questions of

reasonableness might be relevant. And he

continues: "No question of reasonableness comes

into the present discussion. The Act has imposed

liability irrespective of any error of judgment or

negligence on the part of the employer. The only

question to be considered is: Did the death or

incapacity in fact result from the injury? The

County Court judge by inquiring whether death was

the natural or probable consequence of the injury

has applied the wrong standard to the solution of

the question. ...If no new cause, no novus actus

intervenes, death has in fact resulted from the

injury". Mathew and Cozens-Hardy, L.JJ., agreed

for similar reasons."

Smith J. at p.358 stated the relevant principles as follows:

" Confining attention to what is relevant to the

present case the main principles, I consider, are

these. In the first place a wrongful act or

omission cannot ordinarily be held to have been a

cause of subsequent harm unless that harm would not

have occurred without the act or omission having

previously occurred with such of its incidents as

rendered it wrongful. Exceptions to this first

principle are narrowly confined. Secondly, where

the requirements of this first principle are

satisfied, the act or omission is to be regarded as

a cause of the harm unless there intervenes between

the act or omission and the harm an occurrence

which is necessary for the production of the harm

and is sufficient in law to sever the causal

connexion. And, finally, the intervening

occurrence, if it is to be sufficient to sever the

connexion, must ordinarily be either -

(a) human action that is properly to be

regarded as voluntary, or

(b) a causally independent event the

conjunction of which with the wrongful act or

omission is by ordinary standards so extremely

unlikely as to be termed a coincidence: see

Hart and Honore, op. cit., especially at pp.

103-117, 123-134, 151-152 and 157-9."

If the matter were approached along the lines of Haber v. Walker, we would see the chain of causation broken by the failure to provide adequate visual aids to the applicant in her employment.

23. The decision under review will be set aside and a decision substituted that the applicant is entitled to invalidity benefits in accordance with s.66(1) of the Superannuation Act.
