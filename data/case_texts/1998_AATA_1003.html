CATCHWORDS- BANKRUPTCY - DISCHARGE - APPLICATION FOR REVIEW OF THE INSPECTOR-GENERAL IN BANKRUPTCY DECLINING TO REVIEW THE OFFICIAL TRUSTEE IN BANKRUPTCY' CONTRIBUTION ASSESSMENT AND OBJECTION TO DISCHARGE - WHAT AMOUNTS - IF ANY - THE BANKRUPT IS ENTITLED TO HAVE ALLOWED AS DEDUCTIONS FROM HIS ASSESSABLE INCOME AS EXPENSES NECESSARILY INCURRED - WHETHER SUPERANNUATION CONTRIBUTIONS ALLOWABLE DEDUCTION.

FCT v Snowden and Willson Pty Ltd [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431

Magna Alloys Research Pty Ltd v FCT 80 ATC 4542

R v Hunt; Ex parte Sean Investments Pty Ltd [1979] HCA 32; 25 ALR 497

Fletcher Dunlop and Ors v FCT 91 ATC 4950

Bankruptcy Act 1966

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936

Superannuation Guarantee (Administration Act 1992

REASONS FOR DECISION

1. This is an application for review of decisions of the Inspector-General in Bankruptcy, dated 7 January 1998, declining to review the Official Trustee in Bankruptcy's contribution assessment and the objection to discharge, lodged under subs 149D(1)(f) of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 (the Act).

2. The evidence before the Tribunal comprised the documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (the T documents), together with exhibits tendered by the parties. Mr Wyles of counsel appeared for the applicant, the respondent being represented by Ms Davies of counsel. The applicant gave oral evidence and called his (estranged) wife and Mr William Kevin Garner to give evidence. Mr Cockwill was called to give evidence on behalf of the respondent.

3. The applicant is a 61 year old plastic surgeon who has been employed by a company, TH Robbins Pty Ltd (TH Robbins) since 1985 to provide his services as a surgeon. On 17 November 1994 the applicant presented a Debtor's Petition to the Registrar of Bankruptcy, and the Official Trustee is now trustee of his bankrupt estate. The applicant has continued in the employ of TH Robbins since that date of presentation.

4. The applicant was assessed on 2 and 3 September 1997 by a delegate for the Official Receiver for contribution assessments for contribution assessment periods, details of which are as follows:

Contribution Assessment Period Contribution Assessment Amount

17/11/94 to 16/11/95 $34,550.96

17/11/95 to 16/11/96 $34,414.40

5. The issue before the Tribunal is what amounts, if any, the applicant is entitled to have allowed as deductions from his assessable income as expenses necessarily incurred by TH Robbins in connection with the provision of work and/or services by him

6. The legislative context within which this matter is to be resolved is to be found principally within Division 4B of Part IV of the Bankruptcy Act 1992 , as enacted by Act No 9 of 1992. The objects of the division are to be found in s 139J, which provides:

139J Objects of Division

The objects of this Division are:

(a) to require a bankrupt who derives income during the bankruptcy to pay contributions towards the bankrupt's estate; and

(b) to enable the recovery of certain money and property for the benefit of the bankrupt's estate.

7. S 139L of the Act defines income for the purpose of subdivision C as follows:

139L Meaning of income

In this Division:

income, in relation to a bankrupt, has its ordinary meaning, subject to the following qualifications:

(a) the following are income in relation to a bankrupt (whether or not they come within the ordinary meaning of "income"):

(v) the value of a benefit that:

(A) is provided in any circumstances by any person (the provider) to the bankrupt; and

(B) is a benefit within the meaning of the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 as in force at the beginning of 1 July 1992 (other than a benefit that would be an exempt benefit for the purposes of that Act if the provider were the employer of the bankrupt as an employee and the provider had provided the benefit in respect of the employment of the bankrupt);

being that value as worked out in accordance with the provisions of that Act but subject to any modifications of any provisions of that Act made by the regulations under this Act;

(vii) the amount of any money, or the value of any other consideration, received by a person other than the bankrupt from another person as a result of work done or services performed by the bankrupt, less any expenses (other than expenses of a capital nature) necessarily incurred by the first-mentioned person in connection with the work or services;

8. S 139P of the Act imposes the obligation on the bankrupt to pay the contribution. S 139S enunciates the formula by which contribution amounts are to be assessed, and s 139U creates an obligation by which the bankrupt is required to provide to the trustee evidence of income. S 139W sets out the assessment process itself, by which the amounts set out in paragraph 4 above were calculated, providing relevantly as follows:

139W Assessment of bankrupt's income and contribution

(1) As soon as practicable after the start of each contribution assessment period in relation to a bankrupt, the trustee is to make an assessment of the income that is likely to be derived, or was derived, by the bankrupt during that period, of the actual income threshold amount that is applicable in relation to the bankrupt when the assessment is made and of the contribution (if any) that the bankrupt is liable to pay in respect of that period under section 139S.

9. Subdivision G of Division 4B establishes the avenues for review of assessments, s 139ZF providing for review by this Tribunal of decisions:

139ZF Applications to Administrative Appeals Tribunal

An application may be made to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for the review of:

...

(c) a decision of the Inspector-General refusing a request to review such a decision

10. The income initially assessed was based upon receipts derived from Medicare through the applicant's Health Insurance Commission provider numbers. This represents only part of the actual fees billed to any given patient, and thus an assessment based upon it is considerably understated. However, it was agreed between the parties at the outset of proceedings in this matter that the consulting fees of the company for the income years 1994/95, 1995/96, and 1996/97 - the gross fees - are income of the bankrupt as defined by section 139L(a)(vii) of the Act. Those amounts are as set out below, and as was elucidated by the respondent, this concession will necessitate a variation of the original assessments to accommodate these extra amounts. The consulting fees for the three relevant income years are as follows:

* 1994/95 $303,009

* 1995/96 $354,449

* 1996/97 $350,118

11. Mr Wyles, in his opening, directed the Tribunal to the focus of dispute between the parties. As noted above, it was conceded by him on behalf of the applicant that the income derived by TH Robbins was income received as a result of work and/or services performed by the applicant in his capacity as a plastic surgeon; that income was therefore caught by the definition of "income" in s 139L(a)(vii) of the Act, and is thus properly assessable per se. The dispute remaining is whether certain expenses are deductible; particularly superannuation contributions made by TH Robbins for the applicant and his wife, and the cost of various administrative services provided by the Company to the applicant in the course of his professional services.

12. In calculating the assessment periods, only expenses as to the first contribution assessment period were considered by the trustee for deduction purposes; the reason for this being that the trustee was not able to obtain requisite financial information from the applicant (T2/11). The expenses disallowed in calculating the first contribution assessment period are as follows:

* Conference and seminar expenses $13,738

* Management fees $177,505

* Salary and wages (applicant) $31,200

* Superannuation expenses $62,000

13. The applicant provided a statement, undated and marked as Exhibit A2, disclosing that he commenced employment with the TH Robbins in 1985. He last renewed his contract of employment with TH Robbins on 1 July 1992, a copy of the agreement beings annexed to his statement. In accordance with the terms of that contract, the applicant was paid salary at a rate of $420.30 per week on a quarterly basis (indexed to the CPI). Additionally, the statutorily required rate of superannuation was paid, with TH Robbins at its sole discretion able to contribute further amounts. The relevant terms of the contract provide as follows:

Thomas Robbins has no right to private practice and must account to TH Robbins Pty Ltd in respect to any income received in his name as a result of his efforts as a surgeon; this is to include all claims submitted to the Health Services Commission under his provider numbers.

Superannuation: The statutory contributions legally required and other contributions at the discretion of the company.

14. As explained by the applicant in the course of his oral evidence, this situation resulted in a remuneration package commensurate with his position as a provider of surgical services equivalent to around $31,000-32,000 per annum, complemented by substantial superannuation benefits. Mrs Dawn Evelyn Robbins, the applicant's wife, stated in the course of her evidence that TH Robbins also provided a vehicle for the applicant's use.

15. The applicant was both a shareholder and, from 23 October 1985 to 15 November 1994, a director of TH Robbins, together with Mrs Robbins. On 17 November 1994, the applicant presented a Debtor's Petition. It is apparent from the records that this was only two days after his resignation as a director of TH Robbins, being replaced by his (non-medical) brother, Mr Edward Robbins, who also replaced the applicant as a shareholder in 1995. The applicant was queried during cross examination as to whether, given the temporal proximity of the events, his resignation as a director was linked to his imminent entry into bankruptcy. He was unsure as to whether the events were linked, but conceded that they may have been, and expressed awareness that as a bankrupt he would be unable to hold company office.

16. Mrs Robbins was employed by TH Robbins, and also had significant superannuation contributions made by the company for her benefit. During her evidence she confirmed, on questioning from the Tribunal, that the amount of superannuation contributions made were basically calculated to absorb the net profit of the practice (see the figures set out in paragraph 6 above). The superannuation payments made during the contribution assessment periods were $62,000 for 1994/95, $120,000 for 1995/96, and $106,000. It was claimed by the respondent that the larger amounts represent payments made on behalf of both the applicant and Mrs Robbins. This was not disputed by the applicant, and the Tribunal accepts it as fact.

17. The applicant, in both his statement and oral testimony, claimed that Mrs Robbins was in full control of TH Robbins, and that while prior to November 1994 he was legally in a position of control, both as holder of one of two issued shares and as one of two directors, in fact it was his wife who maintained the affairs and direction of the company. Mrs Robbins confirmed during the course of her evidence that as such she was - and is to this day - in day to day control of TH Robbins.

18. The applicant was equivocal in his evidence regarding the issue of control of the company since his resignation as a director, and while he stated that he had not had any involvement in the affairs of the company since that time, he was unable to explain the presence of his signature on a TH Robbins members' resolution, dated 13 December 1995, nor had recall of his having been present at the annual general meeting of the company on 31 December 1995, as recorded in the minutes of that meeting of that date (both documents were annexed to the statement of Mr Garner). In fact, his evidence regarding both the legal framework and the practical operation of the surgical practice, TH Robbins, and associated entities, was vague to say the least.

19. Mrs Dawn Evelyn Robbins provided a statement, dated 9 November 1998 (Exhibit C), and gave oral evidence. She has been a director of the company since March 1986 and is responsible for the day to day management and operation of that company and the practice operated by it. As stated above, Mrs Robbins is also employed by the TH Robbins as a nurse, and she stated that the applicant was the only other employee of the company during the relevant period.

20. Mrs Robbins gave evidence relating to a further company, Timcal Pty Ltd (Timcal), of which she is also a director together with their son, Paul Robbins. Timcal has since 1994 employed two people, being Mrs Robbins and a secretary. Mrs Robbins provides administrative assistance to the practice - non-nursing duties - through the employ of Timcal. This company was established in or about 1983 on the advice of previous accountants as a means of providing administrative services to the principal company, TH Robbins, so as to obtain tax advantages, and is the trustee of the Robbins Family Trust.

21. Mrs Robbins elaborated on the nature of the relationship between TH Robbins and Timcal, stating that Timcal, in addition to providing administrative services to TH Robbins, and on top of actual expenses, levies service charge mark ups of varying percentages. These mark up fees were set by the accountant, Mr Garner. They differ according to the type of service they were levied on, being generally 8 per cent for fee collection, 15-20 per cent for practice expenses, and 20-25 per cent for expenses in respect of salary and wages. This amounted to the following totals of levied expense (exhibit G):

* 1994/95 - $50,104

* 1996/97 - $53,164

22. Charges for the 1995/96 year were not disclosed concisely in any material before the Tribunal; the respondent submitted that an amount of $44,287.75 was disclosed by the invoices tendered as Exhibit E (approximate as the invoice for June 1996 was missing). The Tribunal accepts this figure for the purposes of its consideration.

23. Mrs Robbins was also asked as to whether the Robbins Family Trust distributed income in the years spanning the contribution assessment periods 1995, 1996, and 1997. While she believed same had occurred, she was confident that no distributions had been made to the applicant at any stage, particularly not in 1995 or 1997. Minutes of a meeting produced to the Tribunal disclosed that the Trust did distribute to a further company, Dawmas Nominees Pty Ltd, of which Mrs Robbins and Paul Robbins are shareholders and directors.

24. Mrs Robbins was questioned in cross-examination as to the presence of the applicant at the two meetings in December 1995, a matter first canvassed during his testimony. She did not believe that the applicant was in fact a member of the company when he signed the minutes dated 13 December 1995, ascribing this to "secretarial error". Further, she stated that the recorded presence of the applicant at the AGM on 31 December was again an error. Nevertheless, she did not rule out that the applicant was present at either meetings, and conceded that while she controlled the management, the applicant had continued to be involved to some extent in the affairs of TH Robbins.

25. Mr William Kevin Garner was called by the applicant and attested to a statement provided by him dated 6 November 1998. In his statement, Mr Garner provides in part as follows:

...

2. In April 1991, I was asked to take over the general accounting work for TH Robbins Pty Ltd ("TH Robbins") and Timcal Pty Ltd ("Timcal"). At that time I was a partner in the firm of Harrison, Garner & Dunner.

3. TH Robbins is the medical practice company of which Mr Robbins, the applicant herein is employed as a surgeon. Timcal is the trustee of the Robbins Family Trust, a service entity providing services for the medical practice.

4. Prior to the time that I was approached to take over the accounting work for TH Robbins and Timcal, this work was carried out by Pannell Kerr Foster, who I understand set up the medical practice and the accounting procedures for those companies. Since April 1991, I have prepared the financial accounts for both TH Robbins and Timcal from books and records and from information provided to me by the officers of those companies."

26. The respondent called the one witness, Mr James Cockwill, manager within the Government Programs Functions in the Health Insurance Commission. Mr Cockwill provided a statement, dated 27 October 1998 (Exhibit 1). The witness addressed the issue of medical provider numbers, particularly as to who can hold them, and the nature of their use. The Tribunal accepts Mr Cockwill's evidence, though it finds no significant relevance in it.

Submissions

27. The respondent submits that the business entities and company structures based around the medical practice of the applicant are in fact entirely a family affair. The respondent points to the lengthy directorship and shareholding history of the applicant in TH Robbins up until immediately prior to his delivery of the Debtor's Petition in 1994, and the replacement of him in those capacities by his brother. The respondent further points to the applicant's terms of employment with TH Robbins, and the direction of a goodly portion of his salary package into superannuation contributions made at the discretion of the directors - his wife and his brother.

28. Essentially, the respondent contends that in relation to the first contribution assessment period, the disallowed deductions should stand. Ms Davies submitted that the amount disallowed as salary and wages was technically an incorrect approach - that it should have been included along with gross fees in forming the basis of the contribution amount, but in any case the same result is achieved.

29. In the respondent's submission, allowances made in respect of taxation require reconsideration, as they are overly broad, given that the only tax remitted by the applicant was that paid on his salary and wage earnings.

30. No submissions were made by the respondent as to the conference and seminar fees. Ms Davies concentrated on the two significant items sought to be deducted; the superannuation payments and the management fees.

31. The respondent's position in relation to the superannuation is that only superannuation payments made to the extent of the required statutory level of minimum support as set out in the Superannuation Guarantee (Administration) Act are allowable deductions; that is, non-capital expenses incurred by TH Robbins in connection with the applicant's work. Over and above those basic statutory levels, the respondent's contention is that the superannuation contributions are not expenses of TH Robbins which the trustee is required to deduct in assessing income. This contention is founded on one of two alternate bases. The first of these is that the payments made toward the applicant's superannuation are properly characterised as payments made for the purposes of obtaining superannuation and the relevant employer deductions; not expenses connected with the applicant's operation as a plastic surgeon, that is, with his work. To this end, the respondent directed the Tribunal to the explanatory memorandum to the Bankruptcy Legislation Amendment Bill 1996, illuminating the legislative intent behind section 139L(a)(vii), which states at page 123 (in part) as follows:

The paragraph is designed to address a situation where a third person or structure is interposed between the bankrupt and a person supplying money or other consideration for work or services actually performed by the bankrupt - ready examples are provided by service companies which are established by the bankrupt or an associate, and which employ the bankrupt, and the bankrupt's earnings are paid to the service company. The earnings of the company, which are directly attributable to the bankrupt's work or services are sometimes far greater than the salary or wages actually paid to the bankrupt. In cases such as this, where the income of the entity is substantially attributable to the bankrupt's work or services, the income of the bankrupt is taken to include the income of the entity concerned.

32. Alternatively, the respondent submits that the superannuation contributions are not deductible because they are of a capital nature, and therefore excluded by the express provision of subs 39L(a)(vii).

33. It is the respondent's position that the investment of the amounts by TH Robbins should be considered as direct personal investment by the applicant, that the Tribunal should look beyond the corporate veil and view the payment of the superannuation amounts as investment to secure rights under the superannuation legislative regime, thus being in the nature of capital expenditure.

34. With respect to the management fees, the respondent contends that actual expenses incurred are deductible. However the excess amounts - the mark-ups charged - are wholly a means of minimising tax on the earnings received by TH Robbins, by diverting that income into Timcal, and then claiming same as deductions, and as such are not "necessarily incurred" in connection with the retention of Dr Robbin's services.

35. Mr Wyles for the applicant submitted with respect to the superannuation payments, that these are an intrinsic facet of Dr Robbin's salary package, and a necessary feature or expense involved in engaging his services; that it is a fiction to divorce these payments from the package, and that but for these contributions, the applicant would not provide his services to the company. It was submitted further that the management fees are costs necessarily incurred - that the applicant's performance of medical services is essential to the operation of the companies, and that TH Robbins simply makes payments to Timcal to ensure that the latter can effectively provide the props required to enable the practice to be conducted. Mr Wyles submits that to isolate the marked up amounts merely because Timcal has associations with TH Robbins, is artificial, and that the amounts should be allowed as deductions in their entirety.

Conclusion

36. It should be noted that the parties did not seek from Tribunal findings as to the appropriate figures to be assessed; merely that the Tribunal find on the factual and substantive issues before it, and remit the matter for assessment according to the Tribunal's findings.

37. The question for resolution is whether, examining the totality of the practice and the corporate framework within which it operates, the relevant payments can be characterised as "necessary". There is no authority addressing this section of the legislation. Nevertheless, helpful analogy can be gained through consideration of decisions relating to s 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 , relating to deduction of business expenses, which provides (for the sake of comparison) as follows:

(1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income.

38. The word "necessary" in the context of expenditure related to the carrying on (as opposed to "in connection with" as s139L(a)(vii) is phrased) of a business was considered by Dixon CJ in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Snowden and Willson Pty Ltd [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431, where at pp 436 to 437, his Honour stated:

In The Commonwealth and The Post-Master-General v Progress Advertising & Press

Agency Co Pty Ltd [1910] HCA 28; (1910) 10 CLR 457 Higgins J supplied an interpretation

of "necessary" as not meaning essentially necessary but as meaning

appropriate, plainly adapted to the needs of a department carrying out an Act 1953

(1910) 10 CLR, at p 469 . That was in another connexion but the phrase was

availed of by the Court in the Ronpibon Tin Case (1949) 78 CLR, at p 56 as

throwing light on the use of the word "necessarily" in s 51 (1). Clearly the

expression is used in relation to business. Logical necessity is not a thing

to be predicated of business expenditure. What is meant by the qualification

is that the expenditure must be dictated by the business ends to which it is

directed, those ends forming part of or being truly incidental to the

business. (at p437)

39. In the present case, obviously the word "necessarily" qualifies not expenditure related to business, but to the work or services provided by a bankrupt. Nevertheless, it can be distilled from the above passage that expenditure "necessarily incurred" in the context of the provision of services and work by the applicant, must be expenditure appropriate to his operation as a plastic surgeon, being an expense flowing directly and immediately from - or in the wording of Dixon CJ, incidental to - that work. Brennan, J considered the same test in the same legislative context in Magna Alloys Research Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 80 ATC 4542, where his Honour referred with approval to Dixon CJ's approach, and stated, in part, as follows:

Fullagar and Taylor JJ were of the same opinion (see pp 444, 447, 448). In

that case, it was assumed that the directors would instinctively wish to

defend the company against attacks upon its business methods and reputation.

But it makes no difference that, in the present case, the learned trial judge

was unpersuaded that that was "the dominant, or even a principal reason, why

the expenditure was incurred". The reason why the directors incurred the

expenditure does not define the scope or nature of the taxpayer's business,

nor does it reveal what the expenditure was for beyond assisting to show that

what was sought and obtained was legal representation to defend the directors

and employees. The directors' reasons do not show or tend to show that legal

representation to defend the directors and employees was not connected with

the carrying on of the business. Quite the contrary. The ends of the business

required that an attempt be made to defend the impugned conduct, and the

attempt was made. The necessary connection between the expenditure and the

carrying on of the business thus appears. (at p 4553).

40. Whether an expense is thus "necessarily incurred" is to be considered in light of the scope of the activity supposedly creating the expense - the work or services of the applicant as a plastic surgeon. The concluding sentences of Brennan J's statement above lends itself to an inference that on identifying the ends or purposes of that activity, the costs sought to be deducted must be a required aspect of that activity.

41. The same words "necessarily incurred" were considered in a different statutory context in R v Hunt; Ex parte Sean Investments Pty Ltd [1979] HCA 32; 25 ALR 497. In that case, Mason J considering the phrase as it appeared in legislation, specifically the National Health Act, regulating the fee structures of nursing homes, noted:

The words "costs necessarily incurred in providing nursing home care in the nursing home" mean costs which the proprietor incurs, and is obliged to incur, in the provision of the nursing home care which he is providing in his nursing home. "Necessarily" indicates a distinction between costs which are incurred voluntarily and those which are incurred compulsively because one is obliged to incur them in order to carry on the activity in question. The payment of rent at a level higher than the prevailing rate does not of itself indicate that it is a cost unnecessarily incurred. A lessor may insist on a rent higher than the prevailing rate and, unless the lessee is willing to pay it, he will not secure possession of the premises for use as a nursing home. The liability to pay the rent is then a cost necessarily incurred, notwithstanding that it may be described as excessive. But if the lessee voluntarily agrees to pay rent at a level higher than the prevailing rate because he wishes to benefit the lessor, the lessor being a relation of the lessee or a company in which he has an interest, the whole of the rent is not a cost necessarily incurred. This is because the expenditure has been incurred voluntarily and, in consequence, it lacks the required connexion with the provision of nursing home care in the nursing home. (at p 503).

42. The above exposition makes a distinction betweens costs incurred involuntarily, and those which are undertaken voluntarily, that is, opted for by the person or entity bearing them, presumably for the attainment of some purpose other than that obviously attributable to the expense. For example, in the present case superannuation payments were made obviously so as to provide superannuation, and to an extent, because they were statutorily demanded. However they were borne by TH Robbins as opposed to the applicant personally, and voluntarily up to the limit possible under the superannuation legislation because TH Robbins as an employer could deduct the full allowable amount, whereas the applicant would be restricted to 75 per cent. Again, considering the management expenses - the expenses themselves were incurred so that the applicant could operate a successful practice and concentrate on his professional endeavour without the burden of administrative duties. Yet they were - at least the components over and above the bare expense and referred to as the mark ups - representative of a means to split income and achieve concomitant taxation benefits.

43. Turning firstly the issue of the superannuation payments. The applicant's contention relied in part on a "but for" premise - but for the superannuation contributions being made in addition to the salary received by him, he would not have provided his services. Further to this, that TH Robbins was bound to so make the contributions by the contract of employment, effective from 1992. However, there is nothing actually binding TH Robbins to the making of those payments - they were left to the discretion of the directors of the company. Obviously such contributions were contemplated, and the structure of TH Robbins was such that, at least until November 1994, the applicant, as a director and shareholder, had significant and direct influence on their being made Nevertheless, non-payment of contributions would not, on the face of the employment contract, constitute any breach of that agreement between TH Robbins and the applicant.

44. The evidence of both the applicant and Mrs Robbins was that the formation and utilisation of TH Robbins was done purely as a device to enable the obtaining of the maximum deduction of superannuation contributions. Counsel for the respondent directed the Tribunal to the relevant provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 , which enable superannuation contributions to be deducted - but as noted earlier, in the case of an employee, only up to 75% of the allowable deduction limit accorded an employer. Obviously, then, to have the company as employer and contributor, handling the same income, was of benefit to the applicant in minimising the tax paid on that income. It is clear from the evidence that the contributions were chosen to be made by TH Robbins so as to enable these benefits to be obtained - this was the motive behind them. Reference was made by the respondent to the decision of Fletcher and Ors v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 91 ATC 4950, where in considering the characterisation of expenses in the context of s 51(1) of the ITAA, the Court noted motive for incurring an outgoing as a "possibly relevant factor" (when characterising an expense as `necessary' or not), and went on to state:

At least in a case where the outgoing has been voluntarily incurred, the end which the taxpayer subjectively had in view in incurring it may, depending on the circumstances of the particular case, constitute an element, and possibly the decisive element, in characterisation of either the whole or part of the outgoing (at 4957)

45. Whilst reiterating that the test contained in s 139L(a)(vii) of the Act differs from that in s51(1) of the ITAA, the Tribunal considers that the reasoning enunciated above can be usefully applied in the context of s139L(a)(vii). Of course, application of this principle must be tempered with heed of the caveat expressed later by their Honours in the Fletcher Dunlop case, viz that if characterisation of an expense or expenses can be made without reference to the subjective thought processes, then such should be done (at p 4958). Nevertheless, in the current situation there is, in considering whether the expenses incurred in the discretionary superannuation distributions were necessarily related to the applicant's work, some value in examining why they were made in identifying the main or dominant purpose for their disbursement. It is accepted that they were a part of a reasonable remuneration package befitting the applicant's professional status. Nevertheless, there was a greater design in packaging his remuneration in that manner - to have him work as a plastic surgeon and derive the monies he did, did not require the system adopted. The applicant could have elected to take a greater proportion as salary, or indeed, practised without the company structure.

46. TH Robbins was, as has not been disputed, a vehicle for the achievement tax benefit. The reason for the payments were to provide superannuation in the most effective means possible, at least for the purposes of taxation (as opposed to bankruptcy contribution) assessment. It was not a necessary end to the applicant's continued performance as a provider of surgical service, and in light of the discussion of the law surrounding this phrase set out above, it does not appear to the Tribunal that the expenditure had the requisite immediacy to the applicant's employment - it did not spring directly from his performance, but was rather a means of facilitating greater benefit from the income.

47. For those reasons the Tribunal would consider as non-deductible expenses the superannuation payments above the statutory limits made by TH Robbins in favour of the applicant.

48. As to payments made on behalf of Mrs Robbins - applying the same reasoning as above, it would follow that the amounts paid out on behalf of her over and above the statutory limits were again not "necessarily incurred" for the purposes of the sub-section. Her statutory costs were necessarily incurred, as she, in her employment role with TH Robbins, provided services adjunct as a nurse to the applicant's own role. Aside from this, the extra payments were incurred by TH Robbins as a means of providing for her superannuation.

49. Turning now to the issue of the management fees. The Tribunal considers that the statement of Mason, J in Hunt, extracted in paragraph 41 above, is apposite in relation to this issue. As was expressed above, the expenditure over and above the direct expense entailed in providing the services was to attain benefits beyond the mere administrative services themselves; it was a means of attaining tax relief. They were excessive payments made voluntarily for a specific purpose - splitting income - rather than an intrinsic facet of the applicant's work. Again, the Tribunal considers that the mark up components of the management fees were not necessarily incurred, and are therefore not deductible.

50. The remaining expenses - those for conference and seminar attendance - were not addressed in any detail by either party; the respondent submitted that the applicant carried an onus to evidence their necessity; the applicant stated that there was no need to go behind the statement in the accounts that these expenses were in fact incurred. Indeed, without more it is difficult to reject the applicant's contention that these were part of the essential continuing education process of many professionals, and in fact many of the workforce per se, and that they were a necessary expense incurred so that the applicant might continue to keep abreast of the body of knowledge relevant and pertinent to his performance as a plastic surgeon.

51. The evidence of Mrs Robbins was that a motor vehicle was provided for use by the applicant. This vehicle, as was in fact all but conceded by him, is caught under s 139L(a)(v) of the Act; the provision of a car to an employee is clearly specified as a fringe benefit under s 6 of the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 . It follows that the value of that benefit would need to be assessed under that latter Act, and included in the bankrupt's assessable income for contribution purposes.

52. Finally, as to the issue of deductions of tax payable as provided for under section 139N, the applicant stated in the course of his evidence that the only tax he has paid has been the income tax payable on the salary he received from TH Robbins, as evidenced in document T10. Deductions of approximately $70,000 were made for both contribution assessment periods for tax liability based on a deemed income figure, whereas it was considered by the original decision maker (T11/60), and accepted by the Tribunal, that this is an overly generous deduction, as in fact tax has been paid only on the income paid as salary to the applicant - sums considerably less than his deemed income for assessment purposes.

53. The Tribunal has considered the evidence carefully and in detail, and taken into account the parties' submissions. For the reasons set out above, the Tribunal remits the matter to the Inspector-General for accurate assessment of the contribution periods, with directions that:

(a) the amounts representing superannuation contributions by TH Robbins on behalf of both the applicant and Mrs Dawn Evelyn Robbins and the management fees be included in the applicant's assessable income;

(b) assessable income for contribution purposes accommodate the value of the vehicle provided to the applicant as a fringe benefit, as calculated under the relevant provisions of the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986;

(c) the amounts originally deducted as tax payable be calculated so as to reflect the actual tax on income declared;

(d) the amount representing conference and seminar fees be allowed as deductions necessarily incurred in connection with the applicant's work and services.
