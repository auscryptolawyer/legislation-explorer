Davy and Commissioner for Superannuation [1985] AATA 490 (12 July 1985)

Last Updated: 19 February 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Superannuation - invalidity retirement benefits benefit classification certificate - whether incapacity caused or substantially contributed to by condition specified in a certificate or by condition "connected with" such a condition.

Superannuation Act 1922 Superannuation Act 1976 ss. 66, 184

Words and phrases "connected with".

Hegadues 4 ALN N139

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION ) No. W84/130

RE: JUNE ROSE DAVY

Applicant

AND: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

The applicant is now 40 years of age and was permanently appointed to the Australian Public Service in May 1973.

For the purposes of that appointment and of the Superannuation Act 1922 (now superseded) she underwent a medical examination in May 1973 and was accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account.

In August 1982, after periods of sick leave and following the recommendation of a Commonwealth medical officer, the applicant was retired as unfit for further service on account of polyarthritis.

The applicant had not then reached her maximum retiring age and had been in the service for less than 20 years. In these circumstances, the Superannuation Act 1922 having been superseded by the 1976 Act, the procedures which then had to be followed and the sort of questions which arose were similar to those which have been considered in a number of reported decisions of the Federal Court and of this Tribunal, some of which are referred to later.

On the applicant's retirement it was necessary for the Commissioner for Superannuation - or his delegate - to determine the conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board had been not satisfied that the applicant's health and fitness justified her acceptance to the Superannuation Fund, but only to the Provident Account which provided lesser benefits. This was done by the delegate and a benefit classification certificate was issued under s184 of the 1976 Act. The delegate also formed the opinion under s66(2) of the Act that the incapacity which had been the ground for retirement of the applicant was: caused, or was substantially contributed to by a condition specified in the benefit classification certificate, or by a condition connected with such a condition.

The result of these determinations was to decrease the benefits which the applicant would otherwise have received under the Superannuation Act.

The delegate's decision was confirmed by the Commissioner and the matter now comes before this Tribunal on review.

It is convenient to commence a summary of the relevant facts with: some details of the medical examination of the applicant by Dr Crowe, a Commonwealth medical officer on 24 May 1973.

The form recording the examination and its result consists of a request to the applicant to report for examination, a personal statement regarding her health completed by the applicant before the examination, the report, recommendations and remarks of the Commonwealth medical officer and a declaration by the applicant that to the best of her knowledge the personal statement was true and correct. A form of authority to give to or obtain from her medical adviser any relevant medical details was not completed by her.

When the applicant was examined by Dr Crowe on 24 May 1973, he questioned her regarding the information in her personal statement and made a clinical examination. He referred her to an eye specialist whose report of 4 June 1973 was subsequently received. There was a further examination on 31 May 1973 by another medical officer.

The following portion of the personal statement is relevant to the present enquiry:

"11.

Q. Details of any complaint, illness or injury not mentioned above.

A. Chicken Pox, Rheumatism.

13.

Q. Details of all accidents, surgical treatment or operations.

A. 1.956 Tonsilectomy, 1958 Injury – left knee.

15.

Q. When did you last receive medical or surgical treatment and for what condition? A. None except for syringing of ears due to build up of wax.

Medical Officer's Comments:

(11) Apparently had had Rheumatism in the Knees and elbows since the age of about 3 yrs in cold damp climate only. Did not have much trouble in Perth.

(13) Bumped the (L.) Knee & since then seems to be affected more than the other joints when it is cold & damp.

(15) About a year ago."

Here we note that according to the evidence of Dr. Irvine, a general practitioner in Perth, the applicant attended him for "blockage of the ear" on 8 July 1972 and she returned to him on 14 June 1973, that is after the medical examination by Dr. Crowe "for an ear clearance".

The following extracts from the medical officer's report of the examination on 24 May 1973 should be noted:

"4(d) Is there any disease of the ears? – Yes - she has wax in the (L) ear and an infection in the (R) ear.

7(a) Does the applicant have normal use of the legs and arms? - Yes - but her history (11) above.

(b) Are there any defects in bones or joints?

- No.

(c) Is there full painless movement of the spine? - Yes.

8. Are there any health conditions which it is advisable should be mentioned. If so. give details. - Yes - the rheumatism history needs noting."

After receipt of a satisfactory report from an eye specialist Dr. Crowe recommended that the applicant be deferred for six months because of:

"(i) The history of Rheumatism.

(ii) The infection in the (R) ear and wax in the left ear needing attention.

(iii) Her weight is 30% above average."

A further examination after the expiration of six months was never carried out.

It is not contended that the condition of overweight is relevant in the present enquiry and there is no evidence that the "wax in the left ear" is relevant. The phrase "ear infection" could in some contexts be read as indicating an infection of both ears. We do not understand this to be intended, nor on the evidence justified, and to put the matter beyond doubt the reference to "ear infection" in the benefit classification certificate should be read as meaning "right ear infection".

To define the questions which arise it may be asked first whether the incapacity being the substantial loss of physical mobility and function which the applicant suffered and which led to her retirement in 1982, was caused or substantially contributed to by a condition specified in the benefit classification certificate, or by a condition "connected with" such a condition.

Before issuing a benefit classification certificate the delegate was required pursuant to s.184(7) of the Act to determine the "relevant conditions", that is, the conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board was not satisfied that the applicant's health and fitness were such as to justify her being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund. The delegate formed the opinion that the relevant conditions were "ear infection", "overweight" and "history of rheumatism" and the benefit classification certificate he issued specified those three conditions.

It is clear from the evidence that the cause of the incapacity was the condition of psoriatic polyarthritis which is not specified in the benefit classification certificate. The remaining question therefore is whether the psoriatic polyarthritis was in a relevant sense "connected with" the history of rheumatism or the right ear infection.

The Commissioner says in his recommendation that having regard to various matters which he sets out:

"...there is little doubt in my mind that the evidence referred to above and the matters raised in the foregoing discussion establish that there was, in fact, a common factor in the causation of polyarthritis and Ms Davy's previous episodes of joint pains (variously described as rheumatism) and/or her ear infection(s).

46. I am therefore of the opinion that, on the balance of probability, the retirement incapacity 'Polyarthritis' suffered by Ms Davy was caused or was substantially contributed to by her psoriatic arthritis and that such condition is connected with the conditions 'History of Rheumatism' and 'Ear Infection'."

We address ourselves first to the condition "history of rheumatism".

There is considerable evidence of "aches and pains" said to have been suffered by the applicant and some significance was attached to the circumstances in which the term "rheumatism" came to be used at the examination of the applicant by Dr. Crowe, and in one place on the relevant form the word "rheumatism" was written by the applicant herself.

Dr Crowe has no independent recollection of the examination and in explaining what he understood had occurred he could say only what his; usual practice at similar examinations was. In addition, it will be seen that it was probably 1973, that is, some 10 years after the event before the applicant was prompted to recall the details of her examination by Dr. Crowe and when she gave evidence before this Tribunal she was endeavouring to recall what had happened some 12 years earlier.

The evidence before the Commissioner was to the effect that the applicant, at her medical examination, said that she, her children and her parents had suffered rheumatism; that the applicant had told her own doctor, Dr. Irvine that she had first noted pains in her muscles and joints in March 1973, that is, before the medical examination and had told Dr. Carroll in, 1982 that her illness for which she was referred to the Rheumatology Clinic in that year had begun 9 years previously, which again could have been before, or soon after the medical examination by Dr. Crowe.

In the light of this evidence it is not surprising that the Commissioner reached the conclusion which he did.

However, information which has become available since the Commissioner's decision throws considerable doubt on the accuracy of those statements.

Dr. Irvine, in his evidence indicated that the time at which the applicant had first noticed the muscle and joint pains as reported to him was almost certainly July 1973 after the medical examination and not March 1973, and Dr. Carroll in a subsequent letter resiled somewhat from his recollection of the applicant's estimate of the duration of her condition. In addition, there was independent medical evidence that the applicant's children had not suffered any arthritic complaint or rheumatism.

The applicant's evidence was that her children had had only the normal aches and pains of childhood, and that her parents who lived in the United Kingdom had, when both in their seventies, developed aches and pains which she attributed to a cold and damp climate.

Her understanding was that the use of the term "rheumatism" was suggested by Dr. Crowe as a broad term for aches and pains generally. Dr. Crowe was at first disinclined to accept this suggestion but later this passage occurs in his evidence:

"If someone in your normal practice, interviewing these applicants, said "I have aches and pains which come and go and sometimes I have not got them and sometimes I have and they seem to be worse in cold and damp times than they are other times" you would not write all that down, would you?---No.

What would you put?—We would put down query rheumatism as the common terminology, or rheumatic aches and pains."

Dr. Crowe also said that his physical examination would have involved at least the major joints of the body including knees and elbows and. that the absence of any note to the contrary indicates that he found no pain or restriction of movement in those joints.

Dr. Owen, the physician in charge of the Department of Rheumatology at Sir Charles Gairdner Hospital gave evidence of the relationship between psoriasis and polyarthritis:

"Perhaps I could now take you to a more general matter and that is the relationship between the various medical terms that are used fairly interchangeably in reports and in particular polyarthritis, psoriatic arthritis and synovitis. Would you be able to, in fairly simple terms, explain the relationship between those three?---Synovitis is the common denominator - that is the inflammation of the lining of the joint or the lubricating sheath surrounding tendons. Polyarthritis really means that there are many areas involved with the synovitis - arthritis and synovitis being interchangeable. Psoriatic arthritis is perhaps a lazy way of saying psoriatic polyarthritis.

Could you explain also the actual relationship between psoriasis and arthritis which gives rise to the term psoriatic arthritis?---Approximately 10 per cent of people who have psoriasis, which is a very common skin condition in that there is something in the vicinity of 10 per cent of the population who have psoriasis, can develop some form of associated musculoskeletal disease.

There is no doubt that it is related to her polyarthritis because of the distribution of her aches and pains. There is some doubt as to whether she ever had any spinal involvement because mild backaches are not infrequent in the population. The next statement is not in specific reference to the patient but more often than not, if there is an exacerbation of the synovitis or the spondylitis? They tend to go hand in hand. If, with treatment one can control the psoriasis adequately then often the arthritis becomes remarkably well controlled. The biggest problem is the effective agents in controlling psoriasis are all fairly toxic or have a high risk rate....

Would it be possible, with hindsight, to come to any findings by looking back at, say, an onset of psoriasis and say "Because there was an onset of psoriasis at a given date it follows more likely than not that there was also arthritis present"? Does that follow from what you just said?---No. Can I question you for a moment?

Certainly?---Just because someone gets psoriasis does not mean they are going to get arthritis. If someone develops psoriasis and then an arthritic complaint, one would have to consider both as possible things, but psoriasis can precede or present after the onset of the arthritis, so the patient may have arthritis but not develop the related psoriasis until months or two years later.

Can a person have psoriasis for some time and perhaps never develop arthritis?---Ninety per cent of people with psoriasis do not have arthritis. As I said, 10 per cent of the psoriatics, in round figures, depending upon the population surveyed - - -

(TRIBUNAL): Yes; but those who do, do not necessarily - tell me if I am right in this deduction from what you say - develop it after the first attack of psoriasis?---Not necessarily. People can have psoriasis for years before getting arthritis; people can develop both together, or a very close temporal relationship, or people can have a funny arthritis that we would sit and say "Look, this is bilateral, but it is asymmetrical; it is doing what a psoriatic arthritis should do" and eventually a confirmation of a skin condition of psoriasis can be found.

MR WHITHEAR: Doctor, what symptoms would you expect to find in one of these people who actually had psoriasis and then developed arthritis? What sort of symptoms would you be looking for which would make you say "That person is one of the unlucky 10 per cent"?---The arthritis associated with psoriasis is an inflammatory condition, so you are going to get - whatever joint or joints are involved, - symptoms of inflammation and if the joint is close enough to the surface you are going to have redness, swelling, pain, tenderness, loss of movement. ...

MR McKENNA: Doctor, we have heard evidence from the Commonwealth medical officer only a short time ago that when he examined. Ms Davy in May 1973 he examined her body and there was no swelling in the joints, no obvious or noted restriction of movement or anything like that. He did, however, note a history of rheumatism on the basis that he had been told that during her childhood she had suffered some aches and pains when engaging in energetic activities. That history of rheumatism, in light of the other findings of that examination, ie that there was no joint swelling or restriction of movement, would that suggest that the rheumatism was unrelated to the subsequent arthritis?---I believe so.

...Rheumatism to me is not a condition, but is a descriptive term describing aches in muscles and around joints; it is not a true condition.

MR McKENNA: If, at that examination in 1973, Ms Davy had any of the beginnings of the arthritis that she subsequently was diagnosed as having, would that more likely than not have showed up in the physical examination?---If severe enough. The point, I think, that would be important to know from that medical officer's report is if he recorded any symptoms. If there were no symptoms and he had no findings - - -

He said he examined her and there was definitely no swelling and no restriction?---One has to say there is nothing there. You can have mild symptoms and find it hard to find something, hut no symptoms, no signs, no case.

As I understand your evidence, you would think the history of rheumatism was more probably just aches and pains totally unrelated to the arthritis in view of the other factors?---Yes."

On balance we conclude firstly that "history of rheumatism" was intended to convey as the applicant asserts, the notion of temporary aches and pains occurring from time to time in various parts of the body, and which many people experience, and secondly, that having regard to the abovenamed concessions of Drs. Irvine, Carroll and Crowe, there was in May 1973 no unusual or recurring pain of any particular part of the body which can be identified as having been in any relevant sense connected with the psoriatic polyarthritis which subsequently developed.

The other condition specified was "ear infection" which for reasons we have given should be amended to read "right ear infection". Here the evidence of Dr. Irvine and Dr. Allison is crucial.

Dr. Allison specializes in dermatology. He saw the applicant on 13 October 1973 on referral from Dr. Irvine. He expressed the opinion that at that time the applicant had psoriasis on the scalp and in the ears. The evidence indicates that psoriasis is a recurring scaly eruption of the skin. Dr. Allison's evidence includes the following passage:

"Doctor, could you explain to the tribunal the basis of your diagnosis?---She had the classical lesions that could not be mistaken for any other condition in the scalp and in the ears and just around the ears. The lesions were completely typical. There was no question of any other possible diagnosis. She gave a family history of psoriasis, her mother having had the condition.

When you say it was in the ears, was it possible to see the psoriasis clearly? Was there any ear infection which could have prevented that?---No; there was no obvious ear infection but breaks in the skin related to psorasis are almost invariably infected. If one takes a culture, which one does not do because there is no relevance to the management of the case - she was not literally dripping pus, she just showed typical psoriasis in the ears, which I just assumed would be infected if one had cared to take a swab."

Under treatment the condition subsided and when Dr. Allison saw the applicant on 16 November 1973 he decided no further visit was necessary. He said the treatment was clearing up the lesions:

"...so much so that I did. not feel it was needful to give her a further appointment because I thought that if she carried on in the same way she had done over those few days there was no reason why she should not get herself completely right, bearing in mind that when it was right we had not cured her of the condition. It was purely a question of getting it under control because psoriasis cannot be cured - the tendency to psoriasis cannot be removed.

Is this a complaint that is likely to have remissions and flare-ups?-—Yes."

In his report of 14 October 1983 he said:

"1. The ear condition suffered by Mrs. Cuomo (the applicant's former married name) from 1971 to 1973. was described as otitis externa which simply means inflammation of the ear canal. Inflammation of the ear canal of a psoriatic subject almost invariably ends up, even if it does not begin, as psoriasis.

On the balance of probability, therefore, her ear condition was related to her scalp condition.

2. It is not possible to state definitely that the ear condition suffered by Mrs. Cuomo from 1971 to 1973 was in fact the same condition for which she was treated by me in October, 1973, i.e. psoriasis.

4. The infection noted by the C.M.O. on the right ear in May, 1973 could have been simply infection as stated or it could have been psoriasis or it could have been a combination of both.

At this stage one just cannot be certain.

Psoriatic subjects are prone to skin infection and this includes infection of the ear canal."

His evidence continues:

"---Psoriasis of the scalp looks different from psoriasis of the external ear canal butnevertheless the appearance is completely typical to a dermatologist. Although it doesnot look the same there as on the scalp, they are quite characteristic appearances. Do you want me to say how I reached that conclusion?

Yes, if you could?---There was parakeratotic scaling. Again, I cannot put that in simpler words. It is a characteristic type of scale that psoriasis subjects develop when they get inflamed. It does not develop quite as thickly in the ears as it does on the scalp, in which it can be heaped up and very thick indeed."

Speaking of the applicant's condition before he saw her, he said that on the balance of probability her ear condition had been psoriatic but that he could not say for certain, not having seen her.

We think his evidence is best understood by reference to the following passages:

"MR McKENNA: Doctor, that conclusion that you felt the ear condition in the past had been psoriatic, if that had been the case would there have been likely other signs of psoriasis at the time?---No, not necessarily. There could have been, or there could not have been.

As I think you understand, the essence of the issue before the tribunal is whether the ear infection noted by the Commonwealth medical officer in May 1973 was in fact psoriasis?---I cannot answer that question; I do not know. That question you cannot answer?---—No. I said on the balance of probability it was, but I cannot give a definite answer. On the balance of probability, if you want me to give the balance of probability as I see it, it was psoriatic.

What do you understand by the term balance of probability?---What anybody with any common sense accepts as balance of probability.

I think the way it is understood in medicolegal terms and the way it is to be dealt with in this case is is it more likely than not?---Yes.

Psoriasis?---yes.

Why do you say that it was more likely than not?---From experience of seeing patients who have had similar conditions on and off for years and a psoriatic subject is particularly prone to skin infection. Quite a common place to get a skin infection is in the ears. You only have to get a bit of an itch and scratch and it turns into psoriasis, which becomes infected.

Could it not equally possibly have been something else.---Of course it could. I am not saying what it was because I did not see it.

This lady has given evidence that at the time she saw the CMO, or just shortly before, she had been swimming and had her head under the water. Is it possible that exposure of the ear canal to water could have caused the infection?---It is possible, yes.It is possible it caused the psoriasis to be triggered off.

Is there any particular appearance of psoriasis that makes it recognisable as distinct from just a simple ear infection? ---Yes. There is a characteristic parakeratotic scale.

So if she had psoriasis when she was seen by the CMO, one would have expected that scale to have been there?---If she had psoriasis?

At that time?---Yes; unless it was masked by a secondary infection, which it could have been.

(TRIBUNAL): In the scalp? It could have been masked by a secondary infection?---No, it would be difficult for a secondary infection to mask psoriasis in the scalp. But in the ear canal if there is a lot of pus one could say "If she is dripping pus let's clear that up" and once the actual frank pus had been got rid of then one could see the characteristic appearance of the underlying predisposing psoriatic change.

MR McKENNA: Doctor, the CMO gave evidence yesterday that whilst he noted an ear infection it was not one that was oozing pus or anything because he would have noted that and it may well have just been a very simple one?-—Yes.

Knowing that, would that alter your opinion at all about the probability of that being psoriasis?---I would say it was probably psoriasis, if you ask me for my opinion on what it probably was. But I cannot say it was or it was not because I have no evidence. If you say "In your experience dealing with patients referred by other doctors who are not skin specialists," I would say there had been psoriasis, but I have no evidence for that other than experience.

And the hindsight that she is a person who is a psoriatic subject?---Yes. She has been a psoriatic subject since the day she was conceived and she will always be a psoriatic subject.

Could I put it this way: if you were being asked a question about a person who was not a diagnosed psoriatic subject?---By whom? Was not diagnosed by what - a skin specialist or a GP?

By a skin specialist; and you were just given that history, that she had an ear infection. Would you in that circumstance?---Any skin specialist would have picked it up if it was psoriasis."

In this passage he draws a clear and proper distinction between what he considers certain, probable and possible, which removes any doubt as to his meaning when he says that on the balance of probability the ear condition in May 1973 was related to the scalp condition.

As to the scalp condition, Dr. Allison was careful to say that psoriasis would have been diagnosed by a dermatologist.

That the condition was probably psoriasis appears to be confirmed by Dr. Irvine, himself a general practitioner, who in his report of 4 March 1983 said that the applicant had attended with a. rash in the scalp "which was primarily treated as a dandruff but did not respond well to this treatment and was eventually referred to Dr. Allison". (our emphasis)

His records show that the diagnosis of dandruff was first made on 14 November 1972 in respect of "an irritation of the scalp". It is not suggested that the scalp condition, as then observed by Dr. Irvine, was any different from the condition for which he referred the applicant to Dr. Allison on 30 October 1973. Indeed, in a later report, Dr. Irvine having said that there was "really nothing in her past history which correlates or prognosticates" her later condition added a postscript:

"P.S. In October 1973 a diagnosis of dandruff was made. This did not respond well to treatment and it is only in the light of much more subsequent events that the probability of its being a form of psoriasis emerges."

The date in this note appears to be incorrect. According to his evidence he diagnosed and gave treatment for dandruff in November 1982. October 1983 was when Dr. Allison diagnosed psoriasis.

Dr. Allison's opinion was that a medical practitioner who. was not a skin specialist might not have diagnosed psoriasis, that the applicant had psoriasis on the scalp and in the ears when he first saw her, and that the infection of the ear noted in May 1973 was on the balance of probabilities psoriatic.

The principal facts as we find them may be briefly stated as follows.

The applicant was always a psoriatic subject, that is, one more likely to develop psoriasis, a recurring identifiable skin disease, than a person who is not a psoriatic subject. The ear infection from which she suffered on 24 May 1973 was on the probabilities of psoriatic origin. One person in ten who develops psoriasis develops psoriatic polyarthritis, a clearly identifiable form of arthritis, which may result in substantial physical incapacity. The applicant is one of the ten percent of psoriasis sufferers who has developed psoriatic polyarthritis which caused the physical incapacity leading to her retirement.

The type of arthritis which the applicant developed, psoriatic arthritis, is suffered only by a person having psoriasis and is different from other types of arthritis.

It is not established that the applicant had any clinically demonstrable symptom of psoriatic polyarthritis at the time of the medical examination by Dr. Crowe. She did, however, as mentioned above, have a psoriatic infection of the right ear which was described by Dr. Crowe as "ear infection".

Where psoriasis and psoriatic polyarthritis afflict a person, the onset of psoriatic polyarthritis may precede or follow the onset of psoriasis or both conditions may commence together.

Where both conditions exist together in the one person, then more often than not, if there is an exacerbation of the psoriasis there is an exacerbation of the psoriatic polyarthritis. If with treatment one can control the psoriasis adequately, then often the psoriatic polyarthritis becomes well controlled.

The relevant conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board had been not satisfied that the applicant's health and fitness justified her acceptance to the Superannuation Fund rather than to the Provident Account were a history of rheumatism, ear infection and overweight.

We have already said that in our view the relevant incapacity is the loss of physical mobility and function and that this incapacity was caused by, or substantially contributed to, by the psoriatic polyarthritis. No condition described as psoriasis arthritis or synovitis or by any similar term, was mentioned in the benefit classification certificate, but "ear infection" is.

The condition "ear infection" existed on 24 and 31 May 1973 when the applicant was examined and on the evidence of Dr. Allison, and in the absence of any suggestion to the contrary it probably existed on 20 June 1973 when she became a contributor to the Provident. Account. The evidence does not establish, however, that on any of those dates the condition of psoriatic polyarthritis existedi. The question then is whether the psoriatic polyarthritis which did not exist clinically when the applicant became a contributor to the Provident Account can be said to be "connected with" the condition of the ear infection which did exist when the applicant became a contributor.

Apparently, if Dr Crowe with some perception of the future had specified psoriatic polyarthritis as a ground for deferment, this could not now be accepted as a relevant condition because it did not then exist. (S184(7)(iii) of the Act). Is a different result obtained by saying that the condition which did not then exist, namely, psoriatic polyarthritis is "connected with" the right ear infection the condition which did exist?

Some assistance is to be obtained from previous decisions of the Tribunal such as Vaughan 2ALN N36, Burr 3ALN N1, Brown 3ALD 185 and Hegadues 4 ALN N 139,and in the Federal Court, McMullen (unreported 16 April 1985 ACT) but it seems to us that the present application raises a different problem.

The relevant condition specified in the benefit classification certificate was "ear infection" and we have already said that the ear infection seen in May 1973 was psoriatic. On this basis it can be said that the condition of psoriasis was "connected with" with ear infection. It existed at the same time as the ear infection which was a manifestation of it.

The psoriatic polyarthritis which subsequently developed was "connected with" the psoriasis in that it is a particular type of arthritis suffered only by about one tenth of all persons having psoriasis and not suffered by anyone else.

When the psoriatic polyarthritis is connected with the psoriasis and the psoriasis is connected with the ear infection, is the psoriatic polyarthritis connected in the relevant sense with the ear infection? If so, how far can the chain of possible connections be extended? There may be for instance some further condition or conditions which a small proportion of psoriatic polyarthritis sufferers might develop, which would cause a total incapacity when a mild condition of psoriatic polyarthritis does not, and, it should be noted, the possible length of the chain might be increased by the use in the benefit classification certificate of general descriptions such as "ear infection" which could presumably be a symptom of many medical conditions.

Some of the difficulty is in our view eased if one appreciates when it is that the final decision is to be made. Although the benefit classification certificate for reasons which Counsel explained is not ordinarily issued until retirement, the medical basis for a decision which is to be made when the contributor becomes a contributor, is determined by reference to what is known of the contributor's health and fitness at the time contributions commence.

On the other hand, a decision whether the incapacity leading to retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by a condition connected with a condition specified in the benefit classification certificate, is clearly to be made after retirement of the contributor.

If one surveys the problem from that point, it transpires that the ear infection seen at the medical examination in May 1973 was a manifestation of psoriasis which then existed. In due course the applicant developed psoriatic polyarthritis, a condition which can be developed only by a sufferer of psoriasis, and finally, the psoriatic polyarthritis became so severe as to restrict the applicant's ordinary activities, to an extent which made her unable to engage in her employment.

We reach the conclusion after much consideration that the connection between the psoriatic polyarthritis and the ear infection specified in the benefit classification certificate is real and demonstrable and a link which is sustained on a medical basis (Burr, supra. Hegadues, supra.) and therefore one to which the relevant section applies.

We would therefore amend the benefit classification certificate by amending "ear infection" to read "right ear infection" and subject to that amendment we would confirm the decision under review.

(Sgd.) Gresley Clarkson

Presiding Member
