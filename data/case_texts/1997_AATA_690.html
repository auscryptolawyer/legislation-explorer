Mr L and Commissioner of Taxation [1997] AATA 690 (20 March 1997)

Last Updated: 5 January 2009





Administrative

Appeals

Tribunal



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION No 11706



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL)

N° VT96/80-83

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION)





Applicant



COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal: Mr W.G. McLean, Member



Date: 20 March 1997



Place: Melbourne



Decision: The decisions under review in respect of the years ended 30 June 1989, 1990 and 1991 are affirmed.



(sgd) W.G. McLean

Member

INCOME TAX - director/shareholder of garment pressing company - cheques received from customers of the company converted to cash - failure to include as assessable income - whether a deemed dividend pursuant to s.108 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - whether assessment excessive - penalty tax - onus of proof - decisions affirmed



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss.44(1), 108(1), 227(3)



Taxation Administration Act 1963 s.14ZZK(b)



Martin v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 93 ATC 5200



D and Commissioner of Taxation (Decision N° 11,564, delivered 24 January 1997)



REASONS FOR DECISION



20 March 1997 Mr W.G. McLean, Member



1. This is an application by Mr L, the taxpayer, for review of decisions of the Commissioner of Taxation to disallow objections against an assessment of income tax issued to him for the years ended 30 June 1989, 1990, 1991 and 1992. Similar applications by FH Pty Ltd in respect of the years ended 30 June 1988, 1989, 1990, 1991 and 1992 were withdrawn.



2. At the hearing Mr F. Farrow, of counsel, appeared for the applicant and Ms A. Richards, of counsel, appeared for the respondent. Mr L gave oral evidence. Ms Jasper, an accountant and taxation auditor, employed by the Australian Taxation Office (“ATO”) also gave oral evidence at the request of the applicant's advisers. Mr Dinh Luong acted as the interpreter to assist the applicant.



3. The Tribunal received into evidence the documents lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and other documentary exhibits tendered during the hearing.



4. At the commencement of the hearing the parties advised the Tribunal of the status of the following issues contended by the applicant:



  1. The following contentions of the applicant are agreed to by the respondent:

    • the applicant held a 50% shareholding in FHPL (“the company”) during the year of income ended 30 June 1989

    • the applicant increased his shareholding from 50% to 67% in the company during the year of income ended 30 June 1989 and continued to hold such 67% shareholding in the company at all material times thereafter

    • at all material times that the applicant held 50% of the shareholding in the company, the other 50% of the shareholding of the company was held by Mr H

    • at all material times that the applicant held 67% of the shareholding in the company, the other 33% of the shareholding in the company was held by Mr D

    • the respondent is not privy to the manufacturing process undertaken by the company but does not object to the applicant's contention that the company was at all material times involved in garment pressing

    • at all material times, the applicant besides having a shareholding in the company, was also employed by the company as a garment presser/labourer

    • the respondent has asserted that the company failed to include in its assessable income for the 1989 year $63,700.00 referrable to the value of “laundered cheques” and accordingly deemed a dividend of $31,850.00 in relation thereto to the applicant for the 1989 year pursuant to s.108 of the Act

    • the respondent has asserted that the company failed to include in its assessable income for the 1990 year $88,136.00 referrable to the value of “laundered cheques” and accordingly deemed a dividend of $57,685.00 in relation thereto to the applicant for the 1990 year pursuant to s.108 of the Act

    • the respondent has asserted that the company failed to include in its assessable income for the 1991 year $12,367.00 referrable to the value of ‘laundered cheques and accordingly deemed a dividend of $8697.00 in relation thereto to the applicant for the 1991 year pursuant to s.108 of the Act

    • an amended assessment is to be issued by the respondent in relation to the year ended 30 June 1992 disclosing that no deemed dividend has been paid to the applicant during that income tax year.

  2. The following contentions of the applicant are disputed by the respondent

    • at all material times when Mr H was a shareholder in the company Mr H had total control of the company including but not limited to the administrative and managerial aspects of the company. Similarly, at all material times Mr D was a shareholder in the company, Mr D had total control of the company including but not limited to the administrative and managerial aspects of the company

    • at all material times Mr L had no participation in the day to day decision-making of the company in relation to its administration and management or at all

    • at all material times Mr L’s involvement with the company on a day to day basis was as an employee and as such he acted solely in accordance with the directions and instructions given to him by either Mr H or Mr D as the case may have been

    • but for salary and wages disclosed in his income tax returns for the years ended 30 June 1989, 1990, 1991 and 1992 the applicant received no other assessable income for the purposes of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”) either directly or indirectly from the company for the 1989, 1990, 1991 an 1992 years

    • during the 1989, 1990, 1991 an 1992 years the applicant did not receive directly or indirectly any advances or loans from the company

    • at all material times the applicant relied upon his registered tax agent to prepare his income tax returns in strict compliance with the Act including but not limited to ensuring that all income known by the applicant's registered tax agent to have been assessable during any year of income to the applicant be included in the income tax return of the applicant for any such year of income and believed that this had been done at all material times.

5. The respondent's contentions are as follows:



  • the taxable income of the applicant is as follows:

1989 year of income - $37,561.00

1990 year of income - $71,807.00

1991 year of income - $21,672.00



  • during the years of income ended 30 June 1989, 1990 and 1991 the company paid or credited an amount of $31,850.00, $57,685.00 and $8,697.00 respectively, on behalf of, or for the individual benefit of the applicant which amount, in the opinion of the respondent, represents a distribution of profits and is therefore deemed to be a dividend paid by the company to the applicant by operation of s.108 of the Act.

  • the respondent correctly imposed additional tax pursuant to s.223(1) of the Act and the applicant is not entitled to any remission thereof under s.227(3) of the Act.

LEGISLATION



6. The Act provides:



“ 108 (1) If a private company:



(a) pays an amount to an associated person by way of an advance or loan; or



(b) pays or credits an amount on behalf of, or for the individual benefit of, an associated person;



so much (if any) of the amount paid or credited as, in the opinion of the Commissioner, represents a distribution of profits shall, for the purposes of this Act other than Division 11A of Part III and Division 4 of Part VI, be deemed to be a dividend paid by the company:



(c) to the associated person as a shareholder in the company;



(d) out of profits derived by the company; and



(e) on the last day of the year of income of the company in which the payment or credit referred to in paragraph (a) or (b) is made.”



“ 44 (1) The assessable income of a shareholder in a company (whether the company is a resident or a non-resident) shall, subject to this section and to section 128D–



(a) if he is a resident - include dividends paid to him by the company out of profits derived by it from any source; and



(b) if he is non-resident - include dividends paid to him by the company to the extent to which they are paid out of profits derived by it from sources in Australia.”



7. Mr L came to Australia from Vietnam in 1981. He said that his parents presently continue to reside in Vietnam and that his father is a wealthy businessman engaged in the shipping industry.



8. On 10 July 1989 Mr L was appointed a director of the company, a company engaged in the garment pressing industry, at the invitation of his brother-in-law Mr H. Mr L gave evidence that he worked as a supervisor in the factory and his brother-in-law Mr H continued to run the business and was responsible for all administrative matters such as fixing customer prices, banking, opening of mail and payment of salaries.



9. The Tribunal was provided with a sample bundle of cheques drawn by a customer of the company and made payable to the company. These cheques, which were paid by the customer in return for pressing of garments, were endorsed over to a cheque launderer named Paul Tjia Financial Services. Cheque laundering is a process where cheques are converted to cash through an arrangement with an external party. The external party has the cheques endorsed payable to him and he then cashes the cheques.



10. While Mr L is not disputing that the company did not receive the total amount of cash resulting from the cheque laundering activity as disclosed during an income tax audit of the company, he is denying that he received any of the moneys produced from the cheque laundering activity. Mr L gave evidence that he only received from the company a salary of $300 per week before tax and did not receive any other payment.



11. Mr L gave evidence that between 1989 and 1991 he purchased land at East Keilor for about $80,000. The purchase was funded from Mr L’s past savings of $20,000 and the remaining $60,000 was borrowed. Mr L borrowed $10,000 from his father and the remainder from a bank.

12. In August 1992 Mr L and his wife purchased a property situated at 96 S Street, Richmond which they presently occupy as their residence and to operate a restaurant. The price paid for the property was $237,500 and shortly thereafter Mr L fitted out the restaurant at an additional cost of $30,000, increasing the total outgoings in respect of the property to $267,500.



13. In May 1992 the Westpac Banking Corporation (“the Bank”) agreed to lend to Mr L and his wife a total sum of $140,000 in respect of their acquisition of the Richmond property. The security for the Bank loan was a registered mortgage over both the Richmond property and the East Keilor land. The loan facility of $140,000 was made up of a housing loan of $70,000, repayable at $686 per month. A bridging loan of $70,000 was repayable at $700 per month or within 12 months following the sale of the East Keilor land. Mr L gave evidence that he sold the East Keilor land in 1992 for $75,000 and repaid the Bank. He also renegotiated with the Bank to repay the remaining housing loan at $1000 per month. Mr L contends that he borrowed $10,000 from friends and his father sent him some money from Vietnam to assist with the purchase of the property. Mr L and his wife presently reside at the Richmond property and work in the restaurant for a combined salary of $300 per week.



14. The Tribunal finds that Mr L’s explanation is inadequate concerning the funding of the $267,500 total cost of the Richmond property. At the time of acquiring the Richmond property Mr L owned land in East Keilor in which he had an initial $20,000 equity interest as he borrowed the remainder of the purchase price. Mr L gave evidence that during the period that he owned the East Keilor land he received a salary of $300 per week and did not derive income from any other source. Accordingly, Mr L did not have the financial capacity to materially reduce the total amount that he borrowed to purchase the East Keilor land during his period of ownership. Mr L incurred a $5000 loss when he sold the land in 1992. An additional $70,000 bridging loan was repayable to the Bank following the sale of the East Keilor land. The matters that were not explained by Mr L concern whether he repaid the loan on the East Keilor land and if so where did he obtain the funds. Alternatively, if Mr L did not repay or substantially reduce the $50,000 original bank loan on the East Keilor land he would have received a net $25,000 when he sold the land in 1992 for $75,000. It remains unexplained to the satisfaction of the Tribunal how Mr L paid for the Richmond property costing $267,500. Initially, he borrowed $140,000 from the Bank plus $60,000 from his parents and $10,000 from friends totalling $210,000. When he sold the East Keilor land in 1992 and received a net cash equity amount of $25,000, he was required to repay the Bank the $70,000 bridging loan, which formed part of the $140,000 total bank loan.



15. Mr L sold his interest in the Richmond property to his wife for $20,000. The certificate of title for the property shows that a caveat was lodged in the name of his wife as purchaser fee simple on 13 May 1994. Mr L provided the Tribunal with a copy of a mortgage over the Richmond property given by him and his wife to Mr L’s parents as security for their $60,000 loan. The mortgage, which has a commencement date of 5 August 1992, is dated 3 March 1994. The certificate of title for the property indicates that a caveat was registered in respect of the mortgage on 26 May 1994 and shows Mr L’s parents as mortgagees. Mr L was unable to explain why the caveat dated 26 May 1994 describes Mr L and his wife as caveator when Mr L disposed of his interest the property on 13 May 1994. Also he did not provide any satisfactory explanation why the mortgage, signed and registered in March 1994, commenced in August 1992.



16. Mr L confirmed that he had received a letter dated 31 January 1994 from the ATO indicating that it appeared that the company may have understated its gross income for the years ending 30 June 1988 to 30 June 1992. Mr L contended that shortly after “receiving some papers from the ATO pertaining to his personal case” he appointed Strauss and Associates, barristers and solicitors, and Mr Yianni, a tax agent, as advisors and to act on his behalf in relation to the tax audit.



17. It is noted that clause 5 of the notice of objection against the ATO amended assessments, which is signed by Mr L and dated 18 May 1995, states that “the funds received by Mr L which the Tax Office class as ‘deemed dividends’ were received only by Mr L as an agent for the Company”. On page 5 of the notice of objection it is stated that “funds provided to L do not form any part of profits in any sense of the word as all funds provided were used to pay operating expenses incurred by F” [the company]. Also on page 5 it states that “the act of cashing cheques and paying company expenses in cash is not prohibited at law. As L received no benefit in doing so, it cannot be construed that his actions constituted a ‘Profit Making Scheme or Undertaking’ for the purposes of section 25(a) or any other section of the Act”. Also on page 5 it states “the Commissioner is clearly wrong in his calculation of the amended assessment as he has chosen to deny F or L any deduction for at least the commission paid to the account for cashing the cheques, an expense the Commissioner is fully aware of”. Mr L confirmed that he signed his notice of objection with appropriate legal and accounting advice but could not explain the reason behind his inclusion of the above references in his notice of objection.



18. Counsel for the respondent provided the Tribunal with a bundle of pay in slips relating to the company throughout the period 30 June 1988 to 30 June 1991. Mr L agreed that he had personally signed all of the pay in slips and had at times prepared some of them in his own handwriting.



19. Mr L was the public officer of the company for the financial years ending 30 June 1989, 1990, 1991 and 1992 and signed the company income tax returns for each of those years. The tax return for the year ending 30 June 1990 shows the money launderer Mr Paul Tjia to be the tax agent for the company which is contrary to Mr L’s earlier evidence that he had no involvement or personal dealings with Mr Tjia.



20. An application was made by Mr L in his capacity as a director of the company to the Australian Securities Commission dated 30 January 1996 to deregister the company. The application incorrectly stated that the company had not traded since July 1990 when the company had in fact traded until 1992. The company is now in liquidation with the appointment of a liquidator on 20 November 1996.



21. Ms Jasper gave evidence that she audited the company and after finishing the audit raised deemed dividends on the individual directors of the company, including Mr L. An audit report which forms part of the s.37 documents (the “T” documents) (T3.1 to T3.7) is in respect of the years 1988 to 1992. The audit report which is signed by Ms Jasper and is dated 21 August 1995, indicates that as the company is no longer trading and has no assets, it is not expected to pay the outstanding assessments.



22. The total amount of the laundered cheques is not in dispute nor are the details of the shareholdings of the company during the years of income ending June 30, 1989, 1990 and 1991. What is known is that the laundered cheques were unaccounted for by the company. No books or records of the company were produced and there is no evidence where the cash proceeds from the laundered cheques went.



23. The Tribunal finds that it is open to the Commissioner on the facts known to draw an inference that the applicant as a director and shareholder of the company received a share of the moneys that are unaccounted for by the company and that such a share should be the percentage of the applicant's shareholding held in the company for the relative tax periods. Such amounts shall represent a deemed dividend to Mr L out of the profits of the company in respect of the income years ending 30 June 1989, 1990 and 1991. The fact that the applicant’s tax return included salary and wages is not, in the circumstances, conclusive of anything else.

24. The applicant carries an onus of proof: sub-section 14ZZK(b) of the Taxation Administration Act 1963. The Tribunal is satisfied that the taxpayer has not discharged that onus. There has not been put to the Tribunal any facts which might suggest that the inference drawn from the known facts were not open to be made. Based upon the evidence, the Tribunal finds that the role performed by Mr L as an employee, director and public officer of the company embraced administrative decisions and banking functions, and was not limited to acting solely as a factory supervisor and garment pressing employee.



25. In Martin v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 93 ATC 5200 Davies J after referring to the judgment of Brennan J in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco 90 ATC 4088 (at 4090-4091) said at 5203:



“ It follows that the task of the Court on an appeal from a decision on an objection is to determine whether the taxpayer has satisfied the burden of proof of showing that the amount of taxable income assessed is excessive, a burden which the taxpayer cannot dispel merely by showing that there was an error in the assessment. The taxpayer must go on to establish what is the amount which should be substituted therefor.”



26. On the evidence and material before the Tribunal, the applicant has not provided any facts which would permit a finding to be made that the amended assessments for the financial periods ended 30 June 1989, 1990 and 1991 are excessive or what should be the correct amounts.



27. Turning to the question of penalties, the Tribunal is unable to detect any grounds to justify a remission of additional tax below the 50 per cent imposed by the Commissioner.



28. Accordingly, the decisions under review in respect of the years ended 30 June 1989, 1990 and 1991 are affirmed.

OF Mr W.G. McLean, Member



(sgd) Catherine Thomas
