Re Anthony Edward Kemp v Commissioner of Taxation [1992] FCA 478; (1992) 24 Atr 75 / (1992) 92 Atc 4542 (1992) 110 ALR 375 (24 September 1992)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: ANTHONY EDWARD KEMP

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. N G808 of 1991

FED No. 713

Income Tax

[1992] FCA 478; (1992) 24 ATR 75 / (1992) 92 ATC 4542

[1992] FCA 478; (1992) 110 ALR 375

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Hill J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - deductibility - legal fees incurred by professional footballer with contract to play in Australia to obtain release from restraint of trade imposed by New Zealand football authorities - whether temporal connexion between outgoing and income earning activity - whether expenditure "incidental and relevant" to income earning activity - whether outgoing of capital nature.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth): s.51(1).

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Maddalena (1971) 45 ALJR 426, distinguished.

Ronpibon Tin NL v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at 56-7, applied.

Hallstroms Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634 at 647-8, applied.

Sun Newspapers Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337 at 359-363, applied.

Foley Brothers Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner Taxation (1965) 13 ATD 562 at 563, applied.

BP Australia Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1966) AC 224 at 267, applied.

HEARING

SYDNEY

24:9:1992

Counsel and Solicitors B.J. Gross QC with B.F. Rowe

for Applicant: instructed by Rutter Morgan

Solicitors for the Respondent: The Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

1. The application be dismissed.

2. The applicant to pay the respondent's costs.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

The applicant, Mr Anthony Kemp, a professional footballer, appeals against the decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal, constituted by a Deputy President, which affirmed the disallowance of Mr Kemp's objection to the assessment by the respondent, the Commissioner of Taxation, of his income tax for the year of income ending 30 June 1989. At issue between the parties is the deductibility of an amount of $49,686, comprising legal costs incurred by Mr Kemp in that year of income and an amount of $303 for interest and bank charges incurred by Mr Kemp in borrowing to fund the payment of the legal costs. No different question arises in respect of these two kinds of outgoings and I shall henceforth deal solely with the legal expenses. Mr Kemp claims these amounts to be deductible under the provisions of s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act").

2. Mr Kemp was born in New Zealand. After representing his country in a New Zealand School Boys rugby league side in Australia, he played semi-professionally in New Zealand until 1987. Between seasons he also played in the United Kingdom for the Junior Kiwis. At the end of 1987, Mr Kemp signed a contract to play in Australia with the Newcastle Knights, one of the teams participating in the Winfield Cup competition conducted by the New South Wales Rugby League. That initial contract was for a period of one year covering the 1988 football season.

3. Neither in New Zealand, nor in Australia, is there free competition in the employment market for professional rugby league players. Indeed, as I observed in Adamson v NSW Rugby League Ltd [1991] FCA 9; (1991) 27 FCR 535 at 542:

"The history of professional sport, both in

Australia and overseas, reveals a tendency to

regulation in ways which interfere with the

freedom of players to contract."

4. In New Zealand, the attempt to regulate the sport of rugby league in 1988 took the form of an embargo upon New Zealand players playing in Australia unless they had participated in a certain number of tests or overseas tours and then only upon payment of a transfer fee. The rules of the New South Wales Rugby League required a player to be registered with the league before that player could play for a club in the Winfield Cup Competition. Such registration could not be effected, however, in the case of a New Zealand player, unless the New Zealand Rugby Football League (the "New Zealand League") consented to the registration. There was an exception to the New Zealand embargo, of which Mr Kemp availed himself in the 1988 season, namely that clearance might be given to young footballers ("rookies") to play a season in Australia.

5. Mr Kemp, having played the season in Australia, wished to stay and continue playing for the Newcastle Knights. Indeed, this had been his original intention, notwithstanding the obtaining of what was only a one year clearance. He had discussions with the Newcastle Knights concerning a contract for the next season. His advice from lawyers was that the New Zealand rules were unenforceable as an unreasonable restraint of trade and that there was, in the result, no legal impediment to obtaining a clearance from the New Zealand authorities. He negotiated a new contract with the Newcastle Knights for the 1989 season, containing an option to the club on his services for a further 12 months. This contract was entered into in August 1988. No copy of it was in evidence as, apparently, no copy could be located. The Tribunal found, however, that the contract had been entered into. A letter from the President of the Club to the club's solicitors dated 15 March 1989, which assumed some significance to the submissions made in argument, said:

"As President of the Newcastle Knights I confirm

that the Knights and Kemp have signed a contract

guaranteeing Kemp a sign-on fee plus weekly

match payments. The contract is signed subject

to the granting of a clearance to Kemp by the

NZRL Board and the registration of Kemp as a

player by the New South Wales Rugby League.

I advise that Kemp has directed the Newcastle

Knights to pay portion (sic) of the sign-on fee

to meet his legal costs due and owing to his New

Zealand Lawyers...

Any effort that you could make to have the New

Zealand case finalised so that we the Knights

can press the New South Wales Rugby League to

register Tony Kemp as a player would be greatly

appreciated."

6. The contract took the form of a standard form contract approved by the New South Wales Rugby League under which Mr Kemp agreed when and wherever reasonably required to play rugby league for the Newcastle Knights in such team and grade as the club should specify. Mr Kemp was obliged as well to participate inter alia in training sessions and promotional activities for the club. For its part the club agreed to remunerate Mr Kemp by the payment of match fees as well as a "playing fee". The Tribunal made no findings as to the terms of the contract which probably included the provision of a car and the undertaking by the club to find him employment.

7. Prior to the signing of this contract, Mr Hill, the solicitor for the club as well as the solicitor for and a friend of Mr Kemp, had written on Mr Kemp's behalf to the Chairman of the New Zealand League requesting that Mr Kemp be given a release to enable him to remain in Australia on a long term basis. Following further correspondence, from which it became clear that no clearance would be forthcoming, Mr Kemp commenced proceedings in New Zealand, seeking a judicial declaration that the refusal to grant a clearance was unlawful in that it was made pursuant to a provision of the League's constitution and by-laws which constituted a restraint of trade which was unreasonable and therefore contrary to public policy. He was successful in these proceedings. The judgment in his favour of Henry J of the High Court of New Zealand is reported as Kemp v NZ Rugby Football League Inc (1989) 3 NZLR 463.

8. Mr Kemp assumed, it may be inferred, that thereafter he would have no difficulty being registered by the New South Wales Rugby League. Indeed, he was about to go onto the field to play in the Challenge Cup competition, played by Newcastle against the reigning premiers each year, when he was advised that the New Zealand League had refused to give the clearance he had requested, despite the court decision in Mr Kemp's favour and that accordingly the New South Wales Rugby League would not register him. He was pulled out of the game not longer than forty minutes before kick-off. There were subsequent negotiations with the New Zealand League, in which Mr Kemp was apparently represented by a solicitor. Ultimately he was granted the necessary clearance as a result of terms of settlement reached between the parties. The Newcastle Knights paid a transfer fee for him and there was an agreement that each party to the dispute should bear his or its own costs. There is no finding as to the precise terms of the clearance granted, but it was apparently at least for the period of Mr Kemp's contract with the Newcastle Knights as entered into on 14 April 1989, more or less contemporaneously with the settlement.

9. The contract signed on 14 April 1989 was entered into by Mr Kemp essentially because the contract he had originally signed was inadequate to enable him to pay the legal costs which he had by then incurred. These costs had considerably exceeded the figure he had expected. So he renegotiated with the Newcastle Knights a contract which was for a period terminating on 30 November 1995. The Tribunal found that the financial rewards to Mr Kemp under this contract were considerably greater than under the preceding contract. Further, the contract was for a term of three years with two one year options thereafter capable of being exercised by the club.

10. There was no evidence before the Tribunal as to the make up of the legal expenses which Mr Kemp had incurred. Particularly it is not known how much of the expenses related to the legal proceedings in New Zealand to have the court declare the rules of the New Zealand League invalid and, if it be relevant, how much of the expenses related to the subsequent negotiations or steps taken which led to the settlement between Mr Kemp and the New Zealand League and the granting of clearance to play with the Newcastle Knights.

11. The Tribunal found that the outgoings for legal fees were incurred before the income earning activity of Mr Kemp commenced and were thus non-deductible having regard to the decision of Menzies J in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Maddalena (1971) 45 ALJR 426, 71 ATC 4161. The expenditure came at a point too soon to be properly regarded as having been incurred in gaining assessable income. It was incurred to gain employment, rather than incurred in preserving and securing a then current employment and accordingly lacked the requisite temporal connection with the derivation of income required by s.51(1).

12. In the alternative, the Tribunal was of the view that the legal costs had the hallmarks of capital. The expenditure, in the words of the Tribunal was:

"a `once only' sum paid to secure a lasting

advantage, a structural asset, namely the right

to play remunerative football in New South

Wales. It therefore falls, in any event, within

the exception contained in s.51(1)."

13. For Mr Kemp it was submitted that the Tribunal was in error in treating the present facts as analogous to the facts in Maddalena. Rather, it was said, the present was a case where the relevant income producing activity had commenced. That activity involved, so it was submitted, Mr Kemp being available pursuant to the contract entered into by him for the 1989 season and fulfilling all the requirements of him under that contract in return for which he received and was entitled to receive benefit.

14. The taxpayer in Maddalena was, like Mr Kemp, a professional footballer. He incurred travelling and legal expenses in connection with seeking and obtaining a contract with a new club to play football and it was these expenses which were held by the full High Court not to be deductible. Menzies J, with whose judgment Barwick C.J., Windeyer and Owen JJ. agreed, contrasted the position of an employee and a person who is in fact carrying on his own business. Expenditure by an employee in obtaining employment is incurred "in getting, not in doing work". In contrast, expenditure by a businessman in obtaining contracts would be properly regarded as an outgoing of a continuing business.

15. Barwick C.J. added (at 426):

"The cost to an employee of obtaining his

employment does not form an outgoing incurred in

the course of earning the wages payable in the

employment. That cost is not deductible under

s.51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1967."

16. With respect to the Deputy President, I do not think that it is correct to regard the present case as one where the expenditure lacked a temporal connection with the income producing activity, in the sense used in Maddalena. On the facts found, Mr Kemp had entered into a contract with the Club, prior to incurring the legal expenses. The expenditure was not incurred in obtaining employment, because Mr Kemp had already obtained that employment. It is true that some of the evidence suggested that the contract between Mr Kemp and the club was conditional upon his securing the release from the New Zealand League and becoming registered with the New South Wales Rugby League. The letter of 15 March 1989, set out earlier, is quite explicit on the matter. That is what one would have expected. Were it not so, there would need to be implied in the contract, to give it business efficacy, a term that Mr Kemp would do all things necessary to obtain registration with the New South Wales Rugby League and that in the event that he failed to do so within a reasonable time, the contract could be brought to an end. An interesting legal question could well have arisen as to whether the Newcastle Knights were in fact obliged to pay anything to Mr Kemp until this condition was satisfied, or, for that matter, whether Mr Kemp was obliged to participate in training etc until this happened. Oral evidence of a solicitor was led before the Tribunal of his understanding as to the effect of the contract. This evidence was not helpful and no finding was made on the matter by the Tribunal.

17. The Commissioner submitted that, even if Maddalena was distinguishable, the present was not a case where the outgoings incurred were, within the language of the cases, "incidental and relevant" to the income earning activities of Mr Kemp. These were, so it was argued, playing football. Reference was made to what I had said in Commissioner of Taxation v Cooper [1991] FCA 164; (1991) 99 ALR 703 at 723, yet another case involving a professional footballer and his claim (held by majority to be unsuccessful) to deduct expenditure incurred by him on food and drink consumed in addition to the expenditure which he ordinarily would incur on sustenance.

18. The principles applicable to the interpretation of s.51(1) of the Act have been conveniently collected in the judgment of Lockhart J in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Total Holdings (Aust) Pty Ltd (1979) 43 FLR 217, and in the unanimous judgment of the full Court of this Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Riverside Road Lodge Pty Ltd (in liq) [1990] FCA 205; (1990) 23 FCR 305 at 311-4. It is unnecessary to discuss these principles in detail here. Many expressions, not only "felicitous" but also insightful have been suggested to explain the connection that must be shown between an outgoing on the one hand and the assessable income on the other, in order that the loss or outgoing may satisfy the statutory language of the subsection that it be incurred "in" gaining or producing the assessable income. Ultimately, whether the relevant outgoing satisfies this language will be a question of fact, once the relevant statutory language is understood.

19. Most discussion of the matter commences with the well known words from the judgment of the full High Court in Ronpibon Tin NL v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at 56-7:

"For expenditure to form an allowable deduction

as an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing

the assessable income it must be incidental and

relevant to that end. The words `incurred in

gaining or producing the assessable income' mean

in the course of gaining or producing such income...

In brief substance, to come within the initial

part of the sub-section it is both sufficient

and necessary that the occasion of the loss or

outgoing should be found in whatever is

productive of the assessable income or, if none

be produced, would be expected to produce assessable income".

20. The words "incidental" and "relevant" refer, not to the frequency or likelihood of occurrence but to the nature or character of the expenditure. I find it difficult to see how it could seriously be said that the expenditure by Mr Kemp on the legal fees to secure his ultimate release from the New Zealand League could be said to be irrelevant to his employment as a footballer with the Newcastle Knights, an employment he had secured prior to incurring the expenditure in issue in the present case. To say that the income producing activity of a footballer is the mere playing of football and that without the clearance of the New Zealand League that income producing activity could not have begun, is to fail to acknowledge the close relationship that existed here between the outgoings and Mr Kemp's employment under contract to the Newcastle Knights. The Commissioner indeed acknowledged, properly in my view, that even while Mr Kemp was prevented from playing football in Australia, he was expected by the club to participate in training and to assist the club in other ways, including attending schools in the Newcastle area in relation to the club's development programme.

21. However, it does not follow that Mr Kemp is necessarily entitled to a deduction. The substantial issue in the present case is, in my opinion, whether the expenditure in question is an outgoing of capital or of a capital nature and so denied deductibility under the exclusionary limbs of s.51(1).

22. Counsel for Mr Kemp placed reliance upon evidence that, given the attempts of football authorities to regulate the contractual relationships between clubs and footballers, there were regular contests between players and clubs and players and associations. Further, the evidence suggested that in professional rugby league in Australia litigation was a growing area, so that the risk of incurring legal fees was part and parcel of the game. This evidence was not referred to by the Tribunal which made no findings in respect of it. Certainly the evidence was not contested and no suggestion was made that the credit of witnesses was in any way impugned. It is, however, difficult to see where this evidence would lead in a case such as the present. The fact that litigation and thereby the incurring of legal expenditure is frequent, or for that matter an ordinary incident of the life of a professional footballer, would not lead to the conclusion that all payments for legal fees were deductible. No doubt, if a player incurred legal fees for representation before the League Judiciary, the payment of those fees would be deductible. Similarly, legal expenditure by a player to defend himself against a civil action by a player injured in the course of the game would likely be deductible. But it could not be deduced from that that all payments of legal expenses were similarly deductible. In each case it would be necessary to determine what the legal expenditure was for.

23. I find particularly helpful in cases of the present kind the language of Dixon J in Hallstroms Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634 at 648 and his Honour's illuminating judgment in Sun Newspapers Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337 at 359-363.

24. As the former case pointed out, where the claim of a taxpayer is to deduct legal expenses, those expenses take the quality of an outgoing on revenue or capital account from the "cause or the purpose of incurring the expenditure". As his Honour pointed out (at 647):

"We are, therefore, remitted to a consideration

of the object in view when the legal proceedings

were undertaken, or of the situation which

impelled the taxpayer to undertake them."

25. Later in the same judgment his Honour emphasised (at 648) that the question of whether a particular outgoing was one of capital or revenue depends:

"on what the expenditure is calculated to effect

from a practical and business point of view,

rather than upon the juristic classification of

the legal rights, if any, secured, employed or

exhausted in the process."

26. In Sun Newspapers in the well known passage, his Honour had said (at 363):

"There are, I think, three matters to be

considered, (a) the character of the advantage

sought, and in this its lasting qualities may

play a part, (b) the manner in which it is to be

used, relied upon or enjoyed, and in this and

under the former head recurrence may play its

part, and (c) the means adopted to obtain it;

that is, by providing a periodical reward or

outlay to cover its use or enjoyment for periods

commensurate with the payment or by making a

final provision or payment so as to secure

future use or enjoyment."

27. As the above passage makes clear, it is necessary to identify the "advantage sought" by the taxpayer before it can be determined whether the expenditure under consideration is revenue or capital expenditure. In the present case, that advantage was the freedom for Mr Kemp to play in Australia with a club of his choice, unrestrained by the strictures of the New Zealand League.

28. Expenditure which is made once and for all to obtain an enduring advantage will ordinarily have the character of capital: cf British Insulated and Helsby Cables Ltd v Atherton (1926) AC 205. This does not mean that it is necessary for the expenditure to create an enduring asset before it will be seen to be capital. The reference in the reasons of the Tribunal to the expenditure in the present case securing "a structural asset" is clearly incorrect. Mr Kemp obtained no asset as a result of the legal proceedings or negotiations which he entered into; rather, he secured a freedom from restraint. The fact that no asset was acquired by Mr Kemp is not irrelevant to the issue to be determined, but it clearly will not be determinative of it either: Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Ampol Exploration Limited (1986) 86 ATC 4859 at 4883 and cases there cited. The legal proceedings in New Zealand were undertaken to free Mr Kemp from the restraints which were then imposed upon him and which prevented him while, at least they were valid, from playing football in Australia. The removal of those restraints provided, in one sense, a lifetime advantage to Mr Kemp, notwithstanding that it could perhaps be expected that the New Zealand authorities, faced with a court decision declaring the restraint rules invalid, would attempt to redraft them and perhaps impose some other form of valid restraint.

29. The expenditure was not recurrent in the sense which that word is used in Sun Newspapers (see at 362). As explained by Dixon J, expenditure is recurrent in the relevant sense, even where it may never re-occur, where the purpose of the expenditure brings it within the class of things which:

"in the aggregate form the constant demand which must

be answered out of the returns of a trade or its

circulating capital...".

30. Examples of recurrent expenditure in the case of employees would include accident insurance of the kind discussed in the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Smith [1981] HCA 10; (1981) 147 CLR 578, annual subscriptions, or perhaps in the case of professional footballers, training expenses, or expenses for uniforms. Despite the attempts of counsel for Mr Kemp to argue that the constant restrictive regulation of players made reaction to that regulation by players a recurring matter, and inferentially the incurring of legal expenses to have such restriction set aside equally recurrent, it would be wrong to categorise the expenditure here as recurrent. It stands in considerable contrast to the recurrent expenditure held to be deductible in McLennan v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1990) 90 ATC 4047.

31. As I sought to point out in Cooper, s.51(1) is essentially concerned with what may be termed "working expenses". This phrase must, however, not be taken too far. Expenditure on recurrent items such as rent and interest by an investor will be deductible, albeit that the assessable income derived as a result of the incurring of the outgoings may involve a minimum of work or effort on the part of the taxpayer. The idea sought to be conveyed contrasts with expenditure on the business structure or income earning apparatus, which will ordinarily be on capital account.

32. It would be difficult, in the present case, to characterise Mr Kemp's litigation as being a day to day working expense; rather, it could be seen as directed at producing for him a lasting advantage by removing an impediment upon his playing football with clubs in Australia.

33. Some guidance can be obtained from the decision of the full High Court in Foley Brothers Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1965) 13 ATD 562, where a payment made by a taxpayer under terms of settlement to obtain a release from obligations undertaken by a previous agreement was held to be a payment on capital account. In the course of their judgment, Kitto, Taylor and Menzies JJ. said (at 563):

"The freedom thus acquired was clearly enough an

enduring advantage. In the appellant's argument

it was treated as relating only to day-to-day

decisions in the course of income-producing

activities, but so to describe it is to mistake

its nature entirely. It was a freedom, not to

make decisions, or even to carry decisions into

effect, as part of the process of producing

income within that structure, but to make

changes in the structure of the income-producing

organization.... The true contrast is between

altering the framework within which income-

producing activities are for the future to be

carried on and taking a step as part of those

activities within the framework."

34. There is a view that because the courts have widened the concept of income at the expense of capital in cases determining assessability, so too the courts should correspondingly widen the category of revenue outgoings in respect of which a deduction is allowed at the expense of non-deductible capital items. Whether the premise for this view should be accepted is itself a question of some difficulty. If all that is meant is that in determining questions of deductibility under s.51(1) the courts should not take a narrow or pedantic view, I would embrace the proposition. Just as some payments received as consideration for restraints of trade granted might be assessable as profits of a business, so too some payments made as the purchase price of such covenants may be on revenue account and deductible. The decision of the Privy Council in BP Australia Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1966) AC 224 provides an example. The period of the restraint was a relevant matter in that case. So the Board said (at 267):

"Had the agreements been only for two or three

year periods that fact would have pointed to

recurrent revenue expenditure. Had they been

for 20 years, that fact would have pointed to a

non-recurring payment of a capital nature.

Length of time, though theoretically not a

deciding factor, does in practice shed a light

on the nature of the advantage sought. The

longer the duration of the agreements, the

greater the indication that a structural

solution was being sought."

35. Were the advantage sought by Mr Kemp release from the restraint imposed upon him by the League for a short term, say one season, I think that it might be said that the advantage sought would not have been a structural one and that the payments could more readily be seen to be recurrent expenditure on revenue account. A question of degree is involved. What was gained in a practical sense by Mr Kemp was not a lifetime freedom from restraint, but something less. No evidence seems to have been led concerning the precise release which the New Zealand League gave Mr Kemp. But what was actually gained is not determinative (or perhaps even relevant) to the issue. What is relevant is the advantage sought. On any view of the matter here, what Mr Kemp sought was freedom from the restraint which prevented him from playing in Australia with the club of his choice. He apparently achieved, in a practical sense if not in a legal sense, something less. Nevertheless, the deductibility of his legal expenses depends upon the character of the advantage which he sought. That advantage was a structural advantage and the expenditure incurred in pursuit of that structural advantage itself took on the character of capital.

36. Accordingly, I am of the view that the expenditure by Mr Kemp was not deductible and that the Tribunal was correct in concluding that the objection decision of the Commissioner should be affirmed. I would accordingly dismiss the present application and order Mr Kemp to pay the Commissioner's costs of it.
