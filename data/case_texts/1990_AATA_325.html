NT86/10871 and Commissioner of Taxation [1990] AATA 325 (12 January 1990)

Last Updated: 30 May 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

INCOME TAX - Burden of Proof - Taxpayer's accounting records as evidence of purpose.

INCOME TAX - purpose in purchasing land - Purchaser a dealer in land - Separate purposes attributed to different portions of a single holding.

Income Tax Assessment Act - Section 26 (j)

Chapman v F.C.T, [1968] HCA 4; (1968) 117 C.L.R. 167

F.C.T. v Thorogood [1927] HCA 36; (1927) 40 C.L.R. 454 @ 458

Case W6 89 A.T.C 147

Case W105 89 A.T.C 839

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT86/10871 TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re: NT86/10871

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent,

AAT Decision No 5615

DECISION

Tribunal : Mr. Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

Date : 12 January 1990

Place : Sydney

Decision : The order of the Tribunal will be that the taxable income of the Applicant for the year of income ended 30 June 1982 shall be reduced by $16,500.

(sgd) P.M. ROACH

PETER M. ROACH

Senior Member

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT86/10871 TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re: NT86/10871

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

12 January 1990 Mr. Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. The only person to give evidence in these proceedings was Jack. As a witness, he responded directly, simply and clearly to all questions asked of him, whether by his own Counsel or during cross-examination. He did not vacillate. For many years he was active in real estate dealings and controlled several companies through a holding company. The companies he controlled as subsidiaries bore names which included his family name and also incorporated terms suggestive of a principal field of the company's activity. But the latter terms did not necessarily accurately indicate the nature of the company's activities and in some instances, it was said, did not do so, at least to the extent that the company was not exclusively engaged in the field of activity suggested by the name.

2. The subsidiaries were:

Family Sub-divisions Pty. Limited

Family Estates Pty. Limited

Family Pastoral Pty. Limited

Family Home Units Pty. Limited

Family Sub-divisions Pty. Limited

3. The present Applicant is the company first named and I shall hereafter refer to it as "Sub-divisions". It came under the control of Jack when it was acquired as a shelf-company during the month of June 1972. By June 1973, it held several parcels of real estate. In addition to the properties which are the subject of the present dispute, those other properties, and the uses to which they were put, were as follows:

Cronulla - a suburban site acquired for the purpose of building home-units.

Oyster Bay - a residence purchased with a view to resale at a profit.

Near Moyuya - twenty developed five acre lots acquired for the purpose of resale at a profit.

Pealchurst - a vacant block of suburban land purchased for resale at a profit.

Heathcote - a large suburban lot with residence purchased with a view to sub-division into several lots and resale at a profit.

4. In each case the objective at the point of acquisition was successfully pursued and the profits generated by the undertaking were returned as assessable income. The present problems relate to two other properties. One related to a suburban residential block comprising approximately 0.8 of a hectare. It was a. steep, narrow, irregular block leading to a water frontage of some five metres. A house existed on the property and access to it was provided from street level by some sixty steps. The unchallenged evidence of Jack was that he and his wife had purchased it in 1966 with a view to using it as their matrimonial home, but before they could occupy it the couple found themselves to be expecting their first child. They deferred their plans and let the residence to others. When Sub-divisions was incorporated, the land was transferred to it. Jack's recollection was that that was done with a view to some advantage in relation to death duties. The explanation was not tested and, having regard to the terms of the memorandum and articles of association, it would seem to have been at best only a small part of any explanation. However, I draw no adverse inference from that circumstance. For some periods Sub-divisions let the property for rental, and at other times it made the property available to relatives of Jack and his wife. Several years later the residence on the property was destroyed by fire and in August 1979 Sub-divisions received payment from its insurers of $16,500 as compensation for the loss it had suffered.

The Commissioner contends that by reason of the provisions of Section 26 (j) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, ("the Act") the compensation so received constitutes assessable income. Section 26 (j) of the Act provides:

"The assessable income of a taxpayer shall include:

(j) any amount received by way of insurance or indemnity for or in respect of any loss,

(i) of trading stock which would have been taken account in computing taxable income; or

(ii)

if the loss had not occurred, and any amounts so received for or in respect of any loss or outgoing which is an allowable deduction;

....."

The evidence relied on by the Commissioner in treating the land as "trading stock" will be detailed hereafter.

5. The second issue relates to portion of a parcel of land near the coast on the South Coast area of New South Wales. The whole area comprised 344 hectares and was transferred to Sub-divisions by its former owner (a company I shall refer to as "Pest-Co" for $100,000. That was by a Memorandum of Transfer executed 19 March 1973. The evidence indicated that settlement of the purchase was scheduled, probably from the outset, for 31 January 1973. The contract under which Sub-divisions purchased was not produced in evidence but other evidence makes it clear that, prior to completion, Pest-Co sought to avoid completion; and that Sub-divisions was well aware that there was a plan of sub-division under consideration which had not yet been approved. That proposal was one seeking authority to sub-divide the entire property into ten hectare lots. (In 1973 most of the documentary references to area were expressed by reference to acres rather than hectares and for the most part in the course of his evidence Jack: expressed himself by reference to acres. Further those references were commonly to lots of a nominated minimum size, such as five acres. For convenience I shall refer to lots of five acres and twenty-five acres as indicating lots with a minimum size of five acres or twenty-five acres as the case may be; to lots of two hectares and ten hectares as lots having a minimum area of two hectares and ten hectares; and as if two hectare and ten hectare allotments were identical in size to five and twenty-five acre allotments). In February the Shire Council had formed a view that a quite different method of sub-division such as would later be proposed by Jack would be more appropriate. Whether such a plan could be implemented would depend on decisions of the State Planning Authority.

6. The evidence of Jack was that before purchase, in the interest of Sub-divisions, he had taken care to confer with representatives of the Shire Council and in particular its Town Planner. In that way he became aware that the Council was not in favour of the proposed ten hectare development of the entire site. It preferred an alternative proposal which at that point it was not in a position to approve, whereby something of the order of 20% of the site would be given over to two hectare development and the balance retained as a single farm property. Jack was "quite happy" with the prospect of farming that balance. He gave evidence that he saw an opportunity for it to be a large farm in a prime area and that he could look forward to the day when his three sons might want to work the property. He also saw, as a possible long term prospect a unique opportunity to retire to the farm.

7. By his evidence. Jack has sought to persuade me that it is more probable than not that from the date of acquisition - the date of contracting for the purchase of the property - the object in purchasing one portion of the land was to secure that land to be used for farming purposes. He seeks to persuade me on behalf of Sub-divisions that its "dominant purpose" at that date was not profit-making by sale and that it was not land acquired in the ordinary course of the company's business for use in that business. Secondly, he seeks to persuade me that the relevant portion of the land was the land which, upon its sale, generated the profits now under dispute. That there may be different "dominant purposes" for distinct parts of a single parcel of land is not in dispute (Chapman v F.C.T. ((1968) [1968] HCA 4; 117 C.L.R. 167).

8. I am not persuaded that, prior to contracting for the purchase of the property, any assessment of its farming potential had been carried out, or that Jack had the experience and qualifications to make such an assessment on behalf of the company. I accept that Jack knew that the entirety of the property had been used for grazing and that, at about the time he first knew it, some forty to fifty cattle were being run on it.

9. I accept his evidence that the perimeter fences were in an unsatisfactory state but I am not persuaded that any major work would have been necessary to enable Sub-divisions to run cattle on the property as they had been run immediately before, or at least shortly before, its purchase. Yet I find that no cattle were introduced to the property for some sixteen months after Sub-divisions entered into possession of the property.

10. It is undisputed that, as to a different portion of the property, the objective was to secure development approval permitting sub-division into two hectare lots and thereafter to effect sales at a profit. To achieve that required approval of a completely different sub-divisional proposal than the proposal for sub-division of the whole into ten hectare allotments: the proposal under consideration at the date of contract. That proposal, involving of necessity identification of the lanes to be sub-divided, might present problems as to the definition of the lands to be sub-divided. Only when the lands were so identified would the lands which could be available for farming in the long term be defined. At acquisition there was no basis for assuming that the proposal would be approved promptly, or indeed that approval would ever be granted. Implementation of the proposal, if and when approved, would call for the carrying out of physical works of development. It would also require the construction of fencing to separate the sub-divided lands from the residue of the property said to be intended to be held as farming land. Years might be involved.

11. In the event approval of a sub-divisional proposal came during 1976 and the first sales of two hectare lots commenced following completion of the development works. But at the date of acquisition - at the date of the signing of the contract for purchase - all of that lay in the unknown future. However, what was known was that pending approval and, indeed, pending the completion of fencing of the perimeter of the blocks to be sub-divided, there was nothing to prevent the implementation of any grazing proposal such as there was in relation to the lands said to have been acquired for that dominant purpose. In pursuit of those grazing objectives, the land, however defined, which was acquired for the dominant purpose of sub-division would have been available for grazing purposes. Despite that, no stock was introduced to the property until about August 1974 at the earliest. As to just what was then done the evidence was unclear. The Pastures Board had rated the property as having capacity to carry forty "large stock"; previous owners had carried forty to fifty cattle on the property; no live stock schedules were proved; Jack gave evidence that cattle numbers had ranged from two hundred to three hundred and acknowledged that there had been some agistment on other properties; and there was no evidence to indicate what had had to be done to make the property fit to carry cattle, nor why it had not been carried out earlier.

12. I accept that after August 1974 a substantial grazing operation was carried out, partly on the lands in question and partly on lands of others. I accept that use of the lands in question for that purpose found favour with the Council and the proposals by Jack that it should be so used were significant in relation to the proposals advanced by the Applicant for sub-division of portions of the property into two hectare lots. I accept that in his endeavours to confine the losses being incurred by grazing activities during a period when beef prices were depressed, Jack actually acquired a suburban butchery and operated it through employees for a period of some eighteeen months commencing in January 1975: a project which was itself unsuccessful.

13. Following the failure of the grazing enterprise, as a result of changes effective throughout the Shire, it became possible to sub-divide land into units of 40 hectares minimum. The Applicant availed itself of the opportunities presented. It sold the home site first as, being no longer occupied by farm employees, it was at risk of being vandalised. The residue was later sold in two other portions.

14. Those sales generated profits of $39,758, $33,154 and $90,438 in the years of income ended 30 June 1979 to 1981 respectively - a total of $163,350. If assessable as income, they so reduce the losses available to be carried forward into the year of income ended 30 June 1982 as to give rise to a taxable income. That taxable income was alleged by the Commissioner to be $69,570. The assessment issued in October 1983 (sic).

15. It is not disputed that, in relation to many of its undertakings. Sub-divisions did engage in a business of dealing in land by buying and selling it, with or without sub-division and with or without structural development. When such an entity, whether a body corporate or an individual person, derives profit from the sale of any class of property in which it ordinarily trades, it is more difficult for it to persuade that the disputed profit is not a profit of its trade or profit-making activities than it would be if the profit was generated by dealing in a class of asset in which it did not ordinarily trade. But it is certainly not impossible for a taxpayer to establish that profits which are so generated may have the character of capital gains. In that regard I refer to two decisions expressing the principles to be applied. At the same time they illustrate the fineness of the line which distinguishes a profit in trade from a capital gain. I refer to the decisions in Case W6 (89 A.T.C. 147) and W105 (89 A.T.C. 839).

16. For the present Applicant that task is complicated by the circumstance that in its annual accounts adopted year by year the whole of the land in question was identified as one of the "land development projects" along with the other properties which it acknowledged dealing in for profit. Furthermore, in all years from 1974, save the years in which sales were affected at a profit, the company adopted in its accounts statements such as:

"Major Accounting Policies

The company has applied the following account policies in determining its financial position at balance date and the results of its operations for the year -

(a) Land Development Projects.

Included under this heading are farm allotments, home units and residential sites at various stages of development and which are held for the purpose of sale at a profit. The basis of evaluation is the cost or net realisable value. All development and holding charges, including interest are capitalised only until a project reaches a saleable condition.

(b) Recognition of Income.

Profits arising from sale of projects are recognised when settlement is completed.

(c) Taxation.

Provision for deferred income tax has been made by the company. The provision has been calculated talcing into account the difference between the year in which certain expenses, which have been capitalised in the land development projects (interest and project manager's fees) are claimed as a deduction for tax purposes and the year they are written off for financial accounting purposes. (sic)."

17. The matter is of some significance for as Isaacs A.C.J. said in F.C.T. v Thorogood ((1927) [1927] HCA 36; 40 C.L.R. 454 at 458):

"The Commissioner, or rather the many officials who represent him throughout this vast continent, have sometimes no easy or enviable task in performing very responsible and intricate functions covering every variety of enterprise. The primary material on which an assessment has to be made is necessarily the return furnished by the taxpayer himself; and to test its accuracy the first field of investigation is ordinarily the taxpayer's own information and his books and vouchers. No doubt he is free to select his own method of accountancy, but, if by that method there appears to be a greater liability for income tax than his returns disclose, he cannot complain if the Commissioner, in protection of the Public Treasury and in justice to other taxpayers, holds him to his own accounts unless he satisfactorily proves them erroneous. If he does so, the taxpayer should not be penalized for inaccurate book-keeping; but the burden of correction rests upon him, and with more or less weight according to circumstances."

18. Statements so made in the Applicant's accounts are not conclusive of the matter but, coupled with all the other evidence, I have not been persuaded that the property was not acquired for the dominant purpose of profit-making by sale, nor for the carrying on or carrying out of a profit-making undertaking or scheme, nor that, so far as the land in question is concerned, that it was acquired otherwise than in the course of the ordinary trading activities of the company and later realised in the course of those activities. To that extent the application falls.

19. It remains to deal with the question of the residential site. I accept the history of this holding as previously outlined. However, what lay the foundation for the Commissioner's claim to treat the land as "trading stock" was the fact that this property also had been characterized in the accounts as one of the "land development projects". On that basis the Commissioner treated it as trading stock and, if the accounting principles are accepted as being accurately expressed and applied, it would follow that the Commissioner was correct in doing so. However, having heard the evidence of Jack, I am persuaded that, however it may have come to pass that the accounts were presented in that form, the observations were not accurate and that the land was never held by Sub-divisions for any purpose of trade. As the land was not "trading stock", there is no basis for treating the insurance moneys as assessable income. To that extent the objection of Sub-divisions will be upheld.

20. The order of the Tribunal will be that the taxable income of the Applicant for the year of income ended 30 June 1982 shall be reduced by $16,500.
