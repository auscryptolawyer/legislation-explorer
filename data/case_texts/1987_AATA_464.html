Re Kerry Elaine Cornish and Commissioner of Superannuation [1987] AATA 464 (15 December 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: KERRY ELAINE CORNISH

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. W87/70

AAT No. 4009

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.A. Layton (Deputy President)

Dr J.G. Billings (Member)

C.G. Woodard (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - "spouse" of eligible employee - whether applicant living on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis with deceased - applicant not living with deceased at time of death - whether apart on temporary basis - whether separation resulted from illness or infirmity - whether applicant wholly or substantially dependent on deceased at time of his death.

Superannuation Act 1976, ss. 3, 3(1)(a), 3(1)(b), 3(2)

Family Law Act 1975, s.72

Police Act 1892 (W.A.) sub-s.66(10)

Re Schlatter and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority S.84/123, delivered 19 August 1985 (2293)

Commissioner for Superannuation v. Scott No. G 62 of 1986 delivered 11 March 1987

Kauri Timber Co (Tas) Pty Limited v. Reeman [1973] HCA 8; (1973) 128 CLR 177

Aafjes v. Kearney [1976] HCA 5; (1976) 8 ALR 455

HEARING

ADELAIDE

15:12:1987

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

This is an application for review of a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation made on 5 February 1987 which confirmed a previous decision of a delegate made on 18 October 1983, that the applicant did not meet the requirements of the definition of "spouse" contained in s.3 of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act").

2. The relevant sections of the Act are as follows. Paragraphs 3(1)(a) and (b) define "spouse" in the following terms:

"3. (1) In this Act, unless the contrary intention

appears -

....

"spouse", in relation to a person who has died and

was, at the time of his death, an eligible employee or

a retirement pensioner, means -

(a) a person who was legally married to the deceased

person at the time of the person's death and who,

at that time, was living with the person on a

permanent and bona fide domestic basis;

(b) a person who was legally married to the deceased

person at the time of the person's death but who

was not living with the person on a permanent and

bona fide domestic basis at that time, and who,

in the opinion of the Commissioner, was wholly or

substantially dependent upon the deceased person

at that time;

...."

3. Sub-section 3(1) of the Act is qualified by sub-s.3(2) which deems a person to be living with another on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis if the following requirements are satisfied, namely:

"3. (2) Where the Commissioner is of the opinion

that a person would, but for a temporary absence or an

absence resulting from illness or infirmity, have been

living with another person at any time on a permanent

and bona fide domestic basis, the first-mentioned

person shall, for the purposes of the definition of

"spouse" in sub-section (1) of this section, and of

section 9, be deemed to have been living with the

other person on a permanent and bona fide domestic

basis at that time."

4. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by counsel, Mr P.W. Nichols, and the respondent was represented by counsel, Mr R. Eadie. The evidence before the Tribunal consisted of the documents lodged with the Tribunal pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (the "T documents"), together with further documents tendered as exhibits. In addition, the applicant gave oral evidence in support of her application and the respondent called a number of witnesses; a relative of the applicant's late husband, several friends and three doctors; Dr R.A. Baguley, Dr A. Tresise and Dr M.S. Platell.

5. The issue before the Tribunal in this application for review is whether or not the applicant, at the time of death of her former husband (hereinafter referred to as "the deceased") was living with him on a "permanent and bona fide domestic basis". In order to determine this issue, it is necessary to consider several questions; firstly, whether the applicant, in living separately from the deceased, was apart from him on a temporary basis, secondly, whether the separation resulted from illness or infirmity, and thirdly, whether the applicant was wholly or substantially dependent upon the deceased at the time of his death.

6. A number of facts were not contested between the parties at the hearing of this application and these provide a framework in which to consider those matters which were contested both as to fact and law. The uncontested factual background is as follows:

7. The deceased was born on 20 July 1947 and the applicant was born on 11 September 1961. They were married on 7 June 1980, the deceased then being 32 years of age and the applicant then being 18 years of age. They had known each other for two years before their marriage. Prior to their marriage, both were employed in the Public Service, although later the applicant changed her employment and became a bank clerk.

8. Before marriage, the deceased had maintained an interest in the training and racing of horses in addition to betting on horses. He also enjoyed all sport, in particular, golf, football and cricket. The applicant did not share any of those interests.

9. Following their marriage, the applicant and the deceased rented a duplex house and in late November 1980, approximately five months after their marriage, the applicant left the deceased because of what she regarded as his drinking and gambling habits. At this time, she lived with her parents for a week. The timing of this separation is confirmed by a medical consultation made by the deceased when he visited a Dr Eaton on 24 November 1980 suffering from distress after the applicant left their house. The deceased was given a certificate from the doctor for unfitness for work for a period of one week. The applicant returned to live with the deceased after he agreed to give up his drinking and gambling habits. The deceased had, in her absence, joined Gamblers' Anonymous and had attended a couple of sessions of that group. He had also reduced his drinking.

10. In January 1981, with the assistance of the deceased's parents, the applicant and the deceased purchased a one acre block of land. They raised finance to build a house which was eventually completed in October 1981 ("the matrimonial home"). The deceased kept two horses on the block, at least one of which was being trained and raced by him.

11. At some time late in 1981 (and we are unable to be specific), the deceased and the applicant became aware that the zoning regulations in relation to their block of land had changed so that the block could no longer be used for the agistment of horses. Also at that time, for reasons which appear hereafter, the applicant and the deceased experienced considerable dif ficulty in meeting the mortgage commitments on the house, an amount of some $400 per month.

12. By December 1981, the applicant considered that the deceased had not only resumed his previous gambling and drinking habits but, in her view, they had worsened and she consequently left the matrimonial home on 30 December 1981 to live with her parents.

13. Before the applicant left the matrimonial home on this occasion, she and the deceased had agreed to sell it and had made arrangements through a land agent to put it on the market. When the applicant left, all belongings, other than her clothes, were left in the matrimonial home.

14. On 3 January 1982, the deceased took an overdose of twelve Mogadon sleeping tablets and was admitted to the Royal Perth Hospital. Subsequent to taking the pills, but prior to his admission to hospital, the deceased rang the applicant to tell her of his overdose.

15. On 4 January 1982, following his release from the Royal Perth Hospital, the deceased was seen at the matrimonial home by a general practitioner, Dr Rodrigues, on a requested home visit. During the consultation, the deceased told Dr Rodrigues that the applicant had left him "for gambling debts for the second time" (T5/63). Dr Rodrigues formed the view that the deceased was "suicidally inclined" and recommended his admission to Heathcote Hospital, a psychiatric hospital. The deceased's admission to that hospital was a voluntary admission, even though it was made upon Dr Rodrigues' referral.

16. On his admission to Heathcote Hospital on 4 January 1982, the deceased was seen by Dr Platell, who was the Psychiatric Registrar at the time, although he was not a specialist Psychiatrist. The deceased provided Dr Platell with a history of having taken sleeping tablets expecting them to kill him. He indicated to the doctor that he was still considering suicide. He ascribed his attempted suicide to the applicant having left him and told Dr Platell that twelve months previously, he and his wife had had an earlier separation and that he had "vowed to stop gambling but this only lasted 2 months". He indicated that his present separation arose "out of the blue" and he could not accept it. The marital problems were described by him as being due to what other people believed to be the running of his horses, his gambling, his drinking and his problems with management of finance. He denied to Dr Platell that he gambled excessively, although he admitted that the applicant paid all the bills.

17. The Registrar also observed the deceased's behaviour during the interview of approximately one hour, and noted that the deceased vacillated in his presentation from being drowsy, indifferent, calculating, bland and tearful to self-indulgent. He showed, in Dr Platell's view, little awareness of the consequences of his actions and Dr Platell formed the opinion that the deceased should be admitted as a patient to Heathcote Hospital in Division II. This Division meant that, although the deceased was admitted voluntarily, he could be detained by the Hospital against his will if the Hospital authorities considered it appropriate to do so for his own safety. The Registrar also formed the view that the deceased was "a rather naieve (sic) juvenile fellow who stated that should he sell his horses his wife would come back to him. He did not see any other problems in the marriage" (T5/64).

18. On 5 January 1982, the deceased was seen by a consultant Psychiatrist, Dr M.K. Smith. At that interview, the deceased expressed the view that if he gave up his horses and gambled less, the applicant would stay with him. He wanted the applicant to speak to him face to face and said he would not eat until she did so (T5/44).

19. On 5 January 1982, the deceased signed a form so that his nett salary (after certain deductions) was paid into a joint account with his sister (Mrs Mustard) instead of being paid into the joint account which he had previously held with his wife. The deceased had a close relationship with his sister and they both shared a common interest in a racing venture, in addition to a common interest in some property. The reason given by the deceased to his sister to explain the transfer of the account was that it would be simpler to pay their mutual debts from a joint account.

20. On 7 January 1982, Dr Smith again interviewed the deceased and considered that the deceased at that time was more optimistic and although sad, appeared to be reconciled to losing the applicant. At that time, the deceased expressed to him the view that "where there's life, there's hope" and stated that maybe he would "find someone else" (T5/44). He also indicated at that interview that he would like to meet the applicant for a talk in a couple of weeks time and stated that upon discharge and for the foreseeable future, he intended to stay at his mother's house. He indicated to Dr Smith that his future plans were to sell his horses and his house. The deceased, however, did not wish to discuss any aspects of his matrimonial relationship other than his gambling.

21. Both Dr Smith and Dr Platell independently diagnosed that the deceased was suffering from an immature personality disorder with a "situation reaction". The deceased was dis charged from hospital on 8 January 1982.

22. At some time (probably before his admission to Heathcote Hospital rather than after) the deceased waited for the applicant in the afternoon outside her place of employment. As to what occurred on those occasions is the subject of some doubt and is discussed hereafter.

23. On 11 January 1982, the deceased saw Dr Tresise. The deceased gave Dr Tresise a history of inability to sleep because he was "ruminating" about the applicant leaving him. The deceased did not inform the doctor either of his recent overdose, or of his hospitalisation. At this time, the doctor did not consider the deceased to be suffering from a depression and prescribed sleeping tablets.

24. On 12 January 1982, the applicant closed the joint bank account in the name of herself and the deceased. At the hearing of this application for review, she stated that her reason for doing so was because she was a bank clerk and therefore did not want a joint account to be overdrawn as a result of the deceased's spending.

25. Approximately two weeks later, the deceased came to the house of the applicant's parents where the applicant was still residing. He brought with him their dog for the applicant to look after and indicated that he was going to kill himself. He subsequently left. The applicant and her father then went to the matrimonial home. The deceased became aggressive and violent towards the applicant's father, whereupon the applicant and her father left. On arrival back at her parents' home, the applicant found that pot plants on her parents' verandah had been smashed. Subsequently, the deceased telephoned the applicant and informed her that he had been responsible for the damage to the pot plants.

26. At some time shortly before his death, a couple came to inspect the matrimonial home with a view to its purchase. A telephone communication was made between the applicant and the deceased about the inspection having occurred. The couple subsequently entered into negotiations for the purchase of the house but before those negotiations could be incorporated in a contract for sale, on 30 January 1982, the deceased committed suicide.

27. In a note left for his parents and his sister, he wrote:

"...Sorry it had to end this way but after what Kerry

said to me tonight there was no hope of us ever making

up and I loved her to (sic) much to live without

her....."

28. As at the date of the deceased's death, the applicant was still residing with her parents and she had not returned to live with the deceased at the matrimonial home from the time of her departure on 30 December 1981.

29. Subsequent to the deceased's death a contract for sale was entered into between the applicant and the couple who had inspected the house and the sale was completed.

30. At some time between 30 December 1981 and 30 January 1982 (and the Tribunal is unable to ascertain when) both the applicant and the deceased independently went to see solicitors. The cause of their seeking advice is a matter of dispute between the parties and is discussed hereafter.

31. Also in the weeks prior to the deceased's death, he agisted his race horse on another property away from the matrimonial home.

32. As to the deceased's financial affairs: In the months prior to his death in January 1982, the deceased was earning approximately $15,000 gross per annum. At the time of his death, the deceased had approximately $200 per fortnight deducted from his pay and paid into his credit union, of which $130 per fortnight was owed on his car and the balance was for unknown credit. In addition the deceased had $30 per fortnight deducted for superannuation, $13 per fortnight for general insurance and $13 per fortnight for health insurance. After deduction of those amounts from his gross income and less tax, the applicant estimated that the deceased paid a sum of $200 per fortnight into their joint bank account, being a half share of the repayments on the mortgage.

33. As to the applicant's financial affairs: The applicant was earning $10,000 gross per annum and, at the hearing, she estimated that she received in the vicinity of $300 per fortnight nett from her employer. These moneys were used for general living expenses on behalf of both herself and the deceased, and the balance was paid into a joint account for the payment of her half of the mortgage and for other joint expenses.

34. Both the applicant and the deceased had their own "Bankcard" and each paid their respective monthly repayments.

35. As to their assets: The applicant and the deceased prior to marriage had been given or had bought all necessary household furniture and kitchen equipment. There was therefore no need for additional items, except perhaps by way of minor replacement. The matrimonial home had no curtains or carpets. The applicant said she spent little money on her own clothing.

36. We now turn to the disputed facts. The importance of these disputed facts is two-fold; firstly, they reflect on the credibility of the applicant and secondly, they are relevant in deciding whether or not the separation between the applicant and the deceased at the time of his death was a temporary absence, whether there was an illness or infirmity of the deceased and whether or not the applicant was wholly or substantially dependent upon the deceased at the time of his death. These disputed facts can most easily be considered under headings.

(1) Was the deceased a drinker and a gambler?

37. We heard conflicting evidence as to this question in oral evidence and in addition we had information of conversations between the deceased and others, including medical practitioners.

38. The applicant gave evidence that her husband mainly gambled on horses and stated that this was an everyday event. In addition, he gambled on his own horses when they raced. The applicant stated that in the good times, the deceased contributed half of his wages into their joint account for the purpose of paying the mortgage and for paying other joint accounts. However, in the bad times, the applicant was continually repaying money to him because of his gambling.

39. The applicant also gave evidence that the deceased drank at hotels on Thursday and Friday nights and also at lunch- hours and that he would return home affected by alcohol. She said that after she left him for a week in November 1980, the deceased's gambling and drinking habits improved for a sig nificant period of time, including the time when they were purchasing the land and building a house, but thereafter his conduct gradually deteriorated. The applicant said that she discussed this conduct with him but she seemed to get nowhere and so she thought the best solution was to leave him as she had before in order to "bring him to his senses".

40. Contrary evidence was given by the deceased's sister and two friends of the deceased. The evidence of these three witnesses was essentially similar. None of them regarded the deceased as being a person who gambled in excess. Their evidence was that when he placed bets, it was only for small amounts below $10 although one witness indicated that the deceased bet on every race for three race meetings each week, which meant he placed bets on approximately 26 races per week. In addition, he bet on "Trifector" and other combination betting schemes.

41. The deceased also outlaid betting money on his own horses, which ran at about one race meeting per month for approximately three months in a year. His sister said his outlay would not be in excess of $100 per race. There were also feeding and training costs in respect of at least one horse which was still a racing concern at the time of his death.

42. In relation to his drinking habits, neither his friends nor his sister considered that the deceased had a drinking problem and did not describe him as a heavy drinker. One of his friends, Mr Thomas, expressed the view that the deceased was a person who became affected quickly by alcohol and therefore he did not drink large quantities (transcript, p 30).

43. As to the deceased's view of himself in regard to drinking and gambling as expressed to medical practitioners: The deceased told Dr Platell that whilst he did not consider he gambled excessively, nonetheless, his wife had to pay the bills. The deceased also admitted to Dr Platell that he had joined Gamblers' Anonymous. Further, the deceased mentioned that other people (unnamed) believed he had a drinking problem.

44. The deceased's gross weekly wage was $290 per week (rounded to the nearest dollar), his debts for half the mortgage and deductions from his pay amounted to $165 per week and the balance remaining of $125 per week was used to cover his taxation, clothing, living, rates and taxes, "Bankcard" and his horse training and feeding expenses.

45. Bearing in mind the financial circumstances of the applicant and the deceased, and taking into account the whole of the evidence, we consider the deceased gambled frequently and consistently, although not in large amounts. Nonetheless, the impact which that gambling had on the domestic financial arrangements was substantial, even though the amount involved was probably small, by gambling standards. For example, a bet of $5 per race on 26 races each week would mean an outlay of $130 per week and this would be without the "Trifector" or other betting arrangements, and without the betting on his own horses. The deceased was undoubtedly a compulsive gambler who spent money beyond his means.

46. As to his drinking, we consider that whilst the deceased frequently drank, it does not appear that his drinking was compulsive in nature, although undoubtedly the applicant did not appreciate him returning home from work affected by alcohol, nor his late nights with friends who probably shared his interest in horse racing and sport. For these reasons, the applicant was not impressed with the interference and affect which the deceased's gambling and drinking had on their financial resources and their domestic time together.

47. As to the correlated matter of the deceased's horses. Not only did the training and racing of horses take up time (which the deceased acknowledged to Dr Platell), but it also used valuable finance. Whilst the ownership of horses as such was not an issue at the time of the first separation, it was certainly an issue on the second occasion and in fact, the applicant said that the sale of the deceased's horses was a condition which would have to be met before she returned (transcript, p 22). We accept the applicant's evidence that she specified this as a condition of her return to the deceased.

(2) Did the deceased exhibit violent behaviour?

48. The applicant, in giving her evidence, did not suggest that the deceased was a violent man and instanced only one occasion of violence which occurred, after she had left him for the second time, and approximately a week before his death. On this occasion, he became violent and threatening towards her father when she and her father went to the matrimonial home. This was the same night when the pot plants were smashed. There was, however, mention made in letters written on the applicant's behalf by her solicitors that the applicant "was in general fear for her safety" (T5/58) and in T5/60, it was stated:

"We can only state that it was the irrational and

sometimes violent behaviour together with excessive

drinking of the deceased that caused our client to

leave the matrimonial home."

Evidence was given by friends and his sister that the deceased was not a violent man. In these circumstances, we reject the information contained in letters written by the applicant's solicitors and consider that there is no evidence upon which we would accept that the deceased was a violent man although in a particular incident of frustration and stress after his wife had left him, he became aggressive to the applicant's father. This incident was not typical of his usual behaviour pattern.

(3) What were the reasons for the applicant leaving the

deceased?

49. The applicant, when giving her evidence, said her reasons for leaving the deceased on the second occasion were his gambling and excessive drinking, in combination with his involvement in the racing and training of horses. These reasons contrasted with the reasons expressed in letters written on her behalf by her solicitors which indicated that it was the irrational and sometimes violent behaviour of the deceased in combination with excessive drinking which caused the applicant to leave (see para. 48 hereof). No mention was made in that letter of his gambling nor of his interest in the training and racing of horses.

50. We have no doubt that the applicant told the deceased that her reasons for leaving were his gambling, excessive drinking and his horses and this is verified by the history given at various times by the deceased to medical practitioners. Whilst these may have been the stated reasons for the applicant leaving the deceased, it would be naive of us to assume that they were necessarily the true basis for the applicant leaving the deceased.

51. In endeavouring to form a picture from the various pieces of information before us, the following emerges. The applicant was only 18 years of age at the time of her marriage to the deceased, who was significantly older than she. The deceased was of Greek parentage, being a culturally different background from that of the applicant. The deceased had an intense interest in the racing and training of horses and in other sports, which interests were not shared by the applicant. The deceased was 30 years of age and unmarried when he first met the applicant who was young and very attractive. After marriage, the applicant and the deceased were living in a situation of financial crisis, largely due to the amount of their mortgage repayments. The deceased was a man who lacked financial management skills whereas, on the other hand, the applicant presented as a person who was concerned with finances and in spite of her youth, took a responsible attitude to monetary matters and endeavoured to manage their budget.

52. The applicant probably was aware of, and did not object to, the deceased's keen interest in horses, sport and gambling as well as his drinking before the marriage, but over time, her attitude changed. It is not surprising that the applicant later found the habits of the deceased incompatible with her own and considered them devastating to their relation ship. It is, however, easier to blame gambling and drinking as being the only reasons for leaving rather than the somewhat deeper issues which may have surrounded the existence of those circumstances and the way in which the applicant and the deceased handled them. We accept, however, that the applicant did leave the deceased primarily for the reasons which she expressed, although given her young years at the time, she may not neces sarily have consciously appreciated other underlying factors at the time of the separation.

(4) What conversations took place when the deceased waited for

the applicant after work

53. The applicant gave evidence that the deceased on many occasions waited for her after work outside her place of employment. The applicant's evidence as to what occurred on those occasions was given in the context of whether she expected to live apart from the deceased permanently, as follows:

"Q: ... did you expect to be apart for good? --- No.

Q: Right. Now you went to your mother and father's

place and you stayed there, did you see him again

after this? --- Yes.

Q: Did you see him often? --- Yes. In the beginning

he would wait for me after work every night.

Q: Now we know on 8 January 1982 he was admitted to

Heathcote Hospital. Was this before or after he

was admitted there that you saw him regularly?

--- Before.

Q: Right, thank you. You saw him every night where?

--- He was waiting outside the bank where I

worked.

Q: Did you have a conversation? --- Most times, yes.

Q: A cup of tea perhaps or something like that? ---

Yes, I think we did.

Q: Yes. And what did you talk about? I do not want

the details but anything of importance? --- Just

how he was going, if he was trying to change the

way things were."

...

Q: ... what were you trying to find out from him?

--- If he had stopped gambling as much or

drinking as much.

Q: What did you find out? --- Not a great deal."

(transcript, p 11)

54. Further, at p 21C of the transcript, the applicant gave the following evidence:

"Q: Well when you left, what impression did you leave

him with so far as what you said to him? --- Well

that when he pulled himself together and

straightened himself out we would talk about it

some more.

Q: And when he was meeting you after work what was -

that was an occasion initiated by him rather than

you? --- Yes.

Q: What was he doing in relation to you on those

occasions or saying to you? --- He was asking me

when I was coming back and we discussed his

problems.

Q: And when he said, "When are you coming back" or

words to that effect, what were you indicating to

him? --- When he gave up his drinking and so much

gambling.

Q: So you were at that time giving him an indication

that it was not all finished so far as you were

concerned but he would have to - it was a

conditional separation, as it were? --- Yes."

55. To the extent that the applicant's answers to questions suggest that at those meetings she indicated to the deceased that she would return to live with him if he gave up his drinking and gambling, we do not accept this. We consider it more likely that on those occasions after work, the deceased asked the applicant to return home but that she refused. Having regard to the conversations which the deceased had with friends and with medical practitioners, we do not consider the applicant held out any hope to the deceased that she would return home if he changed his ways.

(5) What furniture was removed from the duplex house at the

time of the first separation?

56. Evidence was given by Mr Thomas that, at the time of the first separation, he assisted the deceased in collecting furniture from the applicant's parents' house to return it to the duplex house. The furniture filled a double horse float and a utility (transcript, p 36). Such furniture included a refriger ator, washing machine and radiogram and was taken from underneath the carport of the applicant's parents' house.

57. The applicant did not give direct evidence as to this removal of furniture but Mr Thomas was cross-examined by her counsel on the basis that only a radiogram had been removed by her from the duplex house at the time of the first separation.

58. It is unsatisfactory that the applicant did not give direct evidence on this aspect, but if the questions put by her counsel reflected her position, then we would certainly accept Mr Thomas' evidence as being correct, namely, that at the time of the first separation, the applicant removed a considerable quantity of furniture from the duplex house.

(6) What stimulated the applicant and the deceased to consult

with lawyers and on what matters did they seek advice?

59. The applicant in her evidence said that she sought legal advice as a result of a note which she found in the matrimonial home which she thought to have been written by the deceased's sister, Mrs Mustard, which note indicated that she (the applicant) was not legally entitled to take anything from the house. The applicant gave evidence that she sought legal advice regarding the contents of that note and as to whether or not she could take items from the matrimonial home. She gave evidence that she did not seek advice on the taking of divorce proceedings against her husband.

60. Contrasted with this oral evidence is documentary material in the form of an annexure to the applicant's application for a benefit (T5/32) in which it is stated:

"For a short time prior to my husband's death we had

been considering divorce proceedings."

61. The applicant stated that she did not give this information to the solicitors and that she had not read the annexure. Further, in a letter written on the applicant's behalf by her solicitors, it was stated that:

"(The applicant) said that the main reason for

consulting lawyers was to ascertain her legal rights,

particularly in respect to gaining access to the

matrimonial home as she was in general fear of her

safety. Her primary intention was not to institute

legal proceedings for divorce."

(T5/58)

62. This statement in the applicant's solicitors' letter contains two elements; firstly, an assertion that the applicant was in general fear for her safety and secondly, a distinction between her primary and secondary intention with the inference that her primary intention was to seek advice as to gaining access to the matrimonial home because of a fear for her safety and that her secondary intention was to consult lawyers in relation to instituting legal proceedings for divorce.

63. In addition, the evidence of Mrs Mustard was that she accompanied the deceased when he saw a solicitor. The deceased told her that the applicant had said she was considering divorce proceedings. Mrs Mustard's evidence is that her brother sought advice on what could be done in relation to any divorce proceed ings commenced by the applicant and the advice he received was that he could do nothing until such time as proceedings were instituted.

64. Taking into account the whole of this evidence, we conclude that the applicant sought legal advice on at least two matters; first, the gaining of access to the matrimonial home for the purpose of taking items from it and second, in relation to the instituting of divorce proceedings. We therefore do not accept the applicant's oral evidence at the hearing and consider that contrary to her oral evidence, some discussion occurred with her lawyers regarding divorce proceedings. We also consider that the applicant was in error when she thought a note had been written by the deceased's sister and any note found by her was not one written by Mrs Mustard.

(7) The applicant's involvement with another man (in particular

Mr Cornish, whom she later married) at the time when she

separated from the deceased

65. The only information before us consists of statements made by the deceased to his sister and to his friends to the effect that he believed the applicant was involved with another man. This information was also repeated by the deceased to medical practitioners. The applicant was not asked any questions as to whether there was such a relationship with another man.

66. The only other information which obliquely reflects on this issue is the evidence of Mrs Mustard that within approxi mately one fortnight of the deceased's death, she and her mother came to the office of the Perth Building Society where they saw the applicant and Mr Cornish. The applicant had earlier given evidence that she had not met Mr Cornish until some two months after her husband's death. Mrs Mustard also gave evidence that on that occasion, she asked the applicant what she was doing there, to which the applicant replied that she was seeking information about another bank account. The applicant, however, denied that such a conversation took place, although agreed that she had been at the Building Society with Mr Cornish some months after the death of the deceased for the purpose of establishing whether or not an account existed in the name of the deceased.

67. There is no way in which we are able to make a finding one way or the other as to when this event in the Building Society office occurred. There is nothing which makes one statement as to timing more credible or more probable than another, although we prefer the evidence of Mrs Mustard as to the content of the conversation. We do not regard this evidence as critical to the major issues which the Tribunal has before it, although if a decision could have been made one way or the other, it may have reflected on the credit of the applicant.

68. We therefore consider that there is no evidence which satisfies us that the applicant was involved with any man, in particular Mr Cornish, at the time of the death of the deceased.

69. Taking then, the whole of the above found facts into consideration. The first question is whether or not the separate living arrangements of the applicant and the deceased as at the date of his death was a "temporary absence". At the hearing, the applicant maintained at all times that she did not intend the separation to be a permanent one but that she left in order to see if the deceased again would respond to the separation by giving up his gambling, drinking and his horses.

70. After consideration of the whole of the evidence, we are not satisfied that the separation between the applicant and the deceased was a "temporary absence" for reasons which include the following.

- The applicant, in not visiting the deceased when he was at

the Heathcote Hospital receiving treatment, displayed an

attitude which is not easily compatible with her expressed

view that at all times she wished to be reconciled with him.

The fact that she remained away and did not contact him

because she thought that the attempted suicide was an effort

to blackmail her, lies uneasily with a proclaimed desire to

become reconciled with her husband.

- We do not accept that the meetings which the applicant had

with the deceased after work were ones in which she indicated

to him a desire to reconcile, albeit conditionally. We

consider the probabilities are that the deceased indicated he

wanted the applicant to return, but on the other hand, the

applicant indicated to him that she was not prepared to

return.

- The information which the deceased gave to his friends and to

medical practitioners seems to indicate that he believed that

the applicant was not prepared to return, which impression we

consider he was given by the conduct of and conversations he

had with the applicant.

- There was no communication between the applicant and the

deceased as to what would happen with the proceeds of the

matrimonial home nor where they would both live in the future

if they reconciled. This would again seem to contra-indicate

that the absence was a temporary one.

- The applicant made no attempts to initiate any communication

with the deceased which behaviour one would expect of a

person who sought to be reconciled and such lack of com

munication tends to suggest that the separation was not a

temporary one.

- If the applicant was serious in requiring the deceased to

give up gambling, drinking and his horses before she would

return, we consider such request was unlikely to be fulfilled

by the deceased. The applicant was therefore placing

conditions on her return which effectively meant that the

separation would have been permanent if the deceased had not

died.

- The final letter written by the deceased at the time of his

death suggests that at that time, he thought that the

separation was permanent and there was no chance of a

reconciliation.

71. As we are not satisfied that the separation was a "temporary absence" and as the parties were living separately and apart at the time of the deceased's death, we are not satisfied that the requirements of sub-s.3(a) of the Act are fulfilled.

72. As to whether or not the applicant left as a result of the deceased suffering from an illness or infirmity, we accept the evidence of Drs Smith and Platell that a personality disorder and "reactive situation" did not constitute an illness nor an infirmity. The attempted suicide and eventual suicide of the applicant were not due to a psychiatric condition as such, but were the result of a person with an immature personality disorder being unable to cope with the particular stress of his wife leaving him and believing she would not return. We reach this conclusion for two reasons; firstly, a personality disorder and "reactive situation" is not of itself an "illness" or "infirmity", as those words are commonly used but, even if it were, we consider that the reason for the applicant leaving was not due to his personality disorder which he had always had, but due primarily to effects of his gambling, drinking and financial irresponsibility; secondly, the deceased's "reactive situation" arose subsequent to the separation and was not a cause of the separation. For these reasons, we are not satisfied that the further deeming provision of sub-s.3(2) applies.

73. The final question is whether or not the applicant, notwithstanding that she was not, at the time of his death, living in a permanent bona fide and domestic situation with the deceased, was nonetheless, as at the time of his death, wholly or substantially dependent upon him. The first question to be decided is whether or not, at his death, the applicant was dependent upon the deceased. The word "dependent" has been given extensive consideration in Re Schlatter and Defence Force Retire ment and Death Benefits Authority S.84/123 (2293) in which three areas were indicated as being a basis upon which someone could establish a dependency. Those areas were, firstly, the payment of money or the provision of support by a deceased to a claimant, secondly, the existence of a maintenance order for maintenance of the claimant by a deceased and thirdly, that the deceased had a legal obligation of support to the claimant with a probability of it being enforced.

74. As to these three factors, the Full Court of the Federal Court in the case of Commissioner for Superannuation v. Scott G.62 of 1986, considered in effect whether the Tribunal in the matter of Re Schlatter (supra) was correct in its conclusions as to the meaning of "substantially" within the phrase "wholly or substantially". The Full Court unanimously expressed the view that the Tribunal in Re Schlatter (supra) was incorrect in its conclusion and that the word "substantially" was to be interpreted as "essentially" or "in the main". However, Fisher and Spender JJ. in Scott's case (supra) added comments as to what they considered was meant by "dependency". At pp 13 and following, their Honours took the view that dependency existed "so long as he or she has a need of support, notwithstanding the fact that the need is for one reason or another not being satisfied by that other". This statement on "dependency" was derived from the cases of Kauri Timber Co (Tas.) Pty Limited v. Reeman [1973] HCA 8; (1973) 128 CLR 177 and Aafjes v. Kearney [1976] HCA 5; (1976) 8 ALR 455. It is clear from the statements made by Fisher and Spender JJ. in Scott's case (supra), that their Honours were endeavouring to find a solution to the undoubted difficulties for a decision- maker in ascertaining dependency using the accepted legal criteria of a legal obligation to support.

75. The common law right of a wife to have support from her husband has now been subsumed by the Federal legislation, namely, s.72 of the Family Law Act 1975 which provides for the circumstances in which one spouse has a right to maintenance from the other. As a consequence, their Honours were endeavouring to overcome the resultant problem of a decision-maker being required to "stand in the shoes" of the Family Court in order to decide in a given case whether a deceased had a legal obligation of support to a claimant. Their solution was to adopt a test of "need for support". However, it is for another day as to whether or not their Honours intended by adopting that test, to thereby exclude the legal obligation to support as being a basis upon which a person may also be able to establish the existence of dependency.

76. Considering dependency from both aspects, namely, whether or not the deceased had a legal obligation to support the applicant and also from the point of view of whether or not the applicant can satisfy the Tribunal that she was in need of support from the deceased, we consider that she must fail on both counts. The applicant's counsel endeavoured to establish both a legal obligation and a need for support on several bases. The first was having regard to the provisions of s.66 of the Police Act 1892 (W.A.) sub-s.(10), which provides:

"Every person leaving without lawful means of support

his or her wife or husband, and any parent wilfully

refusing or neglecting to maintain either wholly or in

part his or her child, shall be liable to a fine not

exceeding $1,000 or to imprisonment."

In our view, this particular sub-section does not reveal that the deceased was under any legal obligation to support his wife, nor does it indicate that the applicant was in need of support. The sub-section refers to leaving a person "without lawful means of support" and there are in our view no grounds upon which it could be said that the applicant, bearing in mind her income, was left without lawful means of support as a result of her separation. Furthermore, the provisions of s.72 of the Family Law Act (supra) may well cover the field to the extent that questions of legal obligation are those contained in the Family Law Act (supra) only. For these reasons, therefore, we do not consider that those matters indicate dependency.

77. As to the provisions of s.72 of the Family Law Act (supra), it was submitted by the applicant's counsel that the dependency and need for support were indicated by the need for the applicant to receive moneys from the deceased in order to discharge the mortgage payments. It was submitted that she could not discharge her obligations under the mortgage unless she received $200 per month from the deceased. We do not accede to this submission. The mortgage was a joint mortgage for which they both were jointly and severally liable and neither could discharge their obligation under the mortgage without the help of the other. It was not a question of the applicant being unable to fulfil her share of the payment in relation to the mortgage which may reasonably be regarded as her half. They were mutually reliant upon each other. To that extent, we do not consider that the existence of a joint debt for which they both had responsi bility is indicative of dependency by the applicant on the deceased, nor the alternative. Further, the facts do not satisfy us that there was an inability on the part of the applicant to support herself adequately on her own income, being the grounds upon which maintenance may be ordered pursuant to s.72 of the Family Law Act (supra).

78. In addition, applying the test suggested by their Honours in Scott's case (supra), we do not consider that the applicant was in need of the deceased's support at the time of his death. If anything, it was the reverse; he was in need of her support.

79. As to whether or not, even if there were dependency, it was substantial, we consider that the economic relations between the parties indicate that the applicant was not dependent on the deceased, but rather, the deceased, having regard to his own personal debts and his gambling habits, was financially dependent on the applicant. For these reasons, therefore, we do not consider that para.3(1)(b) of the Act has been fulfilled.

80. In all of the circumstances, therefore, for the reasons given above, we consider that the decision under review should be upheld, and we so decide.
