Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. Mr Barron (the Applicant) is seeking review by the Tribunal of a decision dated 26 November 2024 (the Objection Decision) made by the Commissioner of Taxation (the Respondent) affirming its previous decision to release the Applicant from a taxation debt.
  2. The Respondent may release individuals from certain tax related liabilities under Division 340 of Schedule 1 to the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (the TAA 1953). The Applicant says that his financial and other personal circumstances are such that he meets the criteria for release under Division 340. The Respondent disputes the Applicant’s characterisation of his circumstances and does not believe release is appropriate.

BACKGROUND

  1. The Applicant is 76 years old, married and has had a career in the film and television industry, particularly as a producer and writer of films and documentaries.
  2. The Applicant and his wife have also held roles as office holders in certain companies. The Applicant was a director and secretary of a company called Great Western Entertainment Pty Ltd (Great Western Entertainment) between March 2009 and November 2020.[1] The Applicant’s wife is currently a director of a company called Sandpiper Entertainment Pty Ltd (Sandpiper), and the Applicant was a director of that company between June 2013 and November 2017.[2]
  3. The taxation liabilities owing by the Applicant arise from the following:
    (a) An income tax debt of $128,580.83 owing in relation to the Applicant’s personal taxation affairs for the 2005, 2006, 2008, 2010, 2011, 2012 and 2014 income years (the Income Tax Debt). The Income Tax Debt includes a significant amount of general interest charge (GIC).[3]

    (b) A director penalty liability relating to taxation obligations for Great Western Entertainment (the DPN Debt).

  4. The Applicant acknowledges his indebtedness to the Respondent for amounts related to his income tax affairs and in his capacity as a former director of Great Western Entertainment.[4]
  5. In each of the income years from 2005 to 2020 the Applicant lodged his income tax return late. For some of these income years he lodged his income tax returns many years late and only after compliance action by the Respondent.[5] The Respondent therefore also imposed administrative penalties on the Applicant for failure to lodge his income tax returns on time for the 2005 to 2011 years. His income tax returns for the 2020 to 2025 income years appear to have been lodged on time.
  6. In April 2011 the Respondent placed the Applicant’s income tax liability (as it was then)[6] on hold on the basis of the Respondent’s view that it was ‘uneconomic to pursue’ at that time. The Respondent ceased the hold on those amounts in January 2016, and re-raised them as part of the Applicant’s outstanding aggregate tax debt.
  7. In November 2016 the Respondent commenced proceedings against the Applicant in the District Court of Western Australia for recovery of the Applicant’s tax debt, which was then around $239,000 in total. Summary judgment was granted by the Court against the Applicant in August 2018, although subsequent procedural matters in the Court meant that judgment was not entered in favour of the Respondent until December 2020 and was for a lesser amount.[7]
  8. On 4 August 2023 the Applicant made an ‘Application for Debt Release’ (the Debt Release Application) to the Respondent seeking release from both his Income Tax Debt and DPN Debt. As part of the Debt Release Application the Applicant provided information about his financial position at that time, including his household income, expenses, assets and liabilities, in support of his claim of financial hardship.
  9. By letter dated 23 February 2024 the Respondent told the Applicant that his Debt Release Application had been refused.[8] The Respondent advised that part of the Applicant’s overall tax debt (being the DPN Debt) was ‘ineligible for release.’ [9] The Respondent’s view was that the Applicant had ‘consistently failed to meet his tax obligations’ and that release from his tax debt (presumably the Income Tax Debt which was eligible for release) would not relieve him from financial hardship because he also had ‘ineligible debts’ that he was unable to pay.
  10. The Applicant lodged an objection dated 6 March 2024 (the Objection)[10] against the Respondent’s refusal decision. In his Objection the Applicant said that he suffered from a number of serious medical conditions, and had previously supplied medical records and other information to the Respondent. He said that the tax debt had had a significant impact on his mental health, resulting in a need for further treatment and medication. He did not agree with the Respondent’s statement that he had consistently failed to meet his tax obligations, and said that the tax debt had arisen from a mistake which had been corrected. He also disagreed with the proposition that release from his Income Tax Debt would not relieve his financial hardship, noting that this debt was his single largest debt and it if was released he could return to work. He said he hoped to propose payment arrangements in relation to his other debts, including the DPN Debt.
  11. The Respondent issued its Objection Decision dated 26 November 2024,[11] disallowing the Applicant’s Objection. Although the Respondent accepted that:
    (a) The Applicant’s fortnightly household expenses exceeded fortnightly household income, such that he had no surplus income sufficient to service his tax debt within a reasonable timeframe,

    (b) The Applicant’s household liabilities exceeded household assets, such that he had no equity in assets which indicated a capacity to pay his tax debt, and

    (c) The Applicant did not have any assets which could be regarded as unreasonable for a person in financial hardship to own,

there were other factors which the Respondent believed weighed against releasing the Applicant from his taxation liability.

  1. Those factors, which were described by the Respondent as ‘strong’ in the context of weighing against release were:
    (a) The Applicant’s failure to meet his tax obligations. The Respondent said that the Applicant had lodged 60% of his income tax returns late in the previous 10 years, had incurred penalties for failure to lodge returns and had not met his payment arrangements.[12]

    (b) The Applicant having other debts which he could not pay, such that release of the Income Tax Debt would not resolve any financial hardship he was experiencing because his household expenses would still exceed household income.

  2. The Respondent acknowledged the Applicant’s medical conditions, the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic and that the Applicant had been owed money by a company which was placed in administration/receivership by the Respondent, in circumstances where the Applicant had paid a significant sum to the manager/receiver in 2020.[13] The Respondent also acknowledged that, at least in relation to the Applicant’s income tax return for the 2011 year, errors had been made by his accountant which delayed payment of his tax. However, the Respondent said that the Applicant should have made active efforts to have sufficient funds set aside to meet his tax debt.

TRIBUNAL PROCESSES

  1. On 4 February 2024 the Applicant lodged an application for review of the Objection Decision. He acknowledged that the Respondent (and Tribunal) could not waive the DPN Debt and confirmed that his request for review related only to the Income Tax Debt. He said that key reasons for asking the Respondent not to pursue that debt were:
    (a) In 2011 the Respondent had agreed that the tax debt was not to be pursued, and then reversed that decision. He had not been given any reason for the reversal of the decision.

    (b) He was 75 years old, in poor health, owned no assets and his only source of income was the aged pension.

    (c) After the Respondent refused the Debt Release Application he had sought the assistance of a financial counsellor and together they had spoken to the Respondent about the refusal. He said that an officer of the Respondent told them that AUSTRAC data indicated that the Applicant had received $150,000 in the previous two years from sources in Canada. The Applicant said that he had not received any such amounts and when he approached AUSTRAC for details he was told that no information relating to such income existed.

  2. In June 2025 the Tribunal was advised that a Mr Robert Guerrini of Lawley Legal would be representing the Applicant. Timetabling directions made by the Tribunal in June 2025 were varied in August 2025 to allow the Applicant further time to file his Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions (SFIC) and evidence. The Applicant told the Tribunal that he had been unable to speak with Mr Guerrini who had been unavailable. The matter was listed for a hearing in December 2025.
  3. On 31 October 2025 another lawyer at Lawley Legal, Mr Sam Paonni, wrote to the Tribunal advising that he now acted for the Applicant, requesting copies of the Tribunal documents and seeking an extension of time to file the Applicant’s SFIC and evidence. With some reluctance the Tribunal again varied the timetabling directions and the matter was relisted for a hearing on 19 February 2026. Mr Paonni then contacted the Tribunal by email on 27 January 2026 advising that Lawley Legal no longer acted for the Applicant.
  4. On 29 January 2026 the Applicant wrote to the Tribunal advising that the ‘abrupt withdrawal’ of his lawyers had created difficulties for him, and he would try to seek alternative representation. He also said that he was scheduled for a medical procedure a couple of days prior to the hearing date. Although he had anticipated being able to participate in the hearing with the assistance of a solicitor, he did not believe he could represent himself a short time after having anaesthesia. The Applicant provided the Tribunal with supporting evidence of his medical circumstances and the Tribunal agreed that a further adjournment was appropriate.
  5. The hearing was therefore relisted for 16 April 2026. The Applicant was unable to retain further legal representation by that date and represented himself at the hearing.

RELEVANT LAW AND GUIDANCE

  1. Under Division 340 the Respondent may release an individual from a liability if the individual would suffer ‘serious hardship’ if required to satisfy the liability: Item 1 of the table in subsection 340-5(3). The Respondent may release the individual from the whole or part only of a tax liability: subsection 340-5(1).
  2. There is no separate definition of the term ‘serious hardship’ in the taxation legislation, However a number of courts and tribunals have considered the meaning of this term[14] and put forward the following propositions:
(a) It is financial hardship which must be considered, not physical or social hardship.[15]

(b) Serious hardship has regard to whether satisfying the tax liability would involve the person in financial difficulty that can be said to be ‘serious’, although not necessarily as extreme as ‘destitution.’[16]

(c) ‘Serious hardship’ is itself the test to be applied and this assessment is based on the individual circumstances of the person seeking release.[17]

(d) The Tribunal can consider changes to the financial position of the person that have occurred since their application for release or the time of the refusal decision, and their circumstances may have changed for the worse or for better during that time.[18] The question of whether a person would suffer serious hardship is to be framed at the moment when it is to be answered, rather than whether they would have suffered serious hardship if they had been required to meet the liability in the past or whether they would suffer serious hardship if required to meet the debt in the future.[19]

(e) Factors which have been considered in the context of whether payment of a tax liability would give rise to serious hardship have included the assets and liabilities of the person, the person’s income earning capacity, whether the person’s living or other expenses were excessive, and the ability of the person to meet reasonable living costs or afford basic necessities (for example, food, clothing, accommodation and medical needs).

(f) Reasonableness may be viewed by reference to ‘community standards’[20] whilst acknowledging that there may be other matters which are relevant to the circumstances of a particular individual.

  1. The Respondent’s administrative guidance for its staff, PS LA 2011/17 adopts many of these concepts and says that the Respondent will consider:

serious hardship to exist where the payment of a tax liability would result in a person being left without the means to afford basics such as food, clothing, medical supplies, accommodation or reasonable education.

and:

whether the consequences of paying the tax would be so burdensome that the person would be deprived of what are considered necessities according to normal community standards.

  1. The Tribunal is not bound to follow PS LA 2011/17, although consistency in decision-making supports the Tribunal taking this guidance into account[21] and in any event this guidance reflects propositions set out in relevant case authority. The Tribunal also notes that PS LA 2011/17 provides context to the reasons provided by the Respondent in its Objection Decision and to the Respondent’s submissions to the Tribunal.
  2. Item 1 in the table in subsection 340-5(3) says, ‘you would suffer serious hardship if you were required to satisfy the liability.’[22] It follows from this language that there must be a causal relationship between the payment of the tax liability and ‘serious hardship.’
  3. Accordingly, the discretion to release cannot be exercised unless circumstances of serious hardship have been found. A two-stage approach is applied, such that the decision-maker must first determine whether payment of the liability would result in serious hardship. If such a finding is made then the decision-maker will consider whether the discretion to release should be exercised.[23]
  4. Division 340 does not set out particular matters which must be taken into account by a decision-maker in determining whether the discretion to release should be exercised. The Tribunal should have regard to the facts of the case and the person’s particular circumstances.[24] This is consistent with the approach outlined in PS LA 2011/17 which sets out examples of matters that the Respondent will take into account,[25] but noting again that the Tribunal is not limited to considering those factors.
  5. Where the Respondent refuses to release a person from the liability the person can make another application for release, or the person may object against the refusal decision: see subsections 340-5(4), (7). A person who is dissatisfied with an objection decision may apply to the Tribunal for a review of that decision: subparagraph 14ZZ(1)(a)(i). Where an objection relates to a taxation decision other than an assessment, the taxpayer has the burden of proving that the taxation decision should not have been made or should have been made differently: subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(ii).

FINDINGS IN RELATION TO THE APPLICANT’S CIRCUMSTANCES

  1. At the hearing the Applicant was cross-examined by the Respondent’s counsel and submissions were made in relation to the following matters.
    The Applicant’s income and income earning potential
  2. The Applicant told the Tribunal that he had been a producer of several major television and film productions which although well received critically, had not always been financially lucrative. The nature of this industry sector meant that his income could fluctuate significantly from year to year.
  3. Currently the main source of income for the Applicant and his wife is the age pension. He has been receiving a government pension since January 2015, and since that time has also relied on part-time work[26] and small loans from family members to meet his living expenses.
  4. The Applicant said in his SFIC that his ability to work is limited due to his age and medical conditions. At the hearing the Applicant told the Tribunal that he believed he could work for another four years (that is, until he turns 80).
  5. He also told the Tribunal that the existence of his Income Tax Debt might limit his ability to work as a producer. He explained that having a poor credit rating was not an impediment to working as (for example) a writer or content creator on a production. However, a producer had responsibility for the financial welfare of a project and the financial status of a producer was important to completion guarantors and for insurance purposes. A producer would need to be a signatory to bank accounts and contracts for a project. Anyone conducting a credit check would be able to see the Income Tax Debt in his name, and this could affect project funding. However, he could work as an executive producer or freelance consultant for a project. This was a reason why he ceased to be a director of Sandpiper but continued to do work for Sandpiper as a contractor.
  6. The Applicant identified certain projects where he had been approached to work as an executive producer or consultant and which might provide opportunities for him to earn income. He said that some of those projects had partial funding from Australian or foreign government entities, although contracts for the sale of the films or programs had not been entered into as yet. He acknowledged that there were no guarantees that all or any of these projects would go ahead, but suggested that a project that did proceed could generate fee income to him of around $30,000 to $40,000.
  7. The Tribunal accepts that there is a window of approximately another four years during which the Applicant may be able to earn income as an executive producer or consultant to a film or documentary production. Although there is uncertainty as to whether any of the projects will ultimately proceed to a point where he can be paid fees, the Applicant was able to identify particular projects and explain the status, including the funding situation, of those projects in some detail. That is, these projects were not entirely matters of conjecture and the Tribunal was provided with some documentary evidence in relation to the projects, including information about financing.[27]
  8. The Respondent queried transfers of amounts between the Applicant and his wife as shown in bank account statements.[28] The Applicant explained that he handles the payment of household expenses and his wife transfers part of her pension to his account for that reason. They also move funds back and forth between their accounts to ensure that any direct debits from those accounts are covered. The Tribunal accepts this explanation, noting that the amounts in the bank statements are modest and consistent with normal household expenses. They do not suggest to the Tribunal any alternative or undisclosed source of income for the Applicant or Mrs Barron.
    Expenses and outgoings of the Applicant and Applicant’s household
  9. The Applicant confirmed to the Tribunal that his household expenditure remained largely unchanged from the most recent information he provided the Respondent in late 2025.[29]
  10. However, the Applicant said that in the three months prior to the hearing he and his wife had discontinued personal insurance policies (such as their private health insurance) due to financial constraints. They have entered into payment plans with utility companies, and will need to catch up on amounts they owe for rates, electricity and similar. His wife had also cut back on her smoking. The Applicant described a process of ‘juggling’ payments of bills and expenses.
  11. The home in which the Applicant and his wife live is subject to mortgage finance (discussed further below). The Applicant told the Tribunal that interest on that home loan is being capitalised, such that he and his wife are not currently making periodic payments of interest (or repayments of principal) on the loan.
  12. The Tribunal did not understand the Respondent to be contending that the Applicant’s household had or might currently have any significant ‘discretionary’ expenditure, and the Tribunal could not see any evidence of such expenditure which could be modified.
    Assets and liabilities of the Applicant and Applicant’s household
  13. The Applicant and his wife jointly owned a family home for many years which they sold in January 2016 for $1.715 million, after which the Applicant’s wife bought their current home (in her name only) for $770,000. The Applicant told the Tribunal that he and his wife bought their previous home in 1986 for around $103,000. With the money from an inheritance they had renovated the home, including adding a second storey in the 1990s to accommodate their growing family. Over time, and with the benefit of the renovation, the property had increased in value substantially and they subsequently borrowed further against the equity in the property. The Applicant said that at the time they sold the property in 2016, they had a bank mortgage of over $1.1 million. Caveats had also been lodged by two other parties. The Applicant said that they sold the property because they no longer needed a six-bedroom house after their children left home, and they were unable to afford the mortgage payments.[30] The Respondent noted that at the time of selling the former family home the Applicant had a tax debt of approximately $84,000. The Applicant told the Tribunal that upon the sale of the property the Applicant’s wife had been left with around $310,000 and the Applicant with nothing.
  14. The Applicant said that his wife had borrowed the balance of the purchase price for their current home. The purchase was in her name because she had the cash that was remaining from the sale of the former family home. This home loan had originally been with two Australian non-bank lenders, and the borrowing was then refinanced on one or more occasions. In 2019 Sandpiper borrowed an amount in Canadian dollars from a Canadian lender, with the Applicant’s wife being the guarantor of that loan and her guarantee obligations being secured by a mortgage over the home.[31] In April 2025 Sandpiper borrowed $945,000 from Prime Capital Securities, with the Applicant’s wife again guaranteeing that loan and the guarantee obligations being secured by a mortgage over the home.[32] The Applicant said that the terms of this loan required the first 3 months of interest to be paid up front and the next 9 months of interests was to be capitalised.[33] At the time of the hearing the Applicant was waiting for the lender to advise whether it would extend this arrangement for a further 6 to 12 months. If the lender did not agree to do so and other funding could not be found, the Applicant believed that they would lose their home.
  15. There was some disagreement at the hearing over what equity might be attributed to the current home. The Respondent suggested that the property might currently be worth around $1.6 million. The basis for the Respondent’s estimate was not clear and no valuation evidence was provided.[34] The Applicant said that the property might possibly be worth $1.2 million to $1.4 million. A copy of a Loan, Guarantee and Security Deed between Sandpiper, Mrs Barron and Prime Capital Securities Pty Ltd (Prime Capital Securities) confirms that Sandpiper borrowed $945,000 from Prime Capital Securities in April 2025 for a term of 12 months, that this loan was subject to interest, that Mrs Barron is the guarantor of that loan and that the home where the Applicant and Mrs Barron reside has been provided as security for the guarantee obligations with that property being subject to a mortgage.[35] The Tribunal does not have reliable evidence as to the current value of the property but can conclude that it is subject to finance of more than $945,000. This will significantly affect the amount of any equity held by Mrs Barron in the property.
  16. The Respondent highlighted for the Tribunal that Sandpiper was the borrower under the Loan, Guarantee and Security Deed, and therefore as a matter of contract the loan obligations rested with the company Sandpiper. That is correct, however that same contract also includes the guarantee obligations of Mrs Barron. These cannot be overlooked, and the effect of the terms of the Loan, Guarantee and Security Deed is that if Sandpiper defaults on the loan and Mrs Barron cannot meet her guarantee obligations from other sources, Mrs Barron and the Applicant will lose the home they currently live in.
  17. In a questionnaire completed by the Applicant for the purposes of his Application for Debt Release the Applicant said that his household assets had a value of around $10,000.[36] The Respondent queried why the Applicant’s home and contents insurance policy had ‘general contents insured’ of $336,000.[37] The Applicant said that the insurance had been arranged through a broker who had proceeded on the basis of ‘replacement’ value.[38]
  18. The Applicant told the Tribunal that neither he nor his wife had any superannuation. This was because historically his remuneration had taken the form of lump sums for writing work or fees as a producer, as an independent contractor rather than a salaried employee. He had not been able to make voluntary contributions to a superannuation fund due to financial constraints while raising four children.
  19. The Applicant noted that a statement in a Centrelink Statement for Age Pension dated July 2023 that he had a 50% interest in an ‘asset’ of $532,707 in an account with Latrobe Financial was an error. This should instead have referred to a liability, with the Latrobe Financial amount representing the home loan he and his wife had at that time.[39]
  20. Counsel for the Respondent asked the Applicant why he had indicated in his income tax return for the 2005 year (and other years) that he owned or had an interest in assets outside Australia. The Applicant said that this referred to his interest in his late father’s will which he thought he was required to disclose.[40]
  21. The Applicant said that more recently he has taken out smaller loans with ‘pay day lenders’ to fund living expenses. He said he realised that this was not a good idea but it was necessary to keep his household going. He provided copies of account statements from two such lenders indicating loans taken out in late 2025.[41]
  22. The Applicant also told the Tribunal that he agreed with the Respondent’s statement that he had paid other debts before his tax debts, in that he paid required amounts off credit cards which were used for daily living expenses. However, he noted that the balance on those credit cards had remained at the same level over time. That is, he had not repaid the issuing banks additional amounts.
  23. The Tribunal could not see any evidence of the Applicant (or his household) owning any lifestyle assets or investments and accepts that the assets of the Applicant’s household comprise their residence (which is subject to substantial debt financing) and some household goods and chattels which are not currently of significant value. The Applicant also has smaller amounts owing on a credit card and to payday lenders.
    Applicant’s relationship with corporate entities
  24. There is no dispute that the Applicant was previously a director of Great Western Entertainment and Sandpiper. The Respondent also asked the Applicant about other companies with which he had been associated. The Applicant confirmed that he had been a director of a public company which had been subject to regulatory investigation, although ultimately no fault had been attributed to him. That company no longer existed, and although historically he might have received some directors’ fees he did not currently receive such amounts.
  25. The Applicant also agreed that he had also been a director of certain other companies which had been established for the purposes of specific film or television projects. He explained that it was common practice to set up a separate company for a particular film or television series, then the company would be wound up at the completion of the project.
  26. The Respondent also asked the Applicant about an email which referred to the possibility of him selling rights in films or programs.[42] The Applicant said that the value in such rights can be transient and if a broadcaster or streaming service turns down a production, the rights may have no value. The Applicant viewed his likely sources of income from any future productions to take the form of fees or salary for production related work.
  27. The Tribunal accepts that the Applicant does not derive (and has not derived in recent years) any directors’ fees or similar residual benefits from companies with which he may previously have been associated, and that he is unlikely to earn any amounts from sales of rights. His most likely source of income going forward would be from earning fees as a contractor to future productions, or possibly a salary as an employee of a production company.
  28. Reference was also made by the Respondent to payments to a family trust. This reference was found in a letter dated 12 December 2010 from the Applicant to the Respondent, in which the Applicant proposed terms for a payment plan to manage his debt as it then stood.[43] The Applicant told the Tribunal that his family trust had been established on the recommendation of a firm of accountants and had been wound up around 8 to 10 years ago. He could not recall whether he or Mrs Barron had been the trustee but he thought that the beneficiaries were Mrs Barron and their children. He could not recall why the trust had been set up and he said that a reason for having it wound up was because he could not understand why it was necessary. The Tribunal accepts the Applicant’s evidence that this trust ceased to exist some years ago, and does not believe it is relevant to the Applicant’s current or recent financial circumstances.
    Applicant’s taxation compliance history
  29. The Applicant told the Tribunal that the primary amount of income tax involved in this dispute arose in relation to his income tax return for the 2011 year when his accountant at the time incorrectly included an amount that the Applicant had received from a Canadian company as (assessable) dividend income, when the amount should have been a (non-assessable) capital reduction. The Applicant said that he was unable to get the relevant company records until after the ordinary period for lodging an objection had expired, and he was not aware of the ability to seek an extension of time to lodge an objection. He agrees that he has not lodged an objection against this original taxation assessment and acknowledges that the origin of his primary tax debt is not directly relevant to the question of debt release, but provided this information by way of context for the Tribunal. Before the Tribunal the Respondent did not offer a view as to whether the Applicant should have been assessed on that amount in the 2011 year, but did say that this was a matter which was not relevant to the present application.
  30. The Applicant said that prior to March 2007 his income and other taxation obligations were not overdue.[44] He does not dispute that he failed to lodge income tax returns for the 2008 to 2011 income years. He told the Tribunal openly that he had been less than diligent and had not paid proper attention to his tax matters. He did not claim to have been suffering his present medical challenges then, but he described himself as being overwhelmed at the time.
  31. The Applicant rejected any suggestion of the Respondent that he had ignored the Respondent in relation to management of his tax debts. He referred to the documents filed with the Tribunal as demonstrating that he was in regular contact with the Respondent about his tax liabilities over many years. He said that in December 2010 he told the Respondent about his inability to pay his tax debt, and entered into a payment plan. A further payment plan was entered into between the Applicant and the Respondent in December 2014.[45] He told the Tribunal that he entered into the payment plans in good faith but his income ultimately did not allow him to meet the required payments. He did not want to enter into further payment plans if he did not think he could afford the payments.
  32. The Applicant was put in touch with a pro bono financial counsellor through a client support program of the Respondent. The Applicant has lodged his income tax returns for the 2020 and subsequent income years on time.
  33. The Tribunal accepts that the Applicant has engaged with the Respondent about his taxation liabilities frequently over an extended period of time, even if the outcome of that engagement was not what the Respondent had been hoping for. The Applicant acknowledges that he has not met his obligations under the payment plans he has previously entered into with the Respondent. The Tribunal also accepts that the Applicant has improved his lodgement record since the 2020 year, following his work with a financial counsellor (to whom he was referred via the Respondent).
    Applicant’s medical and health status
  34. The Applicant said that since early 2017 has suffered from a number of medical conditions, including some requiring surgery and ongoing treatment. This has also affected his mental health. The Applicant has provided documentation in support of his medical circumstances and the Tribunal accepts that the Applicant has suffered significant impairment to his physical and mental health. The Tribunal understands that the Respondent does not dispute this.
  35. However, the Applicant acknowledged that his current health concerns were not a reason for his previous failure to lodge his tax returns.

Other matters

  1. There were three other matters raised by the Applicant which the Tribunal believes are relevant to its consideration of his request to be release from the Income Tax Debt, even if only by way of furthering the Tribunal’s understanding of the background facts.
  2. The first matter involved a suggestion that at some point the Respondent had told the Applicant and his financial counsel that Austrac records showed that the Applicant had received a significant sum of money from Canada in the previous two years and this was a reason for his debt release application being refused.[46] Other than a small Canadian pension and loans from his brother who lived in Canada, as disclosed to the Respondent, the Applicant said that he had not been receiving funds from Canada. The Applicant approached Austrac for further information about these payments, making a freedom of information (FOI) request. Austrac provided 60 transaction reports dated between January 2022 and December 2023, which appear to be consistent with the Applicant’s explanation of receipts from the Canadian government and his brother.[47]
  3. The Respondent said that any payments from Canada to the Applicant as disclosed in Austrac records were not relevant to this application or to the question of whether the Applicant’s Income Tax Debt should be released, although they might have been relevant to any objection the Applicant lodged in relation to the assessments which gave rise to his primary income tax liability (and the Applicant had not lodged any such objection).
  4. The Applicant told the Tribunal that the link between Canadian payments based on Austrac data and the refusal of his debt release request was raised in a telephone call between an officer of the Respondent, the Applicant and the Applicant’s financial counsellor on 4 April 2024, although a different officer of the Respondent subsequently said that foreign income was not a factor in the Respondent’s decision. The Respondent said that it has no record of any call on 4 April 2024, although it did provide copies of call records dated 17 April 2024 and 22 August 2024. The Applicant’s financial counsellor is recorded on 17 April as having questioned whether the Austrac data was correct, suggesting that there had been some previous discussion. On 22 August the Applicant was advised that there was no record of such payments on his personal records. The Objection Decision does not refer to Canadian payments or other foreign income.
  5. On this limited material, the Tribunal can only observe that there is no evidence of the Applicant having received amounts from Canada in 2022 and 2023, other than the government pension and money sent by his brother. It is possible that an officer of the Respondent misinterpreted information at some point when the Applicant’s debt release request was being considered, however this does not ultimately appear to have been a basis for the Respondent’s Objection Decision the subject of this Tribunal application. In this regard the Tribunal agrees with the Respondent that the matter of the Austrac information about Canadian payments is not relevant to this application, although the Tribunal does not accept the Respondent’s explanation that the Austrac data may be relevant to the original assessment of the Applicant’s primary tax as the 2022 and 2023 date range is not consistent with the timing of the primary tax.
  6. The second matter relates to the Respondent determining that the Applicant’s tax debt was ‘uneconomical to pursue’ in April 2011 and July 2014, but then re-raising that tax debt in January 2016. The Applicant said he had sought but not been given a reason for the change in the Respondent’s position and could not understand why it had happened when his financial circumstances in 2016 were unchanged or even worse than they had been previously.[48] The Respondent also viewed this matter as not being relevant to the present application. The Tribunal appreciates that the Applicant is frustrated by an apparent change in the Respondent’s position, which may not have been properly explained, but agrees with the Respondent that this matter is not relevant to the question of whether Division 340 is engaged. There was no challenge by the Applicant to the validity or legality of that debt.
  7. The third matter raised by the Applicant was his concern that correspondence setting out the Respondent’s position contained a number of factual errors. He accepted that those errors were inadvertent on the part of the Respondent and that some of those errors might be relatively unimportant, however he believed it was necessary to correct and clarify them. The Tribunal has sought to deal with these items in its factual findings set out above. This includes such matters as the Respondent providing a photograph of the wrong property, the suggestion that the Applicant did not fully engage with the Respondent in relation to the Income Tax Debt, the application of the sale proceeds of the Applicant’s former family home and the finance and security arrangements for the Applicant’s current home.

CONSIDERATION OF DEBT RELEASE

  1. The Respondent submitted that the Applicant should not be released from all or part of the Income Tax Debt because the Applicant had not discharged his burden of proof in relation to demonstrating that he would suffer serious hardship if he was required to satisfy his tax liability. The Respondent said that:
    (a) The Tribunal could not be satisfied on the evidence that the financial position of the Applicant (or the Applicant’s household) was one of serious hardship. It was possible that the Applicant had a surplus of assets over liabilities; or

    (b) If the Tribunal was satisfied that the Applicant was experiencing or facing serious hardship, there was no causal link between the Income Tax Debt and such hardship. The Applicant’s other debts (including the DPN Debt) were significant such that he was already in serious hardship and even if he was released from the Income Tax Debt he would remain in serious hardship.

  2. Further, the Respondent did not believe that the existence of the Income Tax Debt was a genuine hindrance to the Applicant’s employment prospects as this was inconsistent with the evidence he had provided of contracts being negotiated for potential projects.
  3. On that basis the Respondent contended that the Tribunal was unable to proceed to the ‘second stage’ of considering whether there were circumstances such that the Tribunal should exercise the discretion to remit all or part of the Income Tax Debt. However, if the Tribunal did believe it should proceed to the second stage, the Respondent reiterated that the Applicant bore the burden of proving that the discretion should be exercised and referred to a number of matters as weighing against the exercise of the discretion.
  4. Those matters included that the Applicant had previously been a high-income earner and had incurred other debts which he had paid in preference to his tax liabilities, that the Applicant had acknowledged his lack of diligence in relation to his tax compliance between 2005 and 2014, and that the Applicant had not paid any amounts in relation to his tax liabilities since 2015. The Respondent had concerns that the Applicant had resigned as a director of Sandpiper and disposed of his shares in that company[49] in the context of the Respondent re-raising his tax debt, to avoid the consequences of that debt. The Respondent queried the sale of the former family home and purchase of a new home in the name of Mrs Barron only for similar reasons. The Respondent also suggested that if the Applicant had a real prospect of earning income in the future, it would not be fair to release him now from a tax liability where his financial circumstances might change significantly.
  5. The Tribunal is satisfied that the Applicant is experiencing serious hardship, in the sense of financial hardship. The Tribunal accepts that the Applicant’s current household expenses and outgoings exceed the household income. In this context the Tribunal does not believe that there is evidence of any discretionary expenditure that could be modified and observes that the Applicant has had to resort to borrowing from ‘payday lenders’ as well as receiving assistance from his adult children to meet basic necessities. The Tribunal has also considered the possibility of the Applicant earning some income in the short to medium term from certain potential projects, which must be weighed against his otherwise limited earning capacity arising from his age and medical conditions. The Tribunal does not believe that the Applicant provided sufficient evidence to support his contention that the existence of his Income Tax Debt would be a significant impediment to future work as a producer. However, the Tribunal does accept his evidence, both oral and documentary, relating to projects currently in development which offer opportunities for him to earn some income in the short to medium term, at least as a contractor or employee of a producer. The evidence relating to the equity in their home (which is owned by Mrs Barron but is viewed as a household asset for present purposes) was not entirely clear, however the Tribunal will accept that the household net asset position is limited (or negative) as the home is subject to a substantial security granted to Prime Capital Securities.
  6. The more difficult question is, on the basis that the Tribunal accepts that the Applicant’s current financial situation is serious, whether the Applicant would suffer serious hardship if required to satisfy the Income Tax Debt or whether the Applicant would be in serious hardship regardless of the Income Tax Debt. The DPN Debt of $49,000 is not able to be released and remains an obligation of the Applicant. The Applicant also has credit card debt and has taken out loans with payday lenders. The Tribunal asked the Applicant to elaborate on the impact to him of the Income Tax Debt specifically. He said that the existence of his Income Tax Debt would restrict the type of roles he could undertake in his remaining working life. As noted above, the evidence for this was limited and the Tribunal has some doubts as to whether his Income Tax Debt would be the main challenge for him in finding particular types of work. The Tribunal believes that his age, medical conditions and industry conditions would also represent significant constraints. However, the Tribunal does accept that there is scope for him to earn some amounts as a contractor on projects identified by him. Those amounts may enable him to meet some of his debts.
  7. The Tribunal therefore finds that the Applicant would suffer serious hardship if required to satisfy the full amount of the Income Tax Debt. Put another way, if no part of the Income Tax Debt is released it is almost certain that the Applicant would be unable to address his current financial circumstances, including servicing his overall debts while meeting basic necessities. There is an opportunity for the Applicant to be able to address some of his debt, such that release from part of the Income Tax Debt may mean that he is not in ‘serious’ hardship (albeit not providing his household with a comfortable financial position).
  8. The Tribunal must therefore consider whether it is satisfied that there are circumstances which justify the release of all or part of the Income Tax Debt. The Respondent referred to two recent decisions of the Tribunal which also considered whether a tax debt should be released.[50] In VBNX and Commissioner of Taxation the Tribunal decided that it was appropriate to release a portion of the taxpayer’s debt, after weighing up a number of circumstances applicable to that taxpayer. The Tribunal believes that a similar approach is warranted in the Applicant’s circumstances.
  9. Age or poor health are not of themselves reasons for the release of a tax liability, nor is the inability to meet a taxation liability sufficient reason to relieve the taxpayer of their obligation to pay. However, in this case the Tribunal believes that the Applicant’s age and potential financial capacity in combination is a relevant matter. The Tribunal accepts that the Applicant’s age means that he has a limited window of opportunity to address his tax liabilities, however the Tribunal also accepts that the Applicant has demonstrated a basis for being able to address some of his liability. This is consistent with the view taken by the Tribunal in VBNX as to the capacity of the taxpayer in that matter to meet their tax liability within a reasonable timeframe. However, the Tribunal also agrees with the view in VBNX that a full release would not be appropriate as a matter of administrative justice and fairness. To this extent the Tribunal agrees with the submissions of the Respondent that all taxpayers are expected to meet their taxation liabilities and preferential treatment should not be given to a particular taxpayer unless circumstances warrant doing so.
  10. The Applicant rejected the Respondent’s suggestion that he might have structured his tax affairs to avoid payment of his taxation liabilities. He said that the house sale in 2016 was prompted by a need to downsize and an inability to meet the mortgage payments on that property. There was insufficient evidence for the Tribunal to make a reasoned finding about any link between the sale of the property and the Applicant’s tax debt at that time, and the Tribunal does not view this matter as either supporting or counting against a release of the Income Tax Debt.
  11. In relation to the Respondent’s contention that the Applicant has been paying other debts in preference to his tax debts, the Tribunal agrees that this would usually be a matter counting against release from a tax liability. However, to the extent that the Applicant has been making payment on his credit cards and to pay day lenders, the Tribunal accepts that these debts have arisen from the Applicant using these facilities to cover his household’s daily living expenses and basic necessities. Further, ‘payments’ referenced in the Applicant’s Application for Release form relating to financing of the family home do not reflect periodic outgoings but are amounts that have been capitalised and therefore remain owing.
  12. The Tribunal has also had regard to the Respondent’s submission, and the Applicant’s acknowledgement, that the Applicant should have taken more care to comply with his lodgement requirements and to meet his payment obligations. The Tribunal again agrees that this is a matter which would ordinarily weigh against any release of a liability. However, the Tribunal has also had regard to the Applicant having worked with a financial counsellor and improved at least his lodgement compliance since 2020. The Tribunal accepts the Applicant’s evidence, which is consistent with documents provided to the Tribunal by the Respondent that he has sought to engage with the Respondent about his taxation liability over the years, even though it is agreed that he has not made any payment towards his debt in over a decade.

CONCLUSION

  1. The Tribunal has therefore determined that it is appropriate to release the Applicant from his Income Tax Debt to the extent of an amount equal to the amount of GIC that has arisen on his Income Tax Debt as at the date of these reasons for decision. This represents a release of slightly more than a third of his Income Tax Debt and the Tribunal believes that this will offer him the opportunity to address his remaining tax debt, including the DPN Debt, in a meaningful way. The Tribunal does not believe it would be appropriate to release the Applicant from the entirety of his Income Tax Debt, having regard in particular to the Applicant’s own acknowledgement of a previous lack of attention to his tax affairs and issues of fairness to other taxpayers who have been required to meet their tax liabilities in difficult circumstances.
  2. Although these reasons identify some matters where the Tribunal disagrees with contentions of or conclusions drawn by the Respondent, the Tribunal wishes to be clear that its decision to remit a substantial part of the Income Tax Debt is based on the financial and personal circumstances particular to the Applicant. The Tribunal notes that the Applicant made specific mention to the Tribunal of his appreciation of the professional approach shown to him by various officers of the Respondent.
