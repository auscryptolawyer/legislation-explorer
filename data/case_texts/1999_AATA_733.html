REASONS FOR DECISION



1 October 1999 J. Block, Senior Member



1. The objection decisions in this matter are the disallowance by the Respondent of objections by the Applicant dated 4 August 1997 against amended assessments issued by the Respondent, in respect of the Applicant, for the years ending 30 June 1995 and 30 June 1996 (referred to individually and respectively as the "1995 year" and the "1996 year", and collectively as the "relevant years").

2. The Applicant was represented by Mr C.H. Levingston of Christopher Levingston & Associates, solicitors, while the Respondent was represented by Mr D.B. McGovern of Counsel instructed by Ms Lyn Wilde of the Australian Government Solicitor's Office.

3. (a) When Mr McGovern sought to tender the T Documents lodged under section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth) ("AAT Act"), Mr Levingston objected to the tender of documents T3 to T27 (both inclusive) on the grounds that all of the evidence referred to therein was obtained in breach of the Listening Devices Act 1984 (Nsw); he also objected to the tender of document T28 on the grounds that it contains evidence obtained in breach of the New South Wales Crime Commission Act 1985 (Nsw).

(b) The Tribunal accepted the remainder of the T Documents (excluding all of those referred to in subclause (a)). Mr McGovern reserved his client's right, at any later stage of the hearing, to seek to tender the remainder of the T Documents as referred to in subclause (a); however, he did not in the result find it necessary to do so. It follows of course that it was not necessary to consider whether, as contended by the Applicant, that evidence had been illegally obtained.



4. The Tribunal had before it the T Documents excluding documents T3 to T28 (both inclusive). It also had before it a witness statement by Mr Levingston dated 3 March 1999 which includes the annexures thereto labelled "A" to "H" (both inclusive). The Tribunal received in addition two exhibits, R1 and R2, which are certified copies of the amended assessments referable to the 1995 year and 1996 year respectively. Mr Levingston advised the Tribunal that his client had not made an application that the matter be heard in confidence.

5. Mr Levingston advised the Tribunal that the Applicant did not intend to give evidence. He also advised the Tribunal that the Applicant was not precluded from doing so by illness or any other disability. He accepted that the failure of the Applicant to give evidence might give rise to an adverse inference against him.

6. (a) Mr McGovern objected to the whole of paragraphs 2, 3 and 6 of Mr Levingston's witness statement (referred to henceforth as the "witness statement") but only "insofar as seeks to establish the truth of the facts contained in the income tax return, not insofar as it identifies a tax return in that form was lodged with the Respondent".

(b) The Tribunal was of the opinion that the paragraphs in question did no more than recite the facts set out in those paragraphs, and that Mr Levingston did not, in those paragraphs, seek to suggest that the returns were true and correct. Mr McGovern did not press his objections or require Mr Levingston to submit himself to cross-examination and the witness statement was accepted into evidence accordingly.



7. (a) Oral evidence was given by each of Messrs Graham Smith and Robert Schlosser (in the latter case by telephone) both of whom are officers in the special investigations unit of the Respondent's Parramatta branch; (Mr Schlosser gave evidence by telephone because a train strike on the day of the hearing made it impossible for him to be present in person).

(b) Mr Smith gave evidence that he had played a part in the issue of the amended assessments which were the subject of the objection decisions and also assessments against Walter Hammond Pty Limited (referred to as the "company"). He gave evidence also as to the issue of notices under section 218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("ITAA") in respect of each of the Applicant and the company and in consequence of which amounts of approximately $117,000 and $800,000 were obtained in respect of the Applicant and the company respectively; those amounts were applied in reduction of the assessments against the Applicant and the company respectively. He confirmed also that he had sworn the affidavit which is annexure "G" to the witness statement. Mr Smith's evidence was brief; neither Mr McGovern nor the Tribunal had any questions for him.



(c) Similarly, the evidence given by Mr Schlosser was brief. He confirmed that he played a part in an audit report and recommendations which resulted in the assessments against both the Applicant and the company referred to previously in these Reasons. He agreed also that he had, for a time, been a temporary officer of the New South Wales Crime Commission. Again neither Mr McGovern nor the Tribunal wished to ask any questions.



(d) It should be noted that much of the evidence of Messrs Smith and Schlosser was already before the Tribunal having regard to the annexures to the witness statement. Annexure "F" to the witness statement is an affidavit by Mr Schlosser dated 29 May 1998 which confirms (inter alia) that he was a temporary officer of the New South Wales Crime Commission. Annexure "G" is an affidavit of the same date by Mr Smith, while annexure "H" is a statement of reasons furnished to the Applicant pursuant to section 13 of the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 (Cth) and in respect of the Applicant. Mr Levingston had indicated when the hearing commenced that Mr Schlosser's inability to be present in person might result in an application for a postponement because, so he contended, he expected to elicit evidence from Mr Schlosser which was important for the purposes of his client's case. In the light of his own witness statement and the nature of the evidence elicited from each of Messrs Smith and Schlosser, it is not clear to the Tribunal why their evidence was required.



8. The Applicant having then closed his case, exhibits R1 and R2 were tendered by the Respondent.

9. (a) The Applicant's objection in respect of the relevant years is set out at document T31 (page 472 of the T Documents); it is a brief letter by Mr H Lieberherr, an accountant, and reads as follows:

"Re: Notice of objection against amended assessments of Tawfik Awad TFN 120 334 106 assessment number 538614.

On behalf of the above taxpayer, I hereby object to the above amended assessment due to lack of details provided by you on adjusted business income (non PP) for 1995 of $491,017 and for 1996 of $3,952,321.

According to the above client, he has not derived any additional income other than originally presented in his income tax returns. Furthermore, no discussions appear to have taken place between the ATO, the above taxpayer and our office regarding any alleged discrepancies of income as assessed by your office."



(b) Mr Levingston, appreciating that the objection did not set out the grounds upon which the Applicant objected to the amended assessments, sought to amend the objections in the following manner:



"Notice of amended objection in respect of notices of amended assessment issued 31 July 1997 for the years ended 30 June 1995; 30 June 1996.

Objections

1 In the year ended 30 June 1995 the Commissioner has issued an assessment ascribing taxable income of $503,097 in respect of the taxpayer, Tawfik Awad personally. That assessable income is not referrable to the taxpayer personally but is income the property of Walter Hammond & Associates Pty Ltd and is income derived for and on behalf of the corporation in the conduct of its business.

2 The assessment made in respect of the year ending 30 June 1996 in the sum being taxable income of $4,012,306 is objected to in similar terms as outlined in paragraph 1 above.

3 To impose or ascribe taxable income in the amounts indicated in paragraphs 1 and 2 above is to impose upon the taxpayer an incorrect disposition of such monies which are not his property personally but are the property of the corporation and to do so is to act capriciously or arbitrarily and deprive the applicant of the opportunity of organising his business affairs to take account of revenue laws and organise his business affairs to take account of those laws in a manner conferring upon the taxpayer personally reduced rates of tax. For example, the taxpayer had only intended, in respect of income referrable to the corporation to take a fully franked dividend, should it be available to him, after the lodgment of amended returns by the corporation.

4 The imposition of additional tax was, under the circumstances not warranted as the monies said to be referrable to the taxpayer personally were not his property and that the imposition of a penalty, under the circumstances would be unduly harsh and oppressive.

5 The raising of amended assessments in respect of the same monies in respect of the corporation Walter Hammond Pty Ltd is excessive as is the imposition of penalties in respect of that taxpayer's affairs given that it raises a liability in respect of both entities and has the effect of imposing significant penalties twice, in respect of the same course of conduct.

6 The raising of assessments referred to in paragraphs 1 and 2 above by reference to information said by the taxpayer to be illegally obtained, namely listening device transcripts and information disseminated by the NSW Crime Commission is the exercise of powers conferred on the Commissioner constituting the exercise of powers in bad faith.

7 Having regard to the foregoing the Commissioner should set aside the amended assessments and penalties (x ref paragraphs 1-6 incl.)"



(c) Mr McGovern contended that having regard to the evidence (or lack of it) before the Tribunal, to amend the objections in the manner sought could not make any difference.



10. (a) Mr Levingston then made his closing submission. He said that the Applicant did not hear from Mr Lieberherr after Mr Lieberherr had filed the brief objection which is set out previously in these Reasons. Accordingly, so he said, the Applicant informed him, Mr Levingston, that he, the Applicant, did not have an opportunity to inform the Respondent that the income which gave rise to the amended assessments was derived by the company and not by him, the Applicant. Mr Levingston went on to submit that if the Applicant had been properly advised of his rights, and if his objections had been properly framed, the Respondent might have assessed the company only and not the Applicant. (At the commencement of the hearings, Mr Levingston had indicated that the Respondent had sought in effect to tax the same monies in the hands of each of the company and the Applicant). Mr Levingston then asked the Tribunal to grant an order under section 42D(1) or section 43(1)(c)(ii) of the AAT Act remitting the objection decisions back to the Respondent for reconsideration. It may be noted that Mr Levingston had at the commencement of the hearing foreshadowed the making of just such an application, although there was nothing in writing before the Tribunal indicating that he intended to do so. Put in other words, the matter before the Tribunal was its review of the objection decisions, and not an application to remit.

(b) It is to be noted also that Mr Levingston made a statement from the bar table as to what the Applicant had told him, and as to which there was no evidence whatever before the Tribunal. Nor was there any evidence before the Tribunal in support of the contention that the Respondent had sought to tax the same income in the hands of each of the Applicant and the company. The witness statement, while it includes as annexures returns by the Applicant, does not include returns by the company.



(c) Put succinctly and in general terms, Mr Levingston's closing submission consisted of a request for a remission order.



11. (a) I cannot accept, for the reasons set out in these Reasons, and in particular this clause 11, that there is any basis whatever on which I could or should remit the objection decisions back to the Respondent; to do so would, in my view, be completely purposeless.

(b) The objections by the Applicant were contained in the brief, and in the view of the Tribunal inadequate, letter dated 4 August 1997 (T31 at page 472). Following that letter, the Respondent wrote to the Applicant care of Mr Lieberherr on 28 August 1997 (T32 at pages 473 and 474) in the following terms:

(f) In all the circumstances it is clear to the Tribunal that despite the inadequacy of the objections by the Applicant, the Respondent was aware of the relevant facts, and clearly had no intention of assessing the company alone. To contend as did the Applicant that the Respondent would not have assessed the Applicant if he were aware of the facts is demonstrably untenable, and indeed all the evidence before the Tribunal, indicates the contrary.



(g) It is relevant to note that the Applicant in his return for the 1995 year reflected a salary of $12,000 from Awad Brothers Pty Limited with tax of $1,500 deducted (see annexure "A" to the witness statement). In respect of the 1996 year, the Applicant reflected a salary from Awad Brothers Pty Limited of $18,000 with $3,000 deducted (see annexure "B" to the witness statement); in the 1996 year, the Applicant also reflected a franked dividend paid by the company (referred to at page 11 of the witness statement as Walter Hammond & Associates Pty Limited) in an amount of $28,000 with an imputation credit of $13,791.



(h) The amounts referred to in subclause (g) are to be contrasted with paragraph (e) of the findings of fact in annexure "H" to the witness statement and including in particular, the fact that $117,060 was seized from the residence of the Applicant and that approximately $8 million in cash is alleged to have been exported out of Australia by the Applicant and his associates.



(i) That the Tribunal will not, and should not lightly, remit an objection to the decision maker for reconsideration appears (inter alia) from the decision of Deputy President Forgie of this Tribunal in Re Lavery and Registrar, Supreme Court of Queensland & Others (No. 2) (1996) 23 AAR 52.



(j) In all the circumstances, the application by the Applicant for a remission of the objection decisions to the Respondent for reconsideration under either section 42D(1) or section 43(1)(c)(ii) of the AAT Act must be dismissed.



12. (a) Mr McGovern in his closing reply, cited a number of reported cases. As he correctly contended, the onus is on the Applicant under section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) ("TAA") to establish that the assessments are excessive.

(b) There was, as set out previously, no evidence whatever in support of the proposition that the Respondent had issued assessments for the same amounts to two different taxpayers. But even if this were the case, the decision of the High Court in Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Richard Walter Pty Limited [1995] HCA 23; (1995) 183 CLR 168 indicates in clear terms that the Respondent is entitled to do so. See in this context the passage from pages 200 to 201 as follows (per Brennan J):



"Richard Walter submits that two persons cannot be severally liable each in its own right to have included in its individual assessment the same income for the same year. This is the submission which the unsuccessful taxpayer made in Richardson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation. It cannot be accepted now. The function of the Commissioner in making an assessment is to take a view of the facts, so far as a view can reasonably be taken from the information in his possession, and to apply the general provisions of the Act so as to arrive at the taxpayer's taxable income and to define the tax liability accordingly. In Bailey v Federal Commissioner of Taxation Barwick CJ said: "the process of assessment requires the application of the Act to the facts as known to and accepted by the Commissioner. He must of necessity, as part of that process, adopt a view of the relevant facts." Subject to what follows, I would respectfully agree.

It must be remembered that the Commissioner's function is administrative, not judicial. The power to assess is, as s167 shows, not limited to cases where the Commissioner has enough information on which to make a positive finding of fact. The Commissioner is not required to determine on the balance of probabilities that one person rather than another is the person subject to the tax liability in respect of the particular income. Where the facts known to the Commissioner are such that he is unable to determine which of two or more persons is liable to tax on the same item of income in the same year, he may adopt the view in the case of any or all of those persons that there is a substantial possibility that the item of income is assessable income of that person. If that view is adopted in respect of two or more of those persons, he may validly assess each of them to tax. The making of an assessment on that view of the facts, provided it is not for the purpose of double recovery of the tax imposed by the relevant Taxing Act, is in my opinion a bona fide attempt to exercise the power to assess so that the assessment either is valid or is validated by s175. And the notice of assessment attracts the protection of s177(1)."



(c) Mr McGovern next cited the decision of the High Court in FJ Bloemen Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 27; (1981) 147 CLR 360. Bloemen's case establishes that once the Respondent takes advantage of s177(1) by issuing assessments, the taxpayer is precluded from denying that the Respondent has made the assessment or that in doing so, he has not complied with all necessary formalities. The taxpayer, in such event, is confined to a challenge against the amount assessed. The following extract from pages 373 to 375 will suffice to amplify this point (per Mason and Wilson JJ):



"What then is the effect of s.177(1)? Does production of an appropriate notice of assessment or document provide conclusive evidence that an assessment was actually made, or does it merely provide conclusive evidence that an assessment, if made, was duly made? Although it was said that the second alternative reflected a more natural reading of the provision, our view is that the language of the section is equally susceptible of either reading and that the answer is to be found, not so much in the language, as in the context and in the scope and purpose of the Act.

We reject the submission that the appellants' case is supported by dicta in George v Federal Commissioner of Taxation, notably at p.207, and McAndrew v Federal Commissioner of Taxation. Correctly understood, the judgments in these cases show that the language of the sub-section, admittedly difficult, is to be read in its context so as to give effect to the scope and purpose of the Act. The distinction drawn by s.177(1) is between the procedure by which the taxable income is ascertained (the "due making" of the assessment of which the production of the notice of assessment is conclusive evidence) and the taxpayer's substantive liability to tax ("the amount and all the particulars of the assessment" which may be challenged in Pt V proceedings). See, for example, the passage in George and the comment, at p. 207:

"Obviously the 'due making of the assessment' was intended to cover all procedural steps, other than those if any going to substantive liability and so contributing to the excessiveness of the assessment, the thing which is put in contest by an appeal."

In McAndrew, Dixon CJ, McTiernan and Webb JJ noted that, although the existence of conditions giving rise to the exercise of the power to amend an assessment might be thought otherwise to be part of the "due making" of an assessment, the consequence of this interpretation would be to deny to the taxpayer the right to challenge the exercise of the power. Their Honours went on to say:

"Section 177 [sic] (2) and (3) impose certain conditional time bars which in this dichotomy seem evidently to belong to substantive liability. From this it follows that fulfilment of the conditions which bring a case within s.170(2) is part of the matter governed by the words of exception in s.177(1), viz 'except in proceedings on appeal against the assessment'. An appeal, however, is a proceeding given by statute to a taxpayer for the purpose of impugning an assessment otherwise conclusively imposing liability upon him."

These statements do not support the appellants. They acknowledge that the existence of the antecedent conditions is not part of the "due making" of an amended assessment, but they do not say that the ascertainment of the taxable income also stands outside that concept. Furthermore, as the power to amend, unlike the Commissioner's power to make his initial assessment, is conditioned in this way, it is natural that s.177(1) should be so interpreted as to permit the taxpayer to challenge the existence of the conditions giving rise to the exercise of a power which imposes on him a new substantive liability to tax. There is no like reason for reading the sub-section so as to allow the taxpayer to contest the making of an original assessment, when a notice of assessment has been served, and the making of that assessment is not similarly conditioned, the taxpayer being entitled to contest his substantive liability to tax in Pt V proceedings, without contesting the making of the initial assessment.

Nor are the appellants assisted by the comment of Lowe J. in Kellow-Falkiner Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation. There his Honour, referring to s.39(1)(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922, said:

"It is the "due making" of the assessment of which the due production of the notice is to be conclusive evidence, not of the validity of the assessment when duly made. I think that this sub-section is dealing with the formalities and procedure of the making, and is not even affecting to deal and does not deal with the validity of a purported assessment, though all the formalities of procedure in the making have been observed."

His Honour was considering a case in which the Commissioner had assessed the taxpayer to additional tax, applying s.7 of the amending Act of 1924 which had no application to the income year in question. The application was relevant to the taxpayer's substantive liability. It may also be observed that both in Kellow-Falkiner and Hoffnung the document which was said to be a notice of an assessment bore evidence, if not on its face then in the supporting documentation, which denied it that character. Visual inspection alone was sufficient to compel the conclusion that the document was not a notice of assessment for the purpose of the relevant Act.

An explicit and, in our view, correct statement of the effect of s.177(1) was made by Taylor J in McAndrew. For the reasons there expressed his Honour concluded that "s.177(1) was intended to make it impossible for a taxpayer, in proceedings other than appeal against it, to challenge an assessment on any ground". He conceded that the word "excessive" in s.190(b) was inappropriate. However, he considered that an assessment "made in purported but not justifiable exercise of a statutory power" could properly be described as "excessive" .

This interpretation gives expression to the policy which underlies, and is manifest in, the statutory provisions. The effect of this policy is that, once the Commissioner takes advantage of s.177(1) by producing an appropriate document, the taxpayer is precluded from contesting that the Commissioner has made an assessment or that in making the assessment he has complied with the statutory formalities. The taxpayer is entitled to dispute his substantive liability to tax in proceedings under Pt V."



(d) Bloemen's case has been cited with approval on numerous occasions. See in particular Madden v Madden & Ors (1996) 65 FCR 354, a decision of the Full Federal Court, where Foster J said at page 385:



"Undoubtedly the Commissioner's contention received strong support from the decision of the High Court in F J Bloemen Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) [1981] HCA 27; (1981) 147 CLR 360 (Bloemen). In that case the taxpayer had sought a declaration that the relevant income tax assessments were void and of no effect in law in that (inter alia) they did not "issue as a result of a bona fide exercise of the powers vested in the [Commissioner] [to issue assessments]" and also in that the assessments were issued "in an attempt inter alia to place the defendant in a position: (i) to support the issue of notices purportedly issued pursuant to s.218; (ii) to harass the plaintiff". It may be noted that the allegations are similar to those made in the present proceedings."



(e) And in George v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 21; (1952) 86 CLR 183, a majority of the High Court (Dixon CJ, McTiernan, Williams, Webb, Fullagar JJ) said at pages 203 and 204:



"But, even were it true that the commissioner must, upon the hearing of the appeal, affirmatively prove by evidence that he formed a judgment of the amount of the income upon which the appellant ought to be taxed, it could not be part of his case to establish the facts upon which he acted in forming the judgment or the grounds on which he proceeded, the materials before him, or the reasoning actuating him. The need supposed of showing that he formed such a judgment could be no ground for requiring particulars of the sources of the taxable income ascribed by the assessment of the appellant. The assumption made, however, has no foundation. The formation of the judgment as to what is the amount of the income that ought to be taxed is no condition precedent to the power to assess. It is part of the very process of assessment itself. Section 166 and s.167 do not prescribe distinct duties or functions. They combine to show what the commissioner may or must do in performing his single duty of arriving at an assessment. Section 166 on its own terms covers cases where the commissioner depends exclusively on sources other than a return. It says that he is to make his assessment from (1) the returns, (2) from any other information, or (3) from any one or more of these sources. Clearly enough under s.166 the commissioner can make an assessment which does not adhere to the income returned and yet to do so must involve some want of satisfaction with the return. Section 167 is epexegetical to s.166. It is not an independent power. What it does is to mention with particularity three situations which might arise in carrying out the duty imposed by s.166, and to direct how in those situations the commissioner shall proceed for the purpose of s.166. Just as under s.166 considered alone the commissioner ascertains the amount of the taxable income and thus assesses it so does he under s.167, used in aid of s.166, ascertain the amount upon which, in his judgment, income tax ought to be levied and thus assesses it. By definition "assessment" means the ascertainment of the amount of the taxable income, and of the tax payable thereon. This is the view of ss.166 and 167 adopted by Williams J in McEvoy v Federal Commissioner of Taxation. The fact is that unless the taxpayer discharges the burden laid upon him by s.190(b) of proving that this ascertainment or judgment is excessive, he cannot succeed and it can be no part of the duty of the commissioner to establish affirmatively what judgment he formed, much less the grounds of it, and even less still the truth of the facts affording the grounds. Yet that is what is involved when the demand for particulars of the sources alleged of the appellant's income is justified by reference to s.167. It is an error to treat the formation by the commissioner of a judgment as to the amount of the taxable income as if it were not the ascertainment of the taxable income which constitutes assessment or a necessary part of that process and as if it were but the fulfilment of a condition precedent to the power or authority to assess. If, however, it were a condition precedent the question would at once arise whether the fulfilment of the condition was not part of "the due making of the assessment" of which s.177(1) makes the production of a notice of assessment conclusive evidence. But of this it is unnecessary to speak specifically. It is unnecessary to do so not only because the reasons already given are enough to dispose of the request for particulars of the sources of the appellant's additional taxable income, but also because a similar question must now be dealt with in relation to the additional particulars which the appellant seeks in reference to the year of income ended 30 June 1947, that is to say, particulars as to the person or officer who formed or made a judgment under s.167."



(f) And in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco [1990] HCA 3; (1990) 168 CLR 614, Brennan J said at page 623:



"The ground of objection on which the taxpayer here relies is error in the formation of a judgment as to the amount on which tax ought to be levied. But mere error in the formation of that judgment by the Commissioner does not warrant the setting aside of the amount assessed. Given the validity of the exercise of the power to make an assessment under s.167(b), the ultimate question is whether the amount of the assessment is excessive. The amount of the assessment might not be excessive in fact, though the reasons which led to the assessment were erroneous. In George's Case the Full Court said:

"the law has always been taken to be that in an appeal from an assessment the burden lies upon the taxpayer of establishing affirmatively that the amount of taxable income for which he has been assessed exceeds the actual taxable income which he has derived during the year of income."

Kitto J., from whose judgment the appeal in George's Case was brought, said:

"[Section] 190(b) places the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive upon the appellant; and in order to carry that burden he must necessarily exclude by his proof all sources of income except those which he admits. His case must be that he did not derive from any source taxable income to the amount of the assessment."

Counsel for the taxpayer invited the Court to overrule George's Case in so far as it requires a taxpayer to prove that the amount of taxable income assessed exceeds the taxpayer's actual taxable income, but that proposition does not rest on George's Case alone. At base, it rests on s.190(b) but it is acknowledged in McAndrew's Case, McCormack's Case and F J Bloemen Pty Ltd (at pages earlier cited). In this respect, George's Case is not open to doubt. It follows that Wilcox J was right in holding that "the task for the taxpayer, upon an appeal or a review under Pt V of the Act, is to show that the amount of money for which tax is levied by a particular notice of assessment exceeds the actual substantive liability of the taxpayer"."



(g) In Ma v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 92 ATC 4373, Burchett J said at page 4377:



"Whether, ultimately, Mr Ma should be believed is not, of course, a matter for me. Nor, if it were, could it be decided upon the written record alone. The decision must take account of the onus under s.190(b). But if a taxpayer denies any undisclosed source of income, provides acceptable evidence of how he spends his time, and demonstrates a reasonable explanation for any appearance of the possession of assets, he will generally discharge his burden of proof unless some positive reason is shown why he is to be disbelieved. Any other view would introduce a degree of arbitrariness into liability for tax. In the present case, the very lack of a clear refutation of the applicant's case at present (I emphasize the words "at present", because on a rehearing the picture may change colour altogether) reinforces my impression that the Tribunal saw the matter entirely or substantially through the spectacles of its view of the law."



(h) The decision in Galea v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 90 ATC 5060 indicates that even where the Commissioner attempts to establish a case and fails to do so, he is entitled nevertheless to rely on the fact that the onus of proof is on the taxpayer. See Hill J at page 5067 as follows:



"To the extent that the applicant seeks to rely upon the description of what the Commissioner did here as being an attempt to mount a positive case, it is not clear to me at all why this has any relevance. As is clear from Dalco (supra) and as the Tribunal itself said, it was not necessary for the Commissioner to seek to establish affirmatively that the applicant's assessable income was at least a particular figure. The fact that the Commissioner sought so to do and failed has no bearing, at the end of the day, on the question whether the applicant has discharged the onus of showing, as he is required by sec. 190(b) of the Act to show, that the assessment is excessive. The Commissioner's failure to establish a positive case, if that is what he sought to do, leaves the Tribunal in no different position that it would have been in if the Commissioner had not sought at all to advance a positive case."



(i) And the fact that there may be alternative assessments against different taxpayers does not alter the position. See Davies J in Trustee of the Balmain Trust v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 98 ATC 4433 at pages 4440 and 4441 as follows:



"The assessments against the Trustee of the Balmain Trust and against Mr Ward had been issued as alternative assessments. The Tribunal was not required to be satisfied in each case that the assessments were correct. The onus was on the taxpayer in each case to show that the assessments were wrong and excessive. The Tribunal was entitled to take the view that the Trustee of the Balmain Trust had not satisfied the onus of proof cast upon it if the Tribunal was not satisfied as to what the facts of the case were. The principle to be applied is that stated by Brennan J in DFC of T v Richard Walter Pty Ltd 95 ATC 4067; [1995] HCA 23; (1994-1995) 183 CLR 168 where his Honour said at ATC 4083; CLR 201-202:

"The fact that a tax liability remains outstanding against two taxpayers pending the ascertainment of the taxpayer truly liable is no bar to the exercise of the power to assess both to tax in respect of the same income. As Dixon J observed in Richardson v FC of T [1932] HCA 67; (1932) 2 ATD 19 at 24; [1932] HCA 67; (1931-1932) 48 CLR 192 at 207 when upholding the validity of an assessment to tax against a second person while the first person's assessment remained on foot and unamended:

"it was not unnatural that [the Commissioner] should delay relieving one of two persons whom he considered culpable until the liability of the other was established."

The co-existence of tax liabilities in two or more taxpayers in respect of the same income is, as Dixon J observed, ibid, attended with difficulty. Sometimes the difficulty will be removed by the objection, review and appeal procedures where the taxpayer will establish the facts in order to establish that the assessment is excessive.

The raising of concurrent assessments of two or more taxpayers to tax in respect of the same item of income has not hitherto been regarded as beyond the powers of the Commissioner. ... The appropriateness of alternative assessments to tax of two taxpayers in respect of the same item of income was recognized in a dictum of this Court in DFC of T v Moorebank Pty Ltd 88 ATC 4443 at 4448; [1988] HCA 29; (1987-1988) 165 CLR 55 at 67. And the courts, if not the Commissioner, can diminish the difficulty of concurrent assessments by ensuring that there is no double recovery of tax (DFC of T v Faint [1988] 2 Qd R 494 at 497-498)."

As his Honour pointed out, it is not necessary that the decision-maker, the Commissioner or the Tribunal, should be positively satisfied of the taxpayer's liability before raising an assessment or affirming it. Alternative assessments may be issued where the Commissioner is uncertain as to which taxpayer is liable. The obligation then is on each taxpayer who has been assessed to satisfy the onus of proof cast upon a taxpayer by s14ZZK of the Administration Act to show that the assessment was excessive. It would be wrong, of course, for the Commissioner to recover two amounts of tax. The Commissioner should ensure that there is no double recovery of tax. As Brennan J said, that is an obligation which the courts would enforce, although I assume that they would never need to do so."



13. (a) The annexures to the witness statement are evidence before this Tribunal, because the Applicant made them so by submitting the witness statement.

(b) It is clear, as set out previously, that the small amounts returned by the Applicant are inconsistent with, for example, the large sum taken from his own home.



(c) The very fact that the section 218 notices elicited substantial sums from each of the Applicant and the Company suggests that each of the Applicant and the Company is individually liable for tax.



(d) The Applicant has chosen not to give evidence. It is hard to understand how he could have hoped to discharge his onus without giving evidence. Evidence by his solicitor together with a statement by his solicitor as to what the Applicant told him can hardly be of much assistance. It may be noted that Mr Levingston did not reply to Mr McGovern's closing submissions.



(e) As to the Amended Objections, I agree with Mr McGovern's submissions that:



(1) In respect of clauses 1, 2, 3 and 4, there was no evidence before the Tribunal which would support those amendments;



(2) In respect of clause 5 there is no evidence in support and in any event and even if there was, the decisions in Richard Walter and Balmain Trust indicate that that evidence would not have assisted the Applicant;



(3) In respect of clause 6, the decision in Bloemen and other cases cited above precludes an attack of this nature and in any event there is no evidence whatever of bad faith.



(f) Accordingly, the grant of the application to amend the objections would not have assisted the Applicant in any event and assuming the grant of that application, the result would not differ.



14. The Applicant has, of course, failed to satisfy the onus on him under s14ZZK of TAA. Indeed, it can hardly be said that he has made any real or serious attempt to do so.

15. In the circumstances, the objection decisions under review are affirmed.
