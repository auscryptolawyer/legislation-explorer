Re Nashaat Kozman and Commissioner of Superannuation [1989] AATA 22; 4900 Superannuation 20 ALD 631 (3 February 1989)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: NASHAAT KOZMAN

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V87/404

AAT No. 4900

Superannuation

20 ALD 631

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mrs J.R. Dwyer(1) (Senior Member), G. Brewer(1) (Member) and Dr D Sutherland(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - BCC issued under s.16(10) after applicant retired on ground of invalidity eight and a half years after entry into superannuation scheme - whether time limits or other qualifying factors can be read into s.16(10) imposing obligation upon Commissioner to consider report of medical examination in reasonable time.

Superannuation - Social worker DSS - whether mental or physical conditions existed at time he became an "eligible employee" - three accidents prior to entry - further accidents afterwards including fall from palm tree resulting in fracture dislocation of elbow - remission of symptoms of soft tissue injuries sustained in pre-entry accidents - no evidence of other injuries in those accidents. - Compression of ulna nerves on both sides - surgery to release nerves - failure to return to work after surgery - evidence of frustrating stressful working environment resulting from a heavy workload and discrimination due to ethnic background, obsessional personality - decompensation due to work stresses - development of neurotic symptoms or anxiety hysteria - retirement not "connected with" soft tissue injuries sustained in pre-entry accidents.

Superannuation Act 1976 ss.16(2), (3), (4) and (10), 66(2)(c), 154 and 184(3)

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975

Re Neal and Commissioner for Superannuation (1986) 5 AAR 202

Neal v Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 13 ALD 460

Re Tiknaz and Director-General of Social Services (1981) 4 ALN 44

Re Harrison and the Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN 352

Re Y and Commissioner for Superannuation delivered 5 March 1982

Neal and Secretary, Department of Transport [1980] FCA 45; (1980) 3 ALD 97

Commissioner for Superannuation v Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 63 ALR 237 and [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 8 FCR 153

Commissioner for Superannuation v Benham unreported decision delivered 14 April 1988.

HEARING

MELBOURNE

3:2:1989

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside and in substitution the Tribunal decides that the Benefit Classification Certificate issued under s.16(10) of the Act, is of no effect and that it be set aside as invalid.

DECISION

This is an application to review a decision of the Acting Commissioner for Superannuation (the "Commissioner") made 4 June 1987 on a reconsideration under s.154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 (the "Act"). The Commissioner decided to:

"(i) VARY the decision taken by a delegate

of the Commissioner on 15 Jan 1986 to

issue pursuant to sub-section 16(10)

of the Act in respect of Mr Kozman, a

benefit classification certificate

specifying the condition "History of

Back Injury" by substituting for that

condition the conditions "Ligamentous

injury to the cervical spine" and

"Ligamentous injury to lumbar spine";

and

(ii) CONFIRM the decision taken by the same

delegate on 15 Jan 1986 to be of the

opinion under paragraph 66(2)(c) of

the Act, that the incapacity which was

the ground for Mr Kozman's retirement

was caused or substantially

contributed to, by the condition(s)

specified in the benefit

classification certificate or by

condition(s) connected with that condition(s)."

2. At the hearing Mr A Adams of Counsel appeared for Mr Kozman and Mr R. Whittear, Assistant Commissioner for Superannuation, Reconsideration and Review Branch appeared for the respondent. The hearing took five days and 11 doctors and 5 other witnesses were called as well as the applicant.

3. The scheme of the Act in relation to the issue of BCC's was clearly explained by Davies J and Dr Travers in their reasons for decision in Re Neal and Commissioner for Superannuation (1986) 5 AAR 202 at 202-204 ("Re Neal"). For present purposes it is only necessary to set out in full the material parts of ss.16(10) and 66(2)(c) of the Act. They read as follows:

"16 (10) Where--

(a) a person who is an eligible employee

dies or ceases to be an eligible

employee by reason of retirement on

the ground of invalidity--

................

(ii) if he has undergone such

an examination or

examinations-before the

report or reports of the

result or results of the

examination or

examinations has or have

been considered by the

Commissioner;

..................

(b) the Commissioner is not

satisfied that his death or the

incapacity which was the ground for

his retirement, was not caused, and

was not substantially contributed to,

by a physical or mental condition or

conditions of the person that existed

at the time the person became an

eligible employee, or by a physical

or mental condition or conditions

connected with such a condition or

such conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of

the person a benefit classification

certificate in which there is or are

specified the physical or mental condition or

conditions of the person which, in the

opinion of the Commissioner, existed at the

time the person became an eligible employee

and, for the purposes of this Act, the

certificate shall be deemed to have been in

force in respect of the person immediately

before his death or retirement."

Section 66(2) provides for payment of reduced superannuation benefits where :- -

"(a) a person ceases to be an eligible

employee by reason of retirement on the

ground of invalidity before attaining his

maximum retiring age;

(b) there was in force in respect of the

person, immediately before the person's

retirement, a benefit classification

certificate; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that

the incapacity which was the ground for

his retirement was caused, or was

substantially contributed to, by a

physical or mental condition or

conditions specified in the certificate

or by a physical or mental condition or

conditions connected with such a

condition or such conditions,"

4. Mr Kozman started work as a temporary social worker with the Department of Social Security ("DSS") early in 1976 and became a permanent public servant on 2 December 1976. He was medically examined for the purpose of determining his eligibility to join the Superannuation Scheme under the Act on 7 February 1977. He retired on the ground of invalidity in November 1985. When he decided to accept the recommendation that he be retired, he had been advised by letter (A19) from the Manager Personnel Services as to the superannuation benefits he would receive. That letter set out two options, either 70 per cent of final salary, or 50 per cent of final salary, plus a lump sum refund of accumulated contributions. The letter also stated:

"However you are reminded that in accordance

with Section 184(3) of the Superannuation Act

the amount of pension may be varied by the

Commissioner for Superannuation."

That sentence however had no application to Mr Kozman, as he had no physical or mental conditions that were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act, as referred to in s.184(3) of the Act.

5. The first issue which arose at the hearing concerned the power of the Commissioner to issue a benefit classification certificate ("BCC") under s.16(10) of the Act after Mr Kozman's retirement, notwithstanding that he had become a permanent public servant and an "eligible employee" eight and a half years earlier. Mr Whittear did not choose to argue the justice of s.16(10), which he submitted required the Commissioner to issue a BCC even though eight and a half years had elapsed since Mr Kozman became a contributor to the Superannuation Scheme. He relied on the authorities as establishing that there are no time limits on the Commissioner's duty to act under s.16(10).

6. The Full Court of the Federal Court in Neal v Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 13 ALD 460 (Neal's Case) rejected a submission that there is an obligation on the Commissioner to consider a report of a medical examination in a reasonable time. Fox and Neaves JJ said at 466:

"In considering the argument presented to the

court on behalf of the applicant it is

essential to bear well in mind that ss 16(4)

and 16(10) do not simply confer powers on the

Commissioner - they each impose a statutory

duty upon him to issue a benefit

classification certificate whenever the

prescribed circumstances exist. The language

in which the provisions are cast leaves no

room for doubt that that was the

legislature's intention. Yet, the essence of

the applicant's argument is that the duty

which each sub-section imposes does not

survive the expiration of the period

ascertained by reference to the temporal

limitations which the applicant submits

should be read into the provisions. To

recognise that as being the essence of the

applicant's argument is to demonstrate the

fallacy on which the argument is based

.................

The operation of s 16(10) depends on the

non-occurrence of one or other of the events

for which ss 16(2), (3) and (4) provide. In

terms, the sub-section is not otherwise

relevantly qualified. It does not require an

inquiry to be made why the circumstances upon

which the operation of the sub-section

depends did not occur: the provision comes

into operation once the fact is established."

At page 467 their Honours added:

"It may well be postulated that, as a matter

of due administration, the duty imposed on

the Commissioner by s 16(4) should be carried

out as soon as it conveniently may be after a

person becomes an eligible employee. But, to

say that is to give no support to the

proposition that there should be implied into

the sub-section a time limit which the

parliament has not seen fit by express

language to impose."

Beaumont J dissented and said at page 468/9:

"In my opinion, s 16(10) should be construed

as capable of application where, by reason of

the employee's death or retirement for

invalidity, the process of implementing the

procedures contemplated by ss 16(2), (3) and

(4) is interrupted but not otherwise....It

cannot be assumed that the legislature

intended that in the performance of his

functions under s 16, the Commissioner could

put the file aside for as long as his lack of

resources compelled him to do so (in this

case, and in Miller's case [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 63 ALR 237

at 245, more than two years) and, upon

discovery of the employee's death, seek to

invoke the restrictive provisions of

s 16(10). Parliament may be presumed to have

intended that a coercive power such as the

power to issue a benefit classification

certificate would be exercised promptly if at

all:" see Bennion, op cit, p 785. It is

difficult to imagine that parliament intended

that the Commissioner could make a policy

decision which had the effect that the

results of the medical examination would not

be considered for years and, if death or

retirement occurred in the meantime, the

Commissioner could use the retrospective

powers conferred by s 16(10). On the

contrary, it may be presumed that the

legislature intended that s 16(10) operate in

the special, limited circumstances already

mentioned, that is to say, where the

procedures under ss 16(2), (3) and (4) which

are in the course of implementation are

interrupted by the supervening event of death

or retirement."

7. In his final address Mr Whittear told the Tribunal from his knowledge as Assistant Commissioner, Reconsideration and Review Branch that the delay of eight and a half years before a decision was made whether to issue a BCC in respect of Mr Kozman, was "a result of a policy decision" of the Commissioner, as to priority of work because of the introduction of the Act in 1976, with no additional staff. He said at p 385 of the transcript:

"....there are some 80,000 returns from the

period 1976 to 1979 that have never been

examined, and this is one of those cases."

8. This case therefore falls within the class described by Beaumont J as outside s.16(10). However the High Court on 11 December 1987 refused an application for special leave to appeal, which relied on the reasons of Beaumont J. In these circumstances the Tribunal must follow the decision of the Federal Court in Neal's Case notwithstanding that the delay in this case is not two years as in Neal's Case but over eight years. We are therefore obliged to accept Mr Whittear's submission that no time limits or other qualifications can be read in s.16(10).

9. The circumstances specified in s.16(10)(a) are therefore established. Mr Kozman ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity. He had undergone examination as required under s.16(2) of the Act, but retired before the report of that examination had been considered by the Commissioner (16(10)(a)(ii)). Section 16(10) then requires the Commissioner to issue a BCC specifying a condition which in his opinion existed at the time Mr Kozman became an eligible employee, if he is not satisfied that Mr Kozman's incapacity, which was the ground for his retirement, was not caused and not substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that existed at the time the person became an eligible employee or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions (para 16(10)(b)). Once a BCC is issued under s.16(10) it is deemed to have been in force immediately before the retirement.

10. We must therefore decide what conditions, if any, existed at the time Mr Kozman became an eligible employee on 2 December 1976. There is evidence that prior to December 1976 Mr Kozman had been involved in three accidents. They are:

Accident 1 - 17 October 1975

Accident 2 - 28 January 1976

Accident 3 - 22 October 1976

Accident 1-7 October 1975

11. A vehicle Mr Kozman was driving was involved in a front-end collision at the intersection of Lonsdale and Queen Streets, Melbourne when another vehicle failed to give way (see Police Accident Report A6). Mr Kozman attended the Royal Melbourne Hospital ("RMH") after the accident and was given a certificate to take a week off work because of a bruised left leg (A9), but as he was a final year social work student he simply continued with his studies.

12. Mr Silley, an orthopaedic surgeon saw Mr Kozman on 5 December 1975, less than two months after accident 1, at the request of Mr Kozman's solicitor. He obtained a history of headache soon after the accident, pain in the neck and the middle of the spine and bad nerves for a few weeks after the accident. Mr Kozman described his condition to Mr Silley as follows:

"1. The left lower leg is still sore.

2. The right side of the neck is still sore.

3. He has occipito-frontal headaches daily,

the duration of which varies.

4. He has discomfort in his back when he is

holding any objects."

Dr Silley obtained X-rays of the cervical spine, lumbro-sacral spine and left leg. That of the cervical spine showed slight reduction in the vertical dimension of the body of C5 which was considered more likely to be developmental than due to injury and also slight narrowing of the C5-C6 disc space when the spine was flexed. Mr Silley did not think that Mr Kozman was very seriously injured in the motor car accident. He considered that he suffered a degree of whiplash injury to the neck and that the prognosis for his injuries was good and he should settle down without any significant permanent partial disabilities.

Accident 2 - 28 January 1976

13. Mr Kozman was the driver of a vehicle which was hit in the rear while stationary in Ballarat Road, Footscray. The incident occurred when he was a temporary social worker with DSS and on his way to a meeting. He continued with his work that day but on the advice of more senior staff attended RMH that evening, or the following evening. A report from RMH dated 24 January 1986 (T17/135) contains details from the records relating to Mr Kozman's presentation there. It states:

"The patient presented to the Emergency

Department of this hospital on 28.1.76 at

23.47 hours. It was noted that on 10:30 a.m.

on the day prior to presentation, a car had

run into his stationary car from behind. He

had suffered no loss of consciousness

He was complaining of back pain and

difficulty in breathing, as well as pain in

the back of the neck and headache.

On examination he was conscious and well

orientated. His B.P. was 130/80.

Examination was normal apart from some

limitation of straight leg raising by pain to

45 degrees on each side. Cervical spine,

lumbar spine and skull x-rays were performed

and no abnormality was demonstrated.

A diagnosis of muscular aches was made and

the patient was discharged home at 03.16

hours."

14. Almost six months later, on 6 July 1976, Mr Kozman attended his general practitioner Dr Yazbek complaining of intermittent occipital headache since being involved in the accident on 28 January 1976. He was prescribed Stematil (T19/155) and did not attend Dr Yazbek's surgery again because of headaches until 27 June 1979. (T21/178)

Accident 3 - 22 October 1976

15. Mr Kozman fell down some stairs during a home visit in the course of his employment with DSS and suffered bruising to the right knee, right wrist and lower back. He attended Dr Yazbek on 28 October 1986 and had an X-ray performed by Dr Johnston on the following day. The X-ray showed no abnormality in the thoracic or lumbro-sacral spines or the knee joint. Dr Yazbek certified him unfit to work for five days. His note reads as follows:

"States, he fell downstairs whilst doing a

home visit for a Department client on

22/10/1976. He has been working but has been

having constant pain in the spine and right

knee. Examination revealed tenderness over

T8 and L3, lower part of sacrum and the right

knee joint.

Xrays of the thoraco-lumbar spine and right

knee were normal. Treated with rest,

shortwave therapy, Digesic tablets, Lasonil cream."

16. Those three accidents give rise to the submission of the respondent that Mr Kozman had a physical or mental condition, or conditions, which existed at the time he became an eligible employee and which caused, or substantially contributed to, or was connected with, the incapacity which was the ground of his retirement in November 1985. It is not clear what physical or mental condition can be said to have existed as at 2 December 1976 as a result of these accidents. X-rays after all three accidents revealed no fractures or other bony injuries. As to accident 1, the diagnosis of Mr Silley in December 1975 was of soft tissue or whiplash injury to the neck causing soreness in the right trapezius muscle area and headaches, soft tissue injury to the back and a haematoma to the left lower leg. He expected them all to resolve eventually. As to accident 2, the diagnosis made at RMH was of muscular aches. Dr Yazbek six months later obtained a history of intermittent occipital headache since the accident which could again be a result of a whiplash or soft tissue injury to the neck, but there is no evidence that they persisted after July 1976. Accident 3 gave rise to pain and tenderness in the back and right knee.

17. Mr Whittear conceded in his final address that there was no evidence on which the Tribunal could find that Mr Kozman suffered from a degenerative condition of the spine as at 2 December 1976. There was in evidence a report of a discogram performed on 19 October 1984 (A8) but there was considerable conflict of medical opinion as to whether even at that date the discogram showed damaged discs, such as to explain Mr Kozman's neck pain. The issues for determination are whether the soft tissue injuries suffered in accidents 1, 2 and 3 still existed as at 2 December 1976 and if so whether they caused or substantially contributed to, or were connected with a condition which caused or substantially contributed to the incapacity which led to retirement.

18. None of the three accidents appears to have been serious. After accident 1 Mr Kozman took no time off his studies. After accident 2 he continued with his work and did not attend RMH until late that evening or the following evening. He had three days of sick leave in early February 1976 which may have been related to the accident. After accident 3, Mr Kozman worked for five days before he saw Dr Yazbek. He then had nine days off work according to the sick leave records and saw Dr Yazbek again on 3 November 1976 and 9 November 1976. (See T8/99).

19. This history provides no evidence on which to base a finding that a condition related to accident 1, 2 or 3 existed as at 2 December 1976. Mr Whittear submitted that the Tribunal is entitled to take account of later events in so far as they shed light on the conditions which existed in December 1976. We accept that submission which finds support in Re Tiknaz and Director-General of Social Services (1981) 4 ALN 44 and a number of more recent Tribunal decisions.

20. It is therefore appropriate to look also at the sick leave records for subsequent years (T8 98/99 and T25/224). In 1977 the only sick leave taken appears to have been:

26. 4.77 1 day - flu

14. 6 - 17. 6.77 4 days - asthmatic bronchitis

2.12 - 6.12.77 3 days - fatigue hypertension

19.12 & 20.12.77 2 days - condition not specified

In 1978 the sick leave records are illegible but according to exhibit A22 there was only one period of absence of 3 weeks from 10 July 1978 to 2 August 1978 for chest pain and dizziness. Mr Kozman had 4 weeks of sick leave in June/July 1979 due to neck injury. His first very extended period of sick leave was approximately 25 weeks following a fall from a palm tree on 29 March 1980. Mr Kozman had climbed the tree at his church to cut branches for Palm Sunday. He estimated the distance he fell as 8 metres. He fractured his elbow in the fall. After that he had a further motor car accident while still on sick leave. He then had a total of some seven weeks sick leave in 1981, approximately eight weeks in 1982 and approximately 5 weeks in 1983. In 1984 he had some 8 weeks off in January and February and then very little sick leave before he was admitted to hospital for an operation to release his ulna nerves, in October 1984. He has not worked since the operation.

21. That history does not point to the existence of soft tissue injuries to the back or neck in December 1976, or to their contributing to retirement in 1985. The only possible connection would be if the neck injury in 1979 was an aggravation of a neck injury which first occurred in 1975 or 1976 and which continued to exist right through to 1985.

22. The specialist who treated Mr Kozman in 1979 was Mr MacIntosh. He is an orthopaedic surgeon. He obtained a history on 5 June 1979 of headache and neck pain off and on for 12 months, which had recently become constant. On 28 June 1979 he saw Mr Kozman again. Mr Kozman recounted that he had woken up with terrible pain, and headaches. He also felt dizzy and his hands were numb. Mr MacIntosh arranged for Mr Kozman to be admitted to hospital and treated with bed rest and physiotherapy. He saw Mr Kozman again on 17 July 1979 when he was still complaining of some pain and headaches but he had a good range of movement in his neck. He recommended that Mr Kozman return to work on 30 July 1979. Mr MacIntosh saw Mr Kozman once more in November 1979, after he had woken up with a "terrible" pain in his neck and then not again until after the fall from the palm tree in March 1980.

23. When Mr Kozman saw Mr MacIntosh in 1979, he gave him a history which included accident 2 on 28 January 1976 but not accidents 1 and 3. Mr MacIntosh provided certificates and reports in which he expressed the view that the neck pain in 1979 was directly related to the motor car accident in 1976. When Mr MacIntosh was given a fuller history including accidents 1 and 3 he said that he did not know what relationship Mr Kozman's condition in 1979 had to the injuries sustained in 1975 and 1976, but that Mr Kozman should have recovered from muscular strains in 1975 and 1976, by 1979. He said that if the motor accident in 1976 was a cause of the symptoms in 1979, then they would be due to either disc or joint damage that was persisting.

24. There is however no evidence that Mr Kozman sustained disc or joint damage in accidents 1, 2 or 3 and there is evidence which suggests that he was free of signs or symptoms of soft tissue injuries by 2 December 1976 and remained so until at least mid 1978.

25. First, there is the evidence of Mr Kozman himself. He said that he did not suffer significantly after accidents 1 or 2 and that after accident 2 he only went to RMH because he was advised he should do so by a person at DSS State headquarters. He said he took only a few of the Aspalgin tablets he had been given at RMH. He considered that his headaches after accident 1 could have been due to anxiety about his exams, as well as to the accident and said that they passed quickly.

26. We consider that accidents 1 and 2 may appear more important than they really were, because of the fact that Mr Kozman attended RMH after them and saw Mr Silley, in regard to a common law claim, after accident 1. Mr Hooper's evidence is that it is not unusual for people to attend doctors after accidents as a precaution. He said at pp 297 and 298 of the transcript:

"...many people have minor incidents over the

years and they - that they refer to their

doctors because they are not sure of what it

is all about and I think - that would be my

interpretation of this man's - or his present

problems."

We find that the symptoms described by Mr Kozman to Mr Silley, when he saw him two months after accident 1, were probably somewhat exaggerated. We accept the evidence of Dr Parker as relevant, when he said at p 366 of the transcript:

"I do not think that he is deliberately

manufacturing symptoms for compensation, but

he has got symptoms and he was making the

most of them in each compensation issue that

arose, which led to a distorted picture of

causation of problems, as he saw them."

27. The second body of evidence which tends to show that Mr Kozman did not have any existing soft tissue injuries or more serious problems such as damage to discs or joints, is contained in the sick leave records. Except for periods shortly after accidents 2 and 3 Mr Kozman took no sick leave for or related to any condition which would appear to have been caused or connected with any injuries sustained in accidents 1, 2 or 3 until mid 1979. Nor did he have visits to the doctors for such conditions except for the visit to Dr Yazbek in July 1976 for headaches, which could have been due either to whiplash injury or to other factors such as tension.

28. The third significant factor appearing from the evidence is that from 1976 until March 1980 when Mr Kozman fell from the palm tree, he worked very hard for long hours and was very active in his employment. His colleagues said he appeared fit and they saw no sign of him suffering from soft tissue injuries except for the episodes of neck injury in 1979. Mrs Pregal who started working with Mr Kozman in October 1978, described him as a workaholic who worked till the job was finished and was very active in the office until late 1979 when he started to complain of headaches. She did not remember Mr Kozman having much time off work before his fall from the palm tree in March 1980.

29. Ms Elkins, who first worked with Mr Kozman in 1980, or 1981, decribed him even then as "incredibly busy" and "constantly on the move". Mr Tieppo who worked with Mr Kozman from October 1977 described him as "very conscientious" and "always darting about" and said he was not aware that Mr Kozman had any incapacity until after the fall from the palm tree.

30. This evidence indicates that Mr Kozman was not limited in his capacity to work by any problems due to soft tissue injury, or disc or joint damage between 1977 and mid 1979 at least. He did not complain of headaches after he saw Dr Yazbek in July 1976 until mid 1979 when he gave Mr MacIntosh a history of headaches for about twelve months.

31. Most of the doctors who gave evidence were asked what significance they would attach to a symptom free period of at least twelve or eighteen months, in deciding whether or not Mr Kozman had made a full recovery from his soft tissue injuries sustained in accidents 1, 2 and 3. In fact the symptom free period in regard to soft tissue injuries to the neck appears to be 2 years from July 1976 to mid 1978, when Mr Kozman told Mr MacIntosh his headaches had recurred. Mr Cummins who is a neurosurgeon said at p 102:

"muscular strain could have occurred at the

time, initially, and that would have settled

down and ligamentous strain would have

settled down with time.

Yes?---So, at the - from the history that I

have been given there is no evidence that he

suffered major damage to the major structures

of his neck.

Well, if I can put it this way, it is - I

suppose it is a matter of common sense that

if he has - if he had suffered an injury you

would expect him to be complaining of

symptoms in 1976 and the following

year?---Even if only transient and on or off

over the next few years, yes."

32. Mr Morley, said that soft tissue injuries can last up to six or seven years but he would not usually expect a remission of symptoms to be as long as twelve months. Mr Dooley said that if a person had a remission of symptoms of six months or more after injuries sustained in one accident, and then a recurrence of symptoms after a second accident, he would relate those symptoms to the second accident rather than treat them as an aggravation of injuries sustained in the first accident. When he was given a detailed history relating to Mr Kozman he agreed that it appeared that the 1975 and 1976 injuries had little to do with complaints in 1983 or later, because of the remission of symptoms between July 1976 and June 1978.

33. Mr MacIntosh did not agree with the theory that a remission of symptoms of six to twelve months necessarily indicates a recovery from soft tissue injuries. He said at p 228:

"The natural history of injury to the spine is

that you have symptoms for a certain period

of time. It varies tremendously and it gets

better, so, no, it does not swing me one way

or the other.

Well do those two periods suggest to you that

he had got better from the 76 incidents

then?---His symptoms - his symptoms went.

Well you used the term a moment ago that he

had got better - - -?---He felt he had got

better.

- - - can you not come at that?---I will

rephrase it. His symptoms went away. He

still had degenerative disc disease."

34. However as we have already indicated there is no evidence that Mr Kozman had degenerative disc disease as at 2 December 1976 and this point was conceded by Mr Whittear. Mr MacIntosh himself said, at p 225, that he did not know whether or not Mr Kozman sustained a disc injury in 1976 and that Mr Kozman did not have any neurological signs consistent with a disc injury. His evidence on this point is therefore not helpful to the Tribunal, as it did not relate specifically to the soft tissue injuries or ligamentous strain, the only injuries which we find on the evidence were sustained in accidents 1, 2 and 3.

35. The facts which led to the issue of the BCC are that between 2 December 1976 and his retirement in November 1985 Mr Kozman had another three motor car accidents on 9 February 1977, 29 May 1980 and 8 June 1983 as well as the fall from a palm tree on 29 March 1980. In each of those accidents Mr Kozman had some jarring of his spine and he complained of a sore neck or back on a number of occasions. Mr MacIntosh was not the only doctor Mr Kozman attended who expressed the view in reports that the later accidents had further aggravated soft tissue injuries which occurred in accident 1, 2 or 3. When these views were expressed the doctors of course did not appreciate the detailed analysis, which the Commissioner and the Tribunal would one day be obliged to give to their views. Nor were they given very full histories by Mr Kozman.

36. Having heard the evidence we conclude that at times doctors tended for simplicity and to assist Mr Kozman, to describe his complaints as an aggravation of injuries sustained in accident 2, which Mr Kozman usually mentioned in his history, without any detail as to what happened in that accident or in other accidents. Accordingly, when cross-examined they did not have any firm conviction that the later symptoms were related to accident 2. Thus Dr Yazbek wrote in a report of 26 May 1982:

"Mr N Kozman's condition is a very complicated

case to sort out as he has been unfortunate

enough to be involved in several accidents in

the last few years, each further aggravating

his neck and back problems. However, it is

safe to assume that the motor vehicle

accident which took place on 29th May, 1980,

has aggravated his pre-existent cervical and

lumbar soft tissue injuries.

................

As to the absences off work listed in your

letter, I cannot provide you with much

detail as to whether they were taken as a

direct result of the motor vehicle accident

or his workers' compensation case, due to the

fact that we do not keep records of the

patient's absenteeism dates. Nevertheless,

one can assume that there is a probable 50%

chance that these listed absences were due to

the motor vehicle accident."

37. Similarly Mr MacIntosh had provided a number of certificates for Mr Kozman to have time off work in 1980 and 1981 and on 6 April 1981 he wrote:

"My medical certificates of the 25th November

1980 and 2nd February 1981 were with regard

to this man's neck injury, and I would

consider them directly related to the motor

accident in 1976."

However, at about the same time, on 3 March 1981 Mr MacIntosh wrote to Mr Kozman's solicitor a report which put the issue more tentatively. He wrote:

"This man's symptoms could well be consistent

with degenerative disc disease in his

cervical spine. It is not possible to be

certain of any relationship between his

present symptoms and to the 2 injuries in

1976, it certainly would be consistent with

such a story that he could develop

degenerative disc disease and his present

symptoms might well therefore be related to

these injuries."

In a later report specifically for the purposes of this hearing and after being given a fuller history Mr MacIntosh wrote:

"Basically what matters really is how

significant the accidents in 1976 were. As I

have already reported Mr Kozman's own history

is specifically related to the 1976 injuries

and he did not mention any injuries before or

after. It would therefore be reasonable to

assume that the injuries of 1976, to his mind

in 1979, were the major precipitating problems."

There was no reason given as to why Mr Kozman had not mentioned accident 1 or the 1977 accident, when his car was hit by another car and pushed into a tree. In evidence Mr MacIntosh made it clear that he could not give an expert opinion one way or the other, as to whether there was any relationship between the injuries in accident 2 and the neck pain in 1979 and later.

38. Similarly Mr Morley in reports dated 15 July 1980 and 8 September 1980 gave conflicting opinions as to whether the injuries Mr Kozman sustained in the motor car accident on 28 May 1980 were an exacerbation of earlier soft tissue injuries to the neck or were new injuries.

39. We have set out these examples of changing or tentative medical opinions, not to be critical of the doctors, but to show the difficulties involved in deciding many years later what conditions existed in 1976. After five days of hearing and evidence from eleven doctors and numerous reports by other doctors we understand those difficulties very well. The position was well analysed by Mr Rush (at transcript p 290) when he said of the issues facing the Tribunal:

"The whole thing depends on being able to

define very accurately, which we cannot

define very accurately the injury that

occurred whenever - in 1976. We have no

clear definition of that injury. So, we are

talking about something that is very vague,

and we are now aggravating something that is

very vague, and so that it gets extremely

difficult to know. I mean, after all, if we

knew if God above told us that the discs in

this gentleman were absolutely normal prior

to the accident in 1976, the following day he

told us that the discs were abnormal, then I

would believe that even a long period of

time, of even some years of freedom of pain,

I would be prepared to incriminate the

initial accident. But we do not have that

sort of information. I mean we are not privy

to that sort of information. So it is all

surmise, and I find it very difficult to try

and speak the truth on such vague - when the

pathology of what we are talking about is so

vague."

40. The Commissioner in his reasons for reconsideration relied on the medical reports referred to and concluded that Mr Kozman's neck trouble in 1979 and thereafter was the result of progressive deterioration of the injuries to the cervical spine which occurred in accidents 1 and 2. Neither he nor the doctors who wrote the reports had a history which included the period of two years with no neck symptoms between mid 1976 and mid 1978. There is an alternative explanation of Mr Kozman's complaints of pain after 1978, which does not depend on any lasting injury to the neck prior to 1979. It is apparent from a history of Mr Kozman's symptoms after 1978 and the evidence of Mrs Pregal, Ms Elkins, Mr Tieppo, Ms Harris and Dr Parker.

41. Mr Kozman had some episodes of neck pain in June 1979. There is no evidence that he was troubled by neck pain in 1980 until after the car accident in May. In fact he saw Dr MacIntosh two days after the fall from the palm tree, in March 1980 and told him that he had no pain in the neck. Dr MacIntosh agreed that if Mr Kozman had neck pain in 1980 he would have been unlikely to offer to climb the palm tree. After the car accident in May 1980 Mr Kozman began to complain more consistently of neck pain. He saw Mr Cummins, a neurosurgeon, in February and March 1982 when he was complaining of neck pain and also of pain in the hands and arms. There was then an interval of two and a half years while Mr Kozman attended the St Vincent's Hospital pain clinic. Mr Cummins saw Mr Kozman again in October 1984 when he found signs consistent with an ulna nerve problem. A neurologist, Dr Tomlinson conducted bilateral electrical studies which showed a mild tardy ulna palsy on the right side and a more marked palsy on the left side, the site of the fracture dislocation of the elbow due to the fall from the palm tree. On 17 October 1984 Mr Cummins decompressed the ulna nerves on each hand. He found both nerves were tightly held down and on the right side there was a lump in the nerve at the elbow associated with the compression. It was Mr Cummins' opinion that the ulna nerve palsies could have been work related.

42. Mr Kozman worked up until the day of the ulna nerve release operation. Although Mr Cummins regarded the operations as successful, Mr Kozman has not returned to work since 17 October 1984. He explained this as follows:

"No. After this particular operation I never

because, for the simple reason, my assistant

area manager welfare, when I had the neck and

back problem, used to come to the office, she

counselled me many times to accept

retirement. I was saying, no, I am not

retiring. You are trying to impose a death

sentence on me, I am still in my 40s. I was

refusing. After the arm operation I have to

come to grips with myself. I said - they

were objecting about my collar and my back

pain and so on, on and off, how would they

accept me with inability to write down

reports or carry on my work effectively as I

used to do before. So I had to come to grips

with myself, but I would not be useful to

myself, to my client, to my department. I

had to accept the fact that I became crippled

and accept retirement. It became a psychological barrier."

Mr Kozman said that the main cause of his retirement was his inability to use a pen. He added, - "my only tool is a pen."

43. When it was put to Mr Cummins that the ulna nerve operation had not been successful, he said that he had seen Mr Kozman on 30 October 1986 and the symptoms he had then were not really related to the ulna nerve entrapment. He said that on examination he found that there was a mild sensory loss that corresponded to the ulna nerve distribution that had been there for quite some time, and did not warrant surgical intervention at that time. He said there could be some problem with the muscles or the joints at the elbows but that was outside his area of expertise. He did not consider that the formation of scar tissue from the operation had caused the problem. Mr Cummins suggested that the problem could be due to inflammatory change or perhaps to disuse which makes a joint stiff and painful. Mr Rush, an orthopaedic surgeon, also tested the ulna nerve after the release operation and found it was fine.

44. Mr Dooley saw Mr Kozman in 1982 and 1983. On 5 May 1982 he wrote that he believed Mr Kozman was suffering from:

"Chronic neck and lumbar strain due to

aggravation of cervical and lumbar disc

degeneration with marked anxiety state and

neurosis."

When, in evidence, Mr Dooley was told that Mr Kozman regarded the ulna nerve operation as unsuccessful and said that his arms could hardly be used and certainly not for writing, but the surgeon regarded it as successful because the ulna nerve no longer showed signs of deficit, Mr Dooley said that confirmed his diagnosis of anxiety hysteria.

45. Mr MacIntosh too, in 1988 found only slight restriction of movement of the neck, no muscle spasm and tenderness only in the mid line. He found slight restriction of movement of the elbow but Mr Kozman was complaining of almost complete loss of sensation in the ulna half of both forearms, from the elbow down. Mr MacIntosh said that the description of pain and lack of sensation in the arms did not fit in with any specific neurological problem and he thought it was probably hysterical. He said he did not consider sending Mr Kozman to a psychiatrist because he did not find psychiatrists of any value in this situation but he thought from a physical point of view Mr Kozman was fit to work and should remain fit to work as a social worker unless something else happened.

46. Thus there was a strong suggestion by a number of the medical witnesses that Mr Kozman's problems are hysterical or have a psychological, rather than an organic cause. During the hearing the evidence of Mr Kozman's colleagues shed considerable light on this aspect of the case. It transpired that when Mr Kozman was working at Sunshine he was the only social worker in DSS who had first trained in a non-English speaking country and that he and Mrs Pregal, were very much involved in providing advice to clients of DSS who did not speak English. In those days there was very little understanding of different cultural backgrounds amongst staff of DSS and this led to what Ms Harris, who was formerly the area manager of the area which included Sunshine, described quite frankly as discrimination against Mr Kozman. Ms Harris said that the environment in which Mr Kozman was trying to do his work at the Sunshine office was unsympathetic to him.

47. Evidence of this discrimination and unsympathetic work environment was first given by Mrs Pregal. She said that Mr Kozman received a lot of criticism in the office and jokes were made at his expense. She said he did not fit into the mould of social workers in the office because:

"he is a person for people and not for

systems, not for theories."

Mrs Pregal was asked whether Mr Kozman ever exaggerated his pains. She said:

"I do not think consciously he did. I think

he was suffering from so much rejection that

sometimes he had to talk about his pain in

order to get some of the warmth that he

needed to keep going."

She said that Mr Kozman never broke down and cried in the office but:

"You could see that he was crying inside."

Mrs Pregal said people from DSS were trying to persuade Mr Kozman to retire because he did not fit in with the other social workers.

48. Mr Adams said that Mrs Pregal had expressed concern to him about giving evidence, because she feared that if she spoke the truth about what had happened to Mr Kozman, she would be prejudiced in her employment with DSS. The Tribunal found her evidence very valuable. We are confident that no senior officer of DSS would consider penalising Mrs Pregal for obeying her obligations as a member of the community to give evidence in this matter honestly. We sincerely hope that our confidence is not misplaced.

49. Ms Elkins said that in 1980 and 1981 she had been engaged in doing an evaluation of the work of Mr Kozman and Mrs Pregal in pioneering DSS work in the migrant communities. She said that they were both extremely busy because the migrant community had heard that there were people at Sunshine office who could service them in their own language, and therefore people were coming from within and outside the region simply to see Mr Kozman and Mrs Pregal.

50. Ms Elkins said that she noticed "fairly racist comments" being made to Mr Kozman by people in the office. She said that she noticed that Mr Kozman and Mrs Pregal were blamed for not being professional enough because they were trying to meet the demands of the migrant community and therefore seeing people outside their own region. She said:

"He was always constantly in a position of

having to prove himself in a fairly hostile

environment which is a fairly stressful thing

to do, and as I have said the workload was

enough for three people."

51. After 1981 Ms Elkins did not return to the Sunshine office, except for a short period, until 1984. She said that when she came back in 1984 there was a striking difference in Mr Kozman's appearance. She described it as follows:

"---Well, when I went back in 1984 he was

looking very, very tired. His hair had

turned almost white which was a surprise to

me. He was in constant pain and it was

obvious, the pain was very obvious and he was

under stress."

Ms Elkins explained that as well as being a qualified social worker, she is also a trained nurse. It was her opinion that Mr Kozman was not coping and was in obvious pain when she saw him in 1984. She said he was looking very ill and was very very pale and was no longer able to cope with the constant criticism and hostility in his work environment.

52. Ms Harris who was formely the assistant area manager for the DSS area which included the Sunshine office also gave evidence. The Tribunal found her evidence very important because it was Ms Harris, who Mr Kozman said, had tried to force him to retire from his position 1982 or 1983. He said:

"She came twice and sat with the regional

manager and she was pushing me out by my

health is more important, you should not kill

yourself and you have to accept the fact, the

realities that you are unable now and so on,

but I was carrying on my work."

It was clear from Mr Kozman's evidence that he resented Ms Harris' efforts to persuade him to retire.

53. During Ms Harris' evidence the Tribunal perceived a further reason why Mr Kozman felt resentful towards Ms Harris and also a possible explanation of the incident in June 1978 when Mr Kozman had symptoms which were believed to be related to his heart, but were found to be due to anxiety or tension. Ms Harris explained that due to "a perceived lack of satisfaction" with Mr Kozman's work she had in 1978 arranged for him to be transferred to Head Office to work under the supervision of a social worker who would assess the quality of his professional social work performance. She described the problems that gave rise to this unusual step as follows:

"I mean, some of the problems listed were to

do with the fact that his voice could be

heard throughout the interview areas and that

was not acceptable in terms of affording

privacy to clients. It was also to do with

his intervening in a very directive way with

clients again, as a question of style and a

professional approach, that can be debated

for years. But I considered that I wanted

him to have the benefit of close supervision

so that some of these criticisms of Mr Kozman

could be dealt with and that he would then

have a fair basis on which questions about

his professional competence, could be assessed."

54. Ms Harris knew that Mr Kozman had been a senior social worker in an equivalent department in Egypt where he had qualified, and she agreed that it would be reasonable for somebody to feel upset at being told that he needed supervision after he had been working in a senior capacity. Ms Harris did say that, with hindsight and more awareness of cultural differences today, she may have handled the matter differently by arranging for the supervision to take place in his own office rather than for Mr Kozman to be transferred. Ms Harris gave some idea of the strength of feeling against Mr Kozman when she said that she withstood frequent criticisms from his regional office managers to do something much stronger than was ever done about Mr Kozman. She said:

"I was probably a strong ally for Mr Kozman in

ignoring quite a lot of it, apart from taking

specific action to document his performance

so that we could say afterwards, either there

is - there are grounds for considering his

not performing satisfactorily, or there are not."

55. Because of the implications of this evidence the Tribunal suggested to the parties that Dr Parker a psychiatrist be asked to re-examine Mr Kozman. He had previously seen Mr Kozman and had noted in his report, that Mr Kozman had referred to some conflict with his regional manager but had not wished that to be explored in the consultation. In his report of 8 August 1981 Dr Parker had written:

"I do not think I have a full picture of other

possible sources of stress and when he let

slip the fact that he was having a lot of

conflicts with his female regional manager of

lesser education and has to take Valium to

cope with her, he then said that that should

not be played up."

56. When he saw Mr Kozman again, in September 1988 at the Tribunal's request, Dr Parker had read the evidence of Mrs Pregal, Ms Elkins, Mr Tieppo and Ms Harris. In his evidence Dr Parker said that Mr Kozman has an obsessional personality. He explained that such a personality is not a stigma or a disadvantage because it is very difficult for people to be successful in certain professional areas unless they are obsessional personalities. He then added:

"Now, people with this personality, if they

are frustrated and stressed beyond their

endurance often break down and we use a word,

decompensate, and by that we mean that they

cannot get by without developing some

symptoms, neurotic symptoms. Some people

express this as a physical complaint and skin

rashes and nervous diarrhoea are very common

complaints in this condition and overt

anxiety symptoms are also common but then

again these are disguised as possible heart

attacks and a number of people who are

decompensating with obsessional personalities

are admitted to hospital or investigated for

a possible heart attack and nothing turns up

in the investigations."

He summed up his evidence as follows:

"What I am saying is that the work stress, the

worries at home about his son, the repeated

minor accidents, have all played contributing

parts in his - in the end result that we see

today."

..................

"All I can say is that there have been a

number of matters which have distressed him

and tipped him over the edge and of those I

would think the most important was the

frustrations as work.

Because he was a man who demanded a lot of

himself and frustrations in not being able to

do things in the way he wants to do them are

a very common cause for breakdown in

obsessional personalities, so I would put

that number one........ The accidents, I

think, have only played a very small part and

that is by fixing his attention on his neck

and I think they would be predominantly

responsible for his ongoing neck pain but the

work stress causing his decompensation would

have aggravated that."

Dr Parker was asked how his diagnosis compared with that of doctors who had suggested that Mr Kozman suffered from anxiety hysteria. He said:

"I suppose you could use that diagnosis too.

I think diagnoses in psychiatry are very

flexible really and they are very

interchangeable......... There are some

people who use the term psychosomatic

disorder to refer to physical symptoms, the

expression of emotional disturbance, such as

indigestion with no ulcer and no problem at

all and there are others who would use

anxiety hysteria for those conditions, so

they are interchangeable in a sense."

57. We found Dr Parker's description of decompensation very apt. We find that Mr Kozman developed neurotic symptoms due to frustration and stress beyond his endurance. We accept that the many accidents in which Mr Kozman has been involved have played a small part by fixing attention on his neck but that does not mean that accidents 1, 2 and 3, rather than the later accidents, played that part. On the orthopaedic and neurological evidence we do not find that there was any injury to the neck in existence as at December 1976. We find that the first sign of neurotic symptoms due to frustration and stress was the suspected heart problem, which occurred about the time of the disciplinary transfer to head office in 1978. We find that the neck pains in 1979 were similar neurotic symptoms but that tension and anxiety also played a part by aggravating muscular aches and pains and pain due to age-related degeneration. The decompensation also prolonged Mr Kozman's pain from his later physical problems such as the fracture of the elbow, the car accidents in May 1980 and June 1983 and the ulna nerve entrapment.

58. As we are not satisfied on the evidence that any physical or mental condition existed when Mr Kozman became an eligible employee, we consider that s.16(10)(b) has no application. A condition must be identified, and the Commissioner must be of the opinion that it existed at the time a person became an eligible employee, before he can consider issuing a BCC on the ground that he is not satisfied that the incapacity causing retirement was not caused or substantially contributed to by that condition or by a condition connected with that condition.

59. Mr Whittear acknowledged that it is up to the Commissioner to establish that a condition existed at the relevant date. He said:

"although there is a reverse onus in the main

body of section 16(10) paragraph (b), if one

goes down below that, the Commissioner still

has to be of the opinion that it existed at

the time."

He agreed that if the Tribunal is in a state of uncertainty on this point Mr Kozman gets the benefit of that uncertainty. We are satisfied that the evidence does not establish the existence of any condition as at 2 December 1976.

60. Mr Whittear in his final address referred to Mr Silley's evidence that once you have had a muscular ligamentous strain you have the potential for further injury provided there is another act of trauma. That seems to be the basis of the original decision made on 15 January 1986 to issue a BCC specifying "history of back injury" as the condition which existed at the relevant date. That decision was then varied by the decision under review and the condition specified was changed to:

"Ligamentous injury to the cervical spine" and

Ligamentous injury to lumbar spine";

61. Mr Whittear then went on to consider the concept of "existence" at a certain time and said that the Tribunal "has found a way around that". He referred to Re Harrison and the Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALN 352 and Re Y and Commissioner of Superannuation delivered 5 March 1982. Re Y is of no assistance to the Tribunal as it is covered by an order under s.35 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 which means that it is available only to the parties.

62. We have considered the decision in Re Harrison. However in that matter, although the Tribunal concluded that "past history of anxiety state and alcoholism" may be "a condition", it was not concerned with s.16(10) of the Act and therefore did not address the words "which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, existed at the time the person became an eligible employee...". Those words in our opinion require more than a past history of a condition. They expressly require that the condition have been in existence at the time the person became an eligible employee. We are satisfied that a term like "history of neck or back injury" can not be a description of "a physical or mental condition that existed" at a particular date. Certain prior injuries have lasting effects, for instance, by weakening some parts of the body, and therefore may render a person susceptible to further injuries. In such a case those effects may well be a physical or mental condition in existence at a relevant date. On the evidence, in spite of Mr Silley's comments, we do not find that Mr Kozman had any such susceptibility as at 2 December 1976. It was not mentioned by the other medical experts and the theory conflicts with the evidence of recovery from a soft tissue injury after a six or twelve month remission of symptoms.

63. We believe that the Tribunal in Re Y referred to the decision of the Federal Court in Neal v Secretary Department of Transport [1980] FCA 45; (1980) 3 ALD 97. That case however is clearly distinguishable. It concerned Air Navigation Orders which specifically provided that in order to obtain a student pilot's licence an applicant "shall have no established medical history or clinical diagnosis of ...a psychosis". Thus, a history of psychosis was sufficient to render the applicant ineligible for the licence.

64. At one stage in his address Mr Whittear said that if the Tribunal was to say that there was no power under s.16(10) to issue the BCC and the certificate was set aside "that would just throw the case back into non-disclosure, and primary decisions, internal review and AAT no doubt again". On the findings we have made and on Mr Whittear's statement that a BCC would not have been issued in respect of Mr Kozman prior to his retirement because his was one of the 80,000 returns that have never been examined, we doubt that s.16(11) would be applicable.

65. Because of the conclusion we reached as to the issue of a BCC under s.16(10) it has not been necessary for us to consider s.66(2) in any detail. We do however say that even if we had found that Mr Kozman had some cervical and back weakness due to his prior accidents at the relevant date, we would not have found the necessary connection with the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement. The Federal Court in Commissioner for Superannuation v Miller ("Miller's Case") [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 63 ALR 237 and [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 8 FCR 153 and in Commissioner for Superannuation v Benham ("Benham's Case") an unreported decision delivered 14 April 1988 held that the words "connected with" should be given a narrow rather than a wide interpretation. Neaves J. said in Benham's Case:

"In my opinion the Tribunal was correct in

concluding that the majority of the Court in

Miller's Case construed the provision as

requiring that there be a causal connection

between the physical or mental condition

specified in the benefit classification

certificate relating to the employee and the

physical or mental condition which caused, or

substantially contributed to, the incapacity

which resulted in the employee's early

retirement. The last sentence in the passage

cited above from the judgment of Davies J. in

that case cannot be read in isolation. It

must be read in its context. In my opinion,

it is clear from a reading of the passage as

a whole that his Honour's view was that, in a

case where the early retirement arises from

incapacity which is caused, or substantially

contributed to, by a physical or mental

condition other than that specified in the

benefit classification certificate, the

employee will be deprived of the benefits

provided for by sub-s.66(1) of the Act if the

decision-maker is satisfied that that

physical or mental condition "arose out of or

developed from" that specified in the benefit

classification certificate. So understood,

his Honour's view and that of Pincus J. are

in accord and clearly support the view which

the Tribunal took."

66. Whether the neck pain in 1979 was purely a neurotic symptom or was also due to muscular tension or to some degeneration or even to the memory of earlier pain, which allowed Mr Kozman to fix his attention on his neck, is unimportant. Which ever alternative it was, the pain did not arise out of or develop from the injuries in 1975 and 1976. It developed from the decompensation due to anxiety and frustration in 1978 and 1979 and thereafter. Further, it was not the 1979 neck pain which caused the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement. We are satisfied on the evidence of Mr Kozman, his work colleagues and the sick leave records that it was his pain after the 1980 accidents and most significantly arm pain, numbness and weakness before and after the ulna nerve operation which were the ground for Mr Kozman's retirement. There is no evidence that they "arose out of or developed from" the injuries sustained in accidents 1, 2 and 3.

67. The decision under review will be set aside and in substitution the Tribunal decides that no BCC should be issued under s.16(10) of the Act.
