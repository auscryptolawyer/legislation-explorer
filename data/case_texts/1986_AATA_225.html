305/85 and Commissioner of Taxation [1986] AATA 225 (15 August 1986)

Last Updated: 27 February 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - understatement of income - moneys in bank accounts in false names - whether beneficially owned by taxpayer - whether assessable income of taxpayer.

Income Tax - evidence - understatement of income - moneys in hank accounts in false names - failure to call material witness

Income Tax – Assessment – s 167 – finality – notice of assessment as conclusive evidence of due making of assessment - s 177

AAT No 2814

Income Tax Assessment Act (1936) (Cth.) ss. 177,191,218

FJ Bloeman Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 27; (1981) 147 CLR 360

Jones v Dunkel (1960) 101 CLR 198

Mr HP Stevens (Senior Member)

Mr PM Roach (Senior Member)

15 August 1986

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL) TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

Mr H.P. Stevens (Senior Member)

1. The hearing of this application has been a unique one in that, although it was claimed the bulk of the monies involved had been given to the applicant by his brother to "mind", that person was not called to give evidence. If it were necessary in order to reach a decision for it to be determined to whom the monies actually belonged, i.e. the brother or one (and which one) of the structures in "his" group, and whether they represented assessable income in the "owner's hands", this could not be done for the evidence adduced is inadequate for such purposes. However, the issue involved, relates only to the status of the moneys insofar as the applicant is concerned. Having regard to the evidence adduced overall I am satisfied on the balance of probabilities that the bulk of the total involved did not have in relation to the applicant a taxable source. In this regard I agree with the conclusions reached by Mr Roach and I join in the decision expressed in para. 22 of his reasons.



Mr P.M. Roach (Senior Member)

2. On Friday, 1 July 1983, the applicant went to a suburban branch of a bank at which he was well-known to the Manager and there opened two savings bank accounts in fictitious names with a fictitious address. He deposited $100,000 in cash to the credit of one account and $100,000 in cash to the credit of the other. On the following Monday, 4 July 1983 he deposited further amounts of cash in the sums of $155,000 and $25,000 respectively. Within a few days officers of the Commissioner called on the Bank Manager to ascertain whether accounts had been opened in the names of the fictitious account holders. He confirmed that such accounts were held. The officers left advising that they would return next morning. Following their departure the Bank Manager tried unsuccessfully to contact the applicant and then to contact the applicant's brother interstate. The Bank Manager did not attempt to contact the accountant whom he knew to be active in relation to the affairs of the applicant and of his brother and related companies. On Thursday, 7 July 1983 assessments to income tax were raised in the name of the Commissioner against the persons named as the account-holders. By the assessments the Commissioner contended that each of those non-existent persons had derived a taxable income in relation to the year ended 30 June 1982 of $681,430. No explanation was offered or sought as to how the Commissioner was able to "make an assessment" of taxable income in those amounts. It is sufficient to say that each person named was assessed for tax amounting to $400,000.12. As no other amounts were taken into consideration as due by or to the persons named to or by the Commissioner the total amount payable under each notice of assessment was determined to be $400,000.12. Next morning, Friday, 8 July 1983, officers of the Commissioner attended at the premises of the bank and there presented to the Bank Manager notices pursuant to section 218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1932 (Cth.) ("the Act") relating to the assessments raised the previous day but directed to the trading bank, a distinct legal entity from the savings bank with which the moneys had been deposited. Learning of this, one officer withdrew and later returned with fresh section 218 notices addressed to the savings bank. The other officers waited. The savings bank complied with the demand so made and paid the Commissioner amounts equal to the credit balance in each account, namely $255,000 and $125,000 respectively. Receipts were issued by the Commissioner.

3. It was only after the demands of the section 218 notices had been complied with that the Bank Manager spoke with the applicant and advised him. From that point of time forward the applicant has consistently contended that he had received the funds in question from his older brother to whom he was at all material times, and is now, wholly accountable. Not surprisingly, representatives of the brother were active in seeking the return of the moneys. They were unsuccessful in that, but as a result of their endeavours, on 15 August 1983 a notice of assessment issued against the applicant. Thereby it was alleged that he had derived, in the year of income ended 30 June 1982, a taxable income of $650,458. No explanation was offered or sought as to how the Commissioner had been able to "make an assessment" of the taxable income of the applicant in that year in that amount. Perhaps the explanation lies in the circumstance that the tax assessed on that income amounted to $380,981.47, which after giving credit for provisional tax ($981) and "Other Credits" ($380,000) left 47 cents as the total amount payable on the assessment.

4. Objection to the assessment was made by the applicant by notice of 20 September 1983. On 18 December 1984 the objection was allowed in part and an amended assessment issued based on a taxable income of $474,954. That resulted in a claimed tax liability of $275,679.07 leaving the applicant with a credit balance on the face of the assessment for $105,302.40. This Tribunal was not told whether that amount was refunded to the applicant or, assuming that it was, what happened to it. In this case however the Board has the advantage of knowing how the amount of taxable income was determined. The Commissioner did his calculation on a "Funds Statement" basis and the issues argued before us as to quantum can be identified with two features of that calculation: "Unexplained drawings" ($420,963) and an increase in the balance of a savings account in the name of the applicant from $17,062 to $61,335 (a difference of $44,273) in the twelve months to 30 June 1983. The evidence sought to explain the bulk of "unexplained drawings" first by evidence relating to withdrawals from accounts in fictitious names during June 1983 ($392,094.39) and then by evidence relating to earlier withdrawals from those accounts ($22,429.79). Similarly, there was an attempt to explain the bulk of the increase in the other account. However, overall there was no attempt in the evidence to explain the taxable income of the applicant as being less than $11,817 despite income being returned at $2,897.

5. The Assessments as Nullities. It was submitted for the applicant that the "assessment" of 20 September 1983 was so arbitrary as to be a nullity; and that, being a nullity, it was incapable of being amended so as to constitute the "amended assessment" of 18 December 1984 as a formally valid assessment. Despite all the criticisms which were made alleging misuses of power giving rise to the assessments first raised against the fictitious persons; the attachment of the 380,000; the later assessment against the applicant and the appropriation to the applicant of a credit of 380,000 towards satisfaction of a liability to tax arising pursuant to that assessment, the fact remains that a notice of assessment - or copies of it - have been produced. In consequence they constitute conclusive evidence of the "due making of the assessment" (section 177(1)). To quote from the decision of Mason and Wilson JJ. (with whom Stephen and Aickin JJ. agreed) in F.J. Bloemen Pty. Ltd V FC OF T ((1981) [1981] HCA 27; 147 CLR 360 at P.378):-

"In our opinion, it must follow that a notice in proper form of an assessment necessarily compels the conclusion that there was an assessment made in fact."

"Accordingly in our opinion the Supreme Court is bound, on production of a notice of assessment, to rule that the assessment was duly made ...."

That comment was made of a Supreme Court in relation to both general jurisdiction of the Court. It also applies to a Board of Review and to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal which has no general jurisdiction. That being so, it is not open in these proceedings to question the validity of the amended assessment which, by force of section 191 is the assessment before it. This Tribunal must limit its consideration to the question of whether the assessment of taxable income made against the applicant is excessive.

  1. The Background. The applicant's contention broadly is that all of the moneys in dispute before us were either held for or applied to the use of his older brother or one of the companies of that older brother. He does not seek to establish a beneficial entitlement in his brother to the exclusion of any of the companies associated with his brother or in any one or more of those companies to the exclusion of any other of them, nor is it necessary that he should do so. That being so it would be quite inappropriate for this Tribunal to attempt to identify the beneficial owner. The question for this Tribunal is "what was the taxable income of the applicant?"
  2. Recognising the critical importance of the relationship between the two brothers, it is appropriate to consider that relationship as disclosed by the evidence before us before considering particular issues as to quantum more closely. It is also appropriate to consider the significance of the failure of the applicant to call his brother as a witness. I am satisfied that (as it will appear hereafter) the brother occupied a position of influence and even dominance over the applicant, and that that circumstance explains the failure of the applicant to seek to compel the attendance of his brother. It is also significant that if the applicant fails in the present proceedings, then on his own account, it is his brother, more than himself, who will be the loser because the applicant's capacity to pay what is due to his brother will be diminished by the amount of tax retained by the Commissioner. Having said that I take the view that the failure of the applicant to call his brother is only significant in that one may assume that the brother's evidence would not have helped the applicant thereby enabling inferences to be drawn more readily to the detriment of the taxpayer: Jones v Dunkel (1960) 101 CLR 198. Speculations which might reflect on the credit-worthiness or tax status of the brother cannot be relied on to the detriment of the applicant.

8. The Brothers. In 1983 the applicant was 30 years of age. He was the youngest of three brothers in his family. His family had migrated to Australia when he was two years of age. The two older brothers had moved out into the building industry but had come to the parting of the ways in about 1980 and thereafter the eldest brother had no part to play in the story unfolded before us. The second brother, whom I refer to throughout as "the brother", engaged in the building industry on a large scale, particularly interstate. He controlled numerous companies and undertook many very sizeable projects. In matters of finance he dealt from time-to-time with financial magnates and in matters of building promotion and the like he enjoyed the company of public officials. As is commonly the case the resources of most or all companies within the group were made available in support of particular projects. Notwithstanding all those considerations, and perhaps because of them, in 1982 the group was experiencing substantial financial difficulty and the accountant to the brother and his companies (who also attended to the tax returns of the applicant) was involved in negotiating through those difficulties. The accountant gave evidence. His evidence was that one company in the group had trading losses measured in excess of $1,000,000. The evidence is quite insufficient to enable any detailed or precise findings to be made but in the circumstances that is really unnecessary. The issue before us relates to the affairs of the applicant and not to the affairs of his brother. The circumstances of the brother are mentioned by way of background only.



9. As to the applicant, by age 30 he had not attained the achievements of his brother. On leaving school he had worked as a clerk to real estate agents; as an employed taxi driver; he had undertaken some accountancy studies and had qualified as a real-estate agent; had worked on his own account as a paper hanger; and, as mentioned hereafter, as a builder. In his income tax returns for the years 1979 and 1980 he described himself as a "paper-hanger" and thereafter as a builder. In his tax returns for the four years to 30 June 1982 he had disclosed a taxable income of $4,151; $3,757; $6,239 and $6,963. In the latter two years his gross income (excluding interest) had been $15,000 and $15,600 respectively derived, at least in large part through the brother. Since October 1984 he has managed a retail business conducted in premises controlled by his brother.

  1. Having closely observed the applicant and considered his evidence I am satisfied that throughout he was very keen to demonstrate to the brother that he had building and management skills sufficient to enable him to be trusted with the management of building projects and that generally he sought to help the brother whenever he could. He frequently travelled interstate to attend on the brother. Those initiatives were to some extent successful. The brother, through one of his companies, embarked upon a project of building a block of flats close to the home of the applicant (who resided with his parents). As the brother resided interstate immediate control was entrusted to the applicant. One of the brother's companies was owner and another the builder. He was paid by the building-company for his services and returned the income already mentioned. Construction was complete by mid-1982. The taxpayer thereafter was substantially unemployed until late 1984. Then in the latter part of the calendar year 1982 several factors came together and generated substantial cash funds. I now proceed to consider those matters in detail.
  2. The Fictitious Accounts: - 392,094.39. The applicant's evidence was that his brother started visiting Sydney frequently there entrusting to him large sums in cash which were initially placed in the combination-lock safe maintained by the applicant at the home of his parents. His brother's explanation was in terms of financial difficulty. The applicant claimed he did not count the money and that he was to hold it for his brother. Then it was decided that the money should be placed with a bank at interest. The accountant, who was very well known to the manager of Bank A, made approaches to the Bank Manager of A which resulted in accounts (hereafter numbered 1 and-2) being opened by the taxpayer in fictitious names. One account was opened in the name of a man and the other in the name of a woman. The Bank Manager was called and gave evidence that the taxpayer did not identify himself by his correct name and he supported his testimony generally by producing a manager's Diary Note recording his confidence that "all transactions are above board" in reliance on assurances from the accountant who had "referred one of his clients...to open two...accounts" in different names. Even if it be true that the applicant did not identify himself to the Bank Manager by his correct name I attach little significance to that circumstance. I am satisfied that, directed by the brother and guided by the accountant, the applicant was not concerned to conceal his identity or the fact that he was opening accounts in two names: circumstances hardly consistent with neither account being in a fictitious name.
  3. The amounts deposited there both then and later were dealt with as detailed hereafter until the accounts were closed by cash withdrawals on 21 June 1983.
  4. In October 1982 a third account (account No. 3) had been opened with bank B. That account was opened in the fictitious name used for account No. 2. It was maintained as hereafter detailed until closed by a cash withdrawal on 29 June 1983.
  5. The applicant's evidence was that the moneys so withdrawn were held in cash in the home safe until accounts No 4 and No. 5 were opened with bank C in July 1983 using fictitious names not previously used (cf. para.1).

  1. I conclude that, as to $392,094 withdrawn from Accounts 1, 2 and 3 between 22 & 29 June 1983, he held that sum to the use of his brother or one or more companies associated with his brother at 30 June 1983 and that neither then nor at any earlier time had that sum or any part of it constituted assessable income of the applicant.
  2. Unexplained Withdrawals $22/429. This issue relates to a withdrawal from account No. 2 on 31 December 1982 of $5,000; and withdrawals from account No. 1 of $5,000 4 February 1983; $5,000 11 March 1983; $2,429 6 May 1983 and $5,000 7 June 1983 - all as detailed above. The applicant's evidence was that these sums were wholly expended to the use of his brother or one of his companies in meeting expenses in relation to the block of flats. In that regard it is said that although construction was advanced to the stage whereby the first sale could be effected as early as October 1982, nonetheless there was a continuing major problem with seepage which caused ongoing expenditure. Although the applicant had the management of the flat-project he could give no details of the amounts expended and he produced no records. Further, there was nothing in the evidence to indicate that the amounts so said to have been expended had been taken into account in the books of account or income tax returns of the vendor company. I am not persuaded upon the evidence that the explanation given by the applicant is true, even on the balance of probabilities.

  3. Deposits $26,472.26. This issue relates to a savings bank account maintained at all material times in the name of the applicant until it was closed in July 1983 when the sum of $61,335.57 was withdrawn. In the "Funds Statement" to 30 June 1983 that amount was brought forward as the balance to the credit of that account at 30 June 1983 but the Commissioner's analysis sheet and the bank statements produced in evidence show that that balance was only reached on 7 July 1983 after interest of $1,293.53 had been credited. At 30 June 1982 the balance of the account stood at $60,042.04. It follows that to the extent of $1,293 the assessment is excessive.

  4. However, the principal issue relates to three deposits to the credit of that account. As to the first deposit ($11,640.46 on 7 January 1983), I am satisfied that the amount so deposited was the balance purchase price on the sale of one of the flats remitted to the vendor by its solicitors by a letter of 22 December 1982 addressed to the vendor in care of the applicant. With the exception of an unexplained amount of $28 I am satisfied that a second sum of ($26,472.26 deposited on 18 February 1983) comprised balance settlement moneys on three other transactions which resulted in the solicitors to the vendor remitting (as earlier) $6,280.62; $9,990 and $10,173.64 on 14, 15 and 16 February 1983 respectively. Upon the evidence I am not persuaded even on the balance of probabilities that the third sum ($10,500 deposited on 21 January 1983) had a similar source but in what follows I will assume that it did.

21. The taxpayer's evidence was that all of those moneys were received by him on account of the vendor company and were placed to the credit of his account only because the vendor company had no bank account of its own other than interstate. I am not persuaded that that explanation is true. Other moneys due to the vendor upon sale and from the letting of the flats were remitted interstate. I am not persuaded the applicant did not know how to effect a deposit to be credited to an account interstate. Further, the moneys were not held only for such a period as might be reasonably necessary to enable the moneys to be remitted to the person entitled to them. Further, it is not without significance that the applicant returned all of the interest from the savings account in question as his income.



22. I have rejected the evidence of the applicant as to the matters referred to in paras 17 & ff. In the circumstances the applicant has failed to persuade me that his income as assessed is to that extent excessive. I have reached that conclusion because I am not persuaded by the evidence of the applicant, whether considered alone or in

conjunction with the other evidence, that the moneys were not applied (para 17) or held (paras. 18-20) to his own use; were not the property of the applicant so as to be within his entitlement in spending or holding them, as the case maybe; and not his in consequence of an entitlement arising from his dealings and activities in the interests of his brother (or a company associated with the brother) received as a reward for those services. That being so I am not satisfied that those moneys did not constitute income of the applicant.

23. Accordingly I would reduce the assessment before us by $392.094 (para. 16) and by $1,239 (para. 18) thereby reducing the taxable income of the applicant for the year ended 30 June 1983 to $81,567.
