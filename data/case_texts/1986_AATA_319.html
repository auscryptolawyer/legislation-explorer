B302/1984 and Commissioner of Taxation [1986] AATA 319; (1986) 18 ATR 3006; 86 ATC 1136 (23 October 1986)

Last Updated: 5 March 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Allowable Deductions - bad debts - reinvested interest previously returned as assessable income - debtor in receivership - writing off of bad debts.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s 63,

G.E. Crane Sales Pty Ltd v. F.C. of T. [1971] HCA 75; (1971) 126 CLR 177

Case 28, 13 CTBR 223

Case A18 69 ATC 106

DECISION AND REASONS

Re: B302/1984

Applicant

And: Commissioner of Taxation

Respondent

AAT Decision No 2976

Date: October 23, 1986

Mr K L Beddoe, (Senior Member)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) B.302/1984

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

APPLICANT

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

RESPONDENT

REASONS FOR DECISION

The applicant seeks a deduction in respect of interest derived by him from Cambridge Credit Corporation Ltd ("Cambridge") but which was not paid to him.

2. At all relevant times the applicant was employed in the coal mining industry.

3. In his income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1983 the applicant claimed a deduction amounting to $1724. The claim was supported by the following statement in a schedule to the return:

"In the tax years 1972, 1973 and 1974 I declared interest of $464.85, $601.78 and $657.80 respectively received from Cambridge Credit Corporation Ltd. (Holder No. ...) . This company went into receivership and the interest I received was credited to my account. Some repayments of principal have been received. On contacting the Receiver, he advised that there will be a slim chance of the principal being repaid in full, with no chance of the interest being paid at all. As I claimed the interest as income I now claim it as a deduction."

4. Not only does that statement lack precision of language on its face but the evidence before the Tribunal showed that it also lacked precision as to the factual content.

5. The Commissioner disallowed the claim on making his assessment resulting in an objection by the applicant against the Commissioner's assessment. Because the Commissioner raised an issue before the Tribunal as to the grounds of the objection it is necessary that I set out the details of the objection in full. The notice of objection which followed the style of Form 8 in the First Schedule to the Income Tax Regulations stated:

"I HEREBY OBJECT against the assessment of Income Tax based on income derived during the year ended 30th JUNE, 1983 and issued to me by notice of assessment dated 14th OCTOBER, 1983, and claim that the assessment should be reduced by -

(a) the excision of the following amounts included in the assessable income:- THE REVERSAL OF INTEREST PREVIOUSLY INCLUDED AS INCOME OF $1,724 OR

(b) the allowance of the following amounts as deductions:- THE ALLOWING OF A DEDUCTION OF $1,724 FOR INTEREST PREVIOUSLY INCLUDED AS INCOME BUT WHICH HAS NOT BEEN PAID

The grounds on which I rely are:-

THE INTEREST WAS INCLUDED AS INCOME IN THE YEARS IN WHICH THE INTEREST WAS EARNED ALTHOUGH THE INTEREST WAS CREDITED AND NOT RECEIVED IN CASH.

AS THE COMPANY HAS BEEN PLACED IN LIQUIDATION, AND THE RECEIVER HAS INFORMED ME THAT INTEREST ACCRUED OR CREDITED WILL NOT BE PAID. I WILL NOT BE RECEIVING THE INTEREST IN CASH OR KIND, I THEREFORE CLAIM IT AS A REVERSAL OF INCOME OR AS A DEDUCTION AGAINST INCOME.

THE DISALLOWANCE OF THE SAID AMOUNT OF $1,724 AS PRIVATE EXPENDITURE IS DISPUTED AS NO MONEY WAS PAID OUT BY ME SO CLASSIFICATION OF THIS DEDUCTION AS EXPENDITURE IS INCORRECT.

THE INTEREST WAS INCLUDED AS INCOME IN THE TAX YEARS 1972, 1973 & 1974 AND WAS $464.85, $601.78 & $657.80 RESPECTIVELY."

6. The Commissioner's representative before the Tribunal contended that the objection raised grounds under section 51 only and it was not open to the taxpayer to argue that section 63 of the Income Tax Assessment Act (the Act) was also raised by the objection. I need not consider the authorities as it is patently clear to me that the notice of objection directed the Commissioner's attention to both section 51 and section 63 of the Act. Not only is that clear from the notice of objection itself but the Commissioner's statement of reasons given pursuant to Income Tax Regulation 35 makes it very clear that the Commissioner's attention was directed to section 63. No application was made by the taxpayer's representative for a direction in terms of paragraph 190(a) of the Act. I do not consider it was necessary to make such a direction. The Commissioner's contention as to the grounds of objection was without merit.

7. The parties sought to proceed by way of a statement of agreed facts; a practice which should be encouraged. The facts agreed by the parties were as follows:-

(1) Prospectus No. 25 dated 30 April 1971 invited applications for debenture stock in Cambridge Credit Corporation Limited (See Annexure A ).

(2) On 30 August 1971 the taxpayer and his wife applied for $6,000 debenture stock, over a 4 year period at an interest rate of 9% (See Annexure B).

(3) The application form provided for interest to be paid direct to the investor's bank.

(4) On the taxpayer's application form these words were deleted.

(5) The following words were inserted on the taxpayer's application form:

'Interest to be compounded'.

(See Annexure B ).

(6) These adjustments were initialled by the taxpayers.

(7) By the terms of the prospectus the debenture stock was redeemable at par on 30 August 1975.

(8) Interest was payable quarterly on the last day of June, September, December and March, and on the date of maturity.

(9) Prospectus No. 31 dated 6 May 1974, invited applications for debenture stock in Cambridge Credit Corporation Limited. (See Annexure D ).

(10) On 10 July 1974 the taxpayer and his wife applied for $400 debenture stock over a 12 month period at an interest rate of 12-1/2%.

(See Annexure E).

(11) By the terms of the prospectus the debenture stock was redeemable at par on 17 July 1975.

(12) Interest was payable quarterly on the last day of June, September, December and March and on the date of maturity.

(13) On 30 September 1974 a receiver was appointed to Cambridge Credit Corporation Limited.

(14) By a letter dated 19 September 1984 the receiver advised that the type of security in respect of the $6000 investment is a cumulative debenture stock and the security in respect of the $400 investment is debenture stock. (See Annexure F).

(15) Interest accrued and unpaid as at 30 September 1974 is as follows:

Cumulative Debenture Stock $1667.34

Debenture Stock $ 10.69

$1678.03

(16) Interest on the cumulative debenture stock was not paid to the debenture holders but was credited to their accounts.

(17) Distributions to date have totalled 35 cents in the dollar, ie $2827.30 has been distributed to CR and D Machin.

(18) This distribution represents 35% of $6400 plus $1678 interest ie $2240 of principal has been distributed and $587.30 of interest has been distributed.

(19) Neither the taxpayer nor his wife are registered as money lenders under the Money Lenders Act."

(Annexures not included in these reasons)

8. The statement of agreed facts failed to address issues raised by section 63 such as whether the interest had been brought to account by the taxpayer as assessable income of any year and whether the money had been lent to Cambridge in the ordinary course of the business of lending of money by a taxpayer who carries on that business. That resulted in the taxpayer giving oral evidence and further documentary evidence being tendered.

9. The taxpayer's evidence established that he does not lend money other than in the sense of conducting banking accounts and a deposit account with a credit union and that the loans to Cambridge was the only time he entered into such transactions. The funds invested came from the sale of a house no longer required by the taxpayer and his wife because they had been given the use of a house by his employer.

10. Exhibit B is an Interest Advice Slip issued by Cambridge and dated 30 June 1974. It shows inter alia that $169.98 had been credited to the taxpayer and his wife and that the accumulated balance stood at $7724.43. This advice slip partly explains the basis for the claim for a deduction of $1724 by the applicant. The initial investment was $6000, therefore the difference between the two amounts was $1724. The fact that the loan was in joint names was ignored when the claim for the deduction was made.

11. Exhibit C is two First Distribution Advices issued by the Receiver of Cambridge and dated 31.10.1978. The advices refer to two different loans and take a different format. The first reveals the following information:

A. Interest Calculated on Cumulative Basis $1898.23

B. Certificate Date 30 August 1971

C. Principal $6000.00

D. Interest Calculated on Simple Interest

Basis $1667.34

Distribution at 5 cents on sum of C and D

$7667.34 = $ 383.36

12. The second First Distribution Advice which forms part of Exhibit C revealed the following:

A. Certificate Date 15 July 1974

B. Principal $400.00

C. Interest at 30/9/74 $ 10.69

Distribution at 5 cents on sum of B and C = $ 20.53

13. Exhibit E includes a document described as "Advice to Payee" which takes a different form to the First Distribution Advices in Exhibit C. This document which is dated 24 December 1985 includes the following information:

Debenture Distribution No 6

Distribution at 5.0 cents on claim at

30 September 1974 of $8078.03 $403.90

14. All Distribution Advices put in evidence include the statement that the distribution is wholly in reduction of principal.

15. Exhibit D is copies of the applicant's 1972, 1973 and 1974 income tax returns. These copies were tendered by the applicant without objection from the Commissioners representative who made no attempt to tender the originals of the returns and, I infer, was unable to do so. These copy returns show that the applicant brought the following amounts of interest to account as assessable income in those years:-

Year ended 30 June 1972 $232

Year ended 30 June 1973 $301

Year ended 30 June 1974 $331

In the 1974 year $4 was derived from a source other than Cambridge so that the total interest derived from Cambridge and brought to account as assessable income was $862 which is 50% of $1724.

16. Exhibit E includes a circular letter dated 24 December 1985 from Permanent Nominees Ltd received by the applicant. The letter may be described as a report to debenture holders of Cambridge and its subsidiaries. The report noted that the sixth distribution brought the total repayments to debenture holders to 40 cents in the dollar in respect of their claims at 30 September 1974. It went on to say that although the receivers had previously estimated that debenture holders should receive approximately 55 cents in the dollar of their claim it was likely that they could receive between 65 cents and 74 cents in the dollar. That estimate was apparently made without taking into account the possibility of ultimately recovering damages from the auditors of Cambridge. The circular indicated that it was unlikely that the $145 million judgment against the auditors handed down by the Supreme Court of New South Wales would be recoverable in full but partial satisfaction could include an insurance payout of $20 million.

17. Following completion of the evidence the applicant's representative very properly conceded that the original claim made in the objection for a deduction of $1724 could not be supported. That claim had ignored the fact that only half of the interest had been derived and returned as income by the applicant and also ignored the fact that by 30 June 1983 portion of the interest had in fact been paid go the applicant.

18. The parties agreed that the amount still in dispute was $573.56, this being the amount of interest returned as assessable income less the applicant's share of amounts recovered from the receivers up to that date.

19. So far as it is relevant section 63 reads as follows:-

"63(1) Debts which are bad debts and are written off as such during the year of income, and -

(a) have been brought to account by the taxpayer as assessable income of any year; or

(b) ...

shall be allowable deductions.

(2) ....

(3) Where in the year of income a taxpayer receives an amount in respect of a debt for which a deduction has been allowed to him under this or the previous Act, his assessable income shall include that amount."

20. I think it is beyond argument that there was an existing debt owing by Cambridge to the applicant at all relevant times. Interest was payable on the debenture at 3 monthly intervals. That interest was not paid over to the applicant but was reinvested with Cambridge at the direction of the applicant. It was thereby added to the principal sum owing to the applicant and was therefore capitalised. The fact that it was capitalised did not alter its status as a debt owing by Cambridge to the applicant. The only relevant thing that happened was that the amount capitalised became payable at some time in the future rather than immediately. I am satisfied that the reinvested amounts of interest were debts within the terms of section 63 and remained debts until paid by Cambridge (G.E. Crane Sales Pty Ltd v. FC of T [1971] HCA 75; (1971) 126 CLR 177 AT 186-7). I do not think it was completely understood that the capitalised amounts became principal in the terminology used by the receivers of Cambridge.

21. I turn now to the question as to whether the debts were bad debts. Exhibit E establishes that at 24 December 1985 Permanent Nominees Ltd advised debenture holders that they were likely to receive between 65 cents and 74 cents in the dollar. This was an improvement on the figure previously advised of 55 cents in the dollar.

22. As at 30 June 1983 35 cents had been returned in respect of each dollar owing. Given that the estimate by the receivers of Cambridge at that time was that another 20 cents in the dollar might be returned in the future was the applicant entitled to take the view that the balance of the debt was bad, at least as to 45 cents in the dollar?

23. Was the applicant entitled to say "well 45 cents in each dollar is unlikely to be paid - that is a bad debt and that debt embraces the capitalised income which I previously returned as assessable income"? That was not put in so many words but it must be the effect of the applicant's submission in this matter.

24. The respondent Commissioner's representative submitted that there were four elements to be satisfied before the deduction could be allowed:

(a) there must be a debt;

(b) the debt must be bad - it is not sufficient that the debt merely be doubtful;

(c) the debt must be written off as a bad debt during the year of income; and

(d) the debt must have been brought to account as assessable income of any year of income.

25. It is beyond doubt, in my view, that there was a relevant debt. It is also beyond doubt that the applicant was not carrying on a business of lending money.

26. Notwithstanding the four criteria set down by the Commissioner's representative she then submitted that as the debt was in the nature of capital it could not be an allowable deduction under section 63. I rejected that submission during the course of the hearing because section 63, unlike sub-section 51(1) does not draw a distinction between losses on revenue account and losses on capital account.

27. In the ultimate the Commissioner's representative relied on a submission that the debt was not a bad debt and that there was no evidence that the debt had been written off.

28. As at the 30 June 1983 the prospects for the creditors of Cambridge Credit were not good. It seemed, at that time, that a major proportion of the debts still unpaid would never be paid. An objective estimate at that time would have resulted in such a conclusion. The fact that prospects have since improved so that a greater proportion of the debt may be recovered is beside the point. What was required in the 1983 year of income was an estimate as to what extent the debt was bad. Given that the debts had then been overdue for repayment by approximately eight years from the original maturity date and longer because of the default by Cambridge Credit the debts could only be described as bad debts and the applicant was quite justified in coming to that conclusion.

29. A debt does not have to be absolutely bad for it to be a bad debt within the terms of section 63. This is made clear by sub-section 63(3) which provides where a taxpayer received an amount in respect of a debt for which a deduction has been allowed, his assessable income shall include that amount. Such a provision would not be necessary if sub-section 63(1) only referred to bad debts which were absolutely bad and would not therefore ever result in recovery in whole or part by the creditor.

30. The applicant had a reasonable assurance of receiving a further 20 cents in the $1 of his debt. To that extent I find that the debts were not bad.

31. I find as a fact that the balance, being an amount of $397.08, was a bad debt during the year ended 30 June 1983.

32. The remaining issue is whether the applicant wrote off the amount claimed as a bad debt during the year of income. I was not referred to any authorities in respect of this issue.

33. In Case 28, 13 CTBR 223, 13 CTBR Reprint 141, the Taxation Board of Review considered a claim for deductions for bad debts by a chartered accountant. The Board found that the bad debts had been incurred in the ordinary course of a business of money lending by the taxpayer. The taxpayer kept books of account but did not formally write the debts off as bad debts in those books during the year of income.

34. At paragraphs 37-38 of their reasons Mr J P Hannan and Mr E F Hamilton (Members) stated:

"The Act does not in specific terms require a writing off in books of account, although this course is usually followed in most businesses. In the present case we have the simple fact that the items of 79 pounds and 292 pounds became bad debts and the prospect of recovery was utterly hopeless in June 1945.

In the circumstances we think it would be unduly harsh to insist upon rigid proof that the debts were formally written off by the taxpayer. And since it is not disputed that the items were brought to account as assessable income in the years mentioned, we are of opinion that the deductions should be allowed."

35. I have some hesitation in adopting the view expressed by Messrs Hannan and Hamilton. However, I have no hesitation in adopting the following passage from the reasons of the Chairman (Mr R R Gibson) who stated at paragraph 3 of his reasons in reference to the words "Bad debts written off as such in the year of income":

"I am unable to share the view that the condition denoted by these words can be satisfied where, in respect of a debt owed to the taxpayer concerned, there is no evidence whatever that anything was put in writing, in the year of income, from which it could be gathered that the taxpayer intended to treat the debt as bad. The words are plain and positive and they are so clearly objective that their very purpose seems to me to be to put the onus upon any taxpayer who seeks to obtain the benefit provided by the section to prove (if so required) by sufficient evidence that there was a physical writing off of the debt in the year of income. I do not suggest that the orthodox and usual practice whereby debts actually written off after the end of a year in the closing of the accounts for the year are shown in the accounts as debts written off at the end of the year is not an acceptable interpretation of the limitation literally imported by the words 'in the year' ... However, the need for the condition as to the writing off of bad debts is fairly apparent. Against any suggestion that the condition that the debts must be bad makes the further condition superfluous, it has to be borne in mind that in the average case the taxpayer must be allowed a considerable latitude in the exercise of his judgment as to the extent to which any particular debt is bad, the reason being that it is frequently impossible to foresee future events which might affect the worth of the debt. The Commissioner is in no better position and, although s. 63(3) is the logical and necessarily intended corrective of the excessive writing off of debts by reason of errors of judgment or the unavoidable failure of careful forecasts, the statutory condition as to writing off is a necessary corrective of the difficulties which would arise but for that condition by reason of the choice which would be left to the taxpayer and the overriding choice which would be left to the Commissioner, choices which in either case could be consistent with fair and honest judgment, as to which year among several a debt is to be claimed or treated as bad. And, of course, without the condition as to writing off, the section would give rise to a multiplication of claims in respect of any debt unless it were amended to apply to 'debts which become bad in the year of income'. Even that would leave difficulties of a kind not likely to occur as the section stands, because at present, with the protection of s. 63(3), the emphasis may be safely placed on the fact of writing off rather than on the strict and difficult proof of the depreciation of the debt. However, in view of the opinion which I stated at the outset, I am bound to hold that, because there is no evidence whatever of the physical writing off of the amounts of 79 pounds and 292 pounds which are claimed as bad debts (see paragraphs 35 to 38 of my colleagues' reason), those amounts are not allowable deductions."

36. It is relevant to note that the section dealt with by the Board of Review in Case 28 referred to bad debts written off "during the year of income" whereas Mr Gibson addressed himself to a proposition "Bad debts written off as such in the year of income".

37. The issue arose again in Case A18, 69 ATC 106, where Board of Review No 2 held (by majority) that the debt in question was not written off as a bad debt during the year in which it was claimed as a deduction; a writing off in the accounts after the close of the year of income not being a writing off during the year of income. While the majority took a view, contrary to the decision in Case 28 the views of Mr Gibson quoted above were supported by the dissenting member (Mr J D Davies as he then was) in the following passage at page 109:

"On these facts, it was put by the Commissioner's representative that no part of the debt was written off 'during the year of income'. He submitted that sec. 63 requires a notation in writing to be effected during the year of income. His submission, however, accords neither with the practice of the Income Tax Department nor with accepted accounting procedures. The writing off of bad debts is not a procedure having income tax significance only. It is an accounting procedure relevant to and taking its place most appropriately in the preparation of the final accounts of an accounting period. Its raison d'etre lies in the establishment of profit or loss of a financial period and proprietorship at the close of that period. I have already cited Rowlatt J.'s words: 'and this is for the purpose of a profit and loss account', and reference may also be made to Goldberg's Philosophy of Accounting which deals with the writing off of bad debts in Chapter X, "The Preparation of Final Statements', sec. Ill 'Final Adjusting Entries'. The writing off of a bad debt does not affect the quantum of the debt itself or the debtor/creditor relationship. It serves to reflect in the written accounts the factual value of the asset 'Debtors'. The writing off of a bad debt, then, is properly undertaken by a qualified accountant and in practice is usually effected by an accountant or tax agent after the close of a year of income in the accounts prepared for the year. Of course, bad debts may be actually written off in the year itself and commonly are so in the accounts of large organisations having accounting staff who prepare monthly, quarterly or other periodical balances. Yet, in most cases, because the writing off of bad debts had a place in the preparation of final accounts long before the introduction of sec. 63 of the Income Tax and Social Services contribution Assessment Acts, most bad debts are written off in the accounts for a financial period prepared after the close of that period. And the practice of the Income Tax Department is to allow a deduction of bad debts written off in books of account 'at 30 June'. I think that a decision that such a writing off did not give rise to an allowable deduction would astound the profession and be substantially ignored by the Department. I think that I should not read sec. 63 so narrowly, particularly as it contained, at the time with which we are concerned, the words "and no other bad debts shall be allowable deductions'."

38. In this case the applicant did not carry on a business, did not keep books of account and was not required to do so. In effect the only accounting records maintained by the applicant are his annual income tax returns prepared by his tax agent. That is a perfectly normal and unexceptional situation in the Australian community. Section 63 must be given effect accordingly. It cannot require that a taxpayer write off a bad debt in books of account which he does not have.

39. The only relevant place in which the applicant could write off the bad debt was his annual income return which by necessity must be prepared after the end of the year of income. The applicant did sufficient to comply with the section.

40. I have considered whether sub-section 51(1) applies in this case. I am satisfied it does not for the fundamental reason that the bad debts written off were losses of a capital nature.

41. The respondent Commissioner's decision on the objection should be set aside.

42. The objection should be allowed in part by allowing a deduction of $397.
