Nelson Tobacco Company Pty Ltd and Commissioner for ACT Revenue Collections [1989] AATA 566 (14 September 1989)

Last Updated: 9 April 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Business Franchise - wholesale tobacco - reassessment of licence fees

Fees calculated by reference to value of tobacco sold in the course of interstate trade - interstate trade within the meaning of s.92 of the Constitution - first sale after importation - ACT deemed to be a State - difficulty in determining which of undifferentiated goods constitute interstate trade

Onus of proof - balance of probabilities - Tribunal's function -relevance of onus where Tribunal left in uncertainty - need to construe legislation - relevance of facts within party's knowledge

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. A86/45 GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: NELSON TOBACCO COMPANY PTY LTD

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER FOR ACT REVENUE COLLECTIONS

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

14 September 1989 Deputy President R.K. Todd

1. This is an application for review pursuant to s.37(c) of the Business Franchise (Tobacco and Petroleum Products) Ordinance (now Act) 1984 ("the Act") of a decision, made by the then Commissioner for Business Franchises, that tobacco sold by the applicant, a wholesale tobacco merchant, was the subject of interstate trade and should attract a franchise fee under the Act. The respondent reassessed the fees payable by the applicant for the licence periods commencing 28 October, November and December 1984, and 28 February, March and April 1985.

2. Section 24(1) of the Act makes it an offence for a wholesale tobacco merchant to carry on wholesaling business without a licence.

3. Section 33(2) provides that "... where, in the opinion of the Commissioner the fee assessed in respect of any licence was assessed incorrectly, the Commissioner may at any time reassess the fee".

Section 33(5) provides that "[a] reassessment under this section shall be carried out in accordance with the principles of assessing fees under section 28 ...".

4. The logical order of the legislation is as follows:

"28.(1) Subject to this section, the fees to be paid for tobacco licences shall be as follows:

(a) for a wholesale tobacco merchant's licence -

(ii) which is in force for any period after 31 August 1984 - a fee of the specified amount together with an amount equal to the specified percentage of the value of tobacco sold by the applicant in the course of tobacco wholesaling during the relevant period (other than tobacco sold to the holder of a wholesale tobacco merchant's licence or a group wholesale tobacco merchant's licence);

(6) Where an application is made for a tobacco licence and the applicant carried on the business in respect of which the application for the licence was made during the whole of the relevant period and the Commissioner is of the opinion that he is unable for any reason accurately to assess the fee payable by the applicant under sub-section (1), the fee payable by the applicant in respect of the licence shall be such amount as is assessed by the Commissioner as being just and reasonable in the circumstances of the case, having regard to the tobacco that in the opinion of the Commissioner was sold by the applicant during that | period, and the relevant principles of determining fees under this section.

(13) A reference in this section to tobacco sold shall be read as a reference to tobacco sold in the course of interstate trade.

3.(5) A reference in this Ordinance to interstate trade is a reference to trade carried on in the Territory that would be trade and commerce among the States within the meaning of section 92 of the Constitution if the Territory were a State.

3.(9) Where petroleum products are, or tobacco is, sold in the Territory for the purpose of resale outside the Territory, the first-mentioned sale shall, for the purposes of this Ordinance, be taken to have been made in the course of interstate trade.

28.(8) The value of any tobacco sold for delivery and consumption inside the Territory shall be disregarded in determining fees payable under this section."

  1. The amount of the fee payable under s.28(1) is thus (a) a fee of the specified amount, the "specified amount" appearing in Gazette No.S309 7/8/84 as being $50.00, and (b) an amount equal to the specified percentage of the value of tobacco sold by the applicant in the course of tobacco wholesaling during the relevant period, the "specified percentage" being 15 per cent of the value of tobacco sold.
  2. The "relevant period" referred to in s.28(1)(a)(ii) is defined in s.3(1) as meaning, in respect of a particular month set out in column 1 of the Schedule, the preceding month specified in column 2, that is to say one looks at the sales in the two months preceding the one being examined. We are here concerned with tobacco wholesalers' licences from and including November 1984 to May 1985, so that what are under consideration are sales during the relevant period from September 1984 to March 1985.
  3. The applicant was assessed, in respect of sales during the relevant period, as owing fees payable of both a flat amount of $50.00, which amount is not in dispute, and an amount of $96,453.46 representing 15 per cent of the value of tobacco sold by wholesale during the relevant periods. This latter amount is in dispute and has been paid by the applicant.
  4. The assessment of the Commissioner was based on the findings of inspectors appointed pursuant to s.15 of the Act to carry out an investigation of the applicant's business. These findings are set out below, but the main conclusion drawn by the Commissioner on the basis of these findings was that, pursuant to s.28(1)(a)(ii), 15 per cent of the value of "tobacco sold" by the applicant was $96,453.46, that is to say the percentage of the value of interstate tobacco sales.
  5. The applicant's basic submission is that the sales in question, upon which this assessment was derived, are not interstate sales and were in fact for delivery and consumption inside the territory.
  6. There is a subsidiary contention regarding the interpretation of reg.3(1)(a) of the Business Franchise (Tobacco and Petroleum Products) Regulations 1984 as to whether the wholesale price therein referred to means the price actually paid to the applicant, or the price that would be paid but for the point of sale rebate. There is some evidence before the Tribunal, given by the inspectors who were called as witnesses, that the inspectors intended to take as the wholesale price payable in relation to each cash invoice the last price shown on the invoice, that is to say the price actually paid after deducting the rebate.
  7. In short, the question for the Tribunal is whether that part of the tobacco sold by the applicant between September 1984 and March 1985 inclusive was sold in the course of interstate trade. Parallel to this is the question whether that part of the tobacco sales in dispute were sales within the territory for delivery and consumption inside the territory. A third associated question is whether the sales in dispute were sales in the territory for the purpose of resale outside the territory.
  8. The applicant's Canberra operation was set up in the 1970s. From the evidence of Mr. P.S. Nelson, called by the applicant, it appears, and is not disputed, that when goods arrived at the Fyshwick warehouse they would be re-stored there until they were picked up by van salesmen or sold directly from the warehouse.

13. The licence fee was introduced in August 1984. Pursuant to this an internal memorandum was sent to the manager, a Mr. Hannett, to. inform him that as a 15 per cent assessment applied to all interstate sales as from 1 August 1984, it was now a requirement that the purchasers of tobacco products from the applicant declare the area in which the products were to be resold. If it was to be an interstate sale then the invoice value had to increase by 15 per cent to cover the licence fee. As a result a stamp was devised for use in the cases where the purchasers declared that the products were for consumption within the territory, so that the sale invoice was stamped "I declare that the tobacco products on this invoice are for resale solely in the ACT", and signed. Most of the invoices relevant to the disputed sales bore this stamp.

14. On 14 May 1985, Mr. Wallace, Mr. Brown and Mr. Adams carried out a routine inspection, empowered by the then Ordinance, of the applicant's Fyshwick premises. They examined sales records during the relevant period and apparently noted some office sales invoices, office sales being direct sales from the premises, which they considered suspect. Three bases for this suspicion were -

(i) Irregularities such as the signature of the purported purchasers appeared to be in the same handwriting, although the names were different. The applicant submits that the respondent's evidence on this was "most shadowy".

(ii) The name on the original invoice, in one case, differed from that appearing on the copy of the invoice.

(iii) A number of apparently unconnected customers had recurrent purchasing patterns of an identical type.

These discrepancies were not really put to Mr. Nelson by the respondent. The respondent asked only about Mr. Nelson's belief in the accuracy of the invoices.

15. The inspections revealed also that sales decreased in April 1985. This was one month after the departure of Mr. Hannett, the previous manager, and Mr. Steele, storeman, who were dismissed, apparently because of possible conflict of interest arising from their setting up of a tobacco retailing business in Canberra. Mr. Nelson explained the decrease in sales on the basis of seasonal factors. The respondent submits that the figures cannot be explained purely on this basis.

  1. It was also found that the large element of cash deposits by the applicant were "reasonably similar" to the sorts of sums involved in the suspect sale invoices. The applicant submits that this was not put to Mr. Nelson in cross-examination.
  2. On 17 May 1985 the inspectors visited a number of the establishments of persons named as customers on the suspect invoices. It is these visits that were the subject of most contention and in relation to which much evidence was adduced. Two inspectors, Mr. May and Mr. Wallace, were called for the respondent and cross-examined vigorously by counsel for the applicant. The inspectors had concluded that the sales recorded on the "suspect" invoices must have been for the purpose of resale outside the ACT.
  3. The respondent relied on the following in this regard:

(i) Thirty-one establishments denied any dealings with the applicant. Originally the statements at T.1, 4-6 gave the number of such establishments as twenty-seven.

(ii) Eleven establishments had ceased operation before 1 September 1984 or could not be located. [T.1, 4-6 said nineteen]

(iii) Fourteen establishments acknowledged dealing with Nelson but not as indicated by the invoices - generally the invoices stated a larger quantity of sales than the establishment would have capacity to hold. [T.1, 4-6 said nine].

(iv) Inferences, it was claimed, should be drawn that, where office invoices were false or unreliable, sales had taken place in amounts shown on the invoices but not to customers shown on them.

(v) The constancy of monthly van sales whereas office sales showed a substantial reduction consequent upon certain staff dismissals.

  1. The applicant's primary submission was that the existence of such discrepancies in the above statements was a factor inviting rejection of the accuracy of the inspections, and of the Commissioner's figures. The applicant submitted that neither Mr. May nor Mr. Wallace could explain how the figures came to be changed.
  2. Both inspectors were cross-examined with the intendment of showing that the inspections made of individual establishments were inadequate. It was sought to be adduced that in cases where an establishment could not be located the inspectors had failed to make sufficient inquiries as to the whereabouts of such locations. The applicant also queried individual cases in which there appeared to be a discrepancy between the invoice quantity and the quantity which the interviewees asserted that the establishment was capable of holding. Often, it was submitted, the inspectors did not show the invoices to the interviewees or make sufficient inquiries as to who was the manager or operator of the business at the relevant time. It was also suggested to the inspectors that the proprietors at the establishments may have had some reason or purpose of their own to deny any dealings with the applicant, or to deny dealing with them in the amounts shown on the invoices. Neither inspector had considered this and, in fact, had accepted the truth and veracity of the answers received. In its written submission the applicant contended that the process used was cursory and unreliable and should not be relied upon to establish that the purpose of the applicant was resale outside the territory. This criticism was made in detailed and persuasive form on pp.16-28 of the applicant's submission to which I refer and upon which I place great weight.

21. The applicant submitted that there was no commercial gain in selling the tobacco interstate. This was put to Mr. Nelson in cross-examination. He answered that unless the company received the 15 per cent fee, and failed to pass it on, there was no commercial gain. When the possibility of the applicant keeping the fee was put to the inspectors, Mr. May appeared to believe that the applicant did not receive any part of the 15 per cent although later in his evidence he denied this. Mr. Wallace said that he just assumed that the applicant was receiving it.

22. The respondent, on the other hand, submitted that, on the basis of this evidence, the applicant's system was flawed, and that reliance on the stamped declaration on the invoices was insufficient given that some were signed and some were not.

23. The respondent:

(i) Denied the applicant's assertion that there was no commercial gain to be received if they sold tobacco interstate from the ACT without adding to the price and collecting the 15 per cent fee;

(ii) Said that the applicant was aware of the respondent's allegations that some customers were fictional identities; that customers were buying at a level far in excess of their custom; and that customers were being named as purchasing goods when those customers in truth had earlier ceased to purchase from the applicant;

(iii) Said that nevertheless the applicant had made no inquiries nor did it seek to adduce evidence in rebuttal of any of those allegations;

(iv) Said that the applicant had no system of audit analysis;

(v) Said that the evidence of the witnesses Wallace and May should be accepted.

  1. The main thrust of the applicant's case regarding the express or implied terms of sales transactions and circumstances surrounding the sales was its reliance upon the stamps on the invoices declaring that the tobacco products referred to on the invoice were "for resale solely in the ACT". The respondent, in a submission in reply, said that other than in instances in which there were such signatures, the inference would be to the contrary.
  2. The fact is however that after lengthy consideration of the evidence I have found the criticisms made by each party of the evidentiary strength of its opponent's case unanswerable. Beyond the above recitation of the facts, and although both applicant and respondent contended for various further findings, I am unable to make findings of fact having any degree of certainty. The main issue for the Tribunal arising from the competing contentions is thus whether, and if so which of the parties should be said to bear an onus of proof or disproof if such exists. The evidence indicates that the inspectors may have proceeded on the assumption that the onus was on the applicant to disprove any conclusions drawn by the respondent that the disputed sales were interstate sales. But is it appropriate to speak at all, in terms of the Tribunal's function, in terms of "onus of proof"?
  3. The first consideration is that the essential function of the Tribunal is administrative rather than judicial. In Drake v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1979) 2 ALD 60 at 68 Bowen CJ and Deane J said: "The function of the Tribunal is, as we have said, an administrative one. It is to review the administrative decision that is under attack before it ... The question for the determination of the Tribunal is not whether the decision which the decision maker made was the correct or preferable one on the material before him. The question for determination of the Tribunal is whether the decision was the correct or preferable one before the Tribunal."
  4. As submitted by the respondent, the Tribunal is required by s.43 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") to put itself in the position of the decision maker and, in the light of the material before the Tribunal, to make its own decision in place of the decision maker.
  5. The first discussion of the question of onus was in Re Ladybird Children's Wear Pty Ltd and Department of Business and Consumer Affairs (1976) 1 ALD 1 at 5 where the Tribunal said, in effect, that there is neither an onus upon an applicant to prove that a decision maker's decision is wrong, nor any onus upon a decision maker to prove that the decision is right. The language of the tariff (in that case), or the state of the known facts, might however give rise to some onus. In other words there might be a practical onus placed upon one of the parties.
  6. Unlike a court, the Tribunal has at least to some extent a duty of inquiry, and may satisfy itself as to the existence or otherwise of certain facts. In judicial proceedings the court relies upon one party seeking to prove a case against the other, hence the existence of an onus on one party or the other can become important: see Re Control Investments Pty Ltd and Others and Australian Broadcasting Tribunal (No.3) (1981) 39 ALR 281, 392.
  7. The Federal Court in the crucial decision in McDonald v Director-General of Social Security [1984] FCA 57; (1984) 6 ALD 6 adopted a cautious approach to the "use of legal rules about onus outside a court" because the Tribunal "may inform itself on any matter in such manner as it thinks appropriate": s.33 of the AAT Act. In proceedings before the Tribunal it was, the Court said, more appropriate to look to the statute in question, natural justice or common sense. I return to a consideration of McDonald below.
  8. If the Tribunal is not satisfied on the balance of probabilities (see Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs v Pochi [1980] FCA 85; (1980) 4 ALD 139) that the facts exist to activate a statutory power, then it must decide against the exercise of the power. However if one party is in possession of facts that are peculiarly within its knowledge, a failure to produce the same may lead to an unfavourable inference. See also Re Macpherson and Secretary to the Department of Social Security (1985) 7 ALN N139; Re Eckersley and Minister for Capital Territory (1979) 2 ALD 303.
  9. The Tribunal has from early days sought to avoid reliance on curial rules as to onus of proof: See Re Ladybird to which I have already referred. That philosophy was vindicated by the decision of the Federal Court in McDonald. With respect, there is much that could usefully be set out here from McDonald's Case, but I shall simply include two particular passages. The first is from the judgment of Woodward J. at 11 where he said:

"[The AAT] must act on the material which is before it but, as I have already pointed out, it is not bound by rules of evidence and may inform itself on any matter in such manner as it thinks appropriate.

It is true that facts may be peculiarly within the knowledge of a party to an issue, and a failure by that party to produce evidence as to those facts may lead to an unfavourable inference being drawn -but it is not helpful to categorize this common sense approach to evidence as an example of an evidential onus of proof. The same may be said of a case where a good deal of evidence pointing in one direction is before the Tribunal, and any intelligent observer could see that unless contrary material comes to light that is the way the decision is likely to go. Putting such cases to one side there can be no evidential onus of proof in proceedings before the AAT unless the relevant legislation provides for it, and in the present case the Social Security Act 1947 does not.

If the AAT finds itself in a state of uncertainty after considering all the available material, unable to decide a question of fact either way on the balance of probabilities, it will be necessary for it to analyse carefully the decision it is reviewing. If, for example, it is a decision whether or not to cancel a pension in the light of changed circumstances, then it has failed to achieve the statutory requirement of reaching a state of mind that the pension should be cancelled. If, on the other hand, it is a decision, to be made in the light of fresh evidence, whether or not the pension should ever have been granted in the first place, then it has failed to be satisfied that the person ever was permanently incapacitated for work. For a comparable analysis as to the onus of proof (properly so-called) before a judicial tribunal see Phillips v The Commonwealth [1964] HCA 22; (1964) 110 CLR 347 at 350."

And per Jenkinson J. at 21:

"There may be difficulty, as Woodward J has pointed out, in adapting the curial conception to the processes of administrative determination of individual rights. Some of the purposes which the conception serves in a curial proceeding are achieved by other means in administrative proceedings. A court waits upon the parties to litigation to tender their proofs. When the parties differ as to which shall go first into evidence, their difference is resolved by determining upon which lies the burden of proof. (See W M Best, An Exposition of the Practice Relative to the Right to Begin.) But the administrative decision-maker will commonly inform himself of the facts by his own inquiries, as well as receiving such proofs as the individual citizen and those who may be authorized to oppose the citizen's interest choose to place before him. And he will not ordinarily be free, as a court is ordinarily free, to determine a matter against the party on whom lies the onus of proof, and who fails to offer any proof in discharge of the onus, without further inquiry. When the party to litigation on whom the onus of proof of an issue lies has concluded his evidence, the court may be called upon by the other party to determine the question of law whether that evidence can support a verdict or finding for him on whom the onus lies. Except by special legislative direction no administrative decision-maker could be so constrained. In many cases subject to administrative decision there is in any event no other party in controversy with him on whom the onus may be said to lie.

There is, however, in my opinion a dilemma in which either a court or an administrative authority determining rights or liabilities may find itself, for the resolution of which the same principles are applicable by each tribunal. Either tribunal may find itself unpersuaded either that a circumstance exists or that it does not exist. (The same may be said of a past or a future circumstance.) The court or the administrative authority will determine, by reference to the substantive law, whether it is the existence or the non-existence of the circumstance which is determinative of the question for decision. In this case the Administrative Appeals Tribunal would determine whether the Social Security Act 1947, upon its proper construction, required that the applicant's pension be cancelled if she were found not to be permanently incapacitated for work, or required that the pension be cancelled unless she were found to be permanently incapacitated for work. In the former case the Tribunal's lack of persuasion that permanent incapacity did not exist would preclude cancellation. In the latter case the Tribunal's lack of persuasion that permanent incapacity did exist would result in cancellation. An application of the same principles by a court in resolution of the same dilemma is to be found in Maher-Smith v Gaw [1969] VicRp 47; [1969] VR 371. In a court the principles are expressed in terms of the onus or burden of proof. When those principles are applied in an administrative tribunal, there may be risk of misconception if the curial modes of expression are employed".

33. As the respondent's submission pointed out, the Full Court of the Federal Court followed and expanded the analysis adopted in McDonald in East v Repatriation Commission [1987] FCA 242; (1987) 12 ALD 389. But the dilemma referred to by Jenkinson J. in McDonald's Case is precisely that with which I am faced here. I am "unpersuaded" that certain circumstances exist, or do not exist, respectively as contended for by the parties. Essentially, the applicant can really do no more than refer to the notations on the invoices. The respondent is in turn forced to rely on the evidence of the inspectors which was, in respect of so great a proportion of the transactions the subject of that evidence, so riddled with problems as to cast very substantial doubt on the evidentiary integrity of the corpus of the evidence as a whole. What I must to do therefore is "analyse carefully the decision the Commonwealth Tribunal is reviewing" (per Woodward J. at 11) in terms of the relevant legislation. That should establish upon which party falls the consequence of the Tribunal "(finding) itself in a state of uncertainty after considering all the available material, unable to decide a question of fact either way on the balance of probabilities ...".

  1. On the question of onus, the respondent accepted that there was a civil standard of proof imposed on the respondent to establish whether any circumstances existed to justify the re-assessment and/or the application of s.28(6). That established however, the Tribunal should, it was said, look to all of the relevant facts, including the invoices, and determine again according to the civil onus whether or not any of the relevant facts, including the invoices, were able to be disregarded pursuant to s.28(8) and then determine the fee payable according to the test of what is just and reasonable in the circumstances of the case.
  2. The Act here in question, unlike the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s.190(b), contains no provision for any onus to be cast on one party or the other in proceedings before the Tribunal. The respondent submitted that "the legislation should be viewed as revenue gathering and, in terms of its various deemings, casts an onus upon the applicant to gain the benefit of any exclusion." No authority was cited for the proposition that "revenue gathering" legislation as such connotes the placing of an onus on the person seeking to avoid its impact. If such a proposition were correct, s.190(b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the ITAA") would hardly be necessary. It is worth noting that Professor Pearce in Statutory Interpretation In Australia (2nd ed.) 1981 at p.148 states that the approach of the courts to the interpretation of exemption and exception sections of taxing Acts is that where their construction is in doubt, the interpretation should favour the claimant. The matter of onus is however in my opinion, as directed by what was said in McDonald, to be decided from the terms of the legislation.
  3. Thus for the first time in any case in which I have been involved in the Tribunal, with the exception (perhaps) of Re Eckersley, or in any case of which I am aware save perhaps Re Shearing and Director-General of Social Security (19 83) 6 ALN N12 (Decision No.1030, 9 May 1983), the matter clearly falls, in my opinion, to be decided on what I must still call, for want of a better name, the onus of proof, using that expression guardedly in the light of the analysis provided in McDonald. While I have made a number of references to the legislation so far, the whole of the preceding recitation, approached in particular in the light of McDonald, makes it clear that the crucial factor, given my inability to make from the evidence findings of fact that would lead to a clear resolution of the matter, is an analysis of the legislation.
  4. The crucial nature of the problem is exemplified by the way in which the respondent's contentions were set out in written submissions. It was said (paragraph 3(b)) that the fee payable was a flat amount of $50.00 and 15 per cent of the value of tobacco sold by wholesale during the relevant period, "excluding tobacco sold for delivery and consumption inside the ACT ...". It was then submitted (paragraph 5) that the applicant "(bore) the onus of showing an entitlement to the benefit of an exemption which applies for products of resale solely in the ACT". If this were to be seen as a correct analysis of how the legislation works, then the applicant's case would not be easy to support. But is that how it works?
  5. The respondent argued that it was for a party seeking to be excluded from the application of s.28 to show "entitlement" to the exclusion provision (p.4, paragraph 10). The legislation has been set out in paragraph 4 above. To appreciate the situation better however, the definitional character of ss.28(13) and 3(5) is displayed more readily if their terms are written into the relevant portion of s.28 (1) (a)(ii) . The words within sguare brackets are taken from s.28(13) and the words within round brackets are taken from s.3(5):

"- a fee of the specified amount together with an amount equal to the specified percentage of the value of tobacco sold [in the course of interstate trade] (carried on in the Territory that would be trade and-commerce among the States within the meaning of section 92 of the Constitution if the Territory were a State) in the course of tobacco wholesaling during the relevant period."

  1. It will be observed that the terms of s.3(9) and s.28(8) stand outside these provisions and are, as was stated on p. 5 of the Reasons for Decision in Re Canberra Fuel Distributors Pty Ltd and Commissioner for Business Franchises (1985) 7 ALN N326 -Decision No.2143, 21 May 1985, an "inclusion provision", in the case of s.3{9), and an "exclusion provision" in the case of s.28(8), which is the equivalent of s.31(8) there under discussion. The difficulty in the way of their application is exposed in those reasons. But s.3(9) can apply so as to bring into the definitional field of the expression "in the course of interstate trade", in the compounded legislative expression set out above, trade that would not otherwise have been found to have been within such fold.
  2. The thrust of the respondent's submission being that there was a civil standard of proof to be established by the Respondent as to whether any circumstances exist for being able to involve the re-assessment and/or application of Section 28(6), it was then submitted that "that circumstance having been so established, the Tribunal must look to all of the relevant facts including the invoices, albeit under various categories or style of categories, and determine again on a civil onus whether or not any of the relevant facts including the invoices were able to be disregarded pursuant to Section 28(8) and then determine the fee payable on the test of what is just and reasonable in the circumstances of the case."

41. On my analysis of the legislation, I do not agree with the respondent's submission. It does not reflect the relatively simple requirements of the legislation as set out in paragraph 38 above in the compounded expression of ss.28(1)(a)(ii), 28(13) and 3(5) that is there set out. To do otherwise would be to torture the legislation so as to create out of positive words a negative statement of onus of proof. I agree with the submission for the applicant that the first question is whether that part of the tobacco sold by the applicant between September 1984 and March 1985 which is presently in dispute was tobacco sold in the course of interstate trade (ss.28(1) (a)(ii), 28(13) and 3(5)).

42. This involves consideration of whether the general course of the applicant's trade in the ACT can be seen to have been in interstate trade. For the reasons set out on pp. 6-9 of the applicant's submission I do not consider this to be so:

The applicant's submission was that -

(i) (a) The general course of the applicant's trade in the ACT cannot be seen to have been in interstate trade.

(b) The evidence was that the majority of the tobacco products which came to Canberra was sourced in Sydney through the tobacco manufacturers and was brought into the Sydney major warehouse and redespatched by way of a transfer document to the Canberra depot.

(c) Once the product reached Canberra, being delivered some 3 to 4 times a week, it was either sold within Canberra through sales that were made at the Canberra location or further despatched to other country van salesmen (in New South Wales) via Canberra transport companies.

(d) Something in the order of 50 per cent to 70 per cent of total sales were made outside the ACT.

(e) The purpose of the Canberra depot was ease of transportation through New South Wales.

(f) Once the tobacco products arrived in Canberra from Sydney they would be stored in a warehouse "and then would be there for picking when the orders or transactions took place out of Canberra."

(g) The tobacco products would be warehoused in the ACT predominantly for 2-3 days or less.

(h) This evidence was not challenged.

(ii) (a) The trade as so described would not of itself constitute interstate trade and commerce among the States within the meaning of s.92 if the Territory were a State. In this regard Re Canberra Fuel Distributors was distinguishable. In the present case the goods were brought to the ACT for warehousing and then, of that undifferentiated quantity 50 per cent to 70 per cent was later transported onwards. There could thus, it was submitted, be no question here of all the sales being sales in the ACT since 50 per cent to 70 per cent of them were not.

(b) There is no authority holding that the mere sale of goods in a State in those circumstances, there being no contractual requirement or specification that the goods be from interstate, constitutes interstate trade within the meaning of s.92. North Eastern Dairy Co. Ltd. v Dairy Industry Authority of N.S.W. [1975] HCA 45; (1975) 134 CLR 559 was to be distinguished. There, by design, the shops in Albury were the agents for North Eastern Dairy. The whole trade was seen as inseverable and complete or concluded by the first sale in New South Wales.

(c) An interstate movement of goods ends, and with it the protection of s.92, where goods reach a distribution centre from which they are distributed intrastate: Russell v. Walters [1957] HCA 21; (1957) 96 CLR 177. Likewise where the goods are unloaded and stored for the owner's use: Grannall v Kellaway and Sons Pty Ltd [1955] HCA 5; (1955) 93 CLR 36; and similarly where the goods are mixed with other goods in the State of destination: S.O.S. (Mowbray) Pty Ltd v Mead (1972) 124 CLR 529.

(d) Since it did not appear in the evidence that the first sale of such tobacco products as were sold in the ACT was of goods imported from New South Wales for the purpose of sale in the ACT, the sale of particular tobacco products did not form part of the applicant's interstate trade. The sales were not interstate sales by reason of s.3(5) merely by reason of the goods having their origin interstate.

43. I agree with and adapt the applicant's submission as set out above. The respondent's determined reliance on the contention that the applicant bore an onus of disproof led to a diminished attack on the propositions put by the applicant in relation to s.92, but I wish to add some further considerations. In relation to the sales made from the Canberra depot, should it be said that this part of the applicant's trade constituted interstate trade as a "first sale" after importation? In the North Eastern Dairy Case the majority of the Court found that the first sale by the importer in that case was a part of interstate trade and inseverably connected with it. In that case the owner of a milk processing plant in Victoria distributed milk and milk products from a depot in Wodonga to persons in Albury, using its own vehicles. It also delivered to agents in Albury. It was held by the majority, looking at the whole process, that the end sale was a "consequence of the transaction" (p.599) and "the purpose of the transaction" (p.633). This was reinforced in Permewan Wright Consolidated Proprietary Limited v Trewhitt [1979] HCA 58; (1977) 145 CLR 1, where Permewan Wright acted as agent for a New South Wales egg producer and sold eggs in Victoria which had been brought from New South Wales into Victoria. The test of inseparable connection was applied. The same point underlay the decision in Re Canberra Fuel Distributors.

  1. The differences in the present case have been referred to in my setting out of the applicant's submission. But I would emphasise that in Re Canberra Fuel Distributors, in Permewan Wright and in the North Eastern Dairy Case the first sale of goods after import were always an inseparable part of the interstate trade. Here, the whole of the goods were brought into the ACT and 50 per cent to 70 per cent of them were moved on, leading to great difficulty in identifying sales in the ACT as an inseparable part of an entire interstate transaction.
  2. The respondent as I see it had no satisfactory answer to these problems, nor in my opinion are there answers to them, once the matter is approached within the legal parameters which I have extracted from the legislation. It follows, as the applicant's submission said, that for the respondent to succeed in imposing the additional liability contended for the Tribunal must be persuaded that s.3(9) should apply. The difficulties in establishing that it does, especially in the light of the fact that it is the purpose of the applicant that must be established, were made obvious in Canberra Fuel Distributors.
    1. The applicant, rightly in my submission, submitted that none of the material put forward by the respondent would persuade the Tribunal that s.3(9) could apply, and that none of it went close to showing the applicant's purpose. The doubt, if any, raised would be as to whether every purchaser was truthful with the applicant in respect of some of the disputed sales.
    2. As to 3.28(8), in Re Canberra Fuel Distributors the Tribunal treated the equivalent provision to s.28(8), namely s.31(8), as an exclusionary provision, its operation depending upon the examination of the transactions between the applicant and the purchaser so as to determine the purpose of the sale. It is the seller's purpose, here the applicant's, which is relevant, and the purpose arises from express or implied terms of the contract or by implication arising from the circumstances surrounding the sale.
  3. It seems to me that if it is found that the applicant's trade in the ACT is trade and commerce among the States within the meaning of s.92, it is then that s.28(8) comes to be considered. In Re Canberra Fuel Distributors the Tribunal first considered the exclusion in s.31(8), the equivalent of s.28(8), and found, from the facts of that case, that it did not apply and then went on to consider the main provision. That appears to me to have been because the Tribunal was disposed, the answers to the points in question appearing to it to be clear, to get its consideration of ss.3(9) and 28(8) out of the way. It did not, as I see it, intend to rule that consideration of those provisions was logically prior to consideration of s.28(1)(a)(ii).
  4. I should add that I have not overlooked the principle referred to in McDonald and in Re Eckersley that facts may be peculiarly within the knowledge of a party to an issue, and that a failure by that party to produce evidence as to those facts may lead to an unfavourable inference being drawn. Such an inference is not in my opinion open in the present case. It could be said that the applicant should have had a better system of records. But in the absence of a statutory onus upon the tobacco merchant it is not possible to invoke the principle stated in Trautwein v F.C. of T. [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63 at 87 that -

"There is every reason to assume that the legislature did not intend to confer upon a potential taxpayer the valuable privilege of disqualifying himself in that capacity by the simple and relatively unskilled method of losing either his memory or his books."

  1. The applicant was entitled to keep such records as were necessary having regard to companies and taxation legislation and in particular to s.39 of the Act. It was not required, so far as I am aware, to keep records that would meet every possible governmental demand unless at the end of the day the imposition of an onus of proof to disprove such demands would effectively so oblige it. I have found that no such onus was imposed. The facts might, as a matter of prudence, have been peculiarly within the knowledge of the applicant had it kept more detailed records, but in fact they were not. The applicant was not shown to have any records or other knowledge beyond the fact of the stamp on the invoice, and in practical terms it is a good question how far the applicant had to go in cross-examining purchasers at the point of sale as to their intentions.
  2. In the circumstances there is no need to refer further to s.28(8), the "exclusion" provision.
  3. The respondent's submission on onus of proof not having been accepted, I am unable to see a basis for the application of s.28(6), which was mentioned in that part of the respondent's submission referred to in paragraph 40 above.
  4. It is true that to improve its proofs the respondent could have sought to call as many as possible of the purchasers named in the invoices. The onus lying where I have found it to lie, that is arguably what should have occurred. But I am bound to say that in my opinion to do so would have involved an enormous expenditure of cost and time quite disproportionate to what was at stake, from the point of view not only of the respondent but also of the Tribunal and indeed the applicant. No doubt the question of the insertion of an onus provision into the legislation will come under consideration as a result of this Decision. For it to be effective some attention would also have to be given to the logical sequence, and the drafting of, the legislative provisions which I have been discussing.

54. For the reasons set out above the Tribunal will set aside the decision under review and in substitution therefor decide that the sales by the applicant of the tobacco the subject of the decision under review during the relevant periods did not attract a franchise fee under the Act.
