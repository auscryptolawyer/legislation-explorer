Verma and Commissioner of Taxation (Taxation) [2026] ARTA 343 (13 March 2026)

Applicant: Mr. Deepak VERMA

Respondent: Commissioner of Taxation

Tribunal Number: 2024/3649

Tribunal: General Member M Burch

Place: Melbourne

Date: 13 March 2026

Decision: The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

Catchwords

TAXATION – administrative penalties – whether applicant liable for administrative penalties on the basis of intentional disregard – where applicant gave his personal information to acquaintance and allowed acquaintance to lodge false and misleading Business Activity Statements resulting in fraudulent GST refunds – where applicant claims to be victim of scam – whether penalties imposed were excessive or incorrect – whether penalties should be wholly or partly remitted – objection decision affirmed

Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. This case is about penalties imposed on the Applicant, Mr. Deepak Verma, for intentionally disregarding the taxation laws by allowing an acquaintance to lodge false Business Activity Statements (‘BAS’) on Mr. Verma’s behalf resulting in a GST refund, the benefits of which Mr. Verma and his acquaintance shared.
  2. The documentary evidence in this case is contained in the Hearing Bundle and was identified as Exhibit 1 at the Tribunal hearing held on 11 February 2026 (‘Hearing’). The Hearing Bundle contains Mr. Verma’s Statement of Facts and a statement from a witness, Mr. Sanket Rawal;[1] the documents relevant to the Respondent’s objection decision at issue in this case and required under the relevant legislation (‘Tribunal Documents’);[2] and the Respondent’s Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions (‘Respondent’s SFIC’).[3] Mr. Verma and Mr. Rawal gave oral evidence at the Hearing.
  3. Mr. Verma has applied for a review[4] of the Respondent’s decision[5] to disallow Mr. Verma’s objection[6] to the Respondent’s penalty assessment.[7] This case involves only the Penalty Assessment, as there is no reviewable decision regarding the underlying shortfall and resulting assessments of net amount.[8]
  4. Mr. Verma as the taxpayer has the burden of proving, on the balance of probabilities, that the Penalty Assessment was ‘excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessment should have been’.[9]
  5. The issues in this case are whether:
    (a) the Penalty Assessment for intentional disregard of taxation law and the related uplift was properly imposed under ss 284-75, 284-80, 284-85, 284-90, and 284-220 of Schedule 1 to TAA 1953; and

    (b) the Respondent should remit the penalties in part or in full under s 298-20 of Schedule 1 to TAA 1953.

BACKGROUND AND EVIDENCE

  1. Mr. Verma worked as a taxi driver starting in mid-2011.[10]
  2. At some point in early 2012, Mr. Verma met a man named Mr. Narveen Malhotra through mutual acquaintances.[11] Mr. Verma discussed with Mr. Malhotra the former’s financial difficulties and indebtedness and Mr. Malhotra told Mr. Verma that he could help him get some money.
  3. As discussed below, Mr. Verma has given differing descriptions of the plans he made with Mr. Malhotra in order to get money. However, it is not in dispute that Mr. Verma provided to Mr. Malhotra all the personal information that he would need in order to lodge documents and otherwise interact with the Australian Taxation Office (‘ATO’) in Mr. Verma’s name, including his date of birth, driver’s license number, Australian Business Number (‘ABN’), Tax File Number (‘TFN’), bank account details, and email passwords.
  4. In March and April of 2012, Mr. Malhotra lodged BAS and amended BAS on behalf of Mr. Verma, relating to the quarterly tax periods between 1 October 2011 and 31 March 2012. In July 2012, Mr. Malhotra lodged BAS on behalf of Mr. Verma relating to the quarterly tax period ending 30 June 2012. The period from 1 October 2011 until 30 June 2012 (‘Relevant Period’) is the period to which the penalties that are the subject of this review relate. The BAS and amended BAS that were lodged for the Relevant Period incorrectly claimed GST refunds, leading to a total of $27,102 being paid to Mr. Verma’s account in April 2012.[12]
  5. On 16 August 2012, the Respondent wrote to Mr. Verma to arrange a meeting to discuss the BAS lodged for the Relevant Period.[13] Mr. Verma said at the Audit Interview that this letter prompted him to seek legal representation.
  6. Mr. Verma prepared a letter dated 29 August 2012 (‘Letter’), in which he wrote that Mr. Malhotra told him that ‘he is going to get me money from ATO’ and ‘it’s not illegal it’s your GST claims from previous years’.[14] Mr. Verma continued, ‘[A]s I didn’t have much knowledge about tax thing I believed him’.[15]
  7. On 31 August 2012, the Respondent received a letter from LAC Lawyers advising that Mr. Verma had been the victim of ‘identity theft’ and had retained the firm to make an ‘unprompted voluntary disclosure’ regarding his tax affairs.[16] The letter from LAC Lawyers did not mention Mr. Verma’s Letter dated 29 August 2012.
  8. On 19 October 2012, the Respondent issued a notice to Mr. Verma regarding the Audit Interview and on 2 November 2012 the Respondent confirmed to Mr. Verma in a written notification that it was auditing him for tax periods including the Relevant Period.[17]
  9. On 22 November 2012, the Respondent conducted the Audit Interview. Mr. Verma said at the Audit Interview that LAC Lawyers were no longer representing him.[18]
  10. At the Audit Interview, Mr. Verma said that Mr. Malhotra ‘promised him a fool proof way of getting money, by lodging BAS and getting tax refunds he was entitled to’ by transferring a newsagency business from Mr. Malhotra to Mr. Verma and then back again, which would allow him to ‘get the refund from the ATO for the purchase of business’.[19]
  11. Mr. Verma said at the Audit Interview (as in his Letter) that he gave Mr. Malhotra all of his personal information so that Mr. Malhotra could lodge documents and otherwise communicate with the ATO on Mr. Verma’s behalf. He also said at the Audit Interview (as in his Letter) that he agreed with Mr. Malhotra to split any refund money received 50/50.
  12. At the Audit Interview, Mr. Verma said that he realised that Mr. Malhotra was doing the wrong thing in July 2012, when he saw an ATO officer at Mr. Malhotra’s newsagency business.
  13. On 7 December 2012, Mr. Verma provided the Letter to the Respondent.
  14. On 11 April 2013, the Respondent amended Mr. Verma’s BAS for the Relevant Period,[20] resulting in a shortfall amount of $67,003 in total.[21] The Audit Finalisation Letter explained that the Respondent found that Mr. Verma had reported false information in his BAS for the Relevant Period, resulting in the identified shortfall amount.
  15. An administrative penalty was also imposed on Mr. Verma under s 284-75(1) of Schedule 1 of TAA 1953 for making a false or misleading statement resulting in a shortfall amount and the penalty rate was set at 75% of the shortfall amount under s 284-90(1) of Schedule 1 of TAA 1953 on the basis that Mr. Verma intentionally disregarded taxation law.[22] A 20% uplift of the penalty rate was applied with respect to the latter two quarterly periods in the Relevant Period under s 284-220(1)(c) of Schedule 1 of TAA 1953 on the basis of repeated malfeasance. The penalties in total amount to $58,484.10.[23]
  16. Mr. Verma gave evidence that the Penalty Assessment distressed him to such a degree that he was unable to deal with his tax issues for approximately a decade, until 2023.
  17. On 14 December 2023, Mr. Verma lodged his Objection to the underlying shortfall assessment, the Penalty Assessment, and the GIC related thereto. In his Objection, Mr. Verma offered an account of his arrangement with Mr. Malhotra that was different from his previous accounts, claiming that Mr. Malhotra ‘offered me to do business with him’ and convinced Mr. Verma that he would ‘organise loan under [Mr. Verma’s] name from the creditor, and we can own business together’.[24] In subsequent Tribunal case events (including the Hearing), Mr. Verma maintained that his arrangement with Mr. Malhotra was that the latter would help him get a loan and start a newsagency business together with him.[25] He further claimed in his Objection that he was scammed by Mr. Malhotra, that he did not authorise Mr. Malhotra to commit tax fraud, that he never intended to do anything wrong, that he did not himself commit the fraud, that the penalties were harsh, and that he was committed to compliance with taxation laws going forward.
  18. The Respondent disallowed the Objection on 4 April 2024. Relevantly, the Respondent explained:
    1. By willingly providing your details to a third-party you have given them authority to act on your behalf and are responsible for their actions including, but not limited to, lodging the false activity statements, and nominating the abovementioned bank account as the account for the GST refunds to be paid.
    2. It can be inferred from the surrounding circumstances which include your conduct and the actions of a person acting on your behalf to display an intentional disregard of a taxation law to lodge activity statements containing false information to receive a benefit you are not entitled to.
    3. The Commissioner considers you sought to gain a refund through lodging a false and misleading statement in your business activity statements and that:
    4. you were aware that the statements were false and misleading.
    5. that there was a risk you were not complying with the relevant law.
    6. The Commissioner considers you displayed an intentional disregard of the law in lodging the relevant activity statements.[26]
  19. The Objection Decision and the Reasons for Objection Decision did not directly address the 20% uplift applied to the base penalty amount under s 284-220(1)(c) of Schedule 1 of TAA 1953.
  20. The Respondent did not remit the penalties, explaining that Mr. Verma’s ‘commitment to becoming compliant with the ATO does not warrant remission’ and ‘there are insufficient circumstances present in [Mr. Verma’s] case to support a basis for full or partial remission’ of the penalties.[27]
  21. On 3 June 2024, Mr. Verma lodged his Application for Review, in which he wrote that ‘I was victim of scam by Naveen Malhotra and in no way I knew he was getting money from ATO and only found out when he had done all of them’. Mr. Verma has not been explicit regarding the outcome for which he is arguing, but based on his submissions and evidence I consider that Mr. Verma is contending that (i) the administrative penalties should not be imposed at all or that they should be imposed at a lower level of culpability; or (ii) the administrative penalties should be remitted.

Mr. Verma’s evidence

  1. Mr. Verma said that Mr. Malhotra never held himself out to be a tax agent, and Mr. Verma knew that Mr. Malhotra was not a tax agent. Mr. Verma said that he was young and naïve and was impressed by Mr. Malhotra, who appeared to be a successful businessman. Mr. Verma confirmed that he never actually operated or had anything to do with a newsagency or any other business with Mr. Malhotra.
  2. At the Hearing, Mr. Verma disclaimed any responsibility for the false lodgements that resulted in the tax shortfall. He said that, while he would not trust Mr. Malhotra or do the same thing again now if given the opportunity, at the time he did not have any knowledge that Mr. Malhotra would be lodging false GST refund claims on his behalf, that he did not consent to Mr. Malhotra making such lodgements on his behalf, that he did not participate in any way, and that he did not really benefit from the lodgements.
  3. Mr. Verma gave differing accounts of why he decided to deal with Mr. Malhotra. At first, he was explicit about the plan to obtain GST refunds, saying variously that he did so because he needed money due to gambling debt, credit card debt, and outstanding amounts owed to his lawyers. A decade later, in his Objection, he changed his story and claimed that he did not actually need money because of indebtedness and that his arrangement with Mr. Malhotra was to go into business together and that he understood Mr. Malhotra to be arranging a loan for their planned shared business. He said that he never agreed with Mr. Malhotra that they would share any tax refunds 50/50, but rather that he agreed to let Mr. Malhotra help him get a loan so that they could go into business together.
  4. Then, a decade later, Mr. Verma said that his earlier accounts (regarding getting a GST refund) given at the Audit Interview and in the Letter (and in other communications around that time) were not correct because, at the time they were given, he was scared, depressed, and under immense pressure and stress. He also seemed to place some blame on his former lawyers, claiming that every time he consulted with them it was with a different person and that his early communications with the ATO were based on what different lawyers understood and wrote. Finally, Mr. Verma claimed that his earlier evidence was incorrect because he might have gotten mixed up because of emails that Mr. Malhotra wrote pretending to be Mr. Verma.
  5. Mr. Verma confirmed at the Hearing that he gave Mr. Malhotra all of the personal information that he asked for and that Mr. Malhotra handled all communications (including phone calls and emails) with the ATO. Mr. Verma said that he knew that Mr. Malhotra was pretending to be him when he was called by the ATO, but that he understood that Mr. Malhotra was trying to get a loan for their shared business and that he thought that the above communications related to loans rather than tax matters. Mr. Verma said that he did not check his emails or bank accounts during this time, and so was unaware that Mr. Malhotra was dealing with the ATO on his behalf. He said that he was unaware that tax lodgements had been made on his behalf at the time and only found out later that he had been scammed and that there was no newsagency business as Mr. Malhotra had been telling him. Mr. Verma said that he only found out in June 2012 that the money deposited into his account was from the ATO; he said that before that Mr. Malhotra had told him that the money coming into Mr. Verma’s account was from a lender. Mr. Verma also said that he only discovered where the funds in his account were coming from after another acquaintance of his told him that Mr. Malhotra had done something similar to him. I find this evidence to be contradictory and unpersuasive.
  6. I do not accept that Mr. Verma ever understood his arrangement with Mr. Malhotra to be anything other than to make fraudulent claims for GST refunds. Mr. Verma’s more recent claim that Mr. Malhotra offered to get him money via arranging for a loan for a business that they would operate together is at odds with Mr. Verma’s earlier, contemporaneous, accounts of his dealings with Mr. Malhotra; and is also self-serving, implausible, inconsistent, and unsupported.
  7. Mr. Verma was explicit and specific in his evidence given at the Audit Interview and in his Letter that Mr. Malhotra told him that he could get a GST refund via the lodgement of false BAS. I find this evidence to be frank and credible; it was given spontaneously and contemporaneously with the activity at issue. These initial characterisations of Mr. Verma’s dealings with Mr. Malhotra are supported by subsequent evidence that Mr. Verma received phone calls from the ATO (on which he and Mr. Malhotra dishonestly concealed their identities) and had access to bank statements showing deposits to his account from the ATO. Mr. Verma’s own evidence is that he knew that he was speaking with the ATO when he concealed his identity, and that he made withdrawals from his account after the refunds were deposited by the ATO. While I accept that Mr. Verma provided Mr. Malhotra with full access to all of his personal information, I do not accept that Mr. Verma did not also have such access to his own information or that he refrained from using such access to his own information. It is not plausible that someone desperate enough for money that he would authorise and engage in such activity would not be aware of large deposits into his own bank account, including their origin (especially given that Mr. Verma has acknowledged withdrawing such funds himself).[28]
  8. On the other hand, Mr. Verma’s evidence regarding an arrangement to obtain a loan with Mr. Malhotra’s help is not credible. Such claims were made a decade after the fact and only after Mr. Verma became aware of the Respondent’s compliance activity and that he was potentially in trouble. Furthermore, the claims regarding a plan to get a loan are vague and unsupported by any details. Nor is there any supporting evidence of either Mr. Malhotra or Mr. Verma doing anything, or making any communications, related to getting a loan or carrying on a business. Mr. Verma’s evidence regarding his knowledge that Mr. Malhotra was speaking with the ATO on his behalf undermines his claims that he understood that Mr. Malhotra was trying to secure a loan for a newsagency business. It is not in dispute that Mr. Verma never actually had anything to do with any newsagency or other business for which he might apply for a loan, and there is nothing (besides his own bald and self-serving assertions) to support that he ever intended to do so. Mr. Verma’s claim that the withdrawn funds were not split 50/50 but rather than Mr. Malhotra took the bulk of the refunded amounts does not alter my evaluation of the credibility of Mr. Verma’s evidence.
  9. Mr. Verma’s explanations for why he changed his story from one about GST refunds to one about a loan are neither coherent nor consistent. Being stressed out does not explain why Mr. Verma would provide specific and explicit evidence regarding his arrangement with Mr. Malhotra to apply for and get GST refunds. It is also not plausible or supported that Mr. Verma’s receipt of professional legal advice, even if confusing to him, would somehow cause him to erroneously claim that he had agreed to allow his acquaintance to apply for GST refunds on his behalf rather than a loan, as he later claimed. Mr. Verma’s evidence that he became aware that Mr. Malhotra perpetrated frauds, similar to their scheme to split GST refunds on others, further undermines his claim that Mr. Malhotra employed a different modus operandi in this case. Finally, Mr. Verma’s claim that he somehow got confused with the content of communications that Mr. Malhotra made on his behalf is both contradictory to his claims that Mr. Malhotra did everything without Mr. Verma’s knowledge as well as implausible. That Mr. Verma has given a number of different implausible and unsupported accounts for his changing story contributes to the lack of credibility of his claims regarding a plan to get a business loan.
  10. For the reasons given above, I find that Mr. Verma always knew that he was partnering with Mr. Malhotra to get GST refunds.
  11. I consider that Mr. Verma had to have known that any claims to GST refunds were completely fabricated and not based on anything legitimate. Mr. Verma never operated or had anything to do with any newsagency business that might have plausibly generated input tax credits.[29] There was no actual business activity on which to make any GST refund claims, as Mr. Verma certainly would have known. It is not plausible that Mr. Verma could believe that a nominal transfer of a newsagency business back and forth with someone else could magically yield large GST refunds. There is simply no basis for Mr. Verma to think that his GST refund claims were anything but dishonest and made up out of thin air. No reasonable person would think that the instant GST refund claims are legal. That Mr. Verma agreed to such a tax arrangement with someone he knew not to be a tax agent also indicates that he had to have known that his claims were not legitimate.
  12. Mr. Verma claimed that he was blind to Mr. Malhotra’s activities and did not know that he would be making false GST refund claims. But any attempted blindness was wilful, and once Mr. Verma authorised Mr. Malhotra to manage his tax affairs by agreeing to do so and enabling him to do so by providing all of his personal, tax, and financial information, he could no longer claim that he was not aware of what Mr. Malhotra was doing. I agree with the Respondent’s contention that ‘In a self-assessment tax system, it is incumbent upon Mr. Verma to make enquiries to understand and comply with his relevant tax obligations’.[30]
  13. I note that Mr. Verma’s former lawyers’ letter dated 31 August 2012 purported that Mr. Verma was making an ‘unprompted voluntary disclosure’ to the ATO.[31] Mr. Verma’s evidence at the Hearing was that he might not have received mailed correspondence before that time because he was moving around at the time and that he could not recall if he had heard from the ATO before seeing a lawyer and having them send their aforementioned letter. Because of Mr. Verma’s equivocation on this point, lack of persuasive evidence regarding the reason for and timing of his ‘disclosure’, and lack of substance in such ‘disclosure,’ I consider that Mr. Verma’s ‘disclosure’ came on account of, and subsequent to, the ATO’s commencement of compliance and enforcement activity (and, as such, was neither ‘unprompted’ nor properly characterised as a ‘disclosure’).
  14. Mr. Verma has not paid back the refunds he received. He said that he is willing to pay them back once the instant case is finalised. He admitted to keeping the money knowing that it was fraudulently obtained. He said that he did not previously pay back the amounts he received because he had to pay his lawyers and that he was too scared to deal with the ATO and that this fear only abated after approximately a decade. Mr. Verma said that starting in 2023 he has been trying to put all of his tax affairs in order.
  15. On the whole, for the reasons given above, I find Mr. Verma’s more recent evidence regarding his plan with Mr. Malhotra and his knowledge of Mr. Malhotra’s actions to be inconsistent, implausible, and not credible.

Mr. Rawal’s evidence

  1. Mr. Rawal said at the Hearing that he did not have first-hand knowledge of the dealings between Mr. Verma and Mr. Malhotra, but that Mr. Verma told him at the time about his money issues and his dealings with Mr. Malhotra. He said that Mr. Verma told him that Mr. Malhotra was helping him with a business venture. Mr. Rawal said that he advised Mr. Verma against getting involved with Mr. Malhotra because his plans sounded like fraud; Mr. Verma confirmed that he received such a warning from Mr. Rawal. Mr. Rawal said that he did not think that Mr. Verma intentionally did anything wrong and that he was a victim of Mr. Malhotra.
  2. I give little weight to Mr. Rawal’s characterisations of Mr. Verma’s plans and intentions. Mr. Rawal is a friend of Mr. Verma’s and he knows only what Mr. Verma has told him. He was unable to give any details about Mr. Verma’s dealings with Mr. Malhotra and he was not specific about when he learned of such things from Mr. Verma. The only credible part of Mr. Rawal’s evidence was that he warned Mr. Verma that his plans sounded like fraud.

WERE THE ADMINISTRATIVE PENALTIES EXCESSIVE OR INCORRECT?

  1. A taxpayer is liable to an administrative penalty if they or their agent makes a statement to the Commissioner that is false or misleading in a material particular.[32] Generally, the amount of the base penalty is a percentage of the tax shortfall resulting from the false or misleading statement, and the percentage depends on the taxpayer’s culpability.[33]
  2. Where the tax shortfall results from the taxpayer’s intentional disregard for the applicable taxation law, the base penalty amount is 75% of the tax shortfall.[34] The tax legislation does not define ‘intentional disregard,’ but the relevant legislative history, case law, and ATO guidance provide interpretive guidance, as discussed below.
  3. There is a well-developed body of caselaw that fleshes out the meaning of ‘intentional disregard’ and that is discussed in a number of recent Tribunal decisions.[35] Miscellaneous Taxation Ruling MT 2008/1 aptly summarises the caselaw as follows:

112. Intentional disregard means that there must be actual knowledge that the statement made is false. To establish intentional disregard, the entity must understand the effect of the relevant legislation and how it operates in respect of the entity's affairs and make a deliberate choice to ignore the law.[36]

  1. The ATO guidance provides some further gloss on the meaning of ‘intentional disregard’:

110. The adjective 'intentional' means that something more than reckless disregard of or indifference to a taxation law is required.

...

113. Dishonesty is a requisite feature of behaviour that shows an intentional disregard for the operation of the law. This is another significant difference between this type of behaviour and behaviour that shows a want of reasonable care or recklessness where dishonesty is not an element.

114. Evidence of intention must be found through direct evidence or by inference from all the surrounding circumstances, including the conduct of the entity. (internal citations omitted) [37]

  1. The evidence and the surrounding circumstances in this case establish that Mr. Verma intentionally disregarded taxation law.
  2. Mr. Verma’s explicit and spontaneously given initial evidence establishes that he knew of and agreed with Mr. Malhotra’s scheme to obtain GST refunds, authorised Mr. Malhotra to do so, and proactively aided in perpetrating the scheme. I find that Mr. Verma had actual knowledge that the statements made to the ATO in order to get such GST refunds were false and that his claims for such refunds were contrary to applicable taxation laws. Mr. Verma authorised Mr. Malhotra, someone he knew not to be a tax agent, to lodge BAS on his behalf based on a phantom transaction, and did so even after expressing concern, and being warned, about the legality of such lodgements.[38] That Mr. Verma later changed his story in a self-serving way in response to the Respondent’s compliance activity indicates that he knew that the previous tax lodgements were false.
  3. In addition to knowing that his BAS lodgements were false, I find that Mr. Verma knew that the effect of the law that he was disregarding, as it applied to him and his claims, would be to generate GST refunds. That was indeed the whole point of his arrangement with Mr. Malhotra. In order to understand the effect of the relevant legislation it is not required that Mr. Verma understand in detail the workings of the GST legislation. This is not a case involving a nuanced legal characterisation or interpretation, and Mr. Verma’s own evidence indicates that he knew that the purpose of the falsified BAS lodgements was to generate GST refunds to which he was not entitled, contrary to taxation law.
  4. I further find that Mr. Verma made a deliberate choice to ignore the taxation laws. Authorising Mr. Malhotra to make false and dishonest GST refund claims, and failing to check any of the lodgements or communications made on his behalf in circumstances where he had a duty to do so, amounts to making a deliberate choice to ignore taxation law. Mr. Verma enabled Mr. Malhotra to transact on his behalf, was aware that Mr. Malhotra was not a tax agent and was making lodgements and communicating with the ATO on his behalf, engaged in dishonesty by allowing and aiding Mr. Malhotra to impersonate him, and took no steps to oversee or put a stop to Mr. Malhotra’s actions. The existence of, and Mr. Verma’s access to, objective documentary evidence that the scheme was perpetrated on the ATO, further supports that Mr. Verma knowingly and deliberately disregarded the taxation laws.
  5. ATO guidance indicates that ‘intentional’ means something more than ‘reckless’.[39] The Respondent has cited previous Tribunal cases regarding similar conduct by third party agents that attracted a penalty for recklessness for the taxpayer and has contended that in those cases the taxpayer was in cahoots with someone they understood to be a tax agent, and that Mr. Verma’s situation is different, and he is more culpable, because his arrangement was with someone he knew not to be a tax agent.[40] I agree that this distinction supports the contention that Mr. Verma intentionally disregarded the taxation laws.
  6. I observe that the conduct undertaken by Mr. Verma is the same as that in the illustrative examples of ‘intentional disregard’ in the legislative history for the act that introduced the instant penalty regime and in ATO internal guidance to its staff administering the penalties at issue in this case. The example in the legislative history involves the deliberate claim of a credit knowing that it is not allowable,[41] and the example in the ATO’s internal instructions involves the over-claim of GST credits.[42]
  7. Additionally, I have considered, and agree with, the Respondent’s contentions that, ‘By willingly providing your details to a third-party you have given them authority to act on your behalf and are responsible for their actions’, ‘It can be inferred from the surrounding circumstances which include your conduct and the actions of a person acting on your behalf to display an intentional disregard of a taxation law’,[43] and ‘[A]s you are responsible for the authorised actions of your ... representatives, their behaviour may also be relevant to determining your behaviour for penalty purposes’.[44]
  8. For the reasons given above, Mr. Verma has not overcome his burden of showing that he did not intentionally disregard the taxation laws.
  9. Further, the 20% uplift of penalties was correctly applied under s 284-220(1)(c) of Schedule 1 of TAA 1953. That section operates to automatically increase the base penalty amount by 20% for periods in which a penalty is imposed under s 284-90(1) Item 1 and a penalty was previously imposed under that section. The section applies of its own force[45] and it reflects the additional culpability involved with repeating the same penalty-warranting conduct.
  10. Thus, Mr. Verma has not shown that the administrative penalties imposed on him were excessive or otherwise wrong.

SHOULD THE PENALTIES BE REMITTED IN PART OR IN FULL?

  1. Section 298-20(1) of Schedule 1 to TAA 1953 provides that the Commissioner has discretion to remit all or a part of the penalty. Remission of administrative penalties is warranted to prevent an unjust outcome and relevant considerations are circumstances that tend to mitigate the taxpayer’s culpability. Those circumstances generally do not include hardship related to paying the penalty. The discretion to remit must be exercised in accordance with the purposes of the penalty regime (i.e., encouraging taxpayers to comply with their tax obligations).[46]
  2. Mr. Verma has not demonstrated any relevant factors warranting the remission of penalties in this case.
  3. Mr. Verma claimed that he never planned on perpetrating tax malfeasance and instead was intending to get a loan with Mr. Malhotra for a joint business. He further claimed that he was scammed by Mr. Malhotra, that he did not authorise Mr. Malhotra to commit tax fraud, that he never intended to do anything wrong, and that he did not himself commit the fraud. The foregoing boils down to Mr. Verma claiming that he is innocent, and that he is a victim. Mr. Verma’s continued disclaimers of any participation in tax malfeasance, or responsibility for enabling Mr. Malhotra’s tax malfeasance on his behalf, are not persuasive (as discussed above) and do not provide a basis for remission.
  4. Mr. Verma further claimed that the penalties were harsh, and that he is committed to compliance with taxation laws going forward. Mr. Verma has not provided any evidence to support a contention that the penalty would lead to injustice, including on account of its harshness. Compliance with Australia’s taxation laws is a baseline expectation of those subject to them and is not a factor that mitigates Mr. Verma’s culpability in this case.
  5. I do not accept that Mr. Verma made a voluntary disclosure regarding his false GST refund claims. I consider that Mr. Verma did not address the dishonesty of his tax lodgements until the ATO began auditing him; as discussed above, he has not provided persuasive evidence that would establish otherwise. As such, Mr. Verma’s claimed ‘disclosure’ is not a factor that mitigates his culpability in this case.
  6. Though not at issue in these proceedings, Mr. Verma has also not paid back his ill-begotten GST refunds, even having admitted that he knows that they were fraudulently obtained. He said at the Hearing that he would be willing to pay them back upon the disposition of this case. I consider that this fact does nothing to mitigate Mr. Verma’s culpability in this case.
  7. I consider that there are not factors warranting remission of the penalties imposed in this case, especially in light of the purpose of the penalty regime to ensure compliance with taxation laws.
  8. There is not cause to remit the imposition of the automatic uplift of the base penalty amount in this case. ATO guidance indicates that remission of the 20% uplift is not appropriate when the underlying penalty is for intentional disregard of the law.[47]

CONCLUSION

  1. For the reasons given above, the reviewable objection decision is affirmed.
