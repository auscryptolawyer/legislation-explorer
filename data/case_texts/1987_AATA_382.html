Re Charles William Rowe and Commissioner of Superannuation [1987] AATA 382 (30 October 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: CHARLES WILLIAM ROWE

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V86/36

AAT No. 3870

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

I.R. Thompson (Deputy President)

Dr D.M. Sutherland (Member)

C.G. Woodard (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - full-time Repatriation Review Tribunal member - eligible employee for purposes of Superannuation Act - Repatriation Review Tribunal abolished on 31 December 1984 - applicant appointed as a part-time member to Veterans' Review Board from 1 January 1985 - reasons for part-time appointment incorrect information that applicant had health problems - incorrectness of information pointed out by applicant - unavoidable delays before medical examination - applicant found fit for full-time appointment - direction given by Commissioner on 2 April 1985 that applicant an eligible employee with effect from that date - whether period of contributory service continuous - meaning of "immediately after so ceasing" - relevance of unavoidable delays - gap too long for contributory service to be regarded as continuous

Words and Phrases: "immediately after so ceasing"

Superannuation Act 1976 ss. 3(1), (3) & (4), 154(4) & (6)

Repatriation Act 1920 s. 107VZC

Commissioner for Superannuation v. Bayley [1979] FCA 76; (1979) 2 ALD 913

Re Rattray and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 2 ALD 979

Dorsman v. Nichol (1978) 20 ALR 231

HEARING

CANBERRA

30:10:1987

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

From 1976 until 31 December 1984 the applicant was an eligible employee, as that term is defined in section 3(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"). In 1984 he was holding the statutory office of Services Member of the Repatriation Review Tribunal, which was established under the provisions of the Repatriation Act 1920. In consequence of the enactment of the Repatriation Legislation Amendment Act 1984 the Repatriation Review Tribunal was abolished with effect from midnight on 31 December 1984. However, the Repatriation Act as amended established the Veterans' Review Board, which came into existence on 1 January 1985. Its functions were broadly similar to, but not identical with, those of the Repatriation Review Tribunal.

2. On 18 December 1984 the applicant was appointed to be a Services Member of the Veterans' Review Board with effect from 1 January 1985. However, whereas he had been a full-time member of the Repatriation Review Tribunal, he was appointed as a part-time member of the Veterans' Review Board. He had applied to be appointed as a full-time member of the Veterans' Review Board but had been appointed as a part-time member because the Minister for Veterans' Affairs, on whose recommendation the Governor-General appoints members of the Veterans' Review Board, believed that he had health problems which made him unsuited for appointment as a full-time member. He was offered the appointment as a part-time member in October 1984; he accepted but sought immediately to be appointed as a full-time member.

3. Correspondence ensued between, on the one hand, the applicant and other persons writing on his behalf and, on the other hand, the Minister and members of his staff. As a result the applicant was medically examined by a specialist physician on 4 February 1985 and found to be fit for appointment as a full-time member. Consequently the Minister recommended that he be appointed as a full-time member and on 13 March 1985 the Governor-General accepted that recommendation and appointed him accordingly. The applicant then requested the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") to direct that he be treated as an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act. On 2 April 1985 the Commissioner gave such a direction. By virtue of the provisions of section 14(1) of the Act that direction had effect from the date on which it was given; it was not permissible for the Commissioner to backdate it.

4. In November 1984 the applicant had written to the Commissioner informing him that he was anxious that the period of his contributory service for the purposes of the Act should not be broken by his ceasing to be a member of the Repatriation Review Tribunal and his becoming a member of the Veterans' Review Board. On 26 July 1985 an officer of the Commissioner's office wrote to the applicant referring to that letter and to subsequent telephone conversations in which the applicant had requested that his period of contributory service should be treated as having continued unbroken; he apologised for the delay in responding and informed him that he had ceased to be an eligible employee under the Act on 31 December 1984, so that his first period of contributory service had come to an end then, and that he had commenced a new period of contributory service on 2 April 1985. The applicant requested the Commissioner to reconsider that decision. The Commissioner did so pursuant to section 154(4) of the Act but confirmed it. The applicant then applied to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal under section 154(6).

5. For the purposes of the Act "eligible employee" is defined (so far as is relevant in these proceedings) in section 3(1) of the Act as meaning:-

"'eligible employee' means -

. . . . .

(e) a person -

. . . . .

(ii) who is the holder of a statutory

office to which he was appointed on a

full-time basis; or

(iii) who is the holder of a statutory

office to which he was appointed

otherwise than on a full-time basis

and who is specified in a direction

given under sub-section 14(2) in

relation to that statutory office,

and who, immediately before . . . . . his

appointment to that office, was an eligible

employee by virtue of another paragraph of this

definition or a previous application of this

paragraph"

6. "Period of contributory service" is defined in the same sub-section as follows:-

"'period of contributory service', in relation to a

person who has ceased to be an eligible

employee, means a period equal to the period

commencing on his first day of service and

ending on his last day of service (less any

period during that period when the person was on

leave of absence without pay and in respect of

which sub-section 51(1) applies or was absent

from duty and in respect of which sub-section

51A(1) applies) together with, in the case of a

person who had previously ceased to be an

eligible employee on an occasion earlier than

his first day of service, any period that, under

section 8, is to be added to his period of

contributory service"

Sections 8, 51 and 51A are not relevant to the situation in these proceedings.

7. Section 3(3) of the Act is, and was at all material times, as follows:-

"3. (3) Where a person ceases to be an eligible

employee and, immediately after so ceasing, again

becomes an eligible employee, he shall, for the

purposes of this Act, be deemed not to have so

ceased."

8. Section 14 of the Act reads, and has at all material times read, as follows:-

"14. (1) Where -

(a) a person is the holder of a statutory

office to which he has been appointed for a

term of not less than 1 year; and

(b) the person requests the Commissioner to

direct, under this section, that he be

treated as an eligible employee for the

purposes of this Act,

the Commissioner may direct that the person is, as

from the date of the direction, an eligible employee

for the purposes of this Act.

(2) Where a person is the holder of a statutory

office to which he has been appointed otherwise than

on a full-time basis, the Commissioner may direct

that paragraph (e) of the definition of 'eligible

employee' in sub-section 3(1), or sub-paragraph

123(b)(iv), applies in relation to the person.

(3) Except in accordance with principles

approved by the Minister, the Commissioner shall not

give a direction under sub-section (1) or (2) in

respect of the holder of a statutory office to which

the holder has been appointed otherwise than on a

full-time basis."

Section 123 is not relevant to the situation in these proceedings.

9. At the hearing of the application the applicant was represented by Mr A. Cavanough, of Counsel, and the respondent by Mr R. Davidson, a departmental officer. In addition to the documents which had been lodged by the Tribunal pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, Mr Cavanough tendered copies of a large number of documents obtained under the Freedom of Information Act 1982 from the Commissioner and the Minister for Veterans' Affairs, and also copies of correspondence between the applicant and his representatives and various other persons after 22 April 1986. The applicant gave oral evidence very briefly that as a part-time member of the Veterans' Review Board he had sat for hearings on 22 days between 2 January 1988 and 12 March 1988. There was no dispute between the parties as to any of the facts disclosed by the documents or the evidence given by the applicant.

10. Mr Cavanough presented the applicant's case on the basis that, if events had followed their proper course in 1984, the applicant would have been appointed as a full-time member of the Veterans' Review Board with effect from 1 January 1985 and that in those circumstances, although a substantial time elapsed between the date when he ceased to be a member of the Repatriation Review Tribunal and the date when he was appointed as a full-time member of the Veterans' Review Board, he should be regarded, in terms of section 3(3), as having again become an eligible employee immediately after he ceased to be a member of the Repatriation Review Tribunal. In support of that argument he relied not only on the facts set out above but also on a number of other facts additional to them. Those facts were disclosed by the documents tendered in evidence.

11. First there were the facts relating to the applicant's efforts to be appointed to be a full-time member of the Veterans' Review Board. When he first applied to be so appointed, the ex-servicemen's organisations recommended to the Minister that he be so appointed. When he was offered appointment as a part-time member, he immediately made enquiries of the Minister's office to find out why; he was informed that it was because of his health and for no other reason. On 2 November 1984 he sent the Minister a certificate from a general practitioner to the effect that his health was good and that the doctor regarded him as medically fit for appointment as a full-time member. He observed that it appeared that his appointment as a part-time member had resulted from a mistake of fact and asked the Minister to review his decision and to change the appointment to that of a full-time member.

12. Unfortunately for the applicant Parliament had been dissolved and a general election was to be held on 8 December 1984. Possibly for that reason, the Minister did not reply to his letter for nearly six weeks. When he replied on 11 December 1984, he wrote: "Having had the opportunity to consider the matters raised, I am writing to ask you whether you would be prepared to attend an examination by an independent specialist physician nominated by me". The applicant replied promptly agreeing to such an examination. Again the time at which those events occurred had a disadvantageous effect on the applicant's pursuit of early appointment as a full-time member. The Christmas and January period is one in which it is notoriously difficult to obtain appointments for medical examinations. In the event the examination was conducted on 4 February 1985. The specialist physician who conducted it sent his report to the Deputy Chairman of the Repatriation Commission on 7 February 1985 and she wrote a minute to the Minister on 12 February 1985, noting the fact that the specialist physician had expressed the opinion that the applicant was medically fit for employment as a full-time member of the Veterans' Review Board. She recommended to the Minister that the applicant be appointed a full-time Services Member. The recommendation was approved by the Minister the following day. However, it was not until 13 March that the Governor-General made the actual appointment.

13. Second there were the facts relating to his efforts to have his initial period of contributory service continue unbroken. On 21 November 1984, in response to a telephone call from the Department of Veterans' Affairs regarding his superannuation entitlements, the Commissioner wrote to him informing him that, if he commenced full-time employment with the Commonwealth "shortly after retirement", he might be regarded as not having ceased as a contributor and that in that event a retirement benefit would not be payable. On 20 November the applicant wrote to the Secretary to the Australian Government Retirement Benefits Office informing him that he had been told that he had been appointed to a part-time position on the newly created Veterans' Appeal Board with effect from 1 January 1985, that he had expressed his concern to the Secretary to the Department of Veterans' Affairs that his superannuation would be affected as a result and that the Secretary had informed him that the Commissioner could declare a part-time appointee an "eligible employee" but that the Commissioner could not give such a direction except in accordance with principles approved by the Minister for Finance. He, therefore, asked that his case be put to the Minister on the ground that he had held a statutory office for eight years and, but for the abolition of the Repatriation Review Tribunal, would have reasonably expected to continue to hold it in a full-time capacity until 10 March 1989. He received no reply for nearly six weeks. Then on 27 December 1984 the Commissioner wrote telling him that as yet the Minister for Finance had not declared any classes of part-time employees to be approved part-time employees and that he was referring a copy of the applicant's letter of 20 November to the Department of Finance. On 31 January 1985 the Deputy Commissioner for Superannuation sent a minute to the Assistant Secretary, Commonwealth Superannuation Branch of the Department of Finance, enclosing the applicant's representations and informing the addressee that the applicant had been advised that there was no provision for part-time statutory office holders to contribute to the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme. The memorandum concluded "in view of the policy implicatious (sic) of this matter, Mr Rowe's representations are referred for your information".

14. From those facts it can be clearly seen that the applicant has suffered as the result of delays by the Minister for Veterans' Affairs and his staff, by the Commissioner and, as section 14(3) has been part of the Act since its enactment in 1976, by a series of Ministers for Finance. No doubt all of those delays can be reasonably explained. In the case of the Minister for Veterans' Affairs, Parliament had been dissolved and a general election was about to be held. Traditionally, governments do not move on the appointment of persons to statutory offices after the dissolution of Parliament and before the result of the general election is known. Over Christmas and in January many public officers take leave, departments are short of staff and the general tempo of administrative work and decision-making slows down as a result. During the same period it is difficult to obtain appointments with medical specialists. A Minister for Finance has many pressing demands upon his time. The need for action to be taken by him under an apparently minor provision of the Act may well have been overlooked. In any event, the framing of principles appropriate for approval under section 14(3) of the Act is not an easy task because of the many different types of statutory office and the variations in the nature of the part-time work undertaken by holders of those offices who are not appointed on a full-time basis. However, the fact that the delays are reasonably explicable is small comfort to the applicant if the result is that he suffers a major financial detriment as we have concluded that he does. Clearly, if the legislation permitted his initial period of contributory service to be taken to have continued to run after 31 December 1984 and to be still running now, that is what the justice of the situation would require. However, that depends entirely on whether the provisions of section 3(3) of the Act are applicable, that is to say whether or not he can be held to have become an eligible employee again "immediately after" ceasing to be one on 31 December 1984.

15. The nature of the immediacy required by section 3(3) has been considered by the Federal Court in Commissioner for Superannuation v. Bayley [1979] FCA 76; (1979) 2 ALD 913 and by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal in Re Rattray and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 2 ALD 979. In Bayley Lockhart J., with whom Keely J. agreed, said at page 930:-

"Mr Phillips QC contended . . . . .that 'immediately'

does not necessarily mean instanter; but rather has a

meaning which may vary from case to case, sometimes

allowing a gap in time of hours, days or weeks. In

sub-s (3) it has the meaning of 'within a reasonably

short space of time' in all the circumstances. In

support of his contention, Mr Phillips QC referred to

the definition of the words 'immediately' and

'forthwith' in Stroud's Judicial Dictionary and

Butterworth's Words and Phrases Legally Defined;

Permanent Trustee Co of New South Wales Ltd v. d'Apice

[1968] HCA 29; (1968) ALR 437; 118 CLR 105, and Dorsman v. Nichol

(1978) 20 ALR 231.

Mr Phillips QC contended that sub-s (3) is a deeming

provision merely used for more abundant caution to

put beyond doubt a particular construction that

otherwise might be uncertain. He relied on St Aubyn

v. Attorney-General [1951] UKHL 3; (1952) AC 15, per Lord Radcliffe

at 53 and Hunter Douglas Australia Pty Ltd v. Perma

Blinds [1970] HCA 63; (1969) ALR 676; 122 CLR 49, per Windeyer J. at

65-7.

A deeming provision may be included in an

interpretation section in a Statute for the purpose

of extending the meaning of a defined term to

something it would not mean in ordinary parlance:

see St Aubyn v. Attorney-General, supra, and Hunter

Douglas Australia Pty Ltd v. Perma Blinds, supra, at

the pages mentioned earlier. In my opinion this is

the purpose of sub-s (3). It is intended to ensure

that a person does not lose his status as an eligible

employee merely because he ceases to be one, and

later becomes one again, provided the gap in time is

not unreasonably large. It is designed to cover all

cases where a person has a particular position or

holds a particular office and ceases to do so, but

'immediately' thereafter takes another position or

office, in each case being a person who satisfies the

statutory denotation of a 'permanent employee'. It

matters not whether the interval of time is a second,

a day, or a week, provided the person again becomes

an eligible employee 'immediately' after ceasing to

be one."

16. In Bayley's case the respondent had ceased to be an eligible employee and had not obtained any other employment by the Commonwealth as the result of which he had become an eligible employee. So the comments of Lockhart J. were obiter dicta; moreover he did not need to address himself expressly to whether any particular period of time between cessation of being an eligible employee and again commencing to be an eligible employee was to be regarded as immediate. In Re Rattray there had been a gap of one day. Rattray contended that that was a sufficient gap to have brought his initial period of contributory service to an end and for it to be necessary for a new period to commence. The Tribunal held that he had become an eligible employee again immediately after ceasing to be an eligible employee and that consequently, by virtue of section 3(3), he was to be deemed not to have ceased to be an eligible employee, so that his initial period of contributory service continued without a break.

17. In coming to that conclusion the Tribunal referred to the passage in Bayley set out above. After observing that Lockhart J.'s remarks in that passage might be said to be obiter dicta, it commented that it must nevertheless find them highly persuasive and that in any event it felt no reluctance in adopting what was said there as it appeared to accord with the authorities, in particular as analysed by Forster C.J. in Dorsman v. Nichol (1978) 20 ALR 231, especially at 237. The Tribunal continued:-

"The question then is, as we have said, an objective

one. Further, we are not concerned to say in the

abstract how long a break may be before it becomes

'unreasonably large' and the requirement of immediacy

be lost. We are concerned simply to decide whether,

in relation to the proved facts in this application,

the applicant, having ceased to be an eligible

employee when the Bureau went out of existence, again

became an eligible employee immediately after so

ceasing. In our opinion he did. Our reasons for so

concluding are as follows.

The break was very short. At the very most, it must

have been about 33 hours. The length of that break

must be considered in the light of the following

factors: First, the applicant engaged in no other

employment in the meantime, but went from one to the

other. As far as employment was concerned no

'non-eligible' employment intervened between the

first eligible employment and that which succeeded

it. It is to be noted that in Reseck v. Federal

Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 38; (1974-1975) 133 CLR 45 at

50, Gibbs J. said that 'In most cases in which a

workman ceased his employment on a Friday and

commenced employment again with the same employer on

the following Monday it would be impossible to say

that his employment had ever been terminated'. In

fact in that case it was not open to the High Court

so to conclude because the case stated to the court

contained a finding that there had been a termination

of employment, but His Honour's view is clearly

stated and while the situation is not precisely in

pari materia with that in the present application, it

expresses something of the notion that has commended

itself to us. Again, we consider that it is of

significance that not only was there a very short gap

between the cessation of one eligible employment and

the commencement of another, but also that the

process of seeking appointment to the second

employment had commenced well before the first

ceased. The appointment to the second employment was

in fact made before the first ceased, and needed only

for its consummation the actual commencement of duty.

Indeed before the first employment ceased, the

applicant had not only accepted employment with

Telecom but had nominated 1 July as the latest date

by which he would commence such employment. In the

result we find that by virtue of s 3(3) of the Act

the applicant is to be deemed not to have ceased to

be an eligible employee."

18. In Dorsman the Supreme Court of the Northern Territory was dealing with a case in which a police officer had administered a breathalyzer test. The Act under which that was done required that a standard alcohol solution test be carried out "immediately before" breath analysis. It was in that context that Forster C.J. reviewed the meaning given to the word "immediately" in various cases by English courts. He pointed out that the meaning to be given depended on the particular context in which the word was used. He concluded that in some situations ten days afterwards might be immediately and in others one hour might not be. The intention of the legislature had to be derived from examination of the word used in its context. With respect we adopt that view and also the view expressed by the Tribunal in Re Rattray. The context in which the word "immediately" is used in section 3(3) indicates, we consider, an intention on the part of the legislature to preserve and continue the period of contributory service of an eligible employee where the gap is short and ought in the circumstances to be disregarded.

19. It appears to us to be reasonable to give to the words "immediately before the commencement of the employment" in paragraph (e) of the definition of "eligible employee" in section 3(1) of the Act the same meaning as it bears in section 3(3). That being so, if the applicant's appointment to the statutory office on 13 March 1985 is to be regarded as having been made immediately after he ceased to be an eligible employee on 31 December 1984, no further direction by the Commissioner was required for him to become again an eligible employee. So the gap with which we are concerned is that between 1 January 1985 and 12 March 1985 inclusive, i.e. 71 days. Prima facie that is far too long to be regarded as short.

20. However, Mr Cavanough urged upon us that account should be taken of the fact that during that period the applicant was a member of the Veterans' Review Board and at all times after 31 December 1984 his appointment as a full-time member had been approved subject only to his satisfying the Minister for Veterans' Affairs of his medical fitness, which he would have been able to prove at any time if given the opportunity to do so. Although section 107VZC of the Repatriation Act as amended by the 1984 amending legislation provided for members of the Veterans' Review Board to be appointed by the Governor-General, we accept that as a matter of practicality the Governor-General was unlikely not to appoint the applicant if the Minister recommended it, particularly in view of the provisions of section 107VZC(3) in respect of Services Members.

21. Mr Davidson submitted that the applicant's service as a part-time member of the Veterans' Review Board from 1 January 1985 to 12 March 1985 had a different significance. During the period of that service the Commissioner could not direct that he was an eligible employee under the Act. It was a period of service otherwise than as an eligible employee which was interposed between his two periods of service as an eligible employee. That, Mr Davidson contended, prevented section 3(3) having the effect that he was to be regarded as having continued to be an eligible employee during that period. We find difficulty in accepting that contention; we cannot see why it should be detrimental to the applicant's case that he accepted the part-time appointment to the office until his full-time appointment to the same office was made rather than remaining without employment.

22. However, having had regard to all the matters urged upon us by Mr Cavanough and being conscious of the unfortunate consequences for the applicant, we have come to the conclusion that the gap of 71 days is too great to be regarded as short. We accept that, in deciding whether section 3(3) is applicable in a particular case, it is necessary to have regard to the facts of that case, as well as to the context of section 3(3). Nevertheless, it seems to us that to hold that the applicant became an eligible employee on 13 March 1985 "immediately after" ceasing to be one on 31 December 1984 would strain the meaning of that expression beyond what is permissible. Further, it would accord to section 3(3) an effect which could cause the Commissioner great difficulty in the administration of the Act in future.

23. We note that in the documents tendered in evidence suggestions have been made that the appropriate course in the applicant's case might be for an ex gratia payment to be made to him because of its most unusual facts. The Tribunal has no power to make any order in that regard; nor is it proper for it to make a recommendation. However, in this statement of our reasons we have set out and given consideration to what we believe are the peculiar circumstances of the applicant's case which are likely to need to be taken into account by the Minister for Finance if he is minded to consider whether to direct that an ex-gratia payment be made.

24. For the reasons which we have stated above, we have reluctantly come to the conclusion that we must affirm the decision under review.
