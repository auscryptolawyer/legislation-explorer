Re Commissioner of Taxation [1993] AATA 298; (1993) 26 ATR 1388 (28 September 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. QT92/100

AAT No. 9017

Number of pages - 13

Income Tax

[1993] AATA 298; (1993) 26 ATR 1388

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

P Gerber (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - primary production - whether sharefisherman entitled to benefit of income averaging provisions in Division 16 of Part III of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - whether arrangement was a partnership and he a partner

Foster v Insurance Commissioner (1939) QWN 4

Massey v Crown Life Insurance Co [1977] EWCA Civ 12; (1978) 1 WLR 676

Narich Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Pay-Roll Tax (1984) 58 ALJR 30

Stevens v Brodribb Sawmilling Co Pty Ltd (1985-1986) 160 CLR 16

Income Tax Ruling No. 2137

HEARING

BRISBANE, 12 July 1993

28:9:1993

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr J McGill

Solicitors for the Respondent: AGS

Mr D Desmarchelier appeared for the Applicant

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the objection decision under review.

DECISION

P. GERBER The issue in this case is whether, in respect of the year of income ended 30 June 1992, the taxpayer ("T") is a partner in a partnership operating under the name Pisces Aurora and engaged in a commercial fishing operation, and thus entitled to the benefit of the primary production averaging provisions of Division 16 of Part III of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act").

2. The facts can be briefly told. T is a qualified marine engineer holding both a Master's Certificate and a Master Fisherman's Certificate. Raptis Investments Pty Ltd is the owner of a fishing vessel - the "Pisces Aurora" - the vessel on which T was engaged in the fishing operation in the income year now under review.

3. Included in the "T" documents is a "partnership agreement" (a pro forma agreement for all seasons, which identifies this particular "partnership" on the title page by a holograph heading - "FV 'Pisces Aurora' Office Copy 1992" - the bottom of the front page contains the handwritten date - 21 March 1992. The agreement states that it is between "the Managing Partner", "the Crew Members", and "the Engineer" whose names are then handwritten into a schedule to the agreement. Those parties are (i) John Noah, of Whale Beach, identified as "the Managing Partner", (ii) this taxpayer, named as "the Engineer" and (iii) five persons named "the Crew Members". Paragraph 11 of that schedule is a form headed "Appointment to Partnership" which is signed by all the seven persons mentioned above, pursuant to which all these persons were claimed to have become "partners" of the Pisces Aurora "partnership" on 21 March 1992. Paragraph 10 of that schedule is a form headed "Resignation from Partnership", signed by T pursuant to which he resigned from the Pisces Aurora "partnership" on 25 June 1992 and acknowledging, inter alia, that he received:

the sum of $ in full satisfaction and discharge of all my

rights and entitlements under the said Partnership Agreement ...

(The amount was not filled in)

Paragraph 8 of that schedule sets out the share of profits and anticipated profits, stating that "where the Engineer is a partner in the Partnership, the partners shall be entitled to the following shares of the profit or anticipated profit of the Partnership Business". This is followed by a series of percentages, depending on the number of crew members, ranging from an eight man crew to four man crew. Freudian analysts may attach some significance to the description of these "partners" as "crew".

4. Another part of the overall documentation is a pro forma "Charter Agreement", also bearing the handwritten date "21 March 1992". This agreement states that it is between "the Owner" (of the FV 'Pisces Aurora') and "the Managing Partner". In a schedule to that agreement, Raptis Investments Pty Ltd appears typewritten as "the Owner" and Mr Noah (referred to in paragraph 3 above) is handwritten as "the Managing Partner".

5. The final piece in this documentation is a "Service Agreement", also hand-dated 21 March 1992. Both the agreement and the attached schedule state that the parties thereto are A Raptis and Sons identified as "The Service Entity" and John Noah, as "the Managing Partner". The thrust of this agreement is that "the Managing Partner" agrees to pay to "The Service Entity" some 2% of the gross revenue derived by the "partnership" upon sale of the catch as "consideration payable by the partnership for provision of services". I shall return to these various agreements in greater detail.

6. T, in his evidence, described what was referred to as the "signing on process" (i.e. becoming what is said to be a "partner") in each season:

Well, you - first, you have got to sell yourself to the

partnership. You get all your papers together, you get your

certificates, you get what your qualifications are - they've all got

to be current, and then you go round to wherever the boats are in

whatever city you happen to be in. Well, the most places to go is -

you have either got to go to Darwin, Karumba, Cairns or, now that

Raptis has moved to Brisbane, Brisbane, and you just see the people -

the skipper in charge. You and him get on, no worries. And then you

go on - you sign a contract and basically - there's a lot of word of

mouth because we're a small industry. Up in the Gulf, there is only

about 150 - there's about 200 boats work the Gulf now. Because we're

such a small industry, everybody knows everybody else that's

worthwhile. So if you are not good at your job, you've got a bad

name, you don't get on. (p4 tr)

7. T's evidence was to the effect that, for the 18 months or so he had worked on the Pisces Aurora, for each fishing season there was a similar set of agreements created, i.e. the "partnership", charter and service agreements, and appointment and resignation forms signed accordingly at the beginning and end of each particular season.

8. T claimed that his income from fishing was entirely dependent on the size of the catch, varying between "twenty-eight grand and fifty". T's share of food, personal transport costs and personal insurance were debited to his personal account, he being paid "whatever is left", which, as a matter of practice, was paid straight into his personal bank account.

9. Neither party directed the usual kind of questions designed to determine whether the contract was one of service. In the result, I have little evidence as to the manner this man carried out his duties or under whose - if any - direction he carried them out. The only questions that appear to me to be remotely relevant to this issue was asked by Mr Desmarchelier, who appeared for the applicant:

Mr T, your primary duties on the vessel are what?---Well, on my

first - my primary duty is to make sure that the refrigeration goes

and the main engine is running and the auxiliary plants are running

and all the maintenance, all the winches, the hydraulics, the

electronics, refrigeration - I've mentioned that. Anything mechanical

on board is mine. I'm solely in charge of that, and then, if I have

the time, help out on the deck, just give the boys a hand.

That is with what? Grading fish or pulling in nets or - - -?---Oh,

just grading, just grading the fish and general - where you see where

they need a hand, you just generally give them a hand.

In terms of your duties of, I presume preventative and remedial

maintenance, do you follow some Rule Book provided, or do you rely on

your own judgment?---No, you - well, you know, I've been doing it for

16 years sort of thing, and I do have a set of routine that I go

through that I've developed over that period of time. So everything

just runs along nice and smoothly.

And if it does not?---It doesn't, well, I'm probably looking for

another job.

10. Turning to the law, a useful starting point is the observation of Mason J (as his Honour then was), who, in Stevens v Brodribb Sawmilling Co Pty Ltd (1985-1986) 160 CLR 16 noted (at p 24):-

It has been held, however, that the importance of control lies not

so much in its actual exercise, although that is clearly relevant, as

in the right of the employer to exercise it: Zuijs v Wirth Bros Pty

Ltd [1955] HCA 73; (1955) 93 CLR 561 at 571; FC of T v Barrett [1973] HCA 49; (1973) 129 CLR 395 at

402; Humberstone v Northern Timber Mills [1949] HCA 49; (1949) 79 CLR 389 at 404. ...

But the existence of control, whilst significant, is not the sole

criterion by which to gauge whether a relationship is one of

employment. The approach of this Court has been to regard it merely as

one of a number of indicia which must be considered in the

determination of that question: Queensland Stations Pty Ltd v FC of T

[1945] HCA 13; (1945) 70 CLR 539 at 552; Zuijs Case; FC of T v Barrett; Marshall v

Whittaker's Building Supply Co [1963] HCA 26; (1963) 109 CLR 210, at 218.

11. As will be seen from the above, whether a person is a servant or something else has bedevilled the law ever since the advent of vicarious liability, which rendered a master vicariously liable for the torts of his/her servant acting in the course of his/her employment. Hallowed tests have come and gone, together with the refinements added by Lord Denning, such as the "organisation" test. I can do no better than to cite a passage from the Privy Council (affirming a decision of the Supreme Court of New South Wales) in Narich Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Pay-Roll Tax (1984) 58 ALJR 30, where, at pp 31-2 their Lordships observed:

The question whether, in any particular case, a person, who does

work for and receives remuneration in respect of such work from

another person, is an employee or an independent contractor, is far

from being a novel one, and there is a large number of authorities,

both in Australia and England, concerning it. In their Lordships' view

however, the principles of law relating to the determination of such a

question are well settled, and no useful purpose is to be served by

examining a congeries of reported cases, whether Australian or

English, in which courts have, on the particular facts of the

particular case before them, reached a result one way or the other by

the application of those well settled principles.

The only authority to which their Lordships consider it desirable to

refer in order to explain clearly the principles of law which are

relevant is Australian Mutual Provident Society v Chaplin (1978) 18

ALR 385 (the AMP case). ...

The judgment of the Judicial Committee in the AMP case was delivered

by Lord Fraser of Tullybelton and is authority for three principles of

law applicable to a case of the present kind. The first principle is

that, subject to one exception, where there is a written contract

between the parties whose relationship is in issue, a court is

confined, in determining the nature of that relationship, to a

consideration of the terms, express or implied, of that contract in

the light of the circumstances surrounding the making of it; and it is

not entitled to consider also the manner in which the parties

subsequently acted in pursuance of such contract. ...

The second principle is that, while all relevant terms of the contract

must be regarded, the most important, and in most cases the decisive,

criterion for determining the relationship between the parties is the

extent to which the person, whose status as employee or independent

contractor is in issue, is under the direction and control of the

other party to the contract with regard to the manner in which he does

his work under it (see the AMP case at 387).

The third principle relates to cases where the parties have, as in the

present case, included in their written contract an express provision

purporting to define the status of the party engaged under it, either

as that of employee on the one hand, or as that of independent

contractor on the other. ...

Their Lordships went on to cite a passage from the speech of Lord Fraser of Tullybelton in the AMP case, in which his Lordship quoted from a judgment of Lord Denning MR in Massey v Crown Life Insurance Co [1977] EWCA Civ 12; (1978) 1 WLR 676 as correctly stating the law on this aspect:

The law, as I see it, is this: if the true relationship of the

parties is that of master and servant under a contract of service, the

parties cannot alter the truth of that relationship by putting a

different label upon it.... On the other hand, if their relationship

is ambiguous and is capable of being one or the other (i.e. either

service or agency), then the parties can remove that ambiguity by the

very agreement itself which they make with one another. The agreement

itself then becomes the best material from which to gather the true

legal relationship between them.

12. Mr McGill, of learned counsel for the respondent, placed some reliance on the authority of Foster v Insurance Commissioner (1939) QWN 4, a case said to be "really quite similar" to the one before me. The issue in that case was whether a fisherman engaged in circumstances not unlike those before the Tribunal, was a "worker" within the meaning of the relevant workers' compensation legislation. The matter came before Webb J (then a judge of the Supreme Court of Queensland). The facts, relevant for present purposes, as found by the Industrial Magistrate and adopted by Webb J on appeal, were:-

The usual custom adopted is for the owner of a fishing boat and

fishing gear to arrange for men to act as the crew of the vessel and

to assist in fishing, the owner retaining the position of skipper of

the vessel and general control of fishing operations. The men are

engaged as members of the crew by the owner and they and the owner all

mutually assist in the working operations. The remuneration is fixed

by a share of the profits, which is dependent upon the amount and

value of the catch and the division of profits is made after each

trip. The men are at liberty to come and go after each fishing

excursion, and they have no interest whatever in the boat and fishing

gear. Whilst on the boat, they act under the supervision, direction,

and control of the skipper or owner of the vessel, who retains the

right of engagement or dismissal of the members of the crew. (at p 5)

The case is of marginal relevance, save that on a finding based on "the usual custom adopted (by the) owner of a fishing boat", which closely mirrors those deposed to in the instant case, the court was able to conclude that the relationship of master and servant had been made out. In the that case, such a finding resulted in the conclusion that the appellant was deemed to be a "worker" within the meaning of the (then) Workers' Compensation legislation. It remains to be seen whether someone engaged on those terms can be said to be a "partner".

13. In light of the paucity of the oral evidence, an examination of the provisions of the various documents relating to the operation of the "partnership" in question, particularly those relating to the "Partnership Agreement", is of vital importance as "it is the totality of the relationship between the parties which must be considered." (per Mason J (as he then was) in Stevens v Brodribb Sawmilling Co Pty Ltd (supra) at 29).

18. When the entirety of the circumstances are looked at, I am satisfied that the relevant parties have not succeeded in making a silk purse out of a sow's ear, that is, transmuting what is, as I find, in essence a fairly classic relationship of master and servant into a "partnership". As noted in Narich (supra):

... if the true relationship of the parties is that of master and

servant under a contract of service, the parties cannot alter the

truth of that relationship by putting a different label on it.

19. I am satisfied, on the whole of the evidence - such as it was - that Raptis, as the owner of the Pisces Aurora, not only paid the piper - it provided the boat and paid all expenses (save the personal expenses of the crew), but called the tune even to the extent of directing the "Managing Partner", i.e. the skipper to maintain daily radio contact with it and land the catch where directed. In other words, its control was not only theoretical - as in the case of, say, a hospital board vis-a-vis its medical staff - it was exercised in the instant case to the maximum the circumstances permitted. That is the reality of this relationship, which cannot be altered by a maze of interlocking agreements and paying the ship's crew with a share of the profits of the catch rather than by way of a regular salary. Looking at the facts in this case, it seems to me that all that Raptis has achieved is to obtain its crew members "on the cheap" by the transparent device of misdescribing them as "partners", thereby attempting to avoid the various consequences which flow from a relationship of master and servant, such as the provisions relating to PAYE tax.

20. In the result, I am satisfied on the whole of the evidence that this taxpayer was at all relevant times a member of the crew of the Pisces Aurora and performing his work under the direction and control of the owner of the vessel (through the skipper) in circumstances giving rise to a master and servant relationship. I find that the mode of payment on a share of the catch basis, does not, given the entirety of the circumstances, result in this fisherman carrying on business in common, or being in receipt of income jointly, with the other crew members of the Pisces Aurora. It follows that T is thus not entitled to the benefit of the primary production averaging provisions of Division 16 of Part III of the Act.

21. I was briefly referred to IT 2137, which states, inter alia:

The share of net profits derived by crew-members who are parties

to a share fishing arrangement is not subject to PAYE deductions.

22. Taken out of context, this abbreviated statement might at first blush appear to afford this taxpayer some comfort. However, on the conclusions I have reached, it can have no application.

23. For the above reasons the objection decision under review is affirmed.
