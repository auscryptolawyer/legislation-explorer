Conroy and Commissioner for Superannuation [1992] AATA 767 (4 September 1992)

Last Updated: 1 August 2008







Administrative

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



SUPERANNUATION - Applicant compulsorily retired after suffering work-caused injury. Was anxiety state a separate condition caused by work injury and its sequalae or connected with "history of anxiety state" nominated on Benefit Classification Certificate?



Superannuation Act 1976 - Sections 16 and 62



Perrett v Commissioner for Superannuation 29 FCR 581

Commissioner for Superannuation v Perrett [1989] FCA 348; 23 FCR 425

Commissioner for Superannuation v Scott 13 FCR 404

Commissioner for Superannuation v Benham 22 FCR 413

Re Schlatter and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority 8 ALD 133; 3 AAR 488

EMI (Australia) Ltd v Bes (1970) 2 NSWR 238

Re Whiteford and Commissioner for Superannuation 6 AAR 70

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL ) No. N91/379

)

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )



Re: RONALD JAMES CONROY

Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION



4 September 1992 Mr M.D. Allen, Senior Member

Dr M.E.C. Thorpe, Member



1. The Applicant originally commenced employment with, what was then, the Postmaster-General's Department on 15 September 1969. He resigned from that employ on 11 November 1981 but, after a short period of employment with the Reserve Bank, was re-employed by the Australian Postal Corporation (as it is now called) on 31 August 1981.



2. For permanent employment purposes, he was medically examined by a Government Medical Officer, a Dr Perrottet, on or about 4 June 1982. Dr Perrottet considered at that time that the Applicant did not meet the necessary physical standards for permanent employment and recommended the question of the Applicant's permanent status be deferred for 12 months.



3. The Applicant was re-examined on 20 January 1983 and considered fit for permanent employment. The reports of the Commonwealth Medical Officer were, however, forwarded to the Respondent who, on 25 January 1985, issued a Benefit Classification Certificate in respect of the Applicant which certificate specified the conditions of:

"Left Varicocele

History of Anxiety State

History of Bronchitis

History of Dermatitis

History of Intervertebral Disc Lesion".



4. That Benefit Classification Certificate was issued pursuant to Section 16 of the Superannuation Act 1976 which states inter alia:

"(1) ...



(2) The Commissioner may, for the purposes of this section, require a person (other than a person to whom section 184 applies) who proposes to become or becomes an eligible employee to undergo, within such period as the Commissioner specifies, such medical examination or examinations by an approved medical practitioner or practitioners as the Commissioner determines.



(3) A report or reports of the result or results of the medical examination or examinations shall be furnished to the Commissioner.



(4) The Commissioner shall consider the report or reports, and such other matters (if any) as the Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of the opinion that the person is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the report or reports, to continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age, the Commissioner shall issue a benefit classification certificate to that effect, being a certificate in which the relevant condition or conditions is or are specified."



5. On 9 May 1985, the Applicant suffered a work related injury, namely a right inguinal hernia. An operation to repair the hernia was undergone by the Applicant on 7 June 1985 but, unfortunately, that operation was performed in such a manner that complications developed and the Applicant suffered an atrophy of his right testicle.



6. This complication from the hernia operation was subsequently surgically corrected by Dr Gillett, Surgeon, although post-operatively the Applicant had to have the wound drained at Hornsby Hospital - see Exhibit G.



7. The Applicant had a protracted struggle with Australia Post to obtain ongoing workers compensation consequent upon his work injury. Ultimately matters were settled as between the parties when, on 26 May 1988, Deputy President Bannon QC (as he then was) signed a Consent Order pursuant to Section 34A of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 which Order stated inter alia:

"1. the decision under review is varied;



2. the respondent is liable to pay compensation to the applicant in respect of any incapacity for work from 28 October 1987 to 8 December 1987;



3. the respondent is to pay the applicant's costs. Costs are to be agreed, but, failing agreement, to be taxed by the Deputy Registrar in accordance with the appropriate scale of the New South Wales Workers' Compensation Court."



8. As the Applicant's workers compensation had been terminated with effect from 28 October 1987, he applied for an Invalid Pension pursuant to the Social Security Act 1947 (as amended). Although that claim was initially rejected on the advice of a Commonwealth Government Medical Officer, it was granted following a reconsideration by a Social Security Appeals Tribunal.



9. At this hearing the Tribunal was concerned as to what led to the decision of Australia Post to retire the Applicant. Subsequent to the hearing, there were produced to the Tribunal on subpoena, the files of the Australian Postal Corporation relating to the Applicant's claim for workers compensation.



10. Contained in the material produced is a memorandum, dated 16 November 1987, from the Australian Postal and Telecommunications Union to the Applicant's employer. That memorandum peremptorily states:

"This is to advise that Mr R. Conroy has been incapacitated for in excess of two years due to a hernia. We understand Mr Conroy has not been considered for retirement. However the Dept. (sic) of Social Security is prepared to pay him an invalid pension.



We therefore request due to Mr Conroy's difficult financial situation Australia Post make urgent arrangements to have him medically retired and all appropriate money due to him paid.



I request your prompt response."



11. In the interim, following the settlement of the workers compensation claim, Australia Post had instructed its solicitors to enquire regarding the Applicant's willingness to return to work. The Applicant, by his current solicitors, then informed Australia Post that he was not fit to return to work and provided reports from the Applicant's medical practitioner. Two days after this communication, Australia Post were also advised that the Applicant had been admitted to a Psychiatric Hospital.



12. As a result of these communications, the Applicant was examined by a Commonwealth Medical Officer on 8 July 1988, and a psychiatric referee on 19 August 1988. On 19 September 1988 a decision was made by a Delegate of Australia Post to retire the Applicant on invalidity grounds, that decision being made retrospective to 7 May 1987.



13. It would appear that the decision to retire the Applicant was taken not so much on the basis of advice from the Commonwealth Medical Officer but on the basis that the Applicant was in fact in receipt of an Invalid Pension. At Exhibit A, page 135, a memorandum by the Australian Postal Corporation dated 5 October 1988, following a Ministerial representation on the Applicant's behalf, states inter alia:

"Mr Conroy was reviewed by the CMO in March 1988 in the light of documents received from the DSS. The CMO subsequently advised that there was no new medical evidence in the documents and his previous advice of August 1987 was unchanged.



Despite the CMO's recommendation, the decision was made to initiate retirement action as there appeared to be no apparent alternative, given the DSS's decision to grant a pension."



14. By letter dated 15 December 1988, the Respondent requested further advice from the Senior Medical Officer of the Department of Community Services and Health, Sydney as to the conditions which occasioned the Applicant's retirement. In his reply dated 21 December 1988, the Senior Medical Officer stated inter alia:

"I have perused Mr Conroy's medical file and note that on 9 September 1988 Dr King had recommended continued sick leave with review in three months. The principal reason for this recommendation was to enable Mr Conroy to seek psychiatric treatment as advised by Dr Frame in his report.



In actual fact Mr Conroy was retired on the grounds of invalidity by Australia Post. Whilst he still complained of symptoms relating to the complications he experienced following surgery to his left groin in June 1985 the principal cause for his retirment (sic) appears to have been anxiety state and depression - say 75%. His depression followed surgery in 1985 for repair of left inguinal hernia. The relationship of the development of this hernia to the pre-existing condition of varicocele is unclear."



15. Upon retirement the Applicant applied for superannuation and on 9 January 1989, a Delegate of the Respondent determined as follows:

"... the incapacity which was the ground for your retirement (namely: Anxiety State & Depression, Injured R.Groin), was caused or was substantially contributed to by a physical or mental condition(s) specified in the Benefit Classification Certificate (being: History of Anxiety State), or by a physical or mental condition(s) connected with such a condition(s)."



That determination was made pursuant to Ss 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 which states:

"Subject to sub-sections (3), (3A) and (4) of this section and section 79, where -



(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age;



(b) there was in force in respect of the person, immediately before the person's retirement, a benefit classification certificate; and



(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or such conditions,



the person is entitled -



(d) where the period of contributory service of the person is not less than 8 years -



...



(e) where the period of contributory service of the person is less than 8 years - to a lump sum benefit in accordance with sub-section 73 ..."



16. Pursuant to a request by the Applicant and Section 154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976, the decision by the Delegate was reconsidered by another Delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation and on 4 April 1991 confirmed. The Applicant, by his solicitors, thereupon sought further review by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. The said application for review came on for hearing before this Tribunal on 24, 25 and 26 June 1992, the Applicant being represented by his solicitor, and the Respondent by Mr Withear who is the Assistant Commissioner for Superannuation



17. At that hearing the following documents were taken in as exhibits, namely:

Exhibit A : The documents prepared pursuant to Section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975



Exhibit B : Report of Dr Rob McMurdo dated 3 January 1992



Exhibit C : Report of Dr J.A. Roberts dated 12 March 1992



Exhibit D : Applicant's History of Events



Exhibit E : Report of Hornsby District Hospital dated 18 May 1989



Exhibit F : Report of Hornsby District Hospital dated 17 October 1989



Exhibit G : Copy of Discharge Summary of Hornsby District Hospital dated 20 February 1990



Exhibit H : Copy of Ultrasound Report dated 11 October 1985



Exhibit 1 : Copy of Retirement Instrument dated 19 September 1988



Exhibit 2 : Additional documents prepared pursuant to Section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975



Exhibit 3 : Copy of Certificate by Dr M.H. Bern dated 31 May 1979



Exhibit 4 : Report of Dr D.S. Bell dated 1 September 1989.



In addition the Tribunal heard evidence from the Applicant and Drs McMurdo, Bell, Girgis, Hansen, Frame, Roberts and Perrottet. Of those medical practitioners, all except Dr Perrottet are Psychiatrists.



18. The task of the Tribunal was obfuscated by the plethora of psychiatric opinions raised for its consideration. It transpired that the Applicant was suffering from the following psychiatric disabilities:



Dr McMurdo : The Applicant has suffered discrete episodes of anxiety state and depression as opposed to either anxiety or personality disorder.



Dr Girgis : The Applicant is suffering from anxiety/depression as a result of his work accident and its complications.



Dr Bell : The Applicant is suffering a personality disorder.



Dr Frame : The Applicant is suffering a personality disorder but is fit for work with Australia Post.



Dr Roberts : The Applicant is suffering an affective disorder, namely a primary disorder of mood characterised by periodicity.



In addition the Applicant had, on referral from his general practitioner, consulted Dr Hansen in June 1985 for a specific problem, namely his gambling. Dr Hansen diagnosed him as a compulsive gambler with an adjustment disorder brought about by his financial problems. Dr Hansen referred the Applicant to Dr Allcock who specialises in treating people with gambling problems.



19. It is not necessary for the Tribunal to decide which is the correct diagnostic label to be applied to the Applicant's condition. The question for the Tribunal is what incapacity (howsoever labelled) was ground for the Applicant's retirement and was that incapacity caused or substantially contributed to by a physical or mental condition specified in the Benefit Classification Certificate issued in respect of the Applicant, or by a physical or mental condition connected with such a condition or conditions.



20. In this matter the only relevant condition in the Benefit Classification Certificate is that of "history of anxiety state".



21. At no time during his service with Australia Post did the Applicant request the Respondent, pursuant to Ss 16(6) of the Superannuation Act 1976, to vary or revoke the Benefit Classification Certificate. Neither in these proceedings did the Applicant seek to challenge the inclusion of "history of anxiety state" on that certificate. Suffice it to say, however, there was unchallenged evidence that the Applicant had, whilst previously employed by Australia Post, been absent from work because of what his treating doctor certified as an "anxiety state" and that he had also received inpatient treatment for that condition.



22. As to the degree of connection between the incapacity which occasioned retirement and the condition nominated on the Benefit Classification Certificate, the Full Court of the Federal Court in Perrett v Commissioner for Superannuation 29 FCR 581 approved the formulation adopted by Jenkinson J in Commissioner for Superannuation v Perrett [1989] FCA 348; 23 FCR 425. At page 433 Jenkinson J said:

"In my opinion, the expression 'connected with' in s 66(2)(c) does comprehend, on the proper construction of the paragraph, something short of the relationship between cause and effect. The choice of that expression by the draftsman strongly suggests that something more comprehensive than a causal relationship was to be indicated, for there is no shortage of stock expressions for denoting a causal relationship in the phrase book of legislative drafting, and 'connected with' is not one of them. I respectfully agree in the opinion Beaumont J expressed in Miller's case that 'connected with' is intended to have in s 66(2)(c) its ordinary meaning, and that that meaning comprehends 'those cases where a link or an association exists between two medical conditions'. In my opinion such a link or association might be constituted by a higher incidence, in a community, of a medical condition among those of the community in whom another medical condition is known to exist than among the rest of that community. Unless the one were distinctly higher than the other the requirement that the connection be 'substantial', upon which the Full Court insisted in Benham's case, would no doubt not be satisfied. If the disparity of incidence were explained by the circumstance that those in whom the other condition is known to exist are more frequently or more thoroughly subjected to procedures calculated to disclose the existence of the condition first mentioned than are other members of the community, that might lead to the conclusion that the connection was not 'real', in the sense I take to have been intended by the Full Court in Benham's case, and that the two conditions were not linked in what Beaumont J in Miller's case called 'a relevant medical sense'. What may be an association in a relevant medical sense will depend largely on facts, which it would be unwise to attempt to classify. I apprehend that the connection will be neither 'real' nor 'substantial' unless the disparity of incidence justifies an inference that the risk of suffering the condition which incapacitated the eligible employee is substantially greater for those who suffer the specified condition than it is for the rest of the community."



23. Ss 66(2) also requires that the contribution be "substantial". The word "substantially" in juxtaposition with word "wholly" was discussed by the Federal Court in Commissioner for Superannuation v Scott 13 FCR 404, 71 ALR 408. In Scott's case supra the Federal Court disapproved the interpretation the Tribunal ascribed to the phrase "whole or substantially dependent" in re Schlatter and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority 8 ALD 133; 3 AAR 488. Schlatter's case was referred to by the Tribunal when it discussed the meaning of the word "substantially" as it occurs in para 66(2)(c) in re Whiteford and Commissioner for Superannuation 6 AAR 70 which case was cited by Mr Withear. Having regard to what fell their Honours Fisher and Spender JJ in that case, it is sufficient to say that where para 66(2)(c) speaks of a condition being "substantially contributed to" it means a contribution that was in the circumstances real or of substance, or, as the Tribunal put it in re Whiteford supra, "of such significance in relation to the incapacity as to warrant depriving the employee of a significant part of the benefits which he would otherwise have received ...".



24. Of the medical practitioners called, only Dr Girgis rejected the claims by the Respondent that there was a connection between the history of anxiety state, nominated on the Benefit Classification Certificate, and the incapacity which led to the Applicant's retirement.



25. In a report dated 4 August 1990, Dr Girgis opined:

"The conditons (sic) that rendered Mr Conroy incapable of performing his duties immediately before retirement on 7.5.87 are: severe depression (and paranoid state) caused by the post-operative complications of the hernia operation ... The anxiety state that has been occasionally referred to in the 'sick leave' could not, in my opinion have caused the severe state he was in ..."



26. Dr Girgis was extensively cross-examined and, after a degree of circumlocution in his evidence, he was asked specifically by the Tribunal:

"Q. Has the earlier anxiety condition in any way contributed to the severity of the condition in 1988?"



And replied:



"A. No."



27. This unequivocal opinion of Dr Girgis' can be contrasted with that of Dr McMurdo who was also called by the Applicant. Dr McMurdo's evidence had the advantage that he diagnosed the Applicant's condition using the nomenclature of DSM III R, ie the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 3rd Edition - revised, published by the American Psychiatric Association.



28. As stated above, Dr McMurdo opined that the Applicant has suffered discrete periods of anxiety state and depression. In cross-examination he did concede that the Applicant probably has a greater pre-disposition to anxiety than some people but qualified that statement by adding that the results of the work injury, and subsequent surgery, were of major importance in the Applicant's present anxiety, together with the Applicant's financial problems.



29. Dr McMurdo was asked by Dr Thorpe:

"Q. So I really come back to you again, sir, and ask you that same question in terms of the CMOs written statement that this man had an anxiety disorder in 1984, whether that has changed materially as of today and in particular, as of 1988, as the working diagnosis?



And replied:



"A. I think the working diagnosis is not different.



Q. Do you think it is the same working diagnosis as it was in 1984?



A. Correct."



30. Later Dr McMurdo was questioned by the Presiding Member and the following passage of evidence occurs:

"Q. ... it strikes me that if you have a person who has - whether they are discrete or not - had periods of disabling anxiety previously, you would have to say that he was vulnerable?



A. Certainly.



Q. So that it is quite probably, with a trauma of the - it is not so much the herniorrhaphy, but the resultant testicular atrophy etcetera, it is probable that he would not make the recovery a more robust individual might?



A. Yes, that is true."



31. Accepting as it does these passages of Dr McMurdo's evidence, it is difficult to avoid the conclusion that the anxiety state that led to the Applicant's retirement was connected with the "history of anxiety state" referred to in the Benefit Classification Certificate and that that connection was "substantial" as that term was defined in Scott's case supra.



32. The Psychiatrists called for the Respondent, whilst differing in their assessment of the Applicant's condition, considered that there was a connection between the history of anxiety state referred to in the Benefit Classification Certificate and the anxiety state and depression which occasioned his retirement.



33. The opinions of Dr Girgis, who is the Applicant's treating Psychiatrist, are entitled to particular regard by the Tribunal. However, the Tribunal was more persuaded by the evidence of Dr McMurdo and, whereas it does not accept totally the opinions of those Psychiatrists called by the Respondent, the evidence of Dr McMurdo seems to the Tribunal to be more aligned with the preponderance of psychiatric opinion as to the Applicant's condition and its connection to the condition specified in the Benefit Classification Certificate. That connection is that the Applicant has an underlying personality disorder which at times is exacerbated so as to manifest as an anxiety state with depression.



34. There have been other episodes in the Applicant's life when an anxiety state/depression has manifested itself. On two of these occasions the anxiety state has resulted from financial difficulties caused in large part by the Applicant's compulsive gambling which is itself symptomatic of a personality disorder. As Dr McMurdo pointed out, it is likely that but for his vulnerable personality, the Applicant would not have reacted so disastrously to the post operative complications of his herniorrhaphy. Similarly, the Applicant could have coped with other life events without decompensating.



35. The approach to be adopted by the Tribunal in considering whether, given the Applicant's prior history of suffering from an anxiety state, the history of anxiety state included in the Benefit Classification Certificate was substantially connected with the condition which led to his retirement, was referred to by Asprey JA in EMI (Australia) Ltd v Bes (1970) 2 NSWR 238 at 243, namely:

"Where scientific knowledge properly adduced in evidence as expert opinion deposes to more than one event as a possible cause of a medical condition and where it appears from the evidence accepted by the Tribunal of fact that of those possible causes of that condition one of them, on the balance of probabilities, is more likely than the others to be the cause of the medical condition in question, then the tribunal may properly draw the inference of fact that such was the operating cause of that condition in the particular circumstances. Reliance for the purpose of drawing that inference may be placed by the tribunal on the evidence as a whole as is not confined to medical evidence only, except where all the medical evidence agrees that the matters sought to be relied upon must be excluded from consideration as lacking justification for the drawing of the inference."



See also Herron CJ at page 242 commencing at line 24.



36. Adopting the principles as to burden of proof referred to above and having regard to the decision of the Full Court in both Perrett, supra, and Commissioner for Superannuation v Benham 22 FCR 413, the Tribunal concludes that the anxiety state and depression which occasioned the Applicant's retirement was connected with the History of Anxiety State nominated on the Benefit Classification Certificate and thus the decision under review is affirmed.

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL ) No. N91/379

VETERANS' APPEALS DIVISION )



Re: RONALD JAMES CONROY

Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION



4 September 1992 Mr T.R. Russell, Member



I have read the Decision and the Reasons for the Decision of Mr M.D. Allen (Senior Member) and Dr M.E.C. Thorpe (Member) and agree with them. However, there were some aspects of this matter which concerned me and I therefore feel compelled to add the following observations:



1. The employer made a determination on 28 October 1987 that the Applicant was fit for duty. That determination was made having regard to the reports of Dr L. Nordstrom, CMO, and Drs Bell and Alexander.



2. The Applicant appealed that decision to a differently constituted Tribunal of this Tribunal and, as noted in paragraph 7 of the Reasons, in a consent agreement, the Applicant was determined to be unfit for duty until 8 December 1987.



3. Despite the medical reports referred to in 1. above and the decision in 2. above, the Applicant did not return to work after 8 December 1987 but, on 2 December 1987, the employer wrote to the Applicant directing him to resume duty on 7 December 1987, which the employer was entitled to do under the Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees') Act 1971



4. Despite the employer's view as to the Applicant's fitness, the decision of the Tribunal and the direction to return to work, compensation or sick leave continued to be paid for several months and, on 9 September 1988, Dr King, CMO, was asked to review the Applicant for the purpose of determining whether he should be retired on the grounds of invalidity.



5. Dr King, in supplying the report requested, noted a report from Dr M.C. Frame, a referee psychiatrist, dated 8 July 1988 that the Applicant was, at that time, not fit for work but may be fit to so return in 3-10 month's time and recommending that the views of Dr Frame be followed as far as possible.



6. On 19 September 1988, having noted that the Applicant had not returned to work and that all sick/compensation leave had apparently been exhausted, the employer determined that the Applicant be retired on the grounds of invalidity, retrospective to 7 May 1987 (emphasis added). Whilst a retrospective retirement predated by over one year is in itself unusual, Dr King, CMO, had on 9 September 1988 recommended a further three months sick leave be granted.



7. In the meantime the Applicant had, on 25 March 1987, made an application to the Department of Social Security for an Invalid Pension and, on 15 October 1987, the Social Security Appeals Tribunal made a recommendation that the Invalid Pension be granted. However, it also recommended that the grant of that pension be reviewed in 12 month's time. It thus appears that that Tribunal considered that the Applicant had a potential to return to work, as indeed did the CMO who examined the Applicant for the employer only shortly before the Social Security Appeals Tribunal decision was announced.



8. In evidence before this Tribunal, the Applicant stated that in view of the uncertainty of any further compensation or sick leave payments from the employer, he required income for living purposes and to maintain his family (he was married with two children) and therefore an application for the Invalid Pension was the only alternative then available to him. His course of action is understandable.



9. After receiving the employer's letter of 2 December 1987 directing him to return to work, the Applicant rang the employer on 4 December 1987 and advised that he had been deemed "totally incapacitated" by the Social Security Appeals Tribunal. After receiving that advice (see handwritten note at the bottom of the return to work letter of 2 December 1987), the employer appeared to be then placed in a position of some uncertainty, despite a further report from Dr King, CMO, on 10 March 1988 that despite the findings of the Social Security Appeals Tribunal he considered the Applicant fit for duties as a Mail Officer.



10. One particular uncertainty and concern in the mind of the employer (see Internal Memo dated 15 June 1988 from Mr A.D. Kidson to Manager, International Mail Centre and Staff Movements Officer, Personnel Department) was that if the Applicant was retired on medical grounds it might support a Common Law claim which the Applicant had made on the employer and, as the employer's medical advice was that the Applicant was fit for work, he should be offered appropriate duties or he should resign. It was Mr Kidson's recommendation that the Applicant not be medically retired.



11. After the Respondent had made the primary decision on 9 January 1989 that given the medical circumstances of the Applicant's retirement, the conditions specified in the Benefit Classification Certificate and that Sub-section 66(2) of the Act would apply, the Applicant sought a review of that decision. In carrying out that review, the Respondent wrote urgently to the employer on 1 June 1989 seeking medical details of the Applicant's retirement on 7 May 1987. The Respondent wrote again on 14 August 1989, 22 September 1989, 23 November 1989 and 19 January 1990 and the reasons for this inordinate delay in answering what appeared to be a straightforward request were not readily apparent from the evidence before the Tribunal. However, it does appear that the employer was wrestling with the problem of its own medical advice that the Applicant was fit for duty, the decision of the Social Security Appeals Tribunal, and the advice of Mr Kidson.



12. Quite independently of the aspects mentioned in 11. above, the Applicant had made representations through his Federal Member of Parliament (Dr H. Edwards) on 27 July 1988 and 9 November 1988 - some 3½ months later -the Minister responsible advised Dr Edwards that despite the employer's view that the Applicant was fit for work, the employer had decided to initiate retirement action as it appeared it had no alternative:

"given the Department of Social Security's decision to grant a pension." (emphasis added) (T28/135).



In that advice, the following words also appear:

"The Superannuation Board was made aware of the reason for the retirement action."



Whatever form that advice took, it was not in evidence in any document before the Tribunal. The following handwritten note also appears on the bottom of T28/136:

"Post the above recommendation Mr Backhouse of Aust Post Syd Retirement Section advised Mr Conroy he was to receive 64.4% of his superannuation. Papers for that have been signed and returned to Mr Backhouse. To date Mr Conroy is still waiting."



The receipt of any superannuation may of course reduce the Applicant's Invalid Pension payments or indeed eliminate them.



13. There was of course another "alternative" open to the employer and that was to again direct the Applicant to return to work. The Invalid Pension is a means-tested pension and if and when an applicant is in receipt of income, the Invalid Pension would reduce or be extinguished entirely. Furthermore, it had been the recommendation of the Social Security Appeals Tribunal that such pension be reviewed 12 months after being granted.



14. It appears therefore from the documents obtained by the Tribunal that the Applicant was retired by the employer solely on the grounds that he had been granted an Invalid Pension. He appears not to have been retired expressly (emphasis added) by the employer for any medical reason. In fact, it appeared to me that it was the primary decision of the employer that he was at the relevant times fit to return to work. It appeared strongly to me therefore that the Applicant's retirement by the employer as a consequence of being granted an Invalid Pension became an option available to the employer as a means of solving a dilemma which earlier had appeared to it to be not capable of a "simple" solution.



15. However, whilst the above observations concerning the employer appear to me to be unsatisfactory so far as the Applicant is concerned, they cannot however assist him in that the employer is not the Respondent in this matter. The Respondent has determined as follows:



(a) The Applicant had been granted the Invalid Pension and that in being granted that pension, "anxiety/depression" problems accounted for 45% of the 85% impairment necessary to obtain such pension.



(b) The employer retired the Applicant because of the grant of that pension.



(c) Therefore, the conditions of "anxiety/depression" played a part (substantial, major) in the circumstances of his retirement.



16. As my colleagues have already set out in their reasons, the overall circumstances of the Applicant's retirement require that the decision under review be affirmed.
