Re Bruce John Montgomery and Commissioner of Superannuation [1985] AATA 78 (11 April 1985)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: BRUCE JOHN MONTGOMERY

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. T84/42

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.C. Jennings QC (Deputy President)

D.R.S. Craik (Member)

L.J. Cohn (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - lump sum benefit - whether applicant retired involuntarily - whether he was retrenched - whether he was temporary or permanent - whether his position ceased to exist after change of Government - appointment and termination as State Co-ordination Officer by Minister.

Superannuation Act 1922 ss. 4, 39, 41, 50

Superannuation Act 1976 ss. 3 (1) (3) (5) 11, 45 (1), 58, 62, 79, 80 (1), 157 (1)

Public Service Act 1922 ss. 20, 82

Commonwealth Employees (Redeployment and Retirement) Act 1979 ss. 2 (2) 18, 19

Acts Interpretation Act 1901 s. 15AA (1)

Commissioner for Superannuation v Bayley [1979] FCA 76; 28 ALR 293

HEARING

CANBERRA

11:4:1985

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and remits the matter to the Commissioner for Superannuation for calculation and payment of the amount due to the applicant pursuant to s. 62 (2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 having regard to the amount he has already received by way of refund of his contributions.

The Tribunal reserves liberty to apply.

DECISION

The applicant commenced employment with the Australian Broadcasting Commission as a journalist on 26 February 1974. He became a contributor to the Superannuation Fund established under the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the superseded Act") by virtue of a direction that he should be deemed to be an "employee" for the purposes of section 4 of that Act. That direction was made on 30 June 1975 after his employer had certified that his employment was likely to be continued for at least seven years.

The effect of the direction was to deem him to be "a person employed in a permanent capacity by the Commonwealth" and to entitle him, should his service be terminated "for the reason that his service or position is not necessary" to a choice of benefits in the event of any such "retrenchment". (ss. 39, 41 and 50 of the superseded Act).

On 1 July 1976 the Superannuation Act 1976 ("The Act") came into operation. By virtue of definition in s. 3 (1) the applicant became an "eligible employee" for the purposes of that Act and was thereby required to pay fortnightly basic contributions (s. 45(1)). He was without doubt

(a) "a person who, immediately before the

commencement of this Act, was an employee

for the purposes of the superseded Act and

has continued to be, and is, such an

employee:"

On 20 April 1979 the applicant resigned from the ABC and on Monday 23 April

1979 commenced employment as a State Co-ordination Officer for the Government Information Unit (GIU) in circumstances and on terms which we shall shortly describe. Section 3 (3) of the Act operated so as to preserve his status as an "eligible employee". It provides as follows:

"Where a person ceases to be an eligible

employee and, immediately after so ceasing,

again becomes an eligible employee, he shall,

for the purposes of this Act, be deemed not to

have so ceased."

There can be no doubt that when his new employment began he became either

"(b) a person who is a permanent employee;"

or "(e) a person-

(i) who is a temporary employee;

"and who, immediately before the commencement

of the employment by reason of which he is such

a temporary employee or his appointment to that

office, was an eligible employee by virtue of

another paragraph of this definition or a

previous application of this paragraph;"

It will be necessary to refer later for another purpose into which of these categories he in fact fell.

The applicant worked continuously for the GIU until he was advised by a letter dated 17 March 1983 from the Department of the Special Minister of State that his services were no longer required as from "today's date". We will refer later to the precise terms of this letter.

As a result of that termination of services the question arises as to whether the applicant is a person who "shall be deemed to have retired involuntarily" for the purposes of the Act (ss. 58 (3)) or whether he is a person who is not entitled to benefit under Division 2 ("Early Retirement Benefit" sections 58 to 62 inclusive) and is therefore only entitled to an amount equal to his accumulated contributions (ss. 80 (1)).

It was common ground that events and correspondence which occurred between 17 March 1983 and 26 July 1984 properly raised a reviewable decision within the meaning of s. 154 of the Act. It was also agreed that this Tribunal should treat the applicant as being entitled to a lump sum benefit of an amount equal to three and half times the amount of his accumulated basic contributions pursuant to s. 62 of the Act, if and only if it finds that he is a person "who shall be deemed to have retired involuntarily" by reason that he is a person referred to in subparagraph (b) or (e) of s. 58 (3).

It will be necessary to refer in some detail to that sub-section but in essence there are two basic questions for our decision: firstly was he "retrenched"?: and secondly was he "a temporary employee" within the meaning of the Act? If not, did his position cease to exist "by reason of its being abolished or otherwise"? The applicant will succeed if he was retrenched or if he comes within subparagraph (e) of ss. 58 (3).

For the purpose of answering the first part of the second question it is necessary to refer to the following definitions in s. 3 (1) of the Act:

"In this Act, unless the contrary intention

appears-

"permanent employee"

means

(a) a person who is an officer for the

purposes of the Public Service Act; and

(b) any other person employed by the

Commonwealth or by an approved authority

in a permanent capacity,

but does not include a part-time employee who

is not an approved part-time employee;"

"temporary employee" means-

(a) a person who is an employee for the

purposes of the Public Service Act: and

(b) any other person employed by the

Commonwealth or by an approved authority

otherwise than in a permanent capacity,

but does not include-" (immaterial for present

purposes).

The definition of "approved authority" is not relevant. It was common ground

that the applicant was certainly not employed by an approved authority at the time his services were terminated.

The Tribunal received in evidence the T documents which accompanied the statement for the respondent pursuant to s. 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, but the applicant had some reservations about the factual accuracy of some of the assertions contained in a number of those documents. The applicant gave evidence and produced a series of documents some of which helped us to resolve some of the inconsistencies and confusion which it was admitted arose from a study of the T documents alone. The respondent produced no witnesses but tendered a Public Service circular of 16 April 1967 to which was attached a copy of the Commonwealth Gazette of 17 August 1967 pages 4438-4440 inclusive (and amendments thereof which do not appear to have any relevance for present purposes).

As a result of our consideration of all that material we are able to state our findings of fact. In so far as the T documents tended to confuse, we prefer the evidence of the applicant whose credibility and reliability were not challenged in any respect.

In late January or early February 1979 the applicant responded to a newspaper advertisement inviting applications "for the post of State Co-ordination Officer for the Commonwealth Government in Tasmania". That advertisement included the following information:

"The Officer will be attached to the staff

of the Minister for National Development,

the Hon. Kevin Newman. The Officer would

assist the Minister in reporting regularly

on significant State issues which are

relevant at the Federal level.

The successful applicant who will be based

in Launceston should have an understanding

of Commonwealth/State relations. Experience

in journalism would be an advantage.

Total remuneration including an allowance

in lieu of overtime will be in the range

$25,284 - $25,979. Conditions will be

similar to those applying to Commonwealth

Public Servants".

The applicant was subsquently interviewed by three Commonwealth Ministers

(including Mr. Newman) and a Mr. V. Matthews who was described as "Head of the GIU., Canberra". The Hon. Kevin Newman wrote to the applicant on 21 March 1979 in the following terms:-

"I am writing to confirm that you have been

appointed as the Commonwealth Government's

State Co-ordination Officer for Tasmania.

You will need to have a security clearance

and complete a declaration of your pecuniary

interests, and I will arrange for Tony Ashford

to speak to you about this.

The necessary arrangements for your transfer

are in hand and I will keep you informed of

developments."

Mr. Newman also wrote on the same day to the Minister for Administrative

Services (The Hon. J.E. McLeay) seeking his formal approval for the applicant's appointment and details of the conditions which would apply to his appointment (including "superannuation rights") - T4/65.

Mr. McLeay replied on the 4 April 1979 (T4/66) concurring in the appointment. He referred in some detail to "the salary of the position" and to the fact that he would receive "a ministerial staff allowance". He also advised that provided there was no break in service the applicant would be entitled to carry over all benefits under the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme, as he was currently a contributor.

Copies of both these letters were sent to the applicant who at or about the time he began work for the GIU was given a copy of the job specification which had been prepared by Mr. Matthews in January 1979 (Exhibit 4). That document made it clear to the applicant that he would be operating on behalf of all Ministers and Members in the State; that he would be responsible for practical operational purposes to the GIU in Canberra; that he was required to assist Ministers in the most effective use of their time during visits to the States; that he would liaise and communicate with Ministers and Members, State and Federal - the media - and State and Federal Party organisations; and that he would not travel with the Minister "to whose office he is attached".

The applicant worked for the GIU for nearly four years during which time there were a number of changes in several other States as to the identity of the Senior Minister for that State. When such changes occurred the State Co-ordination Officers became attached to the new Senior Minister. However Mr. Newman remained Senior Minister in Tasmania until March 1983. On an unidentified date (probably late 1981) it was decided that the State Co-ordination officers should be called "Federal Information Director". The applicant suggested that his functions would be more effectively performed in Hobart and as a result he left the office of Mr. Newman in Launceston thereby substantially severing what had become a fairly close personal relationship. At about the time of the change of title of State Co-ordination Officers the GIU was apparently the subject of a separate budget allocation resulting in a reduction in the applicant's salary.

Whether the applicant was engaged "in a permanent capacity" within the meaning of that expression as it occurs in the definitions of permanent and temporary employees in s. 3 (1) of the Act is a question which must be determined by reference to the manner in which the applicant was appointed and the terms of his engagement in so far as they can be inferred from the facts set out or referred to in paragraphs 14 to 19 inclusive.

It seems clear despite assertions to the contrary by both the respondent and the Department of the Special Minister of State that the applicant was never employed as a temporary employee under s. 82 of the Public Service Act 1922. No attempt was made to fulfil any of the requirements of that section or to exclude the operation of any part of it by an Order under s. 8A of that Act. Moreover the 1967 Order referred to in the Gazette produced by the respondent only purports to apply (for present purposes) to an employee included in the following class:

"Employee employed on the personal staff

of a Minister".

We do not consider the applicant was a member of the personal staff of any

individual Minister despite his original "attachment" to the office of the Hon. Kevin Newman. He was appointed by the Minister for Administrative Services and required to perform services for the Commonwealth Government. Accordingly the respondent's argument is not assisted by the provisions of the order which excludes the operation of ss (3) (4) and (8) of s. 82 of the Public Service Act 1922 in relation to personal ministerial staff.

But even if this distinction is not valid no fact was established at the hearing of the Tribunal to justify an inference that the applicant was engaged or dismissed pursuant to any of the provisions of s. 82 of the Public Service Act 1922.

The question as to whether his employment was "in a permanent capacity" therefore turns on the meaning of that phrase in the context of the Act. "Permanent" is defined in the Shorter Oxford Dictionary as:

"Lasting or designed to last indefinitely

without change; enduring; persistent;

opposite to temporary."

"Temporary" is defined as:

"Lasting for a limited time; existing or

valid for a time only; transient; made to

supply a passing need."

If there is no reference to the period of time for which an employment by

the Crown is to last the inference is that it will last indefinitely but not of course, forever. The right of the Crown or for that matter any employer, to dismiss an employee without cause is well understood in the community subject of course to reasonable notice and statutory rights, if any. But when an employment is described as permanent it is generally either a reference to the normal retiring age, intervening sickness or misconduct excepted; or at least to the expectation of the employee that unless exceptional circumstances supervene he or she may anticipate continuous employment for an indefinite time.

If on the other hand an employment is described as temporary it is a reference to some specific period of time or some future event which will obviously result in the termination of the contract. For example, a research assistant engaged to assist a Royal Commission would obviously treat his employment as being bound to conclude when the Commission delivered its report.

It was argued that an appointment by a Minister to work for Ministers necessarily involved an understanding that the employment would almost certainly cease if and when the political party which those Ministers represented ceased to control Government. That argument might have more force in relation to an appointment to serve on the personal staff of a particular Minister.

The GIU was a group of people who had no statutory identity or recognition but who were funded by Parliament to meet the contractual obligations of a Minister who had lawfully engaged them to perform services for the Crown.

Nobody could confidently predict whether a new Government, even if the same political party would retain or dispense with the services of particular persons engaged as part of the GIU. It could not therefore be confidently asserted that the engagement was for a limited or specified time.

The view that the employment was intended to last indefinitely is arguably supported by two other considerations in this particular case. First, the advertisement said that "conditions would be similar to those applying to Commonwealth Public Service". Second, the Minister for Administrative Services said that the applicant would be able to carry over all benefits under the Superannuation Scheme which at that time included the benefit of being deemed to be employed in a permanent capacity for the purposes of the Act.

However both these arguments are equivocal because the Commonwealth Public Service includes permanent officers and temporary employees. Moreover the deeming referred to clearly ceased when the applicant resigned from the ABC.

We derive more benefit from a consideration of the philosophy of the 1976 Act. The only references we can find in the Act to the phrase "in a permanent capacity" apart from the definition of "permanent employee" is in the definition "temporary employee" and in ss. 3 (5) whereby persons on probation are included. The phrase "permanent employee" is only used in the definition of "eligible employee" sub-paragraph (b) and in s. 11 (1) (a).

The only references to a "temporary employee" other than in the definition of "eligible employee" (sub-paragraphs (e) and (f)) is in s. 11 and s. 58 (3). Section 11 contains detailed provisions enabling certain temporary employees to elect to be treated as eligible employees thus ensuring that they may enjoy the benefits of the scheme notwithstanding the lack of permanence in their terms of engagement.

The apparent reason for excluding temporary employees from the early retirement benefits conferred by division 2 of Part V is that by the very nature of the terms of a temporary engagement the position or office occupied by the employee will cease to exist either by abolition or otherwise when the contract has run its course. If it is a term of the contract that it will end on the happening of a certain event or at the expiration of a specific period the eligible employee cannot fairly be deemed to have retired involuntarily. But if there were no such term and the date for termination is entirely in the hands of the employer the employment must be regarded as "in a permanent capacity" regardless of the expectations or predictions of either party to the contract or any objective observer as to when termination is most likely to occur.

In summary because the Act goes a long way to enable persons who so desire to join the Superannuation Scheme, notwithstanding the uncertainties which may surround their employment, it is not unreasonable to construe ss. 58 (3) (d) as being intended to exclude from its deeming aspect only those persons who have affirmatively agreed to accept the cessation of their position upon the happening of a specified event or at the expiration of a specified time.

In this case the applicant made no such agreement. We therefore conclude that he was not a temporary employee for the purposes of ss. 58 (3) (e). We will return later to the question of whether his position or office ceased to exist by reason of its being abolished or otherwise.

The question of retrenchment necessarily depends on the facts surrounding termination of service in March 1983. So also does the remaining question to which we have just referred i.e. whether his position or office was abolished or ceased to exist for some other reason.

The letter of termination of services referred to in paragraph 6 said:

"As a consequence of the election held on

5 March 1983 I am writing to inform you

that your services are no longer required

as from today's date.

You will receive two weeks pay in lieu

of notice."

It was signed by a delegate of the Secretary of the Department of The Special Minister of State.

A similar letter was sent on the same day to all other occupants of positions on the GIU with the exception of support staff. The positions were not formally abolished because they were never created. They simply remained unfilled until Government decided at a later date to establish a group called The National Media Liaison Service.

Following the election there was a change of Government and a new Ministry was sworn in on 11 March 1983. On the same day all ministerial staff received two weeks notice of the termination of their services. (T4/57)

The early retirement benefits provided by s. 59 are available to persons who are deemed to have retired voluntarily, within the meaning of s. 58 (2) or involuntarily, within the meaning of s. 58 (3) provided they did not cease to be eligible by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity or their period of contributory service was not less than one year. (ss. 58 (4))

Voluntary retirement within the meaning of s. 58 (2) refers only to persons who by reason of regulations are employed in particular occupations where 55 is prescribed as the minimum age for retirement. The benefits of early retirement are available to such persons if they choose to retire between 55 and 60.

It is now convenient to state the provisions of s. 58 (3):

"Where, before a person who is an eligible

employee attains the age of 60 years, he

ceases to be an eligible employee by reason

that-

(a) if his minimum retiring age is less than 60

years and he has attained that minimum

retiring age - he is retired, otherwise than

at his own request;

(b) he is retrenched, he is retired from his

employment under section 19 of the

Commonwealth Employees (Redeployment and

Retirement) Act 1979 or he is retired from

his employment, otherwise than under that Act,

on a ground similar to the ground on which

persons might have been retired under section

20 of the Public Service Act 1922 immediately

before the date fixed under sub-section 2 (2)

of the Commonwealth Employees (Redeployment

and Retirement) Act 1979;

(c) in the case of the holder of a statutory

office - the term of his appointment to that

office has expired and notwithstanding that

he was eligible for re-appointment to that

office and desired to be so re-appointed, he

was not re-appointed;

(d) except in the case of a temporary employee -

his position or office ceases to exist,

whether by reason of its being abolished or

otherwise: or

(e) he is retired under section 18 of the

Commonwealth Employees (Redeployment and

Retirement) Act 1979 or his employment or

appointment is terminated otherwise than under

that Act on a ground similar to a ground that

is specified in that section,

the person shall, for the purposes of this Act, be deemed to have retired involuntarily."

Section 19 of the 1979 Act referred to in Sub-paragraph (b) requires the Permanent Head of a Department in the Australian Public Service to retire certain persons if he is unable to employ them on duties he considers they are able to perform and can reasonably be required to perform. The persons who fall into that class must first be the subjects of efforts to redeploy them elsewhere as described.

Section 20 of the Public Service Act provided for the transfer of an officer in the Service to another office of equal or lower classification if available or in default the Board was empowered to retire him or her from the Service. The occasion for such a transfer was a finding that there was in a particular area a greater number of offices than was necessary for the efficient working of a Department or Branch.

Section 18 of the 1979 Act provides for the retirement by the Governor General of First Division Officers who are inefficient, incompetent or incapable of performing their duties.

It will be seen that all the reasons for retirement set out in ss. 58 (3) require some positive action by the employer and all relate without exception to a decision by the employer, for his reasons, to retire the eligible employee before he is 60 years of age.

In all cases except (a) without request after attaining 55 years of age (b) retrenchment and (d) cessation of position or office the circumstances leading to the involuntary retirement are spelt out with particularity.

It becomes necessary to construe the word "retrenched" in the context of this section, this Division and this Act. It was argued for the respondent that the natural meaning of the word imports some curtailing of expenses, some economic consideration.

Both counsel referred to the judgement of Lockhart J. in Commissioner for Superannuation v Bayley [1979] FCA 76; 28 ALR 293 in which the provision now under consideration was in question (as it stood before the amendments effected by the 1979 Act came into operation). At page 310 the learned judge said:

"I turn first to the question whether the respondent

was retrenched. The word "retrench" has received

little judicial consideration. In a decision of the

Industrial Commission of New South Wales in the

"Preference of Employment Case" reported in the

Law Book Company's Industrial Arbitration Service,

Current Review June 1977 at para Y76 their Honours

said at p 230: "Finally there are limitations in

connection with retrenchment flowing from the

connotation of that term. That was a matter in

dispute. For the employers it was said that

'retrenchment' meant reducing the number of persons

employed for a reason such as slackness of trade and

that the word was not apt to include termination

of employment by way of dismissal for misconduct

or by way of retirement at retiring age or because

of an employee becoming permanently incapacitated

for work, but it was argued on behalf of the Labor

Council that 'retrenchment' referred to termination

of employment by an employer for any reason. In our

opinion 'retrenchment' connotes cutting down or

reducing for some cause pertaining to the employer's

business or enterprise and is not a term apt to deal

with termination of employment for reasons peculiar

to an employee. We think that the meaning contended

for by the employers is the correct one and that,

accordingly the section limits the absolute preference

which is to be given on termination of employment to

termination by way of retrenchment in that sense."

In the matter of Federated Miscellaneous Workers'

Union v Adelaide Milk Supplier Co-operative Ltd, a

decision of the Commission in Appeal Session in South

Australia, reported in CCH Australian Industrial Law

Review, vol 21 No 3 (7 February 1979) a comparison

was made between the words "retrenchment" and

"redundancy".

The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary defines the verb

"retrench", so far as relevant, as:-

"2. To cut off, 3. To cut down, reduce,

diminish, esp to curtail (one's expenses)

by the exercise of economy 1625. To do

away with (an item of expense) 1647."

Webster's New World Dictionary of the American

Language defines "retrench", in its transitive

sense, as:-

"1. To cut down or reduce (esp expenses);

curtail. 2. To cut off or out;"

Whether "retrenchment" connotes termination of

employment by an employer for any reason or for some

reason pertaining to the employer's business and not

for reasons peculiar to a particular employee, it is

not necessary to decide. In my opinion, whatever else

the word may mean, it is not apposite to describe the

termination of the respondent's employment by an Act

of Parliament, namely the 1977 Act, which brought to

an end the existence of the employer and the

relationship

employer and employee.

Accordingly, in my opinion, the respondent was not

retrenched".

Smithers J. agreed with those reasons and Keely J. did not find it necessary to express an opinion on the question of retrenchment. Neither did this Tribunal in the decision then on appeal.

Counsel also referred to the following passage from the South Australian Industrial Commission decision cited by Lockhart J;

"This (i.e. redundancy) is to be contrasted with

the concept of 'retrenchment' which, in our view,

is far less definitive and potentially embraces

a wide range of bases of dismissals, again, through

no fault of the employee which can, for example,

comprehend short term staff adjustments due to an

ebb and flow of consumer demand, a rationalization

and adjustment of work processes of longer term

impact, and a host of other circumstances".

The question of the meaning of retrenchment in s. 58 (3) is obviously still

an open one. If it had the restricted meaning contended for by the respondent, and indeed there is much to be said for the view that it is in that sense that the word is most commonly used today, it would be necessary for the Commissioner of Superannuation (and now this Tribunal) in each case to examine the employer's reasons for a decision to retrench. Quite apart from the fact that such decisions are frequently made for composite reasons we do not believe that Parliament intended such an enquiry. The plain purpose of the sub-section is to define as exhaustively as possible the circumstances in which limited early retirement benefits will be available to eligible employees whose retirement is occasioned entirely by the decision of the employer.

Section 79 effectively deals with the unusual case of a person wilfully causing his own incapacity for the purpose of obtaining an invalidity benefit and section 80 picks up all cases which do not qualify for benefit under other Divisions. Thus a person dismissed for misconduct obviously qualifies for no more than the return of his accumulated contributions. The same fate awaits any person resigning of his own volition prior to his minimum retiring age.

The object of the Act as expressed in its long title is

"to make provision for and in relation to an

occupational Superannuation Scheme for persons

employed by the Commonwealth and for certain

other purposes."

If there were any doubt about the proper construction of the term

"retrenched" it would be appropriate to apply s. 15AA (1) of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 by preferring a construction that would promote the purpose or object underlying the Act. In our opinion the underlying object of the Act, in addition to that expressed in the long title, is to ensure that eligible employees whose services are terminated otherwise than by their own act, or agreement, are adequately compensated for their service. A return of contributions without interest is more by way of penalty or punishment than a just reward. A lump sum calculated in the manner provided by ss. 62 (2) is a just recompense for any contributory service which exceeds one year.

Accordingly we are of the opinion that the applicant was retrenched and is therefore deemed to have retired involuntarily. By reason of his election pursuant to s. 62 (1) he is entitled to a lump sum benefit calculated in accordance with ss. 62 (2).

It is now necessary to refer briefly to an incidental matter. No reference was made during the hearing to the fact that the application which the applicant made on 20 April 1983 deleted the words:

"apply for payment of a lump sum benefit"

leaving only the words:

"apply for a refund of accumulated

contributions".

However by letter of 22 March 1983 the applicant had made it clear to the

author of the letter terminating his services that he did not accept that his severance entitlements were as stated therein. Furthermore on 6 June 1983 he informed the respondent's office that he was banking the cheque for his refund of contributions without prejudice to the explanation he was seeking as to his rights.

The correspondence which followed between the applicant's solicitors and the respondent made it clear that the applicant was claiming three and a half times his accumulated basic contributions. The respondent has never raised any question about election within the period specified in ss. 62 (1) and indeed when he confirmed the original decision of 19 August 1983 the respondent made no reference to the question of due notice.

The manner in which the respondent has dealt with the application and these proceedings generally satisfies us that he has very properly exercised his discretion under ss. 157 (1) of the Act to recognise the election as if it had been made within the period allowed (i.e. 3 months). If he has not expressly directed his mind to the question this Tribunal is empowered to exercise the same powers as the respondent and to make the decision which in its view is the right decision to be made.

Accordingly if it is necessary for us so to do we direct that the applicant's election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly.

If we are in error as to the meaning of 'retrenched' and also in our conclusion that the applicant was not "a temporary employee", it becomes necessary to return to the question raised under ss. 58 (3) (d) namely, "did his position or office cease to exist, whether by reason of its being abolished or otherwise?"

The applicant never held any office because we take the view that an office must be created by the act of some duly authorized person. Did he then hold "a position"? The position of "State Co-ordination Officer for the Commonwealth Government in Tasmania" was advertised as "a post". Persons were invited to submit applications. The successful applicant obviously filled that post or occupied that position.

But when the applicant was advised that his services were terminated nothing was said as to whether the position had ceased to exist. All we know of that is that a government decision was to be made in due course. Therefore accepting that assertion, as we do, it is difficult to say that the position the applicant held "had ceased to exist". We are not satisfied that it had at the relevant time.

However, for the reasons stated in paragraph 56, the decision of the respondent is set aside and the matter remitted to him for calculation and payment of the amount due to the applicant pursuant to s. 62 (2) of the Act having regard to the amount he has already received by way of refund of contributions. There will be liberty to apply generally.
