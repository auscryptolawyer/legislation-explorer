NT93/147-148 and Commissioner of Taxation [1995] AATA 185; 95 ATC 353; (1995) 31 ATR 1042 (7 July 1995)

Last Updated: 12 February 2009



Administrative Appeals Tribunal





DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



TAXATION - sales tax - additional tax - whether additional tax appropriately remitted - discretion - whether “deliberate statement” - rebate allowance paid to retailers - purpose of rebate



PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE - taxation - jurisdiction - whether valid assessment - definition of assessment - assessment not document but process - whether letter evidence of process of assessment



Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 1) 1930 ss 3, 25, 25AA, 38A, 45

Taxation Administration Act 1953 ss 14ZL, 14ZQ, 14ZZA

Sales Tax Procedure Act 1934 s 12B



The King v The Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation for South Australia ex parte Hooper [1926] HCA 3; (1926) 37 CLR 368



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

NO s NT93/147 and NT93/148

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal : Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)



Date : 7 July 1995



Place : Sydney



Decision : 1 The Tribunal has no jurisdiction in matter NO NT93/147. The application is therefore dismissed.



2 The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review in matter NO NT93/148 and remits the matter to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the additional tax be remitted to a level of 5 per cent.



.................................................... BJ McMahon

Deputy President





ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

NOS NT93/147 and NT93/148

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re :

Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION



7 July 1995 Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)



  1. The applicant is an importer and wholesaler of electronic appliances. These are sold through a variety of retail outlets. In the relevant period, certain allowances called PPA rebates were given to retailers thus reducing the net amount payable on the sale of these goods. It was alleged by the respondent that there was an agreement, arrangement or understanding that rebates of this nature would be used by the retailers for promotional purposes. This view was formed following an audit, which involved interviews with selected members of the applicant’s staff and with various retailers.
  2. As a result of this audit, the Commissioner’s officers concluded that additional moneys were payable. On 19 March 1992 a letter was sent to the public officer of the applicant which, omitting formal parts, was in the following terms —

“SALES TAX : TAX CREDITS

[Applicant’s] trading arrangements with various retailers have been reviewed in the course of a recent sales tax audit. Rebates paid during the period 1 May 1990 to 30 November 1991 were examined. Pursuant to that review it is advised that sales tax credits claimed in monthly sales tax returns in respect of PPA rebates paid to the following customers are not allowable :



Customer Disallowed Tax Credits



[Retailer NO 1] $ 14 671.00

[Retailer NO 2] $ 64 572.00

[Retailer NO 3] $ 101 846.00

[Retailer NO 4] $ 12 516.00

[Retailer NO 5] $ 57 197.00

[Retailer NO 6] $ 44 761.00

[Retailer NO 7] $ 3 988.00

_______________

$ 299 551.00



Accordingly, your company has remitted in monthly sales tax returns, a lesser amount of sales tax than is required by Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 6). Sales Tax of $299 551.00 is due and payable and it is requested that your company remit this amount to this office.

Where tax remains unpaid after the time when it became due and payable Section 29(1) of Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 1) provides that additional tax at the rate of 20% per annum is payable. Section 12(1) of Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 6) provides that section 29 shall apply mutatis mutandis in relation to the imposition of tax under Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 6).

Accordingly you are required to remit additional tax of $ 51 778.00 pursuant to Section 12(1) of Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 6).”



  1. On 15 May 1992, the applicant wrote to the respondent in terms which omitting formal parts were as follows —

“Sales Tax

[Applicant] Pty Ltd

2 166 032

Under s.40(1) of Sales Tax Assessment Act (“STAA”) NO 1, as applied by s.12(1) of STAA NO 6 to STAA NO 6, [Applicant] objects to your assessment of it of 19 March 1992, copy attached. [Applicant] claims that the amounts assessed should be reduced to nil by

  • allowing credits of $299, 551 in respect of PPA rebates and
  • remitting the $51,778 additional tax both referred to in your assessment.

The grounds on which this objection is made are

In Respect of the Credits in respect of PPA Rebates

These credits are in respect of amounts paid or credited by [Applicant] to retailers in respect of their wholesale purchases from [Applicant] of taxable goods. The amounts paid or credited are deductions from the goods amounts invoiced by [Applicant] to these retailers to determine the amount for which the goods (in respect of which the rebates relate) are sold by [Applicant] to the retailers, within s.18(1)(a) of STAA NO 1 as applied by s.12(1) of STAA NO 6 to STAA NO 6.



In particular the amounts paid or credited are



  • related to particular goods sold by [Applicant] to the retailers
  • paid or credited by [Applicant] only on the condition that the goods to which they relate are bought by the retailer from [Applicant]. If those goods are not bought then the rebates don’t arise.
  • not paid or credited for any other purpose. In particular, but without limiting, they are not paid or credited on condition nor in expectation that advertising, promotion or any other activity or service would be performed by the retailers for the retailers or for [Applicant].
  • calculated and related
    • − as a certain amount per unit of the goods sold and/or
    • − as a proportion of the total invoiced amounts or the goods invoiced amount and/or
    • − to particular periods of buying of the goods from [Applicant] by the retailers and/or
    • − to particular volumes of goods bought from [Applicant] by the retailers.

The rebates are agreed with the retailers at particular points of time, sometimes at the beginning of a sales year, otherwise at other particular points of time. Some rebates are paid or credited all year related to sales in that year, other rebates being paid and related to shorter periods of time or being related to single or limited or one off orders. Sometimes the rebates are paid or credited concurrently in time only with particular lower than otherwise goods amounts invoiced, with the rebate being paid or credited after the goods have been invoiced. The reason why the rebate is not used to lower the goods invoiced amount is to provide an incentive to the retailer to make the volume of purchases which [Applicant] expects the retailer to make as a condition to the retailer receiving the rebate from [Applicant].

In respect of the $51,778 Additional Tax

s.29(1) of STAA NO 1 as applied by s.12(1) of STAA NO 6 to STAA NO 6 is not attracted because there is no tax remaining unpaid after the time when it became due and payable or would, but for s.28, have been due and payable because the lower amount for which the goods were sold (lower, that is than they would have been otherwise without the rebate, and in respect of which lower tax was consequently paid by [Applicant] to you) is the correct amount for which the goods were sold, for the reasons as stated above.

Nor is there attracted any other sales tax provision to impose additional or penalty tax, to these facts.”

  1. On 27 May 1993, the respondent wrote to the applicant. The opening paragraph of the letter is in these terms —

“Your objection dated 15 May 1992 against the assessment of 19 March 1992 in respect of the disallowance of PPA rebates totalling $299,551 and the imposition of additional tax of $51,778 has been disallowed...”

  1. The letter then went on to assert that the applicant had a right of appeal to this Tribunal under s 14ZZ of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 and forwarded details of the appeal procedures that were said to be available to the applicant.
  2. A dispute has arisen whether the above letter of 19 March 1992 constitutes an assessment. If it does not, then the letter of 15 May 1992 would not constitute an objection, the disallowance of which would be reviewable by this Tribunal.
  3. Jurisdiction is conferred by ss 14ZL, 14ZY and 14ZZA of the Taxation Administration Act. This Tribunal is empowered by those sections to review a reviewable objection decision. That term is defined by s 14ZQ to mean an objection decision that is not an ineligible income tax remission decision or an ineligible sales tax remission decision. There is no question that the letter of 19 March falls within either of these categories. An objection decision depends upon whether a taxation objection has been taken as defined in s 14ZL which Part IVC of the Act applies. Section 38A(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 1) 1930 provides —

“A taxpayer who is dissatisfied with an assessment made in relation to the taxpayer may object instead in the manner set out in Part IVC of the Taxation Administration Act 1953.”

  1. Clearly there must be an assessment before there can be an objection followed by an objection decision. The question to be determined, therefore, is whether the letter of 19 March constitutes notice of an assessment.
  2. Assessment is defined in s 3(1) of the NO 1 Act to mean the ascertainment of the sale value of goods and of the sales tax payable on that sale value, or the ascertainment of additional tax payable under Part VIII. An assessment is not a document. It is a process of ratiocination. Was the letter of 19 March evidence that such a process had taken place?
  3. In The King v The Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation for South Australia ex parte Hooper [1926] HCA 3; (1926) 37 CLR 368 at 373, Isaacs J referred to an assessment as “an official act or operation; it is the Commissioner’s ascertainment, on consideration of all relevant circumstances, including sometimes his own opinion, of the amount of tax chargeable to a given taxpayer”.
  4. In my view the letter is not evidence of the official act or operation of the Commissioner whereby an assessment was made. The letter is incapable of being construed as a notice of completion of his ascertainment of the amount. It does not evidence any ascertainment of sale value, nor of sales tax payable thereon, nor does it evidence an ascertainment of additional tax payable under Part VIII.
  5. Assessments may be issued under s 25 of the NO 1 Act, although the making of assessments is optional unless the Commissioner alters the sale value of any goods under sub-sections 18(3A) or (4), or 18A(5) or (6). No such alteration of sale value is alleged in the present circumstances.
  6. The applicant could have requested the respondent to make a special assessment under s 25AA. This section gives the taxpayer a right to request the Commissioner to make an assessment in respect of a specified act done or a specified transaction or operation effected by the taxpayer. The learned authors of “Australian Sales Tax Law and Practice”, edited by Hill J and Economides point out at paragraph 1418—

“On a practical level s 25AA assessments are used in order to crystallise existing disputes with the Commissioner and after a taxpayer has determined to have the dispute resolved by the formal objection and appeal process. Since no assessment is normally issued following lodgment of a sales tax return, the special assessment mechanism fulfills the important function of invoking the “dispute resolution” process.”

  1. No such step was taken by the applicant. The applicant had available to it the services of a legally qualified company secretary. It also consulted a well qualified sales tax adviser. If it did not pursue the s 25AA procedure, it can only have been as a result of what it saw as an informed choice.
  2. The thought processes of the 2 officers of the respondent concerned with this matter are set out in exhibit 2. The deliberations of the field auditor do not amount to an ascertainment of sale value. The observations of his supervisor, the large case audit manager, indicate that the officer concerned envisaged the demand to be made upon the applicant as a “s 12B letter”. In my view, that is in fact what it is.
  3. The letter of 19 March is properly to be characterised as a notice in writing specifying an amount of tax payable by the applicant for the purposes of s 12B of the Sales Tax Procedure Act 1934. It does not on its face purport to be an notice of assessment. Although the respondent referred to it in dealing with the applicant’s “objection” as an assessment, this in no way alters the characterisation of the processes and the letter of 19 March which followed those processes. There can be no question of estoppel against the Commissioner. The respondent is bound to administer the legislation in its own terms and there is no estoppel which would be available to displace that obligation.
  4. Accordingly, in my view, this Tribunal is not empowered to review the decision conveyed in the above letter of 19 March. Proceedings NO NT93/147 will therefore be dismissed for want of jurisdiction.
  5. A second letter dated 19 March 1992 was sent by the respondent to the applicant in terms which, omitting formal parts, are as follows —

“SALES TAX : ASSESSMENT OF ADDITIONAL TAX

Section 12(1) of Sales Tax Assessment (NO 6) provides that Part VIII of Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 1) shall mutatis mutandis apply in relation to the imposition, assessment and collection of the tax chargeable under Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 6).

Your company has overclaimed sales tax credits in respect of PPA rebates. A letter addressing this topic is enclosed with this assessment. It has been decided to remit the additional tax automatically imposed under Section 12(1) of Sales Tax Assessment Act (NO 6) in respect of the making of false or misleading statements (e.g. the overclaiming of sales tax credits) to the following amount, $ 179,730.00

Your company is hereby requested to remit additional tax of $179, 730.00. This amount is now due and payable.”

  1. On 15 May 1992, the applicant wrote to the respondent in terms which omitting formal parts are as follows —

“Sales Tax

[Applicant] Pty Ltd

2 166 032



Under s.40(1) of Sales Tax Assessment Act (“STAA”) NO 1, as applied by s.12(1) of STAA NO 6 to STAA NO 6, [Applicant] objects to your assessment of it to additional tax of $179,730.00. [Applicant] claims that this should be reduced by $179,730.00 to nil.



The grounds on which this objection is made are that s.42(2) of STAA NO 1 (as applied by s.12(1) of STAA NO 6 to STAA NO 6) is not attracted to the facts because



− [Applicant] has not made a statement to a tax officer or any other person that is false or misleading in connection with the operation of a sales tax or

− [Applicant] has not omitted from a statement made to a tax officer or any other person for a purpose in connection with the operation of a sales tax law any matter or thing without which the statement is misleading in a material particular or

− there is no tax properly payable by (Applicant] exceeding the tax that would have been paid by [Applicant] if [Applicant] was assessed or determined on the basis that the statement were not false or misleading (which is not admitted) as the case may be.

In particular, referring to [Applicant’s] objection of 15 May 1992, the $179,730 additional tax is only supported if the $299,551 of rebates are not to be deducted from goods invoice values in determining the amount for which the goods to which they relate are sold. Accordingly the conditions for attracting s.45(2) of STAA NO 1 (as applied by s.12(1) of STAA NO 6 to STAA NO 6) are not attracted here.

  1. That objection was also disallowed and an application was brought in matter NO NT93/148 to have this Tribunal review that decision. The second letter of 19 March is clearly an assessment as it deals with the ascertainment of additional tax payable under Part VIII. There is no question therefore that this Tribunal has jurisdiction to review the second decision of 15 May 1992.
  2. Reduction of statutory additional tax to an appropriate level is principally based on discretionary factors. That discretion, in this case, is largely influenced by the evidence in NT93/147. The first question to be determined was whether an agreement, arrangement or understanding did in fact exist between the applicant and the 7 retailers named in the first letter of 19 March to the effect that “PPA” rebates would be used by those retailers for the purpose of promoting the applicant’s goods. There was, of course, no contract written or oral, to that effect. If such a state of affairs obtained, then it would be said by the respondent that such allowances would not be accepted as reducing the sale value of goods. The respondent’s policy was set out in Sales Tax Ruling ST 2039 which was operative from 15 June 1983. Because of perceived obscurities in the terms of that ruling, it was replaced by ST 2458 which was promulgated on 25 January 1990, but was given a retrospective date of effect to 15 July 1983. Paragraph 26 of that ruling makes it clear that the Commissioner’s opinion was that allowances or rebates given on the above understanding would be ineligible trade incentive payments.
  3. Before turning to the evidence given by officers of the applicant and of the respondent, I will deal with the considerable evidence given by representatives of the various retailers. Although this evidence was extensive, a pattern emerged, which allows one to express in general terms the perceptions held by the retailers of the purpose and the use to which such rebates could be put. With one notable exception, all the retailers denied that there was any agreement, arrangement or understanding as alleged. I will give some examples.
  4. The view of retailer NO 5 was that it was not interested in the way in which any such allowance was to be used. It was concerned only with the net price at which it could obtain the goods and which would enable it to charge competitive sale prices at the retail level. In that company’s view it was entitled to use the rebate for anything it chose. It had a number of allowances as part of the applicant’s pricing structure. All these allowances went into a pool which could be used for any purpose considered acceptable to the retail company. Nevertheless, it was agreed that because retailer NO 5 had a mutual help relationship with the applicant, it would promote the applicant’s product and would advertise from time to time. There were some allowances referred to as “over and above allowances” but these were for specific advertising purposes and were not part of the PPA rebates.
  5. An officer of retailer NO 7 had worked for that company all his working life. He was not even aware what the initials PPA stood for. In fact most of the retailers called had different understandings of the meaning of the initials. As far as this officer was concerned, he was interested only in rebates, however described, which gave him an acceptable net cost. Although some advertising was paid for out of some moneys that might be referrable to the allowances, there was no understanding to this effect.
  6. The general manager of retailer NO 1 also denied any knowledge of the meaning of PPA. So far as he was concerned it was simply part of the company’s discount package. He agreed that it was in return for his company’s support of the applicant, but he thought that this support was principally achieved by selling the applicant’s goods. He agreed that from time to time his company carried advertising of the applicant’s goods, but from his point of view, the allowance simply offset his company’s total advertising budget. Certain claim forms relating to advertising costs which appear in the s 37 documents, and which apparently concerned investigating auditor, were put to him apparently for the first time. His evidence (which was not shaken in cross-examination) was that such claim forms were written internally for the company’s own records and would be claimed on the manufacturer only if the allowance was not paid. In other words, the claims would be for an “over and above” advertising price if a sufficient PPA allowance was not made available. Contrary to the assumption made by the auditor, these claim forms possibly did not even leave the premises of retailer NO 1. The general manager denied that his company was required to account for advertising. He certainly agreed that the applicant would like his company to advertise. This, however, does not take the matter any further.
  7. A former managing director of retailer NO 6 gave similar evidence. In his view many allowances were given by different suppliers under different names. So far as he was concerned, unless allowances were specifically qualified, they were simply to assist the retailer to achieve a gross margin. Like others who gave evidence before him, he was interested only in “the bottom line”. He said that there were no specific obligations recognised by his company consequent upon these allowances. The only occasions upon which he reported specific advertising was when moneys had been allocated on the over and above system. Evidence from all other retailers was to the same effect with the exception of evidence given on behalf of retailer NO 2.
  8. That evidence was given by a person who was its buying or merchandise controller during the relevant periods. Whilst he agreed that at the regular annual meetings on pricing that were held with the applicant, management would instruct their buyers to obtain the best bottom line price and whilst he agreed that buyers had a margin of profit to achieve, and whilst he agreed that all the rebates went towards the bottom line, nevertheless he insisted that his understanding was that his company had to spend the whole of the 4 per cent PPA allowance on advertising and to give evidence of that expenditure. Whilst he agreed that all allowances from all major suppliers went into a basket of money, he insisted that the particular allowances were spent only on advertising the goods of the supplier who had made the allowance available.
  9. Although the PPA allowance was automatic in a sense that it was made available each month, whether or not there was any claim with or without supporting documents and advertising, nevertheless the witness insisted that his company had to show proof of advertising. This, he said, was accompanied by credit claims in accordance with an accrual system. Sometimes he himself issued the credit claims. He said the procedure was not discussed at meetings as it had been going on for some 15 years. He agreed however, that once the claims left him they went to a financial controller, and he was not aware whether the claims would be kept merely for internal information or whether they were sent to the applicant.
  10. In his view, if his company did not carry out its part of the bargain, then the applicant could discipline his company in a number of ways without necessarily withdrawing the allowance. For example, it could give its best new products to a competitor. He agreed that this type of discipline was never threatened by the applicant.
  11. The evidence of this witness was not shaken in cross-examination, or even in chief by counsel for the applicant who called the witness. The evidence, however, is so much at variance with all the other evidence of the retailers that I can only assume on what has been put before me, that the procedures in relation to retailer NO 2 were different from those that applied to all the other retailers.
  12. Exhibit 2, which is a report by the respondent’s auditor to his supervisor, sets out the basis upon which he considered that the additional tax should be assessed. It was said that the claiming of credits represented a false or misleading statement for the purposes of s 45(2). This was because of the contradiction which the auditor saw between some statements made by officers of the applicant and various documents examined in the course of the audit. I am not satisfied that such contradictions have been demonstrated and, in particular, I am not satisfied that the meaning and ramification of various documents examined, were sufficiently explored to support the auditor’s recommendation. The auditor on many occasions was content to rely on documents without seeking any explanation of them from the relevant parties.
  13. The auditor also relied upon what he saw as collusion between the applicant and certain of the retailers. In view of the evidence which I have heard, this is an allegation that simply can not be supported.
  14. The evidence of the auditor was less than satisfactory. It was given in an argumentative way, seeking to support his view of people and events, rather than in the objective way one would consider appropriate in the circumstances. He was principally concerned with what appeared in a document originating from a senior officer of the applicant. In that document, he saw evidence of an intention to deceive. Nevertheless, he did not take the trouble to interview that officer, believing merely that he would probably lie to the auditor. Notes that he took of an interview in Sydney which formed part of the file and which formed part of the material considered by his supervisor in supporting the assessment of additional tax, turned out mostly to be his impression rather than the record of a conversation. He gave every indication that he had made up his mind prior to the completion of the audit and was simply looking for material to support his preconceptions.
  15. An interview with an officer of the applicant in Perth was carried out by the auditor. He took a second officer with him to the applicant’s premises to act as witness. The second officer had only the haziest recollection of the meeting. He wrote notes independently of the auditor and they are attached to his statement. He said that he could not recall seeing the auditor writing notes while he was talking. Although the second officer’s notes were sketchy, he thought he had written down the answers. However he could not recall any questions. He certainly did not write any of them in his notes. Reading the notes he agreed it was possible that some of the words spoken were by the auditor. He also agreed that his notes might have been a summary of the key issues and were conclusions as to what the applicant’s officer was saying.
  16. There are discrepancies between the statement of the auditor and those of the second officer, which tend to indicate that the auditor’s statement was written, perhaps unconsciously, with a view to confirming the answers he had expected to receive, rather than with a view to recording the answers that were actually given.
  17. To the extent that the process of assessment of additional tax depended upon reports of interviews of these 2 officers of the Commissioner, it depended upon unreliable material. To the extent that it depended upon documents, it appears to me that many of the documents were not sufficiently investigated and that this affected the soundness of the decision to make the assessment.
  18. The evidence of various employees of the applicant did not support any of the allegations of blameworthy conduct, implicit in the higher assessment of additional tax. The Commissioner’s guidelines for the remission of additional tax are set out in ST (NS) 5 dated 23 January 1992. Chapter 5 of that ruling sets out the principles which the Commissioner applies in remitting the statutory additional tax of 200 per cent, pursuant to s 45. The additional tax in the present case was remitted down to a level of 60 per cent. This is the top of the range to be applied in the worst case of culpability. In the table set out in paragraph 5.11, the range of 45 to 60 per cent is suggested for “deliberate statement”. This is explained in the ruling in these words : “the taxpayer’s statement was deliberately made without belief in its truth or was intended to mislead in the face of a known liability or obligation — this would be the most highly penalised category of culpability”.
  19. In my view, it is quite inappropriate to apply this characterisation to the applicant’s circumstances. I accept the evidence of the officers of the applicant that whatever the situation was in the past, during the relevant period there was no agreement, arrangement or understanding with retailers that was contrary to the material represented by the applicant to the respondent, except in the case of one retailer. In that case, I can only conclude that there was some failure of communication between the applicant and that retailer and that in asserting the absence of an agreement, arrangement or understanding, the applicant committed an inadvertent error.
  20. This term is explained in the ruling : “The taxpayer’s statement, even though false or misleading, has occurred through an inadvertent error or honest mistake, and there was no intention to deceive, producing a result which the taxpayer could not reasonably be expected to have recognised as incorrect”.
  21. The range of suggested penalty for a false or misleading statement which results from an inadvertent error is from nought to 10 per cent. In my view, a fair penalty would be 5 per cent, in the present circumstances.
  22. The decision in matter NO NT93/148 is therefore set aside and the matter is remitted to the respondent with a direction that the additional tax be remitted to a level of 5 per cent.
