ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Applicant

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Date 2 October 1998

Place Brisbane

Decision The Tribunal decides that:

(a) the objection decision under review is set aside and the objection allowed;

(b) a deduction of $282.00 is to be allowed; and

(c) these proceedings have terminated in a manner favourable to the applicant.



Decision No 13342 (Sgd) K L Beddoe

(Senior Member)



CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX : Allowable Deductions - travel expenses - incurred by soldier in travel between barracks and sports ground for service sports day



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s 51(1)



Fletcher and Ors v. FC of T 91 ATC 4950

Mansfield v. FC of T 96 ATC 4001

FC of T v. Cooper 91 ATC 4396

Re Lake and Department of Defence (AAT Decision No 8592 - 17 March 1993)

Case 48/93 93 ATC 520



REASONS FOR DECISION

2 October 1998 Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)



  1. The applicant seeks review of an objection decision whereby the respondent refused a claim for motor vehicle expenses to be allowed as a deduction under s 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”).
  2. The deduction sought amounts to $282.00. In making the adjustment to the assessed taxable income the respondent gave the following explanation (T8):
“$282 motor vehicle expenses for travelling to and from sporting activities. Australian Defence Force Taxation Ruling TR 95/17 paragraph 127, states that expenses incurred by an ADF member, except when participating as an official ADF representative, are not allowable as there is no direct relationship between the income-earning activity and the expense. Participation in any particular sporting activity is considered to be a personal choice and the activity private in nature notwithstanding that it is a requirement of employment.”


A similar explanation was given in the reasons for the objection decision (T12).

  1. This matter has been heard on the papers at the request of the parties. The Tribunal has before it the documents lodged pursuant to s 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, a statement of agreed facts dated 2 April 1998 with attachments, written submissions on behalf of the applicant and written submissions on behalf of the respondent.
  2. I make the following findings of fact based on the statement of agreed facts and the other documents before the Tribunal:
    • (a) The applicant is an infantry soldier who during the year of income ended 30 June 1995 was employed in 2/4 Battalion, Royal Australian Regiment (“2 RAR”) as part of the Operational Deployment Force stationed at Lavarack Barracks, Townsville.
    • (b) Members of the Operational Deployment Force are not formally required to meet any higher fitness standard than other infantry battalions. However, given that its members are available for short notice active deployment, these standards are more rigorously pursued.
    • (c) “Sport is played in 2 RAR to enhance fitness, develop team-work and foster esprit-de-corps. Sport is an integral part of unit training and due emphasis must be placed on maintaining a priority towards sport.” - 2 RAR Sports Directive 1997. (Attachment 2).
    • (d) In 1983 the Defence Force set out the Defence Force Policy on Sport in a detailed document clearly aimed at encouraging and promoting sport in the Defence Force. In particular it stated that a member undertaking sport within the terms of the instruction was to be considered to be participating in the course of his employment and reference was made to eligibility for compensation in terms of the Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act 1971. And further it said that the active participation by Defence Force personnel in sport is to be encouraged. Participation to be authorised by the Commanding Officer. (Attachment 1)
    • (e) It is standard practice within the Defence Force to set aside Thursday afternoons for sport, 2 RAR is no exception to this practice and its members are required to participate in organised team sports played during this time. Participation may take the form of playing, coaching, officiating or spectating and supporting. (Attachment 2)
    • (f) There are two types of regular Thursday afternoon sport:
      • (i) an “Inter-Unit” competition, involving teams made up of the full range of Defence Force personnel, from front line infantrymen to administrative and support personnel; and,
      • (ii) a “Unit Sport” competition, for those not required for (i).
    • (g) In 2 RAR, each soldier is required to try out for one of four summer and six winter “Inter-Unit” sports (Attachment 2). The choice of the particular sport with which one is involved, is left up to the individual defence force member. Outside of 2 RAR, the choice of sports may be wider (see for example the list of “Accredited Sports” contained in Attachment 2).
    • (h) Thursday afternoon sporting events are conducted either on the base or at a sporting venue located in the wider community. Exactly where, depends on the sport and venue availability.
    • (i) Where transportation to off base sporting events is not provided, it is the responsibility of the member to find their own way to the venue. If a private vehicle is used, the Australian Defence Forces considered that it is under no obligation to pay for this transport. (Attachment 4).
    • (j) Thursday afternoon sport is conducted during what are regarded as normal hours of work. Members participating in these sports are considered to be on duty and the venue a place of parade (albeit a less formal one in keeping with the nature of the activity). For members of 2 RAR at least, attendance is recorded and non-attendance may result in disciplinary action. (This disciplinary action may include: reprimand; loss of pay; diminished promotional prospects or restriction of privileges dependent upon rank.). (Attachment 2)
    • (k) Upon completion of the day’s events, all members of 2 RAR are required to report to the Battalion “Sports Parade”, from which they are formally dismissed (Attachment 2). They are regarded as being “on duty” until formally dismissed.
    • (l) The “Sports Parade” takes place at the enlisted men’s club located within Lavarack Barracks. Although it is an official parade, it has a “social flavour” that involves detailing the results of the day and highlighting noteworthy incidents from the day’s activities. It is accepted that alcohol may be consumed at some point in proceedings
    • (m) A soldier leaving from the base to attend a medical, dental or other appointment would also be considered ”on duty” while absent and would also be expected to return to be dismissed. (I have made this finding of fact because the parties agreed the fact but I do not understand its relevance to the issue to be decided.).
    • (n) During the year ended 30 June 1995, the applicant in this matter chose to play “Touch Football” as his Thursday afternoon sport. This choice was dictated by his love of the game and the fact that he was no longer up to the physical demands of rugby, which he played in his younger days.
    • (o) This particular competition was played off base, and on twenty occasions during the year in question the applicant used his private vehicle to attend. This led to him making a claim for a taxation deduction of $282 for the cost of this travel (20 weeks x 24 kms x 58.8¢). (T4 and T10)
    • (p) Attachment 4 is a copy of a letter dated 17 September 1997 by “Defence Personnel Executive” which I infer is part of the Defence Force or the Department of Defence. That letter responded to a requisition for information on behalf of the respondent. The letter of 17 September 1997 is apparently signed by a Lieutenant Colonel and I infer the Colonel was speaking within his authority when he wrote to the respondent. The advice included the following:
“Where a sporting event is to take place off area it is the responsibility of the member to be there as directed.”

(I understand “off area” to mean away from Lavarack Barracks.) (Attachment 4)


  1. Attached to the respondent's written submissions is a copy of a letter dated 7 May 1998 being a response by the Department of Defence to a further requisition for information on behalf of the respondent. That letter makes it clear that soldiers not participating in the Thursday afternoon sports would be “required to be going about their normal work”.
  2. The Defence Department letter of 7 May 1998 also includes the following:
    • 2 RAR has a compulsory policy of requiring members to return to the barracks on completion of fixtures, whether the fixtures are conducted on or off base.
    • Where a fixture is organised off base, military transport may or may not be organised, depending on the circumstances and availability. Similarly, it is conceivable that a member’s work commitments could be such that they may need or choose to travel separately by their own means, even where some military transport may have been arranged but the timings are not suitable. Alternatively, organised transport may not have the capacity to move all members to and from off barracks locations on all occasions.

While it is clear that the applicant chose to play touch football at Queens Park it is not clear as to why he used his own motor vehicle. It is agreed that the applicant used his private vehicle on twenty occasions during the year of income but I do not know what, if any, arrangements applied for the balance of the year. Nor do I know whether military transport was available but not used by the applicant for personal reasons.

  1. It is, however, clear that the applicant was acting in the course of his employment when he attended Thursday afternoon sport. That finding is consistent with the decision of the Tribunal in Re Lake and Department of Defence (AAT Decision No 8592 - 17 March 1993). In that case the Tribunal had to consider whether an injury suffered by a soldier was an “injury” as defined for the purposes of the compensation legislation. The Tribunal (Senior Member Beddoe, Mrs Pavlin and Brigadier Gibson (Members)) concluded in that case that Mr Lake had undertaken cyclone preparation work in his own time, and away from Lavarack Barracks, because he believed (correctly) that he was required by an order of a superior to do so.
  2. I have come to the view that the reasons in Re Lake apply, mutatis mutandis, to the circumstances of the applicant's case. I am also of the view that the applicant was on duty while undertaking Thursday afternoon sport. He was subject to military discipline (albeit a relaxed discipline) as evidenced by the fact that he was “stood down” after participation in the sports. His case is immediately distinguishable from that of the soldier applicant in Case T74 86 ATC 1064 who was on leave at the relevant time.
  3. The applicant submits that the applicant derives his income as a member of the Defence Force and not from a particular role he may perform in the course of his employment as such a member. Fundamentally he carries out those duties which he is directed by order to carry out. In particular he was acting in accordance with orders of his superiors when attending and participating in sporting activity.
  4. In the alternative the applicant's case is that his prime role as a member of the Defence Force was as an infantryman. In that role he was required to be physically fit and playing sport achieved this end. In this regard the applicant relies on the Tribunal’s decision in Case 48/93 93 ATC 520 where it was decided expenditure incurred by a police officer in training for a pilot’s licence was likely to increase her efficiency and promotion prospects as a police officer and was an allowable deduction within the terms of s 51 of the Act. That decision relied in part upon the following dicta of the High Court in Fletcher and Ors v. FC of T 91 ATC 4950 at 4957.
  5. In Fletcher the Court set out the relevant test as follows at pages 4957-8:
“The question whether an outgoing was, for the purposes of s 51(1), wholly or partly ‘incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income’ is a question of characterisation. The relationship between the outgoing and the assessable income must be such as to impart to the outgoing the character of an outgoing of the relevant kind. It has been pointed out on many occasions in the cases that an outgoing will not properly be characterised as having been incurred in gaining or producing assessable income unless it was ‘incidental and relevant to that end’.7 It has also been said that the test of deductibility under the first limb of s 51(1) is that:

‘it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income or, if none be produced, would be expected to produce assessable income.’ 8

So to say is not, however, to exclude the motive of the taxpayer in making the outgoing as a possibly relevant factor in characterisation for the purposes of the first limb of s. 51(1). At least in a case where the outgoing has been voluntarily incurred, the end which the taxpayer subjectively had in view in incurring it may, depending upon the circumstances of the particular case, constitute an element, and possibly the decisive element, in characterisation of either the whole or part of the outgoing for the purposes of the sub-section.9 In that regard and in the context of the sub-section’s clear contemplation of apportionment, statements in the cases to the effect that it is sufficient for the purposes of s. 51(1) that the production of assessable income is ‘the occasion’ of the outgoing10 or that the outgoing is a ‘cost of a step taken in the process of gaining or producing income’11 are to be understood as referring to a genuine and not colourable relationship between the whole of the expenditure and the production of such income.

Nonetheless, it is commonly possible to characterise an outgoing as being wholly of the kind referred to in the first limb of s.51(1) without any need to refer to the taxpayer’s subjective thought processes. That is ordinarily so in a case where the outgoing gives rise to the receipt of a larger amount of assessable income. In such a case, the characterisation of the particular outgoing as wholly of a kind referred to in s51(1) will ordinarily not be affected by considerations of the taxpayer’s subjective motivation. If, for example, a particular item of assessable income can be earned by making a lesser outgoing in one of two possible ways, one of which is a loss or outgoing of the kind described in s. 51(1) and the other of which is not, it will ordinarily be irrelevant that the taxpayer’s choice of the method which was tax deductible was motivated by taxation considerations or that the non-deductible outgoing would have been less than the deductible one. In such a case, the objective relationship between the outgoing actually made and the greater amount of assessable income actually earned suffices, without more, to characterise the whole outgoing as one which was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income. If the outgoing can properly be wholly so characterised, it “is not for the Court or the commissioner to say how much a taxpayer ought to spend in obtaining his income, but only how much he has spent.’12”

The applicant also relies upon the decision of the Federal Court in Mansfield v. FC of T 96 ATC 4001 and the discussion in the judgment of Hill J relating to the decision in FC of T v. Cooper 91 ATC 4396. However, I have not found the clothing and food cases helpful in deciding this case. While they are certainly authoritative in deciding whether personal expenditure is an allowable deduction within the terms of s 51(1) I do not understand those decisions to affect the line of authorities dealing with travel expenses.

  1. In that context the applicant submits that his case comes within the Commissioner’s ruling TR95/17 at paragraphs 237-8 because the applicant has incurred his travel expenses in travelling between two places of work.
  2. Furthermore, says the applicant, he was performing his duties and deriving assessable income while he was participating in the sport as a requirement of and in the course of his employment. It followed, says the applicant, that he was travelling between two places of employment when he was travelling between Lavarack Barracks and the sportsground.
  3. The respondent Commissioner submits that the claimed deduction was not incurred in gaining or producing the applicant's income and in any event was private in nature. The respondent says that the essential character of the “expenditure is that associated with participation in a social and recreational activity rather than with the gaining of income”.
  4. The respondent concedes that members of the Defence Force are required to maintain a certain level of physical fitness “as a pre-requisite to their job”. I take that to mean that the Commissioner accepts that a downgraded medical classification could adversely affect future income derived as a member of the Defence Force. In that regard the Commissioner relies on the Board of Review decision in Case N72 81 ATC 383. In that case the Board of Review found that expenditure incurred on a fitness course by an airline pilot was neither relevant nor incidental to the duties of an airline pilot. In that case there is no suggestion that the fitness course was undertaken in the employer’s time.
  5. The respondent says that the essential character of the sports afternoon is that of a social and recreational activity rather than specialist physical training. The essential basis of this submission is that participation including level of participation is at the option of the applicant rather than by direction of the applicant's superiors although it is clear on the facts that participation per se is a requirement of the Defence Force.
  6. As to participation during working hours the respondent relies upon the Tribunal decision in Case 14/94 94 ATC 184. That case involved an employee of the Australian Taxation Office who was undertaking a Master of Criminology degree. The Tribunal found that the taxpayer’s dominant motive in undertaking the particular course of study was his interest and involvement in prison reform. While that might be thought to be an advantage for those taxpayers who find themselves incarcerated the Tribunal found that there was not a “manifest connection” between the incurring of the expenditure and the derivation of assessable income and relied on the decision of the High Court (Menzies J) in FC of T v. Hatchett 71 ATC 4184; and the decision of the Tribunal in Case U101 87 ATC 616. In both cases there was some assistance from the employer.
  7. I am satisfied, in the light of those decisions, that the mere fact of assistance from the employer does not change the essential character of the expenditure incurred. I am not satisfied, with respect, that a concept of manifest connection should be substituted for the incidental and relevant test (Ronpibon Tin N L v. FC of T [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at 56). As already noted in paragraph 11 that test was confirmed by the High Court in Fletcher. As was explained in Ronpibon the words “incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income” mean in the course of gaining or producing such income. Therefore, in the circumstances of an employee they mean in the course of gaining or producing remuneration from the employer. Hence the earlier line of decisions given effect in Lunney v F.C. of T. (1958) 100 CLR 478, 11 ATD 404, to confirm the proposition that expenses in travelling to and from a place of employment was not incurred in gaining the assessable income derived from the employment. In Lunney Dixon CJ referred with approval to Dr Hannon’s treatise ”Principles of Income Taxation” at pages 306 and 433 . At page 433 Dr Hannon explained the situation with travelling expenses which are not allowable deductions as follows:
“... the expenditure was incurred in connection with the earning of assessable income, but not in the course of earning it.”


  1. That explanation of the operation of s 51 is consistent with the earlier decision of the High Court in FC of T v. Green [1950] HCA 20; (1950) 81 CLR 313, 9 ATD 142 in so far as that case concerned travelling expenses. The Court held that the expenditure was incurred in relation to the management of the income producing enterprises, a description of the income producing activities which made it immaterial to decide whether the taxpayer was carrying on business.
  2. At that relatively early stage it was made clear that a distinction was to be drawn between expenses incurred in travel to and from work which is not deductible and travel in the course of work and between two places of income production which may be deductible.
  3. In so far as the respondent submits to the effect that a dichotomy applies to the applicant's duty as an infantryman and the duty requiring participation in sport I am not satisfied that a soldier’s service duty can be so divided or characterised. He is first and foremost a soldier serving in the Defence Force and subject to military discipline. He does what he is required to do whether because of direct orders, routine orders or the acceptance of responsibility to perform the task at hand. A soldier performs the allocated duties under the supervision and control of officers but that supervision and control does not deny the obligation of the soldier to make decisions from time to time about performance of duties, whatever the circumstances may be at the time. That those circumstances may vary widely from time to time does not justify compartmentalising those circumstances so that only some of them are said to be in the course of deriving assessable income as a soldier. In doing training at Lavarack Barracks he is acting in the course of deriving his assessable income as is a soldier doing guard duty outside Victoria Barracks and a soldier doing flood rescue work in conjunction with civil authorities. Each is performing duty as a soldier although their duties are entirely different and each may belong to a different operational unit,.
  4. In deciding the essential character of the expenditure incurred it is not correct in my view to say, as the respondent does, that the participation in Thursday afternoon sport is a social or recreational activity and therefore that is the essential character of the expenditure. That is a characterisation that ignores the fact that when the applicant participates in Thursday afternoon sport he does so in the course of his duty as a soldier and in his employer’s time.
  5. In my view, and I so find, the essential character of the expenditure claimed is that of travel expenses incurred in connection with participation at sports organised by the applicant's employer ie the Defence Force or 2 RAR in particular. That participation must also be characterised as participation in the course of duty. Factors influencing that conclusion are that members participating in sports off barracks are required by 2 RAR to return to barracks before they are paraded and stood down at the end of the day’s duty. While it is a fact that such parades are apparently less formal than on other working days I am satisfied that the members of 2 RAR continue to act in the course of their employment until they are formally stood down.
  6. The fact of a soldier going off barracks does not per se suggest that the soldier is not acting in the course of his duty and is therefore off duty. If he goes off barracks because he/she is ordered to do so then the soldier may still be acting in the course of employment. This is not a case where official duty somehow ceases and transforms into a voluntary social occasion (an example is West v. Commonwealth (1968) 13 FLR 221 at 229-30). It is clear enough, and I find, that the official duty in this case terminated upon being stood down after return to Lavarack Barracks.
  7. The applicant stated in document T5 that his regimental sport on Thursdays is touch football at Queens Park. Although that fact has not been agreed I accept that is the case, and so find. My understanding is that Queens Park is in the Townsville suburb of North Ward which is a considerable distance in relative terms from Lavarack Barracks. The applicant's claim asserts a distance of 12 km. It is clear to me that some form of transport between the two locations would be essential.
  8. There is no evidence about the availability of miliary transport provided for travel between Lavarack Barracks and North Ward. I infer that none was provided because it has not been suggested by the respondent or the Department of Defence that such transport was available.
  9. In these circumstances I am satisfied that it was necessary for the applicant to make his own arrangements for travel between the Barracks and Queens Park. I am also satisfied that reasonable expenditure incurred by the applicant in travelling between two places of employment while in the course of his official duty is an outgoing incurred in the course of gaining or producing the assessable income.
  10. In the result I am satisfied, and find, that the applicant was travelling in the course of his employment and not from and to his place of employment when he travelled from the Barracks to Queens Park and returned to barracks before being stood down from duty. Because I am satisfied that the applicant was acting in the course of his employment and incurred the travel expense for that purpose I am satisfied the outgoings were not of a private nature.
  11. It follows that the decision under review should be set aside and the claim allowed in principle. Nothing was said to the effect that the claim had not been substantiated. I will allow the deduction of $282.00 claimed.
  12. I will also certify that these proceedings have terminated in a manner favourable to the applicant.
