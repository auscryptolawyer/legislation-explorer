Statement of Reasons

  1. The Applicant (Mr Longstaff) is 61 years. He was granted access to the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) in June 2018 on the basis of intellectual, neurological and cognitive impairments related to a hypoxic brain injury.[1]
  2. Karen Sanecki Jones (Ms Sanecki Jones) is Mr Longstaff’s sister, plan nominee and legal guardian. Ms Sanecki Jones has applied to the Tribunal on Mr Longstaff’s behalf for review of a decision regarding Mr Longstaff’s Statement of Participant Supports (SOPS).

BACKGROUND AND JURISDICTION

  1. On 28 March 2024, the Chief Executive Officer (CEO) of the National Disability Insurance Agency (Respondent) approved a SOPS for inclusion in Mr Longstaff’s plan (original decision).[2] On 25 June 2024, Ms Sanecki Jones requested that the original decision be reviewed, and on 27 August 2024 a reviewer confirmed the original decision (internal review decision).[3]
  2. On 17 September 2024, Ms Sanecki Jones made an application to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal (AAT) for review of the internal review decision.[4] The AAT had jurisdiction to review the internal review decision under s 103(1) of the National Disability Insurance Scheme Act 2013 (NDIS Act), in combination with s 25 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (AAT Act).[5]
  3. The Administrative Review Tribunal (ART) was established on 14 October 2024 and replaced the former AAT.[6] Mr Longstaff’s review application was not finalised before the transition to the ART. Proceedings in the AAT that were not finalised before the transition to the ART must be continued and finalised by the ART.[7] In this Statement of Reasons, the ART will hereafter be referred to as ‘the Tribunal’.
  4. On 19 December 2024, 2 May 2025, 22 July 2025 and 2 September 2025 the Respondent made decisions under s 47A to vary Mr Longstaff’s plan; and following a request from Mr Longstaff’s representative for a plan replenishment, a new SOPS was approved for Mr Longstaff on 22 April 2026.[8] Pursuant to s 103, the review application made on 17 September 2024 is taken to also be an application for review of those decisions. Mr Longstaff’s operative SOPS is that which came into effect on 22 April 2026, and the operative decision is that which approved Mr Longstaff’s operative SOPS.[9]

ISSUES ON REVIEW

  1. On review Mr Longstaff is seeking funding for the following:
    a) Increased SIL funding comprising 1:1 support for 24 hours per day with inactive overnight support at the high intensity rate (excluding periods when funded for social, economic and community participation (SECP)).[10]

    b) Increased funding for assistance with SECP comprising 1:1 support for 5 hours per day 7 days a week.[11]

  2. The parties agree that Mr Longstaff requires 24/7 SIL support with inactive overnight assistance (excluding hours funded for SECP), and the Respondent accepts 6 hours per day of 1:1 SIL support is reasonable and necessary for Mr Longstaff.[12] The Respondent however contends all remaining hours of SIL support should be funded at a 1:2 ratio, all SIL support should be funded at the standard rate, and the Tribunal could not be persuaded on the evidence before it that SIL funding in excess of this is reasonable and necessary.[13]
  3. In closing the Respondent broadly contended that Mr Longstaff’s current SIL funding in combination with his current funding for assistance with SECP is reasonable and necessary, but the Tribunal could not be persuaded on the evidence before it that any increase in core funding over and above this is reasonable and necessary for Mr Longstaff.
  4. The issue before the Tribunal is whether the support sought on review as outlined in paragraph [7] is reasonable and necessary, meets the criteria in s 34(1), and is most appropriately funded or provided through the NDIS.[14] The Tribunal must be positively persuaded that the statutory requirements are met based on relevant and probative material.[15]

LEGISLATION AND POLICY

  1. The National Disability Insurance Scheme Amendment (Getting the NDIS Back on Track No. 1) Act 2024 (Amending Act) commenced on 3 October 2024.[16] The Amending Act made a range of amendments to the NDIS Act. Those amendments included, but were not limited to, the insertion of a new definition of ‘NDIS Support’ at s 10, an amendment to s 33(5) which prescribes what is required when deciding whether to approve a SOPS, amendments to s 34 which sets out criteria which must be satisfied in relation to reasonable and necessary supports, and amendments to ss 43 and 44 regarding plan management. Those provisions, as amended, apply to the Applicant and any approval or variation of his SOPS.[17]
  2. The objects of the NDIS Act, and the principles guiding actions under the NDIS Act, are set out in ss 3 and 4. In giving effect to the objectives of the NDIS Act, regard is to be had to the need to ensure the financial sustainability of the NDIS.[18] Section 4(17) further provides that the Respondent and any other person or body is to perform functions and exercise powers under the NDIS Act in accordance with the principles in s 4, having regard to the need to ensure the financial sustainability of the NDIS.
  3. Chapter 3 of the NDIS Act provides the statutory framework for individual plans under which NDIS supports will be funded for participants.[19] Section 17A prescribes principles to which regard must be had when performing functions and exercising powers under Chapter 3 of the NDIS Act. The preparation, variation, reassessment and replacement of a participant’s plan, and the management of funding for supports under a participant’s plan, should so far as reasonably practicable be guided by principles relating to plans set out in s 31. A participant’s plan must include the participant’s statement of goals and aspirations and SOPS.[20]
  4. In deciding whether to approve a SOPS, the CEO or the Tribunal on review, must:

(a) have regard to the participant’s statement of goals and aspirations; and

(b) have regard to relevant assessments conducted in relation to the participant; and

(c) be satisfied as mentioned in section 34 in relation to the reasonable and necessary supports that will be funded and the general supports that will be provided; and

(d) apply the National Disability Insurance Scheme rules (if any) made for the purposes of section 35;

(e) have regard to the principle that a participant should manage his or her plan to the extent that he or she wishes to do so; and

(f) have regard to the operation and effectiveness of any previous plans of the participant; and

(g) have regard to whether section 46 (acquittal of NDIS amounts) was complied with in relation to any previous plan for the participant.[21]
  1. Section 34(1) provides that:

For the purposes of specifying, in a statement of participant supports, the general supports that will be provided, and the reasonable and necessary supports that will be funded, the CEO must be satisfied of all of the following in relation to the funding or provision of each such support:

aa) the support is necessary to address needs of the participant arising from an impairment in relation to which the participant meets the disability requirements (see section 24) or the early intervention requirements (see section 25);

a) the support will assist the participant to pursue the goals, objectives and aspirations included in the participant’s statement of goals and aspirations;

b) the support will assist the participant to undertake activities, so as to facilitate the participant’s social and economic participation;

  1. the support represents value for money in that the costs of the support are reasonable, relative to both the benefits achieved and the cost of alternative support;

d) the support will be, or is likely to be, effective and beneficial for the participant, having regard to current good practice;

e) the funding or provision of the support takes account of what it is reasonable to expect families, carers, informal networks and the community to provide;

f) the support is an NDIS support for the participant.

  1. The phrase ‘reasonable and necessary’ is not defined in the NDIS Act. In National Disability Insurance Agency v WRMF [2020] FCAFC 79 the Court stated:

‘... the phrase connotes supports which meet a threshold which justifies – by reference to the context, objects and guiding principles in the Act and the facts of the case – the expenditure of public funds for that support, for a particular participant.’[22]

  1. In addition to the matters of which the CEO, or the Tribunal on review, must be satisfied in s 34(1), the Tribunal must also be satisfied that the support is most appropriately funded or provided through the NDIS, and is not more appropriately funded or provided through other general systems of service delivery or support services offered by a person, agency or body, or systems of service delivery or support services offered as part of a universal service obligation, or in accordance with reasonable adjustments required under a law dealing with discrimination on the basis of disability.[23]

  1. Relevant to this application, the Minister has made the National Disability Insurance Scheme (Supports for Participants) Rules 2013 (Supports Rules) and the National Disability Insurance Scheme (Getting the NDIS Back on Track No. 1) (NDIS Supports) Transitional Rules 2024 (Transitional Rules). These are an important part of the legislative scheme.[24]
  2. Guidelines published on the NDIS website contain information about what the Respondent considers when making decisions under the NDIS. These are essentially policy documents. The Tribunal will take these into account unless there are cogent reasons not to.[25]

CONSIDERATION

  1. Mr Longstaff lived with and was cared for by his parents for the first 56 years of his life.[26] In November 2022 Mr Longstaff moved into Short Term Accommodation (STA) in Edgeworth operated by Elsie; and after a period in STA he transitioned into SIL accommodation.[27] Whilst difficult to discern with precision, the Tribunal understands:
    • From around March 2023 Mr Longstaff lived in a SIL property in Charlesworth with provider 121 Supports Hunter.[28]
    • From around August 2024 Mr Longstaff lived in a SIL property in Nelson Bay with provider Blue Shell Lifestyles.[29] This living arrangement ceased after approximately 8 months and Mr Longstaff thereafter lived in emergency accommodation until alternative SIL accommodation was sourced.[30]
    • From around May or July 2025 Mr Longstaff began living in SIL accommodation in Cessnock with provider Parity; and as at the time of the hearing he remained living in this SIL property.[31]
  2. Mr Longstaff’s presentation and experience whilst living in SIL accommodation, together with his functional capacity and behaviours of concern, are all relevant to the Tribunal’s consideration of the issues on review.

Karen Sanecki Jones

  1. Ms Sanecki Jones’ oral evidence assisted the Tribunal to piece together Mr Longstaff’s housing history as outlined in paragraph [24]. Ms Sanecki Jones also gave evidence:
    • She has not lived with Mr Longstaff since she was 17 years old; she lives in Canberra, as do Mr Longstaff’s aged parents; Mr Longstaff lives in New South Wales. She has not personally attended the abovementioned SIL properties.
    • When Mr Longstaff initially moved into the Nelson Bay property he was the only resident and he did not exhibit behaviours of concern; however a second participant joined the property in November 2024 and from this time it was reported Mr Longstaff exhibited behaviours of concern. It was also around this time Mr Longstaff’s Haloperidol dosage was being reduced.
    • There is currently another participant (Mr AA) residing in the Cessnock property who is quite loud. Mr AA entered the Cessnock property in approximately January 2026 as an emergency placement, so no compatibility assessment was completed. Mr Longstaff does not like noise, and his behaviours have escalated since Mr AA has been in the house.
    • 121 Hunter was operated by ‘Jason’, and Jason worked closely with Mr Longstaff when he lived in the Charlesworth property. Since moving into the Cessnock property, Jason has been accompanying Mr Longstaff for community access. Two other support workers who had done some support work with Mr Longstaff at the Nelson Bay property also work with Mr Longstaff at the Cessnock property.

Jodie Cashman, occupational therapist

  1. Ms Cashman has been Mr Longstaff’s occupational therapist since November 2021.[32] In evidence are functional capacity assessment (FCA) reports prepared by Ms Cashman dated 26 March 2024, 10 February 2025 and 10 February 2025 (as amended on 19 May 2025).[33]
  2. In Ms Cashman’s FCA report dated 26 March 2024, she recommended Mr Longstaff be funded for 24 hour SIL support at a ratio of 1:2, with inactive overnight support to provide him with constant reassurance and redirection to assist reduce escalation of his behaviours.[34] It was reported to Ms Cashman during her assessment that Mr Longstaff often went to bed around 8/8:30PM; mostly sleeps through the night; will on occasion require reassurance and redirection back to bed or assistance with toileting; and wakes around 7/7:30AM.[35] Ms Cashman also opined Mr Longstaff requires 1:1 support 3-4 hours per day, 7 days per week for community access (between 21 and 28 hours per week) to support his social needs, model positive behaviour and communication in community settings, and pursue leisure activities of interest such as going out for walks, for a drive, to op shops, for a meal, catching public transport and engaging in new activities.[36]
  3. There are 2 copies of the ‘unamended’ 10 February 2025 report in evidence – one is unsigned and includes handwritten markups in red ink (unsigned 10 February 2025 FCA report);[37] the other is signed and notably, has incorporated each of the handwritten mark-ups (signed 10 February 2025 FCA report).[38] In both the unsigned 10 February 2025 FCA report and the signed 10 February 2025 FCA report, Ms Cashman recommends Mr Longstaff be funded for 1:1 24 hour SIL support, with inactive overnight care; and 1:1 support 5 hours per day, 6 days per week for community access (allowing up to 2 hours of travel time).[39] Within the body of the 10 February 2025 reports Ms Cashman’s reporting regarding which tasks Mr Longstaff requires 1:1 support to undertake or complete differ. By way of an example:
    • In the unsigned 10 February 2025 FCA report relevant to the activity of mobility, it is reported Mr Longstaff can independently complete sit to stand, bed, toilet and lounge transfers, and requires supervision for safety with shower transfers;[40] whereas in the signed 10 February 2025 FCA report, it is reported Mr Longstaff requires 1:1 supervision for his safety for each of these transfers.[41]
    • In the unsigned 10 February 2025 FCA report relevant to the task of feeding, in the unsigned 10 February 2025 FCA report it is reported Mr Longstaff can feed himself using a standard fork or teaspoon;[42] whereas in the signed 10 February 2025 FCA report, it is reported Mr Longstaff can feed himself using a standard fork or teaspoon with 1:1 supervision for his safety.[43]
    • In both the unsigned 10 February 2025 FCA report and the signed 10 February 2025 report relevant to sleep and overnight care under the heading ‘support needs’, it is recommended Mr Longstaff requires 1:1 inactive overnight support to support him with overnight toileting needs, personal hygiene after incontinence episodes, during times he is unwell, and in the event of an emergency.[44] However, in the signed 10 February 2025 FCA report the explanation for this recommendation is an escalation of behaviours.
    • In the response to targeted questions component of the report, in the unsigned 10 February 2025 FCA report Ms Cashman in response to question 6 opines it is unlikely Mr Longstaff has the cognitive capacity to improve his functional skills to a level where he can be supported at the 1:3 ratio; however in the signed 10 February 2025 FCA report the words ‘at the 1:3 ratio’ have been replaced with ‘at the current ratio’ (which is 1:2).[45]
  4. In the signed 10 February 2025 FCA report (including the responses to targeted questions), all of the hand written mark ups on the unsigned 10 February 2025 FCA report have been implemented and those amendments are the differences in reporting referred to in above paragraph. Ms Cashman gave evidence she emailed the draft report to Mr Longstaff’s support coordinator Simon Crethar (Mr Crethar) to review because she was hoping for some feedback as this was her first time preparing a report for the Tribunal, and the handwritten markups in red ink on the unsigned 10 February 2025 FCA report were made by Mr Crethar. Ms Cashman gave further evidence to the effect the changes incorporated references to a 1:1 ratio, rather than ‘full assistance’; and to her knowledge Mr Crethar has no occupational therapy qualifications.
  5. No explanation is provided in the signed 10 February 2025 report for why Ms Cashman has recommended a different ratio of SIL support in 2025 than she did in 2024. When one compares Ms Cashman’s reporting in 2024 with that in the unsigned 10 February 2025 report and the signed 10 February 2025 report, as it relates to what Mr Longstaff can and cannot do in terms of the range of tasks that comprise the range of activities of daily living, there is no significant change in Ms Cashman’s reporting.
  6. In Ms Cashman’s FCA report dated 10 February 2025 (as updated on 19 May 2025), she recommends Mr Longstaff be funded for 1:1 24 hour SIL support, with inactive overnight care; and 1:1 support 5 hours per day, 6 days per week for community access (30 hours).[46]
  7. Ms Cashman gave evidence she most recently completed an in-person FCA with Mr Longstaff in November 2025 during which:
    • His mobility had declined as he was more unsteady on his feet. He intermittently used walls for balance when mobilising and she concluded he needs standby assistance for mobility.
    • He continued to require 1:1 support in all areas of daily living for direction and also physical assistance, including showering, drying, dressing, undressing, toileting, and grooming. His reliance on staff had increased due to a decline in his mobility and because of his behaviours which impact his participation and completion of tasks.
    • He tends to gulp food and drinks so requires 1:1 supervision for these tasks. He cannot prepare meals without support; needs 1:1 assistance with medication management, cleaning and laundry; needs 1:1 inactive overnight support so he is safe when he is unwell, unsettled, or is incontinent.
    • Mr Longstaff’s behaviours are severe and unpredictable. He requires close 1:1 support in all areas of daily living. When he lived in a situation where there has been another person in the home, his behaviours significantly escalate and there are really high safety risks to Mr Longstaff, his support workers and any participant sharing the home.
  8. In cross examination it was put to Ms Cashman that Mr Longstaff doesn’t really engage in household chores such as cleaning at all and she was asked why in those circumstances she has opined he needs 1:1 assistance with all cleaning. Ms Cashman replied this is because Mr Longstaff needs a support worker to do this for him. When asked whether her implementation of Mr Crethar’s suggestion to change her wording of ‘full assistance’ to ‘1:1’ for tasks such as cleaning which Mr Longstaff will not engage in or does not participate in, was in hindsight inappropriate, Ms Cashman responded that he may in the right environment be inclined to participate in the future; and did not agree her amendment was inappropriate.
  9. When raised with Mr Cashman a concern the Tribunal may have with her reporting from February 2025 is that it cannot be confident the report strictly contains her own observations and recommendations, in circumstances where it contains amendments suggested by someone who is not an occupational therapist, she commented she sought feedback from Mr Crethar on how to make the report a bit clearer for a Tribunal review.

Shannon Spence, behaviour support practitioner

  1. Shannon Spence (Mr Spence) was Mr Longstaff’s behaviour support practitioner from around late 2023 until December 2024 and was the first behaviour support practitioner to work with Mr Longstaff.[47]
  2. In May 2024, Mr Spence recommended positive preventative strategies, which included referring to Mr Longstaff’s visual routine board or calendar so he understands and is comfortable with what will occur throughout the day; and a photo of Mr Longstaff’s 7-day visual board is included with this recommendation.[48] Having reviewed behaviour data for the period 24 March 2023 to 24 April 2023, he reported that 78 percent of Mr Longstaff’s behaviours of concern were repetitive fixated behaviours, 15 percent were entering personal space, 8 percent were verbal aggression, and 1 percent were physical aggression.[49]
  3. Mr Spence most recently prepared a comprehensive positive behaviour support plan (CPBS) for Mr Longstaff in December 2024 to assist those supporting him in providing a consistent approach in understanding and responding to his behaviours of concern.[50] In Mr Spence’s CPBS:
    • He opined Mr Longstaff requires consistency and predictability in his life through formal carers completing his daily routines in a consistent manner.[51] This is consistent with what Mr Spence reported in May 2024.[52]
    • It is reported Mr Longstaff’s behaviours of concern include repetitive and fixated behaviours (11 times since December 2024, duration long), verbal aggression (15 times since December 2024, duration 1 to 5 minutes) and physical aggression (11 times since December 2024, duration 1 to 5 minutes).[53]
    • He recommended positive preventative strategies for Mr Longstaff’s behaviours of concern to be implemented by SIL staff; and provided information to assist SIL staff to identify early warning signs of, and how to respond to, Mr Longstaff’s behaviours of concern.[54]
    • He identified use of a chemical restraint, that being Haloperidol.[55]
  4. Mr Spence most recently prepared a functional behaviour assessment (FBA) for Mr Longstaff in December 2024.[56] In this FBA Mr Spence opined Mr Longstaff requires at least 1:2 support from a support worker for all aspects of his day, with 1:1 supports for community participation and certain (self-care) tasks including personal care tasks, food preparation, overnight continence, cleaning, laundry, and medication management.[57] Mr Spence opined Mr Longstaff will be supported to greatly improve his functional capacity within the 1:2 SIL space.[58] Mr Spence also reported Mr Longstaff’s chemical restraint (Haloperidol) had been reduced on a 4-weekly basis from 29 October 2024 and opined this is adding to the increase in severity and intensity of his most recent behaviours of concern.[59]

Colin Bray, psychologist and behaviour support practitioner

  1. Colin Bray (Mr Bray) was engaged as Mr Longstaff’s behaviour support practitioner from 14 December 2024.[60] In February 2025, in response to targeted questions prepared by the Respondent, Mr Bray:
    • Opined Mr Longstaff requires 1:1 active support throughout the day for all activities, including personal care, meal preparation and supervised eating, house tasks (cleaning, laundry), behaviour management and support to prevent escalation, mobility and fall prevention; 1:1 support during the night to assist with reassurance and redirection if he wakes unsettled, and support if he wakes to use the toilet or experiences incontinence episodes; and given his behavioural and mobility challenges the current 1:3 ratio is not sufficient to ensure his safety and the effective implementation of behavioural interventions.[61]
    • Opined Mr Longstaff requires 1:1 support 5 hours per day, 6 days a week for community access (30 hours per week).[62] He opined a variety of venues and activities is an important preventative strategy for Mr Longstaff’s behaviours of concern.[63] He reported that due to the location of Mr Longstaff’s SIL placement to allow for a variety of venues and programs time is required to be allowed for travel to outside local areas of approximately 2 hours return trip; and that this allows Mr Longstaff to access community activities in areas of Newcastle and Maitland.[64]
    • Recommended fortnightly, transitioning to monthly, training for SIL staff and identified training priorities to include recognising and responding to behavioural escalation, consistent boundary setting and communication strategies, and managing fixated behaviours and de-escalation techniques.[65]

Hannah Smith, behaviour support practitioner

  1. Hannah Smith (Ms Smith) is a behaviour support practitioner who prepared an interim behaviour support plan (IBSP) for Mr Longstaff signed 28 November 2025.[66] At the time of Ms Smith’s report, Mr Longstaff was living in Cessnock in a SIL property supported by Parity with 1 other participant.[67] In the Ms Smith’s IBSP:
    • It is reported that Nathan Walsh (Mr Walsh) of Parity reported to her that from the time Mr Longstaff moved into the Cessnock property, there had been approximately 28 behavioural-related incidents, with 10 occurring in September 2025.[68] Mr Walsh offered to provide copies of all incident reports and ABC charts once available, but these were not provided to Ms Smith by the time she wrote her report.[69]
    • It is reported Mr Longstaff’s behaviours of concern include verbal aggression (daily, can last for hours), physical aggression (multiple times a week up to a few hours), absconding (multiple times a week), and repetitive fixated behaviours (daily).[70]
    • Recommended preventative strategies for each of Mr Longstaff’s behaviours of concern when he is at ‘baseline’ to be implemented by SIL staff; and provided information to assist SIL staff to implement each of those strategies and to understand the purpose of those.[71]
    • Recommended response strategies for when Mr Longstaff is showing early warning signs, at peak escalation, and during de-escalation/recovery; and provided guidance to identify when Mr Longstaff’s behaviours of concern are in each of those stages.[72]
    • Identified use of a chemical restraint, that being Haloperidol.[73]
  2. In an undated letter, Ms Smith stated her involvement with Mr Longstaff was limited to preparing the IBSP and creating a video-based staff training module after being advised by Mr Crethar that staff required training to implement the IBSP.[74] Ms Smith also stated that after providing the training module she contacted the team to confirm whether staff had viewed the training and to invite any questions regarding implementation, however she received no response.[75]

Dr Josef McDonald, neuropsychiatrist

  1. In January 2025 Dr Josef McDonald (Mr McDonald), a neuropsychiatrist, opined that the reduction in Haloperidol may be contributing to an increase in anxiety, which appears precipitated by changes in environmental consistency including the addition of a co-participant in SIL accommodation.[76] He suggested an increase in Haloperidol; and if there is ongoing agitation he suggested increasing that further.[77] Dr McDonald also suggested a visible roster with photos may be helpful in enhancing predictability of SIL staff for Mr Longstaff, which is a longstanding focus of his.[78] Dr McDonald reported Mr Longstaff’s sleep remains undisrupted and he continues to achieve 8 hours of sleep per night.[79] He further reported SIL staff have concerns Mr Longstaff remains funded for 1:3 as a 1:2 support ratio has already resulted in a marked increase in behaviours of concern.[80]
  2. In March 2025 Dr McDonald reported Mr Longstaff is sleeping well and that his aggression and the ease with which he could be redirected improved since the increase in Haloperidol.[81] He again recommended a visible roster with photos of SIL staff.[82]
  3. In May 2025 Dr McDonald opined that optimising behavioural and environmental strategies to target behaviours of concern will be of importance.[83] Based on what was reported to Dr McDonald by Ms Shaw regarding Mr Longstaff’s behaviour towards those he lives with, he opined the provision of 1:1 support appears to consistently improve behaviours of concern.[84] He further opined Mr Longstaff’s executive dysfunction and impulsivity appears to respond to close supervision which is optimally provided through a 1:1 support ratio.[85]

Rebecca Kalinsky, registered nurse

  1. Rebecca Kalinsky (Ms Kalinsky) is a registered nurse who completed a continence assessment and a related report in October 2025.[86] Ms Kalinsky reported Mr Longstaff remains continent during the day with prompting and supervision, his continence needs are primarily related to nocturnal incontinence, and he needs disposable continence pads at night to manage episodes of bedwetting.[87]

Christie Field, speech pathologist

  1. In a report dated 24 May 2024, Christie Field (Ms Field), a speech pathologist reported Mr Longstaff presents with a language disorder and dysarthria which impacts his ability to understand what others have said, find the correct words to express himself, sequence these words into grammatically correct sentences and narratives and clearly articulate them; this results in frequent breakdowns in communication; and when this happens he can become extremely frustrated.[88] Ms Field opined Mr Longstaff requires a consistent, structured and visual routine; reported that to support the use of visuals during the reporting period an Ipad was used with applications such as ChoiceWorks; and recommended appropriate visuals are utilised to support Mr Longstaff.[89]

SIL staff

  1. In evidence are incident reports prepared by various SIL staff between 23 May 2025 and 11 October 2025 regarding Mr Longstaff within his SIL accommodation.[90] Having considered each of these incident reports, the Tribunal makes the following observations:

Mr Longstaff is also funded for 90 hours of specialist behaviour intervention support and 30 hours for a behaviour support plan and training in behaviour support strategies.
  2. It is contended on Mr Longstaff’s behalf that despite him having a degree of independence in relation to some activities of daily living and at times being capable of being by himself, his behaviour is so unpredictable and severe that there is no possibility he and another participant could be cared for safely within a 1:2 ratio of care; and a 1:2 ratio of care poses a risk to Mr Longstaff, his support worker and the participant with whom he shares SIL support.[120] It is further contended on Mr Longstaff’s behalf that the only way Mr Longstaff can be effectively and safely supported is for him to have 1:1 SIL support and outside that there is no comparable strategy that can achieve the same outcome.[121] The evidence does not support these contentions.
  3. Ms Cashman in her March 2024 report, Mr Spence and Ms Ruskin have each expressed a view that based on Mr Longstaff’s functional capacity and behaviours of concern he can be effectively and safely supported with a SIL 1:2 ratio of care. Based on that evidence, and noting the Tribunal’s findings regarding Mr Longstaff’s functional capacity, behaviours of concern and home and living history; the Tribunal is not persuaded on the evidence before it that Mr Longstaff cannot be safely and effectively supported within his current SIL funding, if his SIL provider ensures those delivering SIL support to Mr Longstaff are properly trained regarding behaviour support strategies, SIL staff supporting Mr Longstaff consistently implement those strategies and deliver his support in a routine and consistent way, and a compatibility assessment is undertaken regarding who Mr Longstaff shares SIL support with. The Tribunal is persuaded that Mr Longstaff’s existing SIL funding, implemented in this way, is a comparable support which would achieve the same outcome at a substantially lower cost; and is not likely to cause harm to Mr Longstaff or pose a risk to others.
  4. Ms Ruskin opined 1:1 SIL support for Mr Longstaff would limit his independence and create a reliance on that support; and his ability to make choices about how he wants to live his life or what he wants to do could perhaps be limited by such a high level of support. Based on Ms Ruskin’s evidence, the Tribunal finds the evidence before it does not support a finding that increasing Mr Longstaff’s current SIL support will substantially improve the life stage outcomes for, and be of long-term benefit to, Mr Longstaff; is likely to reduce the cost of the funding of supports for Mr Longstaff in the long term; or will increase Mr Longstaff’s independence and reduce his need for other kinds of supports. Indeed, based on Mr Ruskin’s evidence, the Tribunal finds increasing Mr Longstaff’s current SIL funding to include more support at a 1:1 ratio of care could in fact reduce Mr Longstaff’s independence and produce worse outcomes for him than his existing SIL support.
  5. For the reasons outlined above, the Tribunal is not satisfied that funding more 1:1 SIL support for Mr Longstaff than is already funded in his SOPS represents value for money in that the costs of the support are reasonable, relative to both the benefits achieved and the cost of alternative support; or that such support would be, or is likely to be, effective and beneficial for Mr Longstaff, having regard to good practice. Accordingly, Mr Longstaff’s request for 24/7 SIL support at a 1:1 ratio of care does not meet the requirements in ss 34(1)(c) or 34(1)(d), it is not a reasonable and necessary support for Mr Longstaff, and it cannot be funded in his SOPS.

High intensity rate

  1. Mr Longstaff is seeking to have his SIL support funded at the high intensity rate, rather than the standard intensity rate.
  2. The NDIS Guideline ‘Supported Independent Living’ (SIL Guideline) states:

We’ll consider what intensity of support meets your disability support needs and meets our NDIS funding criteria. Most people will need standard intensity support.

However, you may need high intensity support if you need support workers with extra qualifications and experience to meet your disability support needs.

We usually fund the high intensity prices when you need continual active support from support workers with extra qualifications due to your high support needs. For example, due to unstable seizure activity or respiratory support.[122]

  1. The Respondent contends it generally provides the high intensity rate to participants of the NDIS who require support with complex bowel care, enteral feeding support, dysphagia support; ventilator support, tracheostomy support, urinary catheter support, subcutaneous injections, or complex wound care.[123] This is consistent with the SIL Guideline, and the NDIS Pricing Arrangements and Price Limits 2025-26 which states a support is considered high intensity if it is provided to a participant that is 1 or more of the supports set out in Schedule 2 – Module 1: High intensity daily personal activities of the National Disability Insurance Scheme (Provider Registration and Practice Standards) Rules 2018.[124] It is also consistent with the NDIS Practice Standards: High intensity support skills descriptors published by the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission which provides supplementary guidance for NDIS providers and workers supporting participants with high intensity daily personal activities.
  2. The evidence does not support a finding that Mr Longstaff requires continual active support, indeed Ms Ruskin’s evidence is he does not require this. There is no clinical recommendation in evidence that Mr Longstaff’s support workers need extra qualifications and/or experience, as distinct from adequate training in relation to Mr Longstaff’s behaviour support strategies, due to Mr Longstaff’s support needs. On the evidence before it, the Tribunal finds Mr Longstaff does not require support with complex bowel care, enteral feeding support, dysphagia support, ventilator support, tracheostomy support, urinary catheter support, subcutaneous injections, or complex wound care. In all of these circumstances and having regard to the factors prescribed in r 3.1 of the Supports Rules, the Tribunal is not satisfied that funding Mr Longstaff’s SIL support at the high intensity rate rather than the standard intensity rate represents value for money in that the costs of the support are reasonable, relative to both the benefits achieved and the cost of alternative support; and nor is the Tribunal satisfied that funding Mr Longstaff’s SIL support at the high intensity rate is necessary to address needs of Mr Longstaff arising from an impairment in relation to which he meets the disability requires in s 24 or the early intervention requirements in s 25. Accordingly, the request to fund Mr Longstaff’s SIL support at the high intensity rate does not meet the requirements in ss 34(1)(aa) or 34(1)(c), it is not a reasonable and necessary support for Mr Longstaff, and it cannot be funded in his SOPS.

Increased SECP funding

  1. Mr Longstaff is currently funded for 20 hours of 1:1 support per week at the weekday rate, and 5 hours of 1:1 support per week at the Saturday rate, for SECP (25 hours per week in total). Mr Longstaff is seeking to be funded for 5 hours of 1:1 support 7 days per week for SECP (35 hours per week in total). It is contended on Mr Longstaff’s behalf that the additional hours of SECP sought on review will allow Mr Longstaff to meaningfully and safely participate in community engagement, which has the flow on effect of reducing and helping to manage his behaviour.[125]
  2. It is not in contest between the parties, and the Tribunal is similarly satisfied on the evidence before it, that Mr Longstaff requires 1:1 support for community access.
  3. In March 2024 Ms Cashman opined Mr Longstaff requires 1:1 support 3 to 4 hours per day, 7 days per week (21 to 28 hours per week) for SECP. In February 2025, and in her updated version of that report in May 2025, Ms Cashman opined Mr Longstaff requires 1:1 support 5 hours per day 6 days per week for SECP (30 hours per week). She reported that regular 1:1 community access support will enable Mr Longstaff to increase his social interaction opportunities, improve his social skills, and reduce escalation of behaviours when he is in the community and at home.[126] Ms Cashman does not clearly explain why from February 2025 she recommends 6 days of SECP rather than 7; and she provides no clinical reasoning for why she recommends a specific number of hours of SECP each day, and not a lesser number of hours each day.
  4. In February Mr Bray recommended 1:1 support 5 hours per day 6 days per week and opined a variety of venues and activities is an important preventative strategy for Mr Longstaff’s behaviours of concern. Mr Bray reported Mr Longstaff’s community access sometimes takes place in Newcastle and Maitland, which involves up to 2 hours of travel. He does not however suggest that Mr Longstaff travels to Newcastle or Maitland every time he has community access, and indeed the breakdown for how he recommends Mr Longstaff’s SECP funding be utilised does not involve Mr Longstaff travelling to these areas every time he has community access. It is unclear on the face of Mr Bray’s report why on days Mr Longstaff does not travel to Newcastle or Maitland, he recommends 5 hours of community access and not a lesser number of hours, or indeed whether he has turned his mind to this.
  5. Ms Ruskin considered 5 hours of 1:1 support reasonable for Mr Longstaff to access the community but opined the number of days that would be offered would be dependent on what groups or activities Mr Longstaff would enjoy.
  6. There is no clinical recommendation in evidence that Mr Longstaff be funded for 5 hours per day 7 days per week for community access, or 35 hours of SECP per week in total. Whilst based on Ms Cashman’s, Mr Bray’s and Ms Ruskin’s evidence the Tribunal is satisfied Mr Longstaff requires regular community access during which he attends a variety of venues and undertakes diverse activities, and that this plays an important role in managing Mr Longstaff’s behaviour, there is a lack of probative evidence to find this must be for 5 hours on each occasion he has community access, or that this needs to occur 7 days a week.
  7. The evidence does not explore or explain why Mr Longstaff’s existing funding for SECP, used flexibly and optimally, is insufficient. In the absence of probative evidence to support any finding Mr Longstaff’s existing SECP funding, used flexibly and optimally, is insufficient, the Tribunal considers his existing SECP funding used flexibly and optimally is a comparable support to that sought on review which would achieve the same outcome at a substantially lower price; and is not persuaded the additional SECP funding sought on review will substantially improve the life stage outcomes, and be of long-term benefit to Mr Longstaff; that it is likely to reduce the cost of the funding of supports for him in the long term; or that it will increase his independence and reduce his need for other kinds of support. In all of these circumstances, the Tribunal is not satisfied any increased SECP funding represents value for money in that the costs of the support are reasonable, relative to both the benefits achieved and the cost of alternative support. Accordingly, the additional SECP funding sought on review by Mr Longstaff does not meet the requirement in s 34(1)(c), it is not a reasonable and necessary support for Mr Longstaff, and it cannot be funded in his SOPS.
