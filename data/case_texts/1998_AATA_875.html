REASONS FOR DECISION

19 October 1998 J Block (Senior Member)



  1. By objection dated 2 February 1998 the Applicant objected against an assessment issued on 8 January 1998 for the year ending 30 June 1997 (the “relevant year”) in respect of the disallowance of certain motor vehicle expenses amounting to $2,675 (the “vehicle expenses”), of certain telephone expenses amounting to $122 (the “phone expenses”), and also a shortfall penalty imposed in respect of the assessment (the “penalty”). By notice dated 7 April 1998, the Respondent disallowed the Applicant’s objection.
  2. At the hearing, the Applicant was represented by Messrs Debus and Martel of Martel Ebert & Co, Certified Practising Accountants, and the Respondent was represented by Ms V Rands of the Australian Taxation Office. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged by the Respondent pursuant to s 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. The following exhibits were tendered on behalf of the Applicant:
  • Additional notes provided by Mr Debus (Exhibit A1)
  • Federal Pastoral Industry Award (Exhibit A2)

The following exhibit was tendered on behalf of the Respondent:

  • Bundle of documents comprising requests for information and responses thereto (Exhibit R1).

The only oral evidence at the hearing was given by the Applicant; the Tribunal accepts that his evidence was truthful.

  1. The Applicant is a professional shearer who during the relevant year worked in that capacity in the Dubbo district in western New South Wales. He arranged his own work on a “per head” contract basis with 29 individual wool farmers (the “farmers”); he was treated by the farmers as a PAYE employee and issued with a separate group certificate by each of the 29 farmers during the relevant year.
  2. The Applicant typically worked with other shearers in a team. In respect of approximately one third of the 29 shearing engagements, the Applicant was responsible for assembling the team of shearers. On these occasions, the farmer telephoned the Applicant and asked him to assemble a team; such teams generally consisted of shearers, rouseabouts and wool-pressers. The Applicant then contacted such persons as were required to join the team either by way of telephone or by speaking to them at a public bar. On other occasions, and where the Applicant was not responsible for assembling the team, he became part of a team assembled by another shearer; contact would be made in much the same manner.
  3. The Applicant said that the availability of work was dependent on the weather because wet sheep cannot be sheared, and that it was therefore difficult to anticipate the time required to complete a shearing engagement. The shearers did not receive payment for those periods in which they were unable to continue shearing due to adverse weather conditions.
  4. The Applicant was also frequently required to negotiate the terms of the engagement with the farmer. Notwithstanding that the Federal Pastoral Industry Award (exhibit A2) provides minimum rates for shearing calculated on a per head basis as well as a vehicle allowance for travel between the shearing sheds (the “sheds”) and the employee’s place of residence, the Applicant said that securing a shearing engagement for a team of shearers was “just like putting in a tender” (Transcript, page 38). The Applicant said that in order to secure the engagement he would frequently forgo his right to receive travel allowance from the farmer.
  5. The farmers all lived at various distances from the Applicant’s home in Dubbo and the time spent at each work site varied. Exhibit R1 includes a map of the Dubbo area prepared by the Applicant and on which the Applicant inscribed the locations of each of the farmers for whom he sheared during the relevant year; the map indicates that the distances between Dubbo and the Applicant’s various places of employment ranged from approximately 5 to 110 kilometres. In a letter to the Applicant dated 26 August 1998, the Respondent requested that the Applicant provide an answer to the question: “Did you ever travel directly between shearing sheds on the same day? How often?”; the Applicant replied in a letter to the Respondent dated 3 September 1998 as follows: “Travel between sheds maybe 2-3 times per year” (both letters are included in Exhibit R1).
  6. The Applicant said that in addition to the occasions on which he was telephoned by the farmer and asked to prepare a team, the Applicant solicited work himself by telephoning either farmers or other shearers. The Applicant said that the duration of his shearing season depended on his proficiency on the telephone and his ability to offer attractive terms to potential employers; he stated:
“it’s competitive, see, if I can get in first and convince the man that I’m the man for the job, well, I can get the job. If I don’t do that I sit at home; no work, no pay.” (Transcript, page 41).


  1. In respect of the control exercised by the farmers over the Applicant during the course of the engagement, the Applicant stated that the farmers generally supervised the shearing and that if, for instance, a shearer happened to cripple a sheep or to injure more sheep than would be reasonably expected, the farmer might terminate the employment. The farmers often urged the shearers to work more quickly or to find faster shearers to join the team or to work overtime; if a farmer was dissatisfied with the work rate of a particular shearer, the farmer would usually advise the person who assembled the team that he did not want that shearer to be included in the team the following year. The Applicant also said that the farmer alone had the power to terminate a shearer’s employment.
  2. In his initial objection, the Applicant listed several items of equipment (“the equipment”) which he was required to transport between his home and the sheds as follows (T4):

Three handpieces at 1kg each: 3kg

Tool box and shoes: 11kg

Power cord: 2kg

Short tube: 0.3kg

Grinder: 10kg

Shearing plant: 27kg

Sling: 4kg

Water bottle: 6kg

Fan: 4.5kg

Tucker box: 5kg



With the exception of the shearing plant, the Applicant brought these items to the hearing room and, in the course of giving oral evidence, identified each item and demonstrated its proper handling.

  1. At the hearing, the Applicant accepted the Respondent’s contention that the fan, the esky and water bottle are items of a private nature; it was also agreed that the shearing plant was taken to only one shed during the relevant year.
  2. The Respondent contended that the sling was an item of a private nature. The sling was used by the Applicant to support the weight of his chest whilst he was bent over to shear the sheep; he said that the sling alleviated the strain on his lower back and assisted him to conserve energy, and moreover that all experienced shearers used slings.
  3. Under the union award, shearers were required to provide their own handpiece, comb and cutters. The Applicant told the Tribunal that occasionally farmers did provide the shearers with equipment but that this equipment was often substandard and the shearers preferred to use their own equipment in order to increase their work rate. The Applicant said that he kept tools and equipment at his home with which he would clean and maintain the shearing equipment.
  4. The Applicant said that theft of shearing equipment was very common in the sheds and that he stored his equipment at his home in a reinforced garden shed with a lock on the door. He said that of the 29 sites at which he worked during the relevant year, only one had a shed that could be locked, and that it could not be locked while there were live animals in it due to the risk of fire.
  5. The Applicant stated that he spent some time each day after work (aggregating approximately two to three hours per week) maintaining his equipment at home and that this involved sharpening the combs and the cutters, removing wool from the handpiece, changing safety clutches, and repairing any broken equipment.
  6. Other relevant aspects of the Applicant’s income producing activities were noted by the Applicant in his submissions (Exhibit A1):
Travel all over the place from home each morning, Mr Gaydon cannot choose to live near one client, he has to travel to different places each year in all different directions.

Equipment stored, maintained and worked on at home prior to commencement of engagement - importance of properly maintained equipment.

Needs to organise work (ie ring farmers) make phone calls, negotiate contract rate, conditions and requirements eg: may negotiate travel and vehicle allowances (see award). May contract other shearers to make up team. Storage of shearing plant and tools, clean, repair and maintain shearing plant, prepare tools for work.


  1. It is useful at this juncture to note the Tribunal’s findings in respect of various factual questions which are relevant to the nature of the operations carried on by the taxpayer for the production of his income, and therefore to the essential character of the relevant expenditure.
  2. In respect of the sling used by the Applicant, the Respondent contended that it is a private or domestic item and that it should not be considered to be a tool of trade or an item of equipment. In this context, the Tribunal notes that the Applicant estimated that 97 per cent of shearers use slings, and that the remaining three per cent consisted of young and inexperienced shearers. The Tribunal also notes that after the completion of the hearing the Applicant forwarded to the Tribunal a copy of a facsimile dated 17 September 1998 from National Grazing Services Pty Ltd to the Applicant’s accountant, stating:
“Re: Telephone Conversation - Shearers Back Brace Case: NS 98/36

We are the largest administration body for shearing contractors in Australia. We strongly encourage all our shearers who work in contracting teams to wear a back brace as it saves our Insurance Company large claims for back injuries, plus it increases the overall output per day.”


  1. The sling is used by the Applicant whilst he shears sheep and is directly relevant to the activities by which he produces his income. The sling is not used for any other purpose than that of shearing sheep, and is properly characterised as an item required by the Applicant to do his work. The Tribunal finds that the sling is not an item of a private nature, and that it should properly be regarded as an item of equipment which is necessary for the Applicant to have and to operate in order to perform his shearing duties.
  2. In respect of the adequacy of the storage facilities provided by the farmers, the Respondent contended that whilst the level of security may have been low compared with other work sites in general, other factors such as isolation and alternative storage areas made the work sites sufficiently secure. The Applicant stated that theft was commonplace for shearers and that the security of the sheds was altogether inadequate; in particular, the Applicant said that the sheds were usually located in isolation from the farmer’s house and were therefore susceptible to theft during the night.

The Tribunal accepts the Applicant’s evidence and finds that a secure area for the storage of equipment was not provided at any of the Applicant’s workplaces during the relevant year.

  1. In respect of the Applicant’s equipment, the Respondent contended that the Applicant’s equipment was not bulky and that the items which are considered to be tools of trade could comfortably be carried in a regular sized toolbox or container. For the reasons stated above, I regard the sling as a necessary item of equipment. Including the sling, the total weight of the items constituting the Applicant’s necessary equipment is approximately 30 kilograms. In Crestani v FC of T (AAT 13169, 11 August 1998), I considered that an aircraft engineer’s toolbox weighing 27 kilograms was bulky, stating at paragraph 7:
“I do not think that the term “bulky” should be construed to refer only to an article which is of large size, such as the musical instruments which were the subject of the decision in FC of T v Vogt 75 ATC 4073. The term is, in my view, more aptly to be construed as similar to “cumbersome” in the sense that it is not easily portable.”


  1. The question whether an item or set of equipment is bulky will generally depend on the weight of the equipment and the relative ease of transporting or carrying it. In the present case, the shearer’s equipment is heavier than the toolbox in Crestani; moreover, the Applicant stated that the most practical means of transporting the sling is to stack it in small parts into the boot of a vehicle rather than to place the parts in a bag which would then have to be placed across the back seat of a vehicle. The Tribunal finds that the Applicant’s equipment, having regard to its weight and the difficulty of transporting it, is bulky, and that the use of a vehicle is essential to convey the equipment to the sheds.

THE LEGISLATION

  1. The issue to be decided by the Tribunal is whether the Applicant’s vehicle expenses in respect of travel between his home and the various shearing sheds, and the telephone expenses, are deductions allowable under s. 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 which provides:
“All losses and outgoings to the extent of which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income.


DEDUCTIBILITY OF THE TRAVEL EXPENSES

  1. The submissions on behalf of the Applicant were outlined in Exhibit A1 and can relevantly be set out in these Reasons as follows:
In our view the facts relating to Mr Lindsay Gaydon are remarkably similar to the facts in Case 29, before No. 3 Board of Review, whose views were adopted in Tax Ruling IT2273.

Since that time, the Commissioner has repealed IT2273 to the extent that it relates to employee shearers and, issued ruling TR95/34 Income Tax: employees conveying itinerant work - deductions, allowances and reimbursement for travel expenses.

In our opinion, the interpretation of the Commissioner in TR95/34 does not properly reflect the law set by the Board of Review in S29. The circumstances in the case are also very different to FCT v Genny’s (sic) 1987 before the Federal Court, on which the Commissioner relies.

Findings of Board, and in particular of Dr Gerber were:

The circumstances were exceptional, within the exceptions of (sic) Lunney case as set out in Garrets case.

The taxpayers home constituted their base of operation in that they sought employment from day to day; in addition, they were the places where they kept their equipment in working order.

That on the basis of the organisation test, the taxpayers were individual contractors rather than employees.

The nature of the employment, was classically an itinerant one.

Genys’ Case

The court concluded that the mere receipt of telephone calls from an agency requesting a respondent to work a particular shift is not sufficient enough to constitute the respondents home a place of work.

The facts in this case are very different. Mr Gaydon’s responsibilities extend to storage, maintenance and preparation of his equipment. He does not merely receive phone calls from an Agency in the case of Genys’ but actively pursues opportunities from existing client farmers and where necessary, new shearing opportunities. He also contacts other shearers to make up a team.

The case in Genys’ did not consider the issue of bulky equipment.


  1. The decision in Lunney v FC of T (1958) 100 CLR 478 is always the starting point in travel deduction cases. Lunney established that, in general terms, travel from home to work is not deductible under section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 because it is not incurred in the derivation of assessable income, but is rather incurred in order to enable the taxpayer to derive assessable income. There have emerged (since Lunney) examples of situations where travel expenses have been allowed as deductions, notwithstanding that they were incurred in travelling between a place of residence and a place of work.
  2. These situations were summarised in the judgment of Northrop J in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Genys (1987) 17 FCR 495 at 498:
However, the general proposition laid down in Lunney (supra), notwithstanding that it remains good law, is not exhaustive. In Garrett v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1982) 58 FLR 101; 82 ATC 4060, the Supreme Court of New South Wales constituted by Lusher J, held that it had no application to the following situations:

(a) where the taxpayer keeps necessary equipment or instruments at his home which he needs for the purpose of performing his work, and by reason of its bulk, such equipment needs to be transported by vehicle from the home to his place or places of work and where the equipment is used at home;

(b) where the taxpayer incurs expenses for travel between two places of business or work; and

(c) where the employment can be construed as having commenced at the time of leaving home.

A fourth situation, not enunciated in Garrett (supra), is where the taxpayer travels between home and shifting places of work, that is, an itinerant occupation.


  1. The Tribunal notes at the outset that it agrees with the Respondent’s submission that the Applicant’s travel expenses do not fall within the third of the exceptions listed above by Northrop J. In particular, the Applicant’s situation can be distinguished from that of the taxpayers in Owen v Pook (1970) AC 244 and FC of T v Collings 76 ATC 4254; these cases involved taxpayers who received telephone calls at their homes which had the effect of triggering the commencement of their employment duties such that their subsequent travel to a workplace occurred in the course of their work duties. The Applicant in this case also makes and receives telephone calls from his home; however, whilst these calls may be relevant to whether his home is also a place of work or base of operations (as discussed below), these calls did not signal the commencement of the “per head” shearing duties in respect of which he received payment from the farmers. Consequently, when the Applicant travels to the sheds, he does so in order to commence an aspect of his employment rather than to complete an aspect of his employment already under way before the journey commences.
  2. The first of the exceptions listed above by Northrop J refers to the deductibility of travel expenses involving the carrying of equipment between home and work. This situation was considered in FC of T v Vogt 75 ATC 4073 which involved a professional musician who was employed to perform at a particular social club and was also employed by a band which carried out rehearsals and conducted performances at a variety of locations; the taxpayer maintained his equipment and practised at his home. In allowing the deductions, Waddell J held at page 4063 that:
“where a taxpayer, for example, keeps necessary equipment or instruments reasonably at his home and which he needs for the purpose of performing his work at his various places of work and, by reason of their bulk or otherwise, these need to be transported by vehicle from the home to such places, and where the equipment was used at home for purposes of practice, the expenditure was deductible within the section [sec. 51(1)], even though the taxpayer was also thereby transported. This is because the expenditure incurred was part of the operations of earning the income and was essential to the carriage of the instruments rather than the taxpayer's own travel.”


  1. Waddell J outlined the matters which he considered relevant to a determination of the essential character of the expenditure at page 4078:
"Firstly, the expenditure was incurred as part of the operations by which the taxpayer earnt his income. Secondly, it was essential to the carrying on of those operations: there was no other practicable way of getting his instruments to the places where he was to perform. Thirdly, in a practical sense, the expenditure should be attributed to the carriage of the taxpayer's instruments rather than to his travel to the places of performance.''


  1. In Genys, Northrop J considered the deductibility of travel expenses incurred by a nurse who worked on a sub-contract basis with various hospitals in and around Brisbane; the taxpayer was registered with an employment agency which contacted the taxpayer by telephone and often at very short notice to inform her of available shifts. Northrop J approved Rath J’s statement in Collings, derived from the decision in Taylor v Provan, that “if a man has several places of work, travelling between them constitutes travelling on his work, even if one of the places of work is also his place of residence”; Northrop J then stated at page 4881:
“Hence, the fact that one's residence is one's choice does not prevent the residence being regarded as a place of work. The element of choice is the location of one's residence and except in the simple case of a regular daily journey, does not automatically deny a taxpayer a deduction. To interpret sec. 51(1) of the Tax Act in this manner is to read into it a ''preconceived limitation''.

The question that falls to be decided by this Court is whether, on the facts of the present case, the taxpayer's home could be said to be her place of work. Whether a home constitutes a taxpayer's place of work necessarily depends on the nature and extent of the activities undertaken by the taxpayer in the home.”


  1. The Tribunal agrees with the Respondent that, for the purposes of these Reasons, the question of whether there is a distinction between a “place of work” and a “base of operations” is not material. As stated in the Respondent’s Written Submissions (“Submissions”):
“The important characterisation is that the nature and extent of the work carried out at home is sufficient to make the home a place of work or base of operations, because a taxpayer actually performs their regular work duties there.”


There was some discussion during the hearing as to whether the Applicant is properly, and in relation to the farmers, to be regarded as an independent contractor or an employee; this issue was discussed at some length by the Board of Review in Case S29 85 ATC 276 upon which the Applicant sought to rely. As Dr Gerber (Member) pointed out in Case S29 at page 278, the various tests applied by courts to distinguish between independent contractors and employees were devised in the context of a determination of the circumstances in which a master is vicariously liable for the torts of servants in the course of their employment. I do not think it is either necessary or relevant to reach a finding on this issue in order to decide whether, having regard to the nature of the Applicant’s income-producing activities, his home is a place at which he performs regular work duties.



  1. The Tribunal does not agree with the Respondent’s submission that the Applicant was necessarily an “employee” in respect of the 29 different employers during the relevant year; the fact that PAYE tax was deducted is not conclusive. The Tribunal notes in particular in this context that the Applicant does not receive sick pay or leave pay; he is not paid during periods in which he is unable to shear due to adverse weather; he frequently dispenses with the provisions of the Award in order to secure employment, and he does not receive notices of termination of employment. It is also relevant to note that Exhibit R1 indicates that two of the group certificates were issued by the same farmer for two separate shearing engagements during the relevant year.

Apart from these considerations, the Applicant obtained his income in the relevant year by means of 30 separate shearing contracts. The securing of these contracts was an indispensable part of his income producing activities. These features of the Applicant’s income producing activities are of necessity relevant in any consideration of the characterisation of expenditure referable thereto.

  1. The Respondent’s Submissions included the following paragraphs under the heading “Is the Applicant’s home a place of work”:
“The applicant is not performing his work duties merely by keeping informed about working conditions over the phone, and his duties certainly do not commence until he arrives at the work shed.

The nature of the other activity which the applicant performs at home, maintaining his tools, would be only an incidental task to his occupation as a shearer. He does not shear sheep at home. The activity undertaken is not to a sufficient extent to maintain that he had a place of work at home. The choice to maintain his tools whilst at home is his own.

...

There is nothing in the applicant’s tasks of making phone calls or maintaining equipment which makes the home a place of work. The mere undertaking of incidental tasks in the home does not make it a place of work. The fact is that the applicant cannot perform his work at home, as a shearer’s place of work is the shearing shed, the only place where the sheep are physically available.”


The Tribunal notes that the Respondent’s Submissions were prepared before the hearing and that during the hearing it transpired that the Applicant’s work-related telephone activities were not restricted to keeping informed about working conditions.

  1. As the Respondent submitted, the fact that the Applicant does not shear sheep at home is an important factor in deciding whether his home is a place of work; however, it does not follow that the Applicant does not perform any work at home. The question whether the Applicant’s home could be said to be his place of work, or base of operations, must be decided having regard to the nature of the Applicant’s income-producing activities and the extent of the work-related activities performed at his home: see in this context the passage quoted from Genys above.
  2. The work-related activities undertaken by the Applicant at his home are extensive. The Applicant is required to store his tools at home because of the lack of security at the sheds and because he does not work at any single shed for extended periods. He also maintains his tools at home at the end of each working day; such maintenance is not of a general nature but is directed towards the specific employment activity he is then undertaking and in particular the type of sheep he will be expected to shear on the following day. Furthermore, he uses his home as a base from which to make and receive telephone calls in order to secure employment. In this context, it is also important to have regard to the fact that the Applicant is paid on a “per head” basis, that the duration of the Applicant’s shearing engagements are irregular and unpredictable, and that his income is dependent both on his reputation as a shearer and his skills on the telephone. In my view, the work-related activities which the Applicant undertakes at home are all indelibly interwoven into the Applicant’s income-producing activities, and these activities transform the Applicant’s home into a place of work or a base of operations.
  3. The Tribunal finds that the circumstances in which the expenses were incurred, like the exceptional circumstances outlined in Vogt, enable the Applicant to succeed. The Applicant’s home is the base from which he travels to various sheds; the lack of security at the sheds requires him to store and maintain his equipment at home; the bulkiness of his equipment requires him to travel to work by motor vehicle; all of these factors bring the Applicant within the principle established by Vogt. The Applicant’s travel is not merely a prerequisite to the earning of his income but is an essential feature of his work duties which include duties undertaken both at the sheds and at his home. To paraphrase the judgment of Waddell J in Vogt at page 4078, the mode of the Applicant’s travel was simply a consequence of the means which he employed to get his equipment to the sheds, that is by carrying them in the motor vehicle which he drove. Accordingly, the essential character of the expenditure was such that it should be regarded as having been ''incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income''.
  4. The Respondent’s representative requested that the Tribunal include in its Reasons for Decision a finding as to whether the Applicant’s work is inherently “itinerant”; although I have already found that the travel expenses are deductible, I shall therefore consider this issue.
  5. In Genys, after holding that the receipt of telephone calls from the agency was not sufficient to constitute the nurse’s home as a place of work, Northrop J turned to “the other exception to Lunney, namely whether the taxpayer is engaged in itinerant employment” (at page 4881). At pages 4881-2, His Honour approved the following passages in the judgment of Brightman J in Horton v Young (1972) Ch. 157 at pages 163-4:
"There are, however, some occupations in which the self-employed person does not have any location which can readily be described as his place of business but, rather, a number of places at which from time to time he exercises his trade or profession. It seems to me that there is a fundamental difference between a self-employed person who travels from his home to his shop or his office or his chambers or his consulting rooms in order to earn profits in the exercise of his trade or profession and a self-employed person who travels from his home to a number of different locations for the purely temporary purpose at each such place of there completing a job of work, at the conclusion of which he attends at a different location. I do not think it matters in the latter type of case whether the taxpayer does nor does not effectively carry on any trade or professional activities in his own home. The point is that his trade or profession is by its very nature itinerant. When the chimney sweep leaves his home in the morning and goes from house to house with the aid of his car or van, it appears to me unrealistic to deny that he incurs all such travelling expenses wholly and exclusively for the purposes of his trade. There must be plenty of other self-employed persons whose jobs are similarly itinerant. The test cannot be whether the job keeps the taxpayer at a particular location for perhaps two hours, as in the case of the chimney sweep, or three weeks, as in the case of Mr Horton.

In my view, where a person has no fixed place or places at which he carries on his trade or profession but moves continually from one place to another, at each of which he consecutively exercises his trade or profession on a purely temporary basis and then departs, his trade or profession being in that sense of an itinerant nature, the travelling expenses of that person between his home and the places where from time to time he happens to be exercising his trade or profession will normally be, and are in the case before me, wholly and exclusively laid out or expended for the purposes of that trade or profession. I have used the adverb 'normally' because every case must to some extent depend on its own facts. ''


Northrop J proceeded to hold that the nurse’s employment situation could not be regarded as itinerant because she was not required to travel between different places of work after the commencement of her duties. He stated at 4883:

“By contrast, in the situations referred to in Horton v. Young, the taxpayer (whether a salesman, plumber, chimney sweep, etc.) travels from home to work, but also travels from that first place of work to other places of employment throughout the course of the day before his return home. This was also the case with the schoolteacher in Wiener, where the taxpayer travelled to five different schools per day, not one school per day for five days (which would make it comparable to the present case).

In conclusion, in my opinion, the mere fact that the taxpayer in this case does not have a regular place of employment in the sense of a permanent employment at one hospital is not sufficient to take her outside the general principles expressed in Lunney.”


  1. The Respondent’s submission in respect of the question “Is the applicant an ‘itinerant’ worker?” was as follows:
“In the applicant’s case, travel is not a fundamental part of his work. Whilst he travels to and from work each day, he does not travel on work. His duties involve the shearing of sheep, and he does not need to travel in his car in the performance of these duties. Nor is there continual movement between shearing sheds. He travels to work at a particular shed and returns home from there at the end of the day.

...

Whilst the applicant works in a number of different places, the work site for each day is generally known prior to the day and a site may be worked at for several consecutive days. Most importantly, only one work site is visited on each day, and there is no continual movement between farms. Under such circumstances the applicant could not be considered to be ‘inherently itinerant’.”


  1. In Wiener, Smith J cited with approval the following passage from the decision of Lord Simon of Glaisdale in Taylor v Provan [1975] AC 194, at page 4009:
"Applying the rule in Ricketts v. Colquhoun (1926) A.C. 1 - i.e. that the obligation to incur the expenses of travelling in question must arise out of the nature of the office or employment itself, and not out of the circumstances of the particular person appointed to the office or employed under the contract of employment - two different classes of travelling expenses readily come to mind. The first is where the office or employment is of itself inherently an itinerant one. Examples are various sorts of inspectorate (say of weights and measures or to check stock) or commercial travel or supervision of duties carried out by local subordinates. In such cases the taxpayer may well be travelling in the performance of the duties of the office or employment from the moment of his leaving home to the moment of his return there - a visit to any head office might well be purely incidental or fortuitous. The second class of case is where the taxpayer has two places of work and is required by the nature of his office or employment to travel from one to the other.


  1. I do not think that because the Applicant does not travel between different sheds on any one day his employment cannot be regarded as itinerant. Having regard to the extent of the work-related activities undertaken by the Applicant at his home, it is clear that the home - rather than each of the sheds at which he worked - is his base of operations. This is the principal distinction between his situation and that of the nurse in Genys whose base of operations shifted between the different hospitals at which she was employed.
  2. In this case, the following facts satisfy me that the Applicant is an “itinerant”:
    • (i) his home is the locus in quo of his trade;
    • (ii) as the map included in Exhibit R1 indicates, he undertakes work at a web of workplaces in the Dubbo region;
    • (iii) his sequence of workplaces, and the period of time he spends at each workplace, both vary and cannot be predicted with any degree of certainty; and
    • (iv) he is required to carry bulky equipment to work.

I note also that the farmers are required by the Award to pay a travel allowance to shearers who reside at home and travel daily to the sheds.

  1. I therefore agree with the decision of the Board of Review in Case S29, and find that the Applicant’s employment is “inherently itinerant” and that all of the travelling in issue must be regarded as arising out of the nature of the employment itself.
  2. Paragraphs 21 to 74 of Taxation Ruling 95/34 provide a useful summary of the characteristics which indicate that an employee is engaged in itinerant work. The Tribunal notes that it agrees with the submission advanced in the Respondent’s Additional Written Submissions that paragraphs 63 and 64 of that ruling restate the concepts relevant under general law, and that the ruling does not necessarily apply to the Applicant’s travel arrangement in a manner which differs from that which would obtain under the general law.

DEDUCTIBILITY OF THE TELEPHONE EXPENSES

  1. The Applicant initially claimed deductions for telephone expenses incurred in respect of telephone calls to renew contracts and secure employment with farmers, and also in respect of telephone calls to make arrangements in relation to wet days, start times and the equipment required. Prior to the hearing, the Respondent decided to allow a deduction in respect of the telephone calls made to a farmer during the course of the Applicant’s shearing engagement with that farmer.
  2. At the hearing, the issue to be decided by the Tribunal was whether the Applicant is entitled to deductions for telephone expenses in respect of the renewal of contracts, and generally securing employment with farmers. The Respondent submitted that such calls occurred at a point too soon to be properly regarded as incurred in gaining assessable income. The Respondent argued that the Applicant is an employee, and that at the conclusion of one engagement he is in effect considered to have ceased one employment, and to be looking for other work. The Respondent referred to FC of T v Maddalena 71 ATC 4161 in which the taxpayer, who earned his living as an employee electrician and as a professional footballer, claimed deductions for travel and legal expenses incurred in seeking and obtaining a contract with a new and different Rugby League Club. It was held by the Full High Court that moneys spent to obtain new employment are not allowable deductions for income tax purposes.
  3. It was contended on behalf of the Applicant that he ought to be considered as an independent contractor, or as self-employed, and that the success of his job is dependent on his ability to obtain work whilst on the telephone; accordingly, the Applicant submitted that the telephone calls are an inherent feature of his production of assessable income.
  4. In Maddalena, it was held that the occupation of a professional footballer vis-a-vis his football club is as an employee; as stated above, I have held that the Applicant is not necessarily an employee of each of the 29 farmers during the relevant year. The Applicant makes telephone calls to secure short-term work engagements; moreover the terms and conditions in respect of each engagement vary; and the duration and the remuneration received by the Applicant in respect of each engagement depends on the weather and the speed with which the shearing team performs its job. Unlike the taxpayer in Maddalena, the Applicant’s telephone calls are a regular and indispensable feature of the means by which he produces assessable income. Accordingly the telephone expenses claimed should be allowed.
  5. It follows of course that it is unnecessary for me to consider the penalty which should be remitted.
  6. Accordingly, the objection decision under review is set aside, and the Respondent is directed to assess the Applicant in respect of the relevant year in accordance with these Reasons.
