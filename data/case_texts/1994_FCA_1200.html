Peter Graeme Hutchins v Kenneth Howard Collins, Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1994] FCA 1200; (1994) 123 ALR 133 (1994) 94 Atc 4442 (14 July 1994)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

PETER GRAEME HUTCHINS v. KENNETH HOWARD COLLINS, DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF

TAXATION

No. VG443 of 1993

FED No. 448/94

Number of pages - 7

Administrative Law

[1994] FCA 1200; (1994) 123 ALR 133

(1994) 94 ATC 4442

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

VICTORIA DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

JENKINSON J

CATCHWORDS

Administrative Law - Administrative Decision (Judicial Review) Act 1977 (Cth) - "Decision" within Act's application - "Under an enactment" - Commissioner of Taxation - Decision to vote against motion for acceptance of composition at creditors' meeting under Part X of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 not made under an enactment.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss. 8, 208, 209

Australian Broadcasting Tribunal v. Bond [1990] HCA 33; (1990) 170 CLR 321

Australian National University v. Burns [1982] FCA 191; (1982) 43 ALR 25

Australian Wool Testing Authority Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1990] FCA 361; (1990) 96 ALR 756

General Newspapers Pty. Ltd. v. Telstra corporation [1993] FCA 473; (1993) 117 ALR 629

James v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1957] HCA 36; (1957) 97 CLR 23

Re W. Carter Smith; ex parte Commissioner of Taxation [1908] NSWStRp 49; (1908) 8 SR (NSW) 246

Smiles v. Commissioner of Taxation [1992] FCA 208; (1992) 35 FCR 405

Strictly Stainless Pty. Ltd. v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (Federal Court of Australia; unreported; judgment 5 November 1993)

Terrule Pty. Ltd. v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation No. 4 (1985) 5 FCR 153

The Hells Angels Ltd. v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (1984) 85 ATC 4028

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 8 February 1994

14:7:1994

Counsel for the Applicant : Mr. P.M. Bornstein

Solicitors for the Applicant : Jeffrey P. Salinger and

Associates

Counsel for the Respondent : Mr. K.H. Bell

Solicitor for the Respondent : Australian Government

Solicitor

ORDER

The Court orders that:

1. The objection to competency notice of which was filed on 1

December 1993 be upheld.

2. The proceeding be dismissed.

3. The applicant pay the respondent's costs (including costs

reserved) of the proceeding.

(Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.)

DECISION

JENKINSON J Objection to the competency of an application under the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 for an order of review in respect of the respondent's decision that his vote be cast against a motion put to a meeting of the applicant's creditors.

2. The applicant was at material times indebted to the Commonwealth in the sum of $954,105.06 for income tax and other liabilities accrued under income tax legislation. The applicant having authorised a registered trustee to call a meeting of his creditors under Part X of the Bankruptcy Act 1966, there was put to a meeting of the applicant's creditors called in pursuance of that Part a motion for a special resolution that the creditors accept a composition of his debts. The respondent decided that the Commonwealth would vote against the motion and the vote of the Commonwealth was cast accordingly. The motion was not carried by the majority required by s.204(1)(c) of the Bankruptcy Act 1966. If the vote of the Commonwealth had been for the motion, or if the Commonwealth had abstained from voting, the motion would have been passed as a "special resolution", within the meaning of that expression in the Bankruptcy Act, with the consequences which Part X ordains.

3. The respondent's decision so to exercise the Commonwealth's right as a creditor to vote is said by Mr. Bornstein of counsel for the applicant to be "a decision of an administrative character made .... under an enactment", and so subject to review under the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1971 ("the Judicial Review Act"). The enactment under which the decision was made was said to be sections 8 and 209 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. Section 8 provides:

"The Commissioner shall have the general administration of this

Act."

Section 209 provides:

"(1) Any tax unpaid may be sued for and recovered in any Court of

competent jurisdiction by the Commissioner or a Deputy

Commissioner suing in his official name.

(2) In subsection (1), 'tax' includes additional tax under section

207 or Part VII."

Those two sections should be read, Mr. Bornstein submitted, in the light of s.208 and of what has been said of the three sections in James v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1957] HCA 36; (1957) 97 CLR 23, The Hells Angels Ltd. v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (1984) 85 ATC 4034 and Terrule Pty. Ltd. v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation No. 4 (1985) 5 FCR 153. The latter two cases provide authority for the conclusion that a decision by a delegate of the Commissioner of Taxation to institute a curial proceeding for recovery of income tax due and unpaid is "a decision of an administrative character made ...... under an enactment", within the meaning of those words in the Judicial Review Act. The "enactment" indicated was one or several of ss. 8, 208 and 209 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. Section 208 provides:

(1) Income tax when it becomes due and payable shall be a debt due

to the Commonwealth, and payable to the Commissioner in the

manner and at the place prescribed.

(2) In subsection (1), 'income tax' includes additional tax under

section 207 or Part VII."

The first of the three cases provides authority that s.209 authorises not only suit for recovery of the debt but also a petition for sequestration of the debtor's estate in bankruptcy. So, too, in Mr. Bornstein's submission, do sections 8 and 209 authorise a decision to vote for or against such a motion as s.204(1)(c) contemplates.

4. The reasoning which was approved by the High Court in James v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (1957) 97 CLR at 34-35 was that of Street J, in Re W. Carter Smith; ex parte Commissioner of Taxation [1908] NSWStRp 49; (1908) 8 SR (NSW) 246 at 249-250, who in construing words relevantly indistinguishable from those in s.209(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 found a legislative intention "not to limit the available remedies for the recovery of the tax, but to clothe the Commissioners with power to resort to any tribunal competent to assist them in recovering amounts which might be owing".

5. To exercise a right conferred on creditors by s.204 of the Bankruptcy Act 1966, to vote upon a motion for acceptance of a composition, is not to resort to a tribunal, in my opinion. The decision whether or not to exercise that right, and the decision whether to vote for or against such a motion, are not in my opinion decisions "under", that is "in pursuance of" or "under the authority of" s.208 or s.209. Section 8, to which Northrop J drew attention in The Hells Angels Ltd. v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (No. 4) (1984) 85 ATC at 4028, 4039, and which in Australian Wool Testing Authority Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1990] FCA 361; (1990) 96 ALR 756 he identified as the enactment in pursuance of which the Commissioner had made a decision affecting a taxpayer, might be identified as the enactment in pursuance of which the Commissioner or his delegate would make such a decision as is here in question. But, as was pointed out in Australian National University v. Burns [1982] FCA 191; (1982) 43 ALR 25 at 36, a general power of management or administration conferred by an enactment may be said to make susceptible of review under the Judicial Review Act a "vast array" of decisions which it seems unlikely that Parliament intended to be subject to such a review.

6. In Australian Wool Testing Authority Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation, supra a Deputy Commissioner had by letter advised a taxpayer that its income was considered to be exempt income under s.23(h) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 and that accordingly it was not necessary that the taxpayer lodge returns of its income. Later the Commissioner by letter advised the taxpayer that its income was considered not to be exempt and that the earlier contrary advice "is withdrawn". Northrop J observed (96 ALR at 763-764):

"Counsel for the Commissioner contended that each of the letters from

the Commissioner contained an expression of opinion only and that an

expression of opinion was not a decision. Further, on that basis, it

was contended there was no decision under an enactment within the

meaning of the Judicial Review Act. Counsel relied upon opinions

expressed by the High Court in Australian Broadcasting Tribunal v. Bond

[1990] HCA 33; (1990 94 ALR 11. In that case, the Chief Justice discussed at some

length the meaning to be given to the word 'decision' in the Judicial

Review Act. It is not necessary to refer in detail to what his Honour

said. It is sufficient to say that his Honour took the view that a mere

expression of opinion or a statement which could of itself have no

effect on a person was not such a decision. The decision, however, need

not be a final decision. A decision being an intermediate determination

could, in appropriate circumstances, be a decision within the meaning of

the Judicial Review Act.

In their joint judgment Toohey and Gaudron JJ, in speaking of the

Judicial Review Act, said (at 53-4):

'It does not follow that, because s 5 is not confined to acts

involving the exercise of or a refusal to exercise a substantive

power, the acts which constitute a decision reviewable under s 5

of the ADJR Act are at large. They are confined by the

requirement in s 3(1) that they be made "under an enactment". A

decision under an enactment is one required by, or authorised by,

an enactment: cf Australian National University v Burns 1982) 43

ALR 25; [1982] FCA 191; 64 FLR 166. The decision may be expressly or impliedly

required or authorised: see Minister for Immigration and Ethnic

Affairs v. Mayer [1985] HCA 70; 1985) 61 ALR 609; 157 CLR 290 at 302-3; Chan v

Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs [1989] HCA 62; (1989 87 ALR 412 at

429; [1989] HCA 62; 63 ALJR 561 at 571. If an enactment requires that a

particular finding be made as a condition precedent to the

exercise of or refusal to exercise a substantive power, a finding

to that effect is readily characterised as a decision "under an

enactment". However, it is otherwise with respect to findings

which are not themselves required by an enactment but merely bear

upon some issue for determination or some issue relevant to the

exercise of a discretion. Findings of that nature are not

themselves "decisions under an enactment"; they are merely

findings on the way to a decision under an enactment.'

In the present case, the letter of 4 January 1990 does disclose a

decision made by the Commissioner. That decision which was that the

applicant need not lodge returns of income unless specifically requested

to do so, is of a substantive character, and is of an administrative

nature made under an enactment namely s 8 of the Assessment Act. For

similar reasons, the letter of 14 June 1990 discloses at least one

decision, the revocation of the decision referred to in the letter of 4

January 1990. It follows, in my opinion, that the letter of 14 June

1990 does contain evidence of a decision of an administrative nature

made under an enactment, namely s 8 of the Assessment Act involving the

construction and application of s 23(h) of the Assessment Act to the

applicant and the request of the applicant that it need not lodge a

return of income.

In my opinion, the express provisions of s 8 of the Assessment Act, by

implication at the very least, should be construed as conferring upon

the Commissioner a power to make a decision that the income of

particular persons is exempt income under s 23 of the Assessment Act and

thus relieving those persons from the obligations of lodging a return of

income. Any such decision need not be included in any notice under s

161 of the Assessment Act but can be published to the person concerned.

Where such a decision is made and communicated, that decision is a

decision under an enactment pursuant to the Judicial Review Act. This

conclusion is supported by views expressed in Minister for Immigration

and Ethnic Affairs v Mayer [1985] HCA 70; (1985) 61 ALR 609."

7. Mr. Bell of counsel for the respondent based the objection to competency primarily on the statement by Davies and Einfeld JJ, in General Newspapers Pty. Ltd. v. Telstra Corporation [1993] FCA 473; (1993) 117 ALR 629 at 634, "that to be subject to review under the ADJR Act an action or refusal to act must amount to an ultimate or operative determination which an enactment authorises or requires, and thereby gives it statutory effect". The statement was made in support of conclusions that decisions by a statutorily constituted corporation to enter into contracts with a corporation constituted under company legislation were not decisions "under" the section of the constitutive statute which conferred on the former corporation capacity to enter into contracts. The statement was justified principally by reference to the observations made by Mason CJ in Australian Broadcasting Tribunal v. Bond [1990] HCA 33; (1990) 170 CLR 321 at 335-339 concerning the meaning of the word "decision" in the Judicial Review Act. One sentence in those observations is in my opinion particularly apt in the resolution of this objection to competency. Mason CJ said (170 CLR at 737) that "a reviewable 'decision' is one for which provision is made by or under a statute". I do not consider that a provision such as s.8 can be understood as making provision for any of the many decisions which will be made in exercising the authority which that section confers on the Commissioner. Section 8 of course contemplates the making of decisions by the Commissioner, but it does not in my opinion make provision for any of them, in the sense which I understand the Chief Justice to have intended in the sentence I have quoted. The function of the section is merely to nominate the person by whom decisions of the character described in the section are to be made. So, too, it may be said that an enactment empowering a body corporate to make contracts does not make provision for decisions concerning the exercise of the capacity it confers.

8. If I am wrong in my opinion that decisions of the Commissioner and his Deputies for which the only authority is s.8 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 are not made "under an enactment", yet I think the decision as to the exercise of the creditor's vote in this case lacks a characteristic which Northrop J found to be present in the decision he was considering in Australian Wool Testing Authority Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation, supra. He saw the decision evidenced by the Commissioner's letter to be "of a substantive nature since it deprived the applicant of a benefit which had been conferred upon it by" the earlier letter, that benefit being freedom from the obligation to lodge income tax returns. Here the decision as to the casting of the creditor's vote could not be seen to confer any benefit or to impose any disadvantage when it was made. The disadvantageous consequences of the decision arose only when the votes of all the creditors were cast and the disadvantage was caused by the cumulative effect of all the negative votes.

9. My conclusion is that the decision to vote against the resolution is not subject to review under the Judicial Review Act. That conclusion requires that the objection to competency be upheld. No statutory provision other than ss. 8, 208 and 209 was suggested as the enactment under which the decision about voting might have been made. Other submissions were advanced for and against the objection. One submission on behalf of the respondent was that the reasoning in Bond's Case, supra and the General Newspapers Case, supra led to the conclusion that a decision by the Commissioner of Taxation to institute an application for an order for the winding up of a company owing an income tax debt was not a decision reviewable under the Judicial Review Act. Such a conclusion has been drawn by Davies J in Strictly Stainless Pty. Ltd. v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (Federal Court of Australia; unreported; judgment 5 November 1993). His Honour in that case said:

"In my view there is nothing in the nature of an ultimate or operative

determination, or of a substantive determination, in the step of issuing

a summons, which is the first procedural step in the course of

proceedings in a court."

It is unnecessary to express a concluded opinion on the point. But I would take a decision to institute a curial proceeding for recovery of a debt, or for a winding up order, to be an "ultimate", "operative" and "substantive" determination, in the sense intended by Mason CJ in the passages which were quoted from his reasons for judgment in Bond's Case by Davies and Einfeld JJ in the General Newspapers Case and by Davies J in the Strictly Stainless Case. The language used by the Chief Justice is to be understood having regard to the subject with which he was dealing : whether any one or more of a series of findings and rulings and discretionary decisions on the way to the reviewable decision that certain licensees were no longer fit and proper persons to hold their broadcasting licences was itself a decision reviewable under the Judicial Review Act. The contrast being drawn was that between the decision which has effect of itself and the decision which, however crucial in its influence on the outcome of the inquiry being undertaken by the Australian Broadcasting Tribunal, was no more than a step along the way to the decision which has effect of itself. That antithesis does not in my opinion provide an answer to the question whether decisions to institute legal proceedings, whether criminal (as in Smiles v. Commissioner of Taxation [1992] FCA 208; (1992) 35 FCR 405) or civil (as in the Strictly Stainless Case) are reviewable decisions. As at present advised I do not think the decisions in The Hells Angels Case, supra and Terrule's Case, supra to be inconsistent with what was said in Bond's Case or with what was decided in the General Newspapers Case. It may be, however, that neither The Hells Angels Case nor Terrule's Case can stand with this statement by Davies and Einfeld JJ in the General Newspapers Case (117 ALR at 633):

The ADJR Act provides the structure for judicial review, which is

review, not of acts taken under the general law applicable in the

community, but of acts which have statutory effect because of the

provisions of a federal enactment. Thus, a 'decision' taken under a

federal enactment is an action or a refusal to act which, by virtue of

the statute, affects legal rights and/or obligations."

10. There is a further and different question about the correctness of the decision in Terrule's Case. Having reconsidered my reasons for judgment in that case I now have doubt about the correctness of the conclusion I there expressed that s.209 confers on the Commissioner and on a Deputy Commissioner not only power to bring curial proceedings for the recovery of income tax but also power to decide that such a proceeding shall or shall not be instituted against a particular person at a particular time. I see more force now than when I rejected it in the submission of counsel for the Deputy Commissioner in Terrule's Case that s.209 did not make provision for decisions of that character, but merely authorised suit by the Commonwealth for recovery of income tax by the Commissioner of a Deputy Commissioner suing in his official name, and that no other section of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 made provision for such a decision. I have already given my reasons for thinking that s.8 does not make that provision. On the view for which counsel for the Deputy Commissioner contended in Terrule's Case the decision whether or not to institute the suit for recovery of the tax is an exercise of the executive power of the Commonwealth conferred by s.61 of the Constitution.

11. The objection to competency is upheld and the proceeding is dismissed with costs.
