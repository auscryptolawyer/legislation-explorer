Re Gerhardt Walter Kolb and Nancy Kolb Ex Parte: Richard Anthony Fountain England and the Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1994] FCA 1145 (7 June 1994)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

RE: GERHARDT WALTER KOLB and NANCY KOLB

EX PARTE: RICHARD ANTHONY FOUNTAIN ENGLAND AND THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF

THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

No. SG171 of 1988

FED No. 359/94

Number of pages - 7

Bankruptcy - Costs

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

SOUTH AUSTRALIA DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

BANKRUPTCY DISTRICT OF THE STATE OF SOUTH AUSTRALIA

BURCHETT J

CATCHWORDS

Bankruptcy - set-off under s. 86 - claim of Commissioner of Taxation to set-off against his liability to refund to a bankrupt deductions under the Prescribed Payments System, amounts owed by a partnership of which the bankrupt was a member, being deductions made under the same scheme and deductions of Group Tax - Commissioner claiming entitlement to set- off under s. 221YHG(2)(b)(ii) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, and alternatively under s. 86 of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 - application of s. 86 to statutory debts - effect of s. 86(2) - meaning of "giving credit" and "receiving credit" - whether Division 2 of Part VI of the Income Tax Assessment Act excludes the operation of s. 86 of the Bankruptcy Act.

Costs - circumstances in which, though the application was dismissed, no order was made as to costs.

Bankruptcy Act 1966, s. 86

Bankruptcy Rules, r. 106

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss. 221YHF(1)(b) and 221YHG(2)(b)(ii)

Gye v. McIntyre [1991] HCA 60; (1991) 171 CLR 609

Day and Dent Constructions Proprietary Limited (in liquidation) v. North

Australian Properties Proprietary Limited (provisional liquidator appointed) [1982] HCA 20; (1982) 150 CLR 85

R v. Miller (1977) 3 All ER 986

Taylor v. Commissioner of Taxation (1987) 16 FCR 212

In re D.H. Curtis (Builders) Ltd (1978) 1 Ch 162

Re Cushla Ltd (1979) 3 All ER 415

HEARING

SYDNEY, 13 and 14 October 1993

7:6:1994

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr R.J. Whitington

Solicitors for the Applicant: Messrs Barratt Lindquist

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr F.P. Carnovale

Solicitor for the Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

1. The application be dismissed.

2. There be no order as to costs.

NOTE: Settlement and entry of Orders is dealt with in Rule 124 of the Bankruptcy Rules.

DECISION

BURCHETT J The applicant is the trustee in bankruptcy of the estates of Gerhardt Walter Kolb and Nancy Kolb who, prior to the sequestration orders made on 15 February 1988, were in partnership in the business of builders. They had become indebted to the Commissioner of Taxation in respect of deductions made by the partnership from prescribed payments to various subcontractors, and also in respect of deductions made by the partnership from wages paid to various persons employed by it. At the same time, the bankrupt Gerhardt Walter Kolb was entitled to claim a credit, as against the Commissioner, on the basis that deductions made and forwarded to the Commissioner from prescribed payments to which he personally was entitled, during the year ended 30 June 1987, exceeded the income tax payable by him for that year by the sum of $17,148.59. The month before the making of the sequestration orders (but after the presentation, on 18 December 1987, of the petition against Mr Kolb), on 22 January 1988, the Commissioner issued a document in the form of an assessment to Mr Kolb showing that the credit to which he was entitled had been applied, as to the sum of $10,146, to the partnership's account in respect of deductions under the Prescribed Payments System, and, as to $7,007.59, to the partnership's account in respect of deductions of Group Tax. This action of the Commissioner, subject to an amendment it is unnecessary for present purposes to discuss, set the scene for the dispute with which these reasons are concerned.

2. Following the making of the sequestration orders, Mr England, as the trustee of the bankrupt Mr Kolb, wrote on 10 August 1988 asking that the sum of $17,148.59 be forwarded to his office. On learning what the Commissioner had done, Mr England wrote to request details of the authority on which the money was applied to the particular obligations of the partnership in question. After the lapse of some time, he was advised by a letter which may have been dated 20 September 1988 that the "authority to apply the credit in this way is contained in section 221H(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act". On 22 February 1989, Mr England wrote denying the right of the Commissioner to rely on that section. The response was a new claim by the Commissioner:

"Authority for the transfer of credit is contained in Section

221YHG(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act and not Section

221H(2) as previously advised."

On 7 June 1990, Mr England wrote to the Commissioner a detailed refutation of his claim now made under s. 221YHG(2), and again asked for payment of the credit. The Commissioner's answer, sent on 23 August 1990, almost entirely ignored the questions raised by Mr England's letter. It consisted in substance of a somewhat surprising appeal (in all the circumstances) to "good faith ... in the ordinary course of business". In it, the assertion was made: "We (sic) were unaware of the extent of Mr and Mrs Kolb's financial difficulties at the time the credit assessment issued." Mr England replied on 10 September 1990, pointing out that notice of a meeting of creditors had been sent to all creditors on 23 December 1987, a statement which has never been disputed by the Commissioner. (In the proceedings before me, it was also admitted that the Commissioner became aware on 18 December 1987 of the presentation of the petition against Mr Kolb.) The Commissioner contented himself with a reply, given on 11 March 1991, asserting: "We have obtained a legal opinion from the Australian Government Solicitor on this matter and it is considered that the application of the credit of $17,148.59 to the Prescribed Payment and Group Tax accounts was correct." Whether the legal opinion depended on any of the three grounds successively raised in the correspondence, or on yet another ground, was not disclosed. After a formal demand, these proceedings were instituted.

3. In the proceedings, a "Notice of Intention to Appear at Hearing of Application" was filed by the Australian Government Solicitor, as required by rule 106 of the Bankruptcy Rules and form 46 in Schedule 1 to the Rules, setting out the grounds on which it was intended to oppose Mr England's application for payment by the Commissioner of the sum of $17,148.59. That notice relied on s. 221YHG(2)(b)(ii) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 as the Commissioner's authority to apply the credit in the way in which it had been applied.

4. But just over a month before the hearing, the Commissioner, for the first time, raised the question whether he was entitled to resist the trustee's claim on the basis of s. 86 of the Bankruptcy Act 1966. Even then, this was not done by an amendment of the notice filed under rule 106, but in the course of stating the Commissioner's contentions in a document sent to the trustee's solicitors called a Statement of Facts and Contentions. No amendment was made or sought to be made, before the hearing, to the grounds raised in opposition to the application. At the hearing, I granted leave to the Commissioner to amend to raise the point under s. 86 of the Bankruptcy Act, while indicating that the course taken might be relevant to costs.

5. Section 86 provides:

"(1) Subject to this section, where there have been mutual

credits, mutual debts or other mutual dealings between a

person who has become a bankrupt and a person claiming to

prove a debt in the bankruptcy:

(a) an account shall be taken of what is due from the one

party to the other in respect of those mutual

dealings;

(b) the sum due from the one party shall be set off

against any sum due from the other party; and

(c) only the balance of the account may be claimed in the

bankruptcy, or is payable to the trustee in the

bankruptcy, as the case may be.

(2) A person is not entitled under this section to claim

the benefit of a set-off if, at the time of giving credit to

the person who has become a bankrupt or at the time of

receiving credit from that person, he had notice of an

available act of bankruptcy committed by that person."

6. The section was the subject of detailed consideration in Gye v. McIntyre [1991] HCA 60; (1991) 171 CLR 609. The joint judgment of the High Court (at 623) explains that there are three aspects of the requirement in the section of mutuality - that the credits, debts or claims in question "be between the same persons"; that "the benefit or burden of them lie in the same interests"; and that, so far as the section refers to "other dealings", the credits, debts or claims "must ultimately sound in money", or as it has been said, they must be "commensurable". But the joint judgment also makes it clear (at 625-626) that the first requirement of mutuality does not mean that other parties may not be involved:

"The critical matters for the purposes of s. 86 are that there

had been dealings in which the creditor and the bankrupt

were both involved and that those dealings gave rise to

mutual claims between them in the relevant sense."

7. What is involved in mutual credits and mutual dealings was stated in the joint judgment by reference to the decision, given well over a hundred years ago, in Naoroji v. Chartered Bank of India (1868) LR 3 CP 444. The joint judgment says of that case (at 624):

"Byles J remarked that mutual credits are `reciprocal demands

which must naturally terminate in a debt' (emphasis added).

Montague Smith J said that, to bring a case within the set-off

provision, `it is not necessary that the credits should

be dependent the one upon the other, nor that there should

have been any agreement beforehand'. He added that the

operation of the provision was to require that an `account

shall be taken ... of all such credits and dealings as in

the natural course of business would end in debts, and the

balance shall be the debt due from the one to the other'

(emphasis again added)."

The joint judgment also (at 625) makes it clear that "dealings" within s. 86 are matters "having a commercial or business flavour", and a very wide scope should be given to this part of the section. Indeed the section itself, their Honours go on to say, "should be generously construed".

8. This approach, and the reliance placed on the old decision in Naoroji, echoes the judgment of Gibbs CJ in Day and Dent Constructions Proprietary Limited (in liquidation) v. North Australian Properties Proprietary Limited (provisional liquidator appointed) [1982] HCA 20; (1982) 150 CLR 85 at 95, where the Chief Justice (with whom Aickin J agreed) said:

"However, I consider that no more was meant (by statements in

certain earlier authorities) than that there may only be a

set-off in respect of `all such credits and dealings as in

the natural course of business would end in debts' to use

the words of Montague Smith J in Naoroji v. Chartered Bank

of India ... . The modern authorities favour this view -

that it is enough that the dealing would naturally, and

does, terminate in a debt. It is well understood that the

law which relates to the set-off of mutual dealings in

bankruptcy, which has a long history, exists to prevent the

injustice of a man who has had mutual dealings with a

bankrupt from having to pay in full what he owes in respect

of such dealings while only receiving a dividend on what the

bankrupt owed him in respect of them ... ."

9. With respect to the application of s. 86 to the facts of the present case, counsel for the trustee conceded that the relevant partnership liabilities relied on by the Commissioner of Taxation were joint and several liabilities; that there can be a set-off for the purposes of s. 62 of a separate liability against a joint and several liability; and that the liabilities in question in the present case are mutual within the meaning of the section. However, he disputed the application of s. 86 to statutory debts; that, even if it did generally have application to them, it could be applied to the taxation liabilities in question; and that the Commissioner was entitled to rely on it in a case where he claimed to have acted under s. 221YHG(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. Counsel argued also that s. 86(2), in the circumstances of this case, operated to exclude a set-off under s. 86(1).

10. I am unable to see any merit in the proposition that the Commissioner's reliance on s. 221YHG of the Income Tax Assessment Act debars him from relying on s. 86 of the Bankruptcy Act. Nothing in the nature of an election is involved. If the Commissioner's reliance on the taxation provision is misplaced, that provision may be put on one side, and the Commissioner may be seen as a creditor of the bankrupt Mr Kolb who, at the same time, owes money to the estate of the bankrupt. The question then is simply whether s. 86 applies to the situation in hand.

11. So far as s. 86(2) is concerned, counsel for the trustee contended that the Commissioner gave credit to the partnership of which Mr Kolb was a member when deductions became payable to him but were not immediately paid over, whether under the prescribed payments scheme or in respect of wages paid to employees. It was said this continued to occur after the Commissioner had notice of an available act of bankruptcy committed by Mr Kolb. He had that notice, at latest, by 18 December 1987 when the petition was presented. But the Commissioner took no step, nor did he enter into any agreement, having the effect of postponing the obligation of the partners to remit to him the deductions they had made from prescribed payments or from wages. In R v. Miller (1977) 3 All ER 986, Roskill LJ (delivering judgment on behalf of himself and Scarman LJ and Griffiths J) distinguished between the situation where parties agree upon a purchase for a cash consideration, but stipulate that the purchaser shall have credit for the price for a reasonable time, which is a true case of credit being given, and the situation where, a payment being due, the debtor simply fails to pay for some period. His Lordship said:

"There is, in our view, on the happening of the default no

giving of credit by the person to whom the instalment is

due. On the contrary the finance company, on the happening

of the default, acquires an instant cause of action for the

overdue instalment. This seems to us the antithesis of

giving credit, which, as has been pointed out in R v. Peters

(1886) 16 QBD 636 and in other cases, involves, in one way

or another, an agreement to postpone payment of what, in the

absence of such agreement, would be immediately due."

12. The trustee did not advance any analysis of the circumstances according to which the other potential aspect of s. 86(2), the receiving of credit by the Commissioner from Mr Kolb, could be said to have been involved in the present matter. There is certainly no suggestion that Mr Kolb agreed to the postponement of the Commissioner's obligation by as much as a single day. By s. 221YHF(1)(b), it is only upon an assessment being made of the tax payable, or alternatively the Commissioner being satisfied that no tax is payable, that a person in the position of Mr Kolb becomes entitled, by the operation of the statute itself, to a credit such as that which is involved here. There is no evidence that either of these things occurred before 22 January 1988. Indeed, it seems clear that that was the relevant date. It follows that s. 86(2) does not deny the Commissioner entitlement to claim the benefit of the set-off asserted by him.

13. The argument that s. 86 does not apply to statutory obligations of the nature of those created by the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act in question here seems to me to be inconsistent with the English cases In re D.H. Curtis (Builders) Ltd (1978) 1 Ch 162 and Re Cushla Ltd (1979) 3 All ER 415, the principle of which was accepted by the High Court in Gye v. McIntyre (supra, at 633-634). The joint judgment of the High Court (ubi cit.) expressly denies any general proposition that s. 86 is confined to claims arising out of contract, and it also asserts (at 619) that s. 86 is a provision that should be given "the widest possible scope". In my view it does extend to the claims to which this case relates.

14. The suggestion that the taxation provisions constitute a code excluding the Bankruptcy Act is based on the decision of the Full Court in Taylor v. Commissioner of Taxation (1987) 16 FCR 212. But I think it misreads the judgment of the members of the Court. That case was concerned with the effect, in relation to a bankruptcy, of the provisions of Division 2 of Part VI of the Income Tax Assessment Act, concerned with the collection of tax by deductions from wages payable to employees. This is the division which includes s. 221H. The Full Court took the view, as appears from the judgment of Jenkinson J at 223, that Division 2 involves a scheme

"designed to utilise the amounts deducted in a process of

precisely prescribed set-off which is to be completed

without regard to what might otherwise be the operation of

the bankruptcy laws in relation to the employee. Whatever

remains unpaid at the conclusion of that process will be the

measure of any provable debt, in my opinion."

What this means, translated to the present case and assuming that the decision may be applied to Division 3A as well as to Division 2, is that the bankruptcy laws cannot intrude upon the process by which the Commissioner, on 22 January 1988, arrived at the result that Mr Kolb was entitled, under the prescribed payments scheme, to a refund of $17,148.59. But so to conclude is not to deny that s. 86 then operated in a way that affected the ultimate destination of the moneys to be refunded. By the comment that "(w)hatever remains unpaid at the conclusion of that process will be the measure of any provable debt", Jenkinson J clearly indicated that the figure produced by the operation of Division 2 was not itself immune from the operation of the Bankruptcy Act. Accordingly, s. 86 is able to require such a figure to be set off against other obligations.

15. I conclude that the Commissioner is entitled to succeed by virtue of s. 86. That makes it unnecessary to decide the abstruse questions which were argued in relation to the alleged application of s. 221YHG of the Income Tax Assessment Act.

16. The remaining question is costs. The amount involved is not large, but the principle concerned in relation to s. 86 of the Bankruptcy Act is of importance to the Commissioner for other cases. So far as s. 221YHG of the Income Tax Assessment Act is concerned, that too, of course, is important to him. So one aspect of the matter is the difference between the parties so far as the significance of the decision of this case is concerned. Another is that the Commissioner played a considerable part in bringing about the litigation. Initially, and again at a later stage, he raised inappropriate justifications for his action. When he did raise the difficult defence under s. 221YHG, he gave no explanation how, according to his contention, the provision operated, and did not deign to discuss the trustee's attempt at analysis of it.

17. The argument ultimately presented on his behalf under that section, if right, suggests its drafting makes it little better than a trap for the unwary, and in my view the Commissioner had some responsibility to be more forthcoming than simply to tell the trustee, as he finally did, that his legal advice justified him. (I interpolate that the Commissioner should seek to have the section amended, if he wishes to rely on it in cases of this kind so that its application to them can be reasonably understood.) So far as s. 86 of the Bankruptcy Act is concerned, this defence was only raised at the eleventh hour.

18. In the circumstances, the application is dismissed, but I make no order as to costs.
