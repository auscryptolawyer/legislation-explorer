Chandra Dahia and Tax Agents' Board of Victoria [1997] AATA 265 (29 July 1997)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

CHANDRA DAHIA v. TAX AGENTS' BOARD OF VICTORIA

No. V97/221

AAT No.

12070

Number of pages - 6

Taxation



COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

G.L. McDONALD (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - cancellation of registration as a tax agent - agent convicted for failure to lodge personal returns - applicant failing to respond to notices issued by Tax Agents' Board and directions of Tribunal - no other complaints made against applicant - whether applicant fit and proper - whether suspension more appropriate than cancellation

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ss.37, 41(2)

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss.251A, 251BC(3), 251K(2)(d), 251QA

Taxation Administration Act 1953 ss.8C(1)(a), 8E(2)

Re Houvardes and Tax Agents' Board of New South Wales 91 ATC 2032

Re Prudzinski and Tax Agents' Board of Victoria (unreported, Decision No. 11,882, delivered 23 July 1997)

Re Su and Tax Agents' Board of South Australia 82 ATC 4284

Re Tighe and Tax Agents' Board of Victoria 96 ATC 2016

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 24 July 1997 (hearing), 29 July 1997 (decision)

29:7:1997

Counsel for the applicant: Mr S. Maguire

Solicitor for the applicant: Brenda J. Cooke

Solicitor for the respondent: Mr S. McBurney, Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The Tribunal:

1. sets aside the decision under review, and remits the matter to the Tax Agents' Board with the direction that the registration of the applicant be suspended until 31 December 1997; and

2. orders that the implementation and operation of this decision be stayed until 12 August 1997.

DECISION

G.L. McDONALD

1. This is an application to review a decision dated 19 November 1996 of the Tax Agents' Board of Victoria ("the Board") cancelling the applicant's registration as a tax agent, pursuant to the provisions of s.251K(2)(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Tax Act"). The authority for the Tribunal to review the decision is to be found in s.251QA of the Tax Act.

2. At the commencement of the hearing the applicant was represented by Ms B. Cooke, a barrister and solicitor, but later in the same day Mr S. Maguire, of counsel, took over the conduct of the case. Mr Dahia gave oral evidence in support of the application. Mr S. McBurney, a solicitor from the Australian Government Solicitor, represented the respondent. The respondent called no oral evidence. The Tribunal had before it the documents filed for the purposes of s.37 (the "T" documents) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act"), a copy of the applicant's income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1995 and a document generated by the National Tax System dated 23 July 1997, detailing the applicant's history with respect to the filing of his tax returns, covering the years 1987 to 1997.

THE LEGISLATION

3. Section 251K(2)(d) of the Tax Act is as follows:

" 251K (2) A Board may suspend or cancel the registration of any tax agent upon being satisfied that-

. . .

(d) if the tax agent is a natural person - the tax agent is not a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters;

. . . ."

4. It is worthwhile outlining the history of this matter with the Tribunal. The application was lodged on 24 February 1997. A letter dated the same date signed by the applicant requested that the matter be heard urgently and that the Tribunal direct a stay with respect to the decision until the hearing. The respondent consented to a stay, which the Tribunal granted on 28 May 1997. On the same day, at a telephone directions hearing in which the applicant participated, the Tribunal directed the applicant to file witness statements for all witnesses whom he proposed to call at the hearing of his matter, on or before Tuesday, 17 June 1997, and further directed that the matter be listed for hearing on 24 July, commencing at 10:00 a.m. No witness statements were filed. Two days prior to the commencement of the hearing the applicant contacted the Tribunal by facsimile requesting an adjournment. A telephone directions hearing was arranged on that date between the applicant and the respondent. The applicant's reason for seeking the adjournment was that he had been delayed on an overseas trip, which he had originally believed would take a week or a week and a half, and which apparently took over three weeks. It appears that he did not leave for that overseas trip until at or about 17 June, the date on which he had been directed, in any event, to file witness statements. Given-



* the applicant had requested an urgent hearing of his matter and the Tribunal had complied with that request,



* the applicant had failed to comply with the Tribunal's Direction of 28 May 1997, in that he had not filed his witness statements and had not contacted the Tribunal to seek a variation of the Direction,



* a stay had been granted,

the Tribunal refused the application for adjournment.

5. On the day of the hearing the applicant's solicitor renewed the application for an adjournment. The respondent opposed that application. The Tribunal determined to refuse the application and stood the matter down in order for the applicant's solicitor to obtain instructions to allow the matter to proceed or be withdrawn. Upon resumption, the application for adjournment was renewed and again refused by the Tribunal. The matter was further stood down for the applicant's solicitor to obtain instructions. Upon resumption, Mr Maguire appeared for the applicant. He obviously, because of the short time involved, had not had the opportunity to obtain detailed instructions and the matter, again, was stood down until the afternoon to allow Mr Maguire to obtain instructions. The matter ultimately proceeded to hearing.

6. It was conceded on the part of the applicant that he had not filed his personal income tax returns for the tax years ending June 1993 and 1994 on time and that, as a consequence, he had been prosecuted for two offences under the provisions of s.8C(1)(a) of the Tax Administration Act 1953 ("the Tax Administration Act"). The respondent also alleged that the applicant had failed to respond to a "show cause" letter from the respondent as to why his registration should not be cancelled, and that the applicant had failed to lodge his taxation return for the tax year ending June 1995. There was some dispute over the latter two matters and in relation to the allegation that the applicant failed to lodge his tax return for the 1995 year, the Tribunal gave the applicant time in which to produce verification as to whether or not this had occurred (e.g. production of an assessment form). Subsequent information filed at the Tribunal indicates that the applicant filed his return for the 1995 tax year, but that the return was either incomplete or some of the details included in the return, possibly the relevant tax file number, were incorrect. The respondent concedes that the applicant did attempt to lodge a return for the 1995 year.

7. The applicant was born in Fiji and is now an Australian citizen. He is an accountant with qualifications in Business Administration and Law, which he obtained in New Zealand. He has been working in Australia since 1986. He has worked in international accounting firms, both in Fiji and in Australia. In 1991, following the amalgamation of Deloites Ross Tohmatsu, he was retrenched. He told the Tribunal that there was an economic downturn at that time and that he was unable to secure equivalent permanent employment. He and his wife had two young children. His wife, who is a pharmacist, was, presumably because she was looking after the children, unable to work at that time. Thereafter the applicant took a number of consultancy positions, which he told the Tribunal involved him in providing taxation advice. He said that, while he continued to try to find permanent work, including applying for jobs in Sydney and Perth, not any were forthcoming. As time went by, he determined that he should commence his own practice. He currently operates that practice from an office at home, and also from an office in the city.

8. The applicant told the Tribunal that he has 30 to 50 clients and that part of his work with those clients he is required to file tax returns. He told the Tribunal that he had been engaged to assist one client whose business had suffered as the result of one or more employees embezzling funds. He said that he had been doing substantial work, including going overseas, for a number of years with that business in order to assist it in recovering its position, but that the business had not been able to pay him any money, or any sum that would reflect the amount of work he had undertaken for it. Now that the business was apparently returning to a profitable situation, the applicant hoped that he would be appropriately reimbursed. He said that a number of clients had had difficulty paying him for the work he had done on their behalf.

9. The applicant told the Tribunal that, as a result of his inability to earn sufficient income to support his wife and children, he was forced to rely on family and friends for financial support. He said that this had been embarrassing and he had found it particularly hard to deal with given culturally based expectations, particularly from his wife's family, that he should succeed and be seen to have succeeded in providing sufficient material support for his wife and family. He said that his inability to meet such expectations had had a "devastating" effect and, as a result, he became incapable of dealing with his own affairs, including as to the filing of his annual tax returns. He told the Tribunal that, in his efforts to overcome the problem, he had focussed more on satisfying the needs of his clients than on keeping his own affairs in order. This problem, he claimed, was reflected not only in his not filing tax returns for the years 1993 and 1994, but also in his inability to respond to final notices that his tax returns should be filed, to respond to show cause notices issued to him by the Board, to correctly complete his 1995 tax return and latterly to comply with the Directions of the Tribunal as to the preparation of the material necessary to support his application before it.

10. The Tribunal notes four favourable referee reports supporting the applicant's initial application for registration as a tax agent. The applicant has no criminal convictions other than those relating to his failing to lodge his income tax returns. There is no suggestion in these proceedings that any complaint has been lodged against the applicant for any matter by any of his clients.

11. The applicant was convicted on 20 August 1996 for two offences of failing to file his tax returns for the 1993 and 1994 tax years, contrary to the provisions of s.8C(1)(a) of the Tax Administration Act. At that hearing he was represented by a solicitor, pleaded guilty and was fined $400 on each count. The returns for the two years in dispute have subsequently been filed. The applicant had made losses for each of the 1993, 1994 and 1995 years and, consequently, there was no outstanding taxation liability.

12. The penalties applicable to offences under s.8C of the Tax Administration Act are contained in s.8E. Section 8E provides a sliding scale of penalties. An offence under s.8C is normally punishable by a fine not exceeding $2000: s.8E(1). Where a person has been previously convicted of such an offence, the maximum penalty for a further offence is a fine not exceeding $4000: s.8E(2). Where a person has previously been convicted of two or more such offences, the court may impose a penalty of a fine of not more than $5000 or imprisonment for a period not exceeding 12 months, or both: s.8E(3). The provisions governing the registration of tax agents are contained in the Tax Act. Section 251BC(1)(e) of the Tax Act, which deals with the initial registration of tax agents, provides that a person is not a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters if the person has been convicted of a "serious taxation offence" in the previous five years. A "serious taxation offence" is defined by s.251A of the Tax Act to include any offence against Part III of the Tax Administration Act (which includes all offences under s.8C). Accordingly, both offences for which the applicant was convicted constitute serious taxation offences for the purposes of the Tax Act. The powers of the Board with respect to the re-registration of tax agents are contained s.251BC(3). That section provides that, where the Board considers whether a person is a fit and proper person to be re-registered as a tax agent, it may disregard a conviction incurred by the applicant if it is satisifed that special circumstances exist: s.251BC(3)(c).

13. It is clear that in the context of the Tax Act, a tax agent who himself or herself does not comply with the provisions in relation to the operation of their own affairs places himself or herself at risk of a finding that he or she is not a fit and proper person for registration as tax agent. His Honour Mr Justice Davies made this quite clear in Re Su and Tax Agents' Board of South Australia 82 ATC 4284 when he said at 4287:

" A tax agent who allows his own tax affairs to get into a state of disorder, who has constant problems himself with the Taxation Department, may not be a proper person to handle clients' affairs for there may come a time when dissatisfaction which officers of the Department may have with the tax agent personally may be reflected in their handling of his clients' affairs. Clients who seek extensions of time should not be embarrassed by the fact that the tax agent acting for them is himself continually late in complying with the time limits imposed by the Act and the regulations. Undoubtedly, even minor offences, if sufficient in number, can so interfere with the tax agent's standing that he is rendered not a fit and proper person to be registered as a tax agent."

Despite that decision being reached under the then provisions of the Tax Act, which have subsequently been amended, the statement or principle which it raises remains relevant (see comments of the Tribunal in Re Prudzinski and Tax Agents' Board of Victoria (unreported, Decision N( 11,882, delivered on 23 July 1997).

14. There may be many reasons why a person is not a "fit and proper person" and without seeking to limit the application of that term, the reasons why a person is fit and proper may not necessarily reflect adversely on the person's honesty or integrity. For example, a person may develop a psychiatric illness which renders him or her not a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns. The Tribunal is not suggesting the latter circumstance is present in the instant case.

15. The exercise of a discretion to disqualify a person from being a tax agent is not to be used in punishment of that person (see comments of O'Connor J in Re Houvardes and Tax Agents' Board of New South Wales 91 ATC 2032 at 2039). The purpose of the power to disqualify tax agents' is to protect the public. Each case needs to be considered having regard to its own particular circumstances. Whilst no two cases will be the same, other decisions on this point are instructive. In Re Houvardes (supra) there were two principal issues which the Tribunal considered, namely the commission by the applicant of "serious tax offences", and whether or not the agent had neglected the business of his principal to the detriment of a number of clients. The latter allegations concerned a number of complaints lodged on behalf of clients of the tax agent's business. In that case the Tribunal found that the applicant had committed a number of serious tax offences, had made false statements to the Board in respect of those matters on more than one occasion and he had seriously neglected his clients' interests. In Re Tighe and Tax Agents' Board of Victoria 96 ATC 2016 the Tribunal affirmed a decision of the Board to cancel the registration of the agent on the basis that group tax from his professional practice had not been remitted. The agent had been convicted and fined in relation to 27 offences of failing to remit group tax. In Prudzinski (supra) the applicant had been convicted of two counts of stealing choses in action (namely, a cheque that was credited for an incorrect amount, and the proceeds then misapporpriated by the applicant) for large sums of money. This along with other factors adverse to the applicant, including a favour to lodge annual returns, led the Tribunal to the view that disqualification was appropriate in order to protect the public.

16. In the instant case, while the applicant has not produced any medical evidence as to his psychological or other condition, the Tribunal accepts his evidence that he has been under considerable pressure with respect to his ability to provide for a young family since his retrenchment in 1991. The Tribunal also accepts that he has been undertaking work for firms (one in particular) which have not been able to pay him or pay him sufficiently to maintain even a modest standard of living. The latter aspect has resulted in an advantage to his clients, and also provides a basis for the applicant to establish a long term relationship which should be profitable to him as well as the client. The Tribunal also accepts the fact that he had to face that course of conduct at the hearing of this matter and that he apologised for the inconvenience he caused, which the Tribunal takes to indicate contrition on his part. He clearly now appreciates the consequences which may flow from neglecting the conduct of his own affairs vis-(-vis the Taxation Department.

17. Having regard to the total circumstances of this case, including the fact that there are no complaints by any client of the applicant, the Tribunal is satisfied that the decision to cancel the applicant's tax agent's registration when there was an alternative of suspension, was, in the circumstances, too harsh. In the view of the Tribunal, the appropriate penalty in the circumstances of this case is for the applicant's tax agent's registration to be suspended.

18. Accordingly, the decision under review is set aside, and the matter is remitted to the Board with a direction that the registration of the applicant be suspended until 31 December 1997. As the implementation of the decision under review had been stayed pending the determination of this review or until further order of the Tribunal made under s.41(2) of the AAT Act, the Tribunal now orders that the implementation and operation of this decision be stayed until 12 August 1997. This short further period is to enable the applicant to take the necessary steps to make appropriate arrangements for his clients.
