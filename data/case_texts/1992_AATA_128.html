Re Taxation Appeals [1992] AATA 128; (1992) 92 ATC 208; (1992) 23 ATR 1177 (15 April 1992)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. ST91/29

AAT No. 7909

Income Tax

(1992) 92 ATC 208

(1992) 23 ATR 1177

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

Dr P. Gerber(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Inocme Tax - Assessable income - Derivation of income - Profit on sale of land held as trading stock - Contracts executed one year and settled in another - Whether income derived in year contracts became unconditional or year of settlement - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1986, sub-sec 25(1).

Kingston v Preston (cited in Jones v Barkley (1781) 2 Dougl 684

Ruddenklau v Charlesworth (1925) NZLR 161

McDonald v Dennys Lascelles Ltd [1933] HCA 25; (1933) 48 CLR 457

The Commissioner of Taxes (SA) v The Executor Trustee and Agency Company of South Australia Ltd (Carden's case) [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108

Farnsworth v F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 27; (1949) 78 CLR 504

Henderson v F.C. of T. [1969] HCA 14; (1970) 119 CLR 612

J Rowe and Son Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. [1970] HCA 57; (1971) 124 CLR 421

St Hubert's Island Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. 78 ATC 4104

F.C. of T. v Australian Gas Light and Ors 83 ATC 4800 Sunbird Plaza Pty Ltd v Maloney [1988] HCA 11; (1988) 166 CLR 245

HEARING

ADELAIDE

15:4:1992

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the objection decision under review.

DECISION

Once upon a time there was a discretionary trust ("the Trust"), the trustee of which - "T Pty Ltd" - undertook a joint venture with another company ("C Pty Ltd") to subdivide and develop the Adelaide Mall into 88 allotments (the "Venture"). It is sufficient for present purposes to note that the applicant, together with his brother, owns all the shares in T Pty Ltd. In the year under review, the applicant was the ultimate beneficiary of the Trust, so that any adjustment to its assessable income from the Venture will flow through to the applicant and become part of his assessable income by virtue of sec 97 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act").

2. To flesh out the details (none of which were in dispute), contracts for 73 of the 88 allotments carved out of the Mall by Venture were executed between October 1984 and 17 June 1985. The contracts contained special conditions to the effect, inter alia, that:-

1. Notwithstanding anything hereinaftersaid, this

contract is subject to the acceptance, deposit and

registration of the plan of subdivision in the Land

Titles Office on or before the 30th day of July, 1985

...

2. Settlement shall be effected within fourteen days of the

issue of a Certificate of Title by the Registrar-General for

the said land or such other date as may be agreed in writing

by the parties ...

3. The Plan of Subdivision was registered on 25 June 1985. Nine of the 73 contracts were settled prior to 30 June 1985, the remaining 64 contracts were settled and accounted for in the subsequent financial year. It is the latter 64 contracts with which I am concerned.

4. Venture's Statement of Trading and Profit and Loss for the year ended 30 June 1985 disclosed no income from the 64 contracts as having been derived in that year. The dispute between the parties in this hearing thus arises from the fact that the accounts for the Venture only recognised income from the sale of land after settlement.

5. The respondent undertook an audit review of Venture, during which he concluded that it had understated its income for the 1986 year by some $188,353.

6. Messrs Touche Ross, the accountants for both the applicant as well as the various investment and family trusts wrote to the respondent on 28 October 1988, accepting both the factum of an understatement and quantum, but pointing out that the understatement had the consequence that the their client suffered an increase in his taxable income of $94,176 (i.e. half the purported understatement). By way of a plea in mitigation, the accountants pointed out that the understatement of income was caused by circumstances outside their control or that of their client, "and that it was only as a result of enquiries by our client and ourselves that the error was detected and corrected by the accountant of the Joint Venture". They requested that an amended assessment issue to their client.

7. Leaving aside the involved corporate structures and discretionary trusts, the issue in this case thus turns once again on the vexed question: when did Venture "derive" the proceeds from the sale of the 64 allotments? Put another way: are the proceeds from the sale of allotments, sold in the Mall pursuant to the relevant contracts and executed in the 1985 tax year, but settled according to the terms of the contracts in the 1986 tax year, derived in 1985 or 1986? The Commissioner adopts the view that income from the sale of land should be recognised for tax purposes in the (tax) year in which all conditions necessary to give the vendor a valid claim for the purchase price are satisfied, i.e. 1985. The taxpayer, on the other hand, submits the income is only derived when the contracts are settled, i.e. 1986. The Commissioner's view of the law led him to include as income of the joint venture an amount equal to the total of the sale prices of the 64 contracts on hand at 30 June 1985.

8. It was submitted by the applicant that the point is res integra. Being novel, Mr Robinson QC who, with Mr O'Loughlin, appeared for the applicant, tendered an opinion from one Brenton W Ellery, a chartered accountant of impeccable qualifications and a partner in the firm Edwards, Marshall and Co, Chartered Accountants, who stated that his considerable research into this question has led him to conclude that "neither the date of (an) unconditional contract, nor the date of settlement, are generally accepted as the proper method in Australia of recognising revenues from sale of real estate. It appears that each method has been adopted".

9. Turning to what law there is on the subject, a number of propositions can be advanced with some degree of confidence:

(i) For present purposes, there is no relevant

difference between contracts for the sale of

land and contracts for the sale of anything else.

(ii) The Act makes no distinction between traders

and professional men, albeit both groups are not

necessarily taxed alike (but see Latham C.J.

(diss) in The Commissioner of Taxes (SA) v The

Executor Trustee and Agency Company of South

Australia Ltd (Carden's case) (1938) 63 CLR

108);

(iii) the contracts in issue ("the relevant contracts")

are binding contracts in conditional terms, i.e.

the condition "subject to registration by a

certain date" has the effect that the parties are

bound at once, but the obligation may never

become enforceable since the condition may

defeat it.

(iv) the plan of subdivision, having been

registered in the Land Titles Office on 25 June

1985, the relevant contracts, having been

conditional contracts, henceforth became

unconditional and legally enforceable.

Thereafter, the purchasers became the owners

in equity, albeit if any purchaser had indicated

an inability or unwillingness to complete the

contract, Venture could elect to treat the

contract at an end, with all the consequential

rights which flow from the renunciation;

alternatively, it could elect to affirm the

contract on proof of both willingness and ability

to perform its part of the bargain on the due

date for performance; cf Kingston v Preston

(unreported, cited in Jones v Barkley (1781) 2

Dougl 684 at 689.

(v) given the nature and magnitude of the Venture's operation, the

land in question clearly constituted its trading stock (cf St

Hubert's Island Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. 78 ATC 4104).

10. Mr Robinson submits that, accepting the land is the Venture's trading stock, it remained such until completion; alternatively, the revenue from each allotment was "derived" for tax purposes when a recoverable debt had arisen. He added that the latter proposition did not depend on the land remaining Venture's trading stock after the contracts became unconditional.

11. "Completion" is not a term of art, it comprehends a discharge of the principal obligations of both parties, involving, amongst other things, execution and delivery of a memorandum of transfer in registrable form, delivery of an unencumbered certificate of title and payment of the purchase money.

12. Dealing with Mr Robinson's first submission; viz that the land, the subject of the relevant contracts remained Venture's trading stock until settlement, he sought much comfort from the decision of the Full High Court in Farnsworth v F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 27; (1949) 78 CLR 504. The facts of the case are taken from the headnote:

The taxpayer, a fruitgrower, delivered dried fruits

grown by her to a packing company to be dealt with

in accordance with the rules of a marketing

association of which she was a member. The fruit was

graded, processed and packed by the company, and

the processing involved the mixing of the taxpayer's

fruit with that of other growers so that it became

impossible to identify the fruit of any particular

grower. The fruit was sold by a selling agent some of

it prior to 30th June 1943 and the remainder

thereafter. The rules of the association provided for

the distribution of the proceeds of the fruit when

sold - less the expenses of marketing - among the

suppliers ratably according to parity in respect of

the grade, description and quantity of the fruit

supplied. Progress payments were made to the

growers from time to time, and in the financial year

which ended on 30th June 1943 the taxpayer

received such payments to the extent of pounds 340 in

respect of fruit supplied by her in that year. The

packing company made an estimate as at 30th June

1943 of the net proceeds which it anticipated would

become available for distribution on the completion

of the sale of the fruit supplied during the year

ending on that date, and it issued to each grower a

statement of the net balance of his estimated share.

A statement was accordingly issued to the taxpayer

in which her prospective share - after deduction of

the progress payments - was estimated at pounds 648. In

assessing the taxpayer's income derived during the

year ending on 30th June 1943 the Federal

Commissioner of Taxation included this sum of pounds 648,

as well as the pounds 340 actually received by her in that

year, in her assessable income.

Held that the sum of pounds 648 was wrongly so included.

The interest of the taxpayer as at 30th June 1943 in

the fruit in the pool was not, within the meaning of

s.28 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1943,

"trading stock on hand" of the taxpayer as at that

date. The sum of 648 was not the value of "trading

stock" within s.36 of the Act, and it was not on any

other ground part of the taxpayer's assessable

income of the year in question.

13. Rich J. found the case both interesting and complicated. His Honour concluded that:

A review of the facts leads me to the conclusion that

after a fruit grower has delivered his fruit to the

packing company and it has been processed he has

lost all control and power of disposition of it and his

fruit has become so inextricably mixed with the fruit

of other growers as to be incapable of identification.

The property in the fruit passed to the packing

company and became converted into a claim for an

account. The grower's interest is not an equitable

interest but an equity to an account enforceable by

action. (at p 515).

Dixon J. (as he then was) stated

In my opinion it is not possible to treat the sum as

the value of the taxpayer's stock in trade on hand at

the end of the accounting period. The taxpayer's

dried fruit had ceased to be "on hand" within the

meaning of s. 28 of the Income Tax Assessment Act

1936-1943 when she delivered it to the packing shed.

The packing house cannot be treated as a mere agent

of the taxpayer holding her stock in trade for sale.

The taxpayer's dried fruit had been delivered

irrevocably to the packing house upon terms,

complicated no doubt, which entitled her to a money

sum and left her with no dispositive power over the

fruit and no power to direct or control the disposal

by the packing house of the fruit either alone or in

combination with other suppliers. (at p 518).

14. It seems to me that the decision depends a good deal on its own peculiar facts. Thus Rich J. attached some significance to the fact that the taxpayer's fruit had become "so inextricably mixed with the fruit of other growers as to be incapable of identification (supra at p 515). Again, at p 516, his Honour noted "that the sum in question was merely a prophetic estimate of the share of the net proceeds of sale she might receive after the sale". In other words, a grower is not to be taxed on income quantified by crystal ball gazing. Be that as it may, the decision is authority for the proposition that goods cease to be a trader's "trading stock on hand" at the point where he loses his dispositive power over the goods, retaining a mere right to a sum of money in the future. If the property in the goods had passed to the purchaser in Farnsworth (supra), leaving the vendor with a mere claim for an account, Venture's position in the instant case does not appear to me to be so radically different, in the sense that if the thrust of Farnsworth (supra) is that goods cease to be "trading stock on hand" when a trader is no longer in a position to dispose of the goods, then the land, the subject of the relevant contracts, ceased to be Venture's trading stock after 25 June 1985, i.e. when the relevant contracts became unconditional, notwithstanding that the bundle of rights retained by Venture is substantially different from that of the taxpayer in Farnsworth (supra). Thus, on and after 25 June 1985, Venture still retained substantial interests in the subject land until settlement. Until completion (which the contract provided was to be within 14 days of the issue of a Certificate of Title for the said land or such other date as may be agreed, i.e. in the 1986 tax year), Venture retained both the legal title and an equitable interest in the land, including the right to possession until settlement, with the right to protect its interest in the land should anything endanger it. But whatever those interests, I am satisfied that Venture, in the absence of an anticipatory breach by a purchaser, was left - in the words of Dixon J. - "with no dispositive power over the land", or, in the words of Rich J. - "it had lost all control and power of disposition over it". Weighing up these various interests, it seems to me that the loss of dispositive power over the subject matter outweighs the substantial interests retained. In fiscal terms, possession is thus not nine-tenths of the law.

15. To counter this argument, Mr Robinson submitted that a vendor of realty does not lose his dispositive powers until settlement, if only because he can sell the land in circumstances where the purchaser, for example, indicates an unwillingness or inability to complete. It is an interesting argument but, with the utmost respect to Mr Robinson's plausibility, I have not been persuaded that a vendor of land has not lost his dispositve power on execution of the contract where final settlement is postponed merely because his power of sale may revive in exceptional circumstances.

16. I am therefore satisfied that the land ceased to be Venture's trading stock in the 1985 tax year. Does it follow that the taxpayer's claim must fail?

17. Mr Robinson's alternative argument (what he referred to as "the worst case scenario"), viz if I were to find that the land in issue had ceased to be Venture's trading stock in the 1985 tax year, his client was still entitled to succeed on the basis that a debt due from the purchaser to the vendor can only arise when (i) the date of settlement arrives; (ii) the vendor delivers to the purchaser an executed memorandum of transfer in registrable form which is then executed by the purchaser and (iii) the vendor delivers to the purchaser an unencumbered Certificate of Title.

18. Mr Robinson placed much reliance on Henderson v F.C. of T. [1969] HCA 14; (1970) 119 CLR 612. The facts of that case are too well known to require recapitulation. For present purposes, the issue turned on the method of calculating the income of an accounting business partnership. On appeal and cross-appeal from the decision of Windeyer J, the Full High Court remitted the matter back to the Commissioner for reassessment of the appellant's taxable income by including in his assessable income his share of the net earnings of the partnership. In his judgment, Barwick C.J noted:

In ascertaining such earnings, only fees which have

matured into recoverable debts should be included

as earnings. In presenting figures before (Windeyer

J.), allowance was made for what was termed "work in

progress". But this, in my opinion, is an entirely

inappropriate concept in relation to the performance

of such professional services as are accorded in an

accountancy practice when ascertaining the income

derived by the person or persons performing the

work. When the service is so far performed that

according to the agreement of the parties or in

default thereof, according to the general law, a fee or

fees have been earned, then it or they will be income

derived in the period of time in which it or they have

become recoverable. But until that time has arrived,

there is, in my opinion, no basis when determining the

income derived in a period for estimating the value

of the services so far performed but for which

payment cannot properly be demanded and treating

that value as part of the earnings of the professional

practice up to that time and as part of the income

derived in that period. ... I have used the word

"recoverable" to describe the point at which income

is derived by the performance of services. I ought to

add that fees would be relevantly recoverable

though by reason of special arrangements between

the partnership and the client, time to pay was

afforded. (at 119 CLR at 650-651)

19. Mr Robinson derived considerable comfort from the learned Chief Justice's observation that income is not "derived" if payment for same "cannot properly be demanded". This observation, however, was made against the background of "work in progress", which at general law, must have reached that degree of completion to have fructified into a recoverable debt. Having satisfied that test, "it or they will be income derived in the period of time in which it or they have become recoverable".

20. Applied to this case, Mr Robinson argued persuasively that Venture could not have demanded the purchase price at any time in the 1985 tax year, if only because no debt had yet arisen for which payment could properly be demanded.

21. It was submitted that a similar view prevailed in the later decision of F.C. of T. v Australian Gas Light Co and Ors 83 ATC 4800. The facts (taken from the headnote), were as follows:

Each of the taxpayers was a gas company in the

business of manufacturing and supplying gas subject

to the provisions of the Gas and Electricity Act, 1935

(NSW) and the regulations made thereunder. The

taxpayers were required to supply a meter to each

domestic user to measure consumption. The supply

of gas was on the basis of units which were recorded

by the gas passing through the meter supplied. The

tariff was struck in terms of a price per unit used in

the period of a quarter. The registration of a meter

was prima facie evidence of the quantity of gas used

by the consumer.

The quarterly reading of meters and the sending out

of accounts based on those readings was staggered

over different days throughout the year. As a result,

as at 30 June in any year, there were large numbers

of domestic consumers whose current quarter would

not have expired, whose meters would therefore not

have been read, and who would not receive accounts

until after the close of the quarter some time in the

ensuing financial year.

The taxpayers did not bring into their accounts for

any year ended 30 June any amount in respect of gas

consumed up to 30 June where the relevant meter

had not been read and an account based on that

reading issued.

The Commissioner considered that the value of such

gas (referred to as unbilled gas) was to be regarded

as assessable income of the taxpayers. The

taxpayers appealed to the Supreme Court of New

South Wales on the ground that no account should

have been taken of unbilled gas in determining their

assessable income. The Supreme Court allowed the

appeals on the basis that no debt or obligation on the

part of the consumer to pay for gas used during the

relevant quarter arose until the meter had been read

and an account based on that reading issued.

Accordingly, as there was no sale of unbilled gas in

the relevant quarter up to 30 June each year, the

unbilled gas supplied up to 30 June did not result in

the taxpayers deriving any income during the

relevant year within the meaning of sec. 25(1).

22. Bowen C.J., Fisher and Lockhart JJ., in dealing with the punctum temporis of derivation, noted:

There is no doubt that what the transactions with

consumers produce is income, but the question for

decision is when is that income derived? The

practice or method of accounting adopted by the

taxpayers was not to bring to account in respect of

years of income expiring on 30 June the revenue

which ultimately would be received on account of

unbilled gas. Thus what each taxpayer returned as

assessable income did not include the revenue which

it anticipated it would receive, and was contingently

entitled to receive, for gas supplied after the last

billing before 30 June and before that date. The

Commissioner submitted that, as the taxpayers had

done all that was required of them under their

obligation to supply gas during this period, they

should bring to account as additional sales the value

or estimated value of the unbilled gas. The

Commissioner placed particular emphasis on the fact

that the property in the gas supplied through meters

during this time had passed, and passed beyond

recall, to the customers.

Many tests have been propounded and many

expressions adopted by the Courts in attempting to

state when income is derived. Those tests have

inevitably been conceived in different circumstances

and to determine different facts and issues. The

following examples may be cited. The fees of

accountants are derived when they have matured into

recoverable debts: Henderson v F.C. of T. 70 ATC 4016;

[1969] HCA 14; (1970) 119 CLR 612. Fees paid in advance for

provision of dancing lessons are not derived until

they are earned: Arthur Murray (NSW) Pty Ltd v F.C. of

T. [1965] HCA 58; (1965) 114 CLR 314; 14 ATD 98. The income of a

trading business is derived when its stock is sold and

a debt is created: Rowe J and Son Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. 71

ATC 4157; [1970] HCA 57; (1971) 124 CLR 421. Conversely, fees for

the price of goods sold are not earned, and thus not

derived, if a further step is required before the

taxpayer is entitled to payment: Rowe's case. The

passing of property in stock in trade does not

necessarily signify the derivation of income if the

consequence is merely the creation of a right to an

account rather than entitlement to a debt:

Farnsworth v F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 27; (1949) 78 CLR 504; (1949) 9 ATD

33. If a taxpayer accounts on the basis of cash

receipts, the income is derived when the "gains have

during the period of account come home to the

taxpayer in a realized or immediately realizable

form": C of T (SA) v Executor, Trustee and Agency Co of

South Australia Ltd (Carden's case) (1938) 63 CLR

108 at p 155; [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 5 ATD 98 at p 132 per Dixon J.

These are some examples of the tests which the

Courts have used in determining the time when

revenue transactions are brought to account or

income is derived.

Helpful as these tests may be as signposts, each of

them has been conceived in and applied to varied and

contrasting circumstances. As signposts they

indicate that invariably something more than

provision of goods or services by the taxpayer is

required. It is necessary to determine whether the

consequence is that a debt has been created or

whether the taxpayer is obliged to take further

steps before becoming entitled to payment. It may

often be possible to reach the proper conclusion by

the application of these tests if the circumstances of

the taxpayer are unexceptional. However, the

taxpayers in this case operate under exceptional

circumstances. They contend that these

circumstances dictate the manner in which they bring

their revenue to account.

...

The consequence of the exceptional manner in which

the taxpayers operate is that as at 30 June in each

year their claims against customers for current

liabilities for gas supplied had not matured into

recoverable debts. (at pp 4804-4806).

23. Whilst the case seems to lend some support for Mr Robinson's argument, nevertheless I am satisfied that the res decisa turns on the fact that the gas company was subject to the mandatory provisions of the local Gas and Electricity Act and the Regulations made thereunder, which provided, inter alia, that:

Reg 42(1) A demand for payment for gas supplied

shall not be made by a gas company ... until an account

therefor has been rendered.

(2) An account rendered by a gas company shall bear

the actual readings of the meter upon the first and

last days of the period for which the account is

rendered.

(3) A gas company shall not, except as provided by

these Regulations, render an account for any quantity

of gas supplied by meter other than the quantity

registered by the meter as having been supplied by

the consumer.

In other words, the inclusion in their accounts of an amount labelled "unbilled gas" was, like the dried fruit in Farnsworth (supra) a mere guess - and guesses are immune from tax.

24. Each party, albeit for different reasons, placed considerable reliance on the decision of the Full High Court in J Rowe and Son Pty Ltd v F.C. of T. [1970] HCA 57; (1970-1971) 124 CLR 421. The facts, taken from the headnote in the Commonwealth Law Reports, were that:

At all material times the taxpayer owned a retail

store at which it sold household goods either for

cash or on payment within thirty days, or on terms

requiring payment over longer periods of up to five

years. Where terms of this nature were to be given,

the buyer agreed to pay by the appropriate

instalments an amount that was set out, comprising

the cash price together with eleven per cent per

annum thereof and an additional charge of one dollar.

It was provided that on default of payment the whole

of the unpaid balance would become payable

forthwith, and the obligations of the buyer were

secured by bill of sale.

The taxpayer contended that the amounts to be

included in its assessable income in respect of terms

sales were only such amounts as were received or

receivable in the year of income: and alternatively it

contended that income from terms sales should be

treated as being derived as and when it emerged, so

that only so much of the amount of any instalment

paid or payable should be included in its assessable

income as represented income. The Commissioner,

however, assessed the taxpayer on the basis that in

the assessable income of the year of sale there

should be included the full amount to be received

under the material agreement of sale, except to the

extent to which that amount included interest on

instalments payable in future years.

25. In the result, both Walsh J at first instance, and the Full High Court on appeal, concluded that the assessments were correct.

26. Walsh J. examined the opposing views expressed by each of the parties. Thus, his Honour noted (albeit in the context of instalment contracts):

The Commissioner's contention that the sale price

should be brought to account in the year when the

transaction takes place is based on the propositions

that this accords with accepted accountancy

principles applicable to trading of this kind, that it is

necessary in order that a true picture may be

obtained of the result of the year's trading, and that

what the Act requires is that the net gain of the

trader in that year should be included in his income

for the purposes of taxation.

(at pp 432-433)

His Honour concluded:

"that when a decision is required as to the time at

which a transaction is to be brought into account or

the extent to which accepted business and

accountancy methods are relevant, the making of

such decision is not greatly affected by any

distinction between the ascertainment of the profit

of the year and the ascertainment of the income of

the year under assessment . (at pp 434-435)

His Honour furthermore found that:

"it has nevertheless been accepted that the book

debts of a trader may be treated as his income". (p

435)

27. In the result, the learned trial judge decided that there seemed to be no legal principle that made it legitimate to treat as "income" trading debts already payable, but not debts which are not yet payable but in respect of which no further step is required from the seller to entitle him to payment. The question was one of fact rather than of law on which evidence of business practice is of assistance.

28. On appeal, Menzies J. (with whom Barwick C.J. agreed) noted that:

In a system of annual accounting, ordinary business

considerations would indicate that what becomes

owing to a company from trading stock sold during a

year should, in some way, be brought into account to

balance the reduction of trading stock which the

transaction effects. Any other method of accounting

would lead to a misrepresentation of the trader's

financial position. ...

Acceptance of the taxpayer's contention would, of

course, largely destroy the accepted basis for the

taxation of most trading and business concerns. It is

accepted that, for taxation, as well as for business

purposes, the income of such a business is derived

when it is earned and the receipt of what is earned is

not necessary to bring the proceeds of sale into

account. The acceptance of this basis of accounting

is recognized by the provisions of the Act relating to

the writing off of bad debts which "have been brought

to account by the taxpayer as assessable income of

any year". see s. 63

The leading cases on the subject are Commissioner of

Taxes (SA) v Executor Trustee and Agency Co of

South Australia Ltd (Carden's Case) (1938) 63 CLR

108 and Henderson v Federal Commissioner of

Taxation (supra).

In Carden's Case (at p 109) the High Court decided,

upon South Australian legislation framed like the

Income Tax Assessment Act, that:

When there is nothing analogous to a stock

of vendible articles to be acquired or produced

and carried by a taxpayer, where outstandings

on the expenditure side do not correspond to,

and are not naturally connected with, the

outstandings on the earnings side, and where

there is no fund of circulating capital from which

income or profit must be detached for actual

enjoyment, but where, on the contrary, the

receipts represent in substance a reward for a

professional skill and personal work to which

the expenditure on the other side contributes in

only a minor or subsidiary degree, the receipts

basis forms a fair and appropriate basis for

estimating professional income for the purpose

of the Taxation Act 1927-1935 (SA), provided

there be continuity in the practice of the

profession.

In so deciding it was recognized that, when what is

subject to tax is income not derived from trading,

"there must be something `coming in'; that is for

income tax purposes receivability without receipt is

nothing": see per Dixon J. (at pp 154 and 155). His

Honour, however, stated very clearly the basis upon

which trading concerns are taxed upon earnings

rather than receipts. It was said (at p 156):

The basis of a trading account is stock on

hand at the beginning and end of the period and

sales and purchases. In such an account book

debts represent what before sale was trading

stock and it is almost inevitable that they

should be taken into consideration upon an

accrual and not a cash basis.

Earlier his Honour had said (at p 155):

The reasons which underlie the practice of

estimating for taxation purposes the income

from trade or manufacture by means of a

commercial profit and loss account consist in

the impracticability of computing income in any

other way and in the adoption for fiscal

purposes of recognized commercial principles.

The computation of profits from manufacture

and trading has always proceeded upon the

principle that the profit may be contained in

stock-in-trade and "outstandings".

Latham C.J., who dissented, did so for reasons which are of weight here. The Chief Justice said (at p 123):

In the case of traders, where tax is

imposed upon the profits of a trade, profits are

calculated both in Australia and in England on an

earnings basis: that is to say, the trade debts

which fall due to the taxpayer during the year

are credited and allowance is made for bad

debts.

Later the learned Chief Justice stated (at p 124):

In the case of a trader it is well established

that he must take into account book debts owed

to him as part of his income, at least where

those book debts fall due during the year in

respect of which he is making his return.

And at p 125:

... trade debts which have accrued due in the

relevant year but which have not been paid must

be included for the purpose of ascertaining

whether or not the business has earned a profit

for the year, just as stock in trade at the

beginning and end of the year must be taken into

account for the same purpose.

In a statute which made no distinction between traders and professional men, the basis of his Honour's dissent from the decision of the Court was that both groups should be taxed alike, i.e. on earnings. His Honour, however, as will have been seen, left open the possibility that trading debts not payable within the year of tax do not form part of the income of that year. For reasons indicated earlier, I consider income from the sale of stock is derived when the stock is sold and a debt is created (my italics), it need not be payable in the year of income. (at pp 448-450)

So it remains as trading stock until the derivation of

income comes about. And there is no

misrepresentation of the trader's accounts.

29. Mr Robinson sought further support for his submission that there can be no derivation until the existence of a debt from the decisions in McDonald v Dennys Lascelles Ltd [1933] HCA 25; (1933) 48 CLR 457 and Sunbird Plaza Pty Ltd v Maloney [1988] HCA 11; (1988) 166 CLR 245.

30. In McDonald v Dennys Lascelles Ltd (supra) Dixon J. (as he then was) quoted with approval the decision of Salmond J. in Ruddenklau v Charlesworth (1925) NZLR 161 at 164:

As a general rule, on the failure or refusal of a

purchaser to complete an executory contract for the

purchase of land, the vendor is not entitled to sue for

the purchase money as a debt. He is entitled merely

to sue for specific performance or for damages for

the loss of his bargain. It is only when the contract

has been completed by the execution and acceptance

of a conveyance that unpaid purchase money may

become a debt and can be recovered accordingly.

This general rule is sufficiently illustrated and

established by the case of Laird v Pim (1841) 7 M and W

474; [1841] EngR 237; 151 ER 852. The sale of land is in this respect

similar to the sale of goods. In the case of goods

sold and delivered, and of goods bargained and sold,

the property in each case having passed to the buyer,

the seller's remedy is to sue for the price. But if

under any executory contract the buyer wrongfully

refuses to accept the goods, the seller's only remedy

is an action for damages. The general rule, however,

that in an executory contract for the sale of land, the

vendor cannot sue for the price, is excluded

whenever a contrary intention is shown by the

express terms of the contract. And it seems

established by authority that a contrary intention is

sufficiently shown in all cases in which by the

express terms of the contract the purchase money or

any part thereof is made payable on a fixed day, not

being the agreed day for the completion of the

contract by conveyance. In all such cases the

purchase money or such part thereof becomes, on the

day so fixed for its payment, a debt immediately

recoverable by the vendor, irrespective of the

question whether a conveyance has been executed

and notwithstanding the fact that the purchaser may

have repudiated his contract. Notwithstanding such

repudiation, the vendor is not bound to sue for

damages or specific performance, but may recover

the agreed purchase money.

31. The above view was again cited with approval in the Sunbird Plaza case (supra) by Mason C.J. (at p 253) and Gaudron J. (at pp 267-268).

32. The thrust of these cases is to the effect that a vendor of land cannot sue for the purchase price before the contract is completed by conveyance, unless a contrary intention is shown by the express terms of the contract, e.g. by expressing the price to be payable on a fixed day, not being the day fixed for completion. In short, one cannot keep one's cake and eat it in the sense of retaining the land and suing for the price. However, as Mrs Branson, of learned counsel for the Crown, rightfully pointed out, provided the vendor in such a situation does what the contract obliges him to do, he is absolutely entitled to recover the purchase price through his right to sue for specific performance. From this, Mrs Branson, in my opinion, correctly submitted that the non-existence of a "debt" in the 1985 year was therefore not fatal to the derivation of the balance of the purchase price in that year.

33. I have summarised Mr Robinson's argument in great detail, partly out of respect for the formidable assault he has amounted against the proposition that in the case of a trader, where tax is imposed upon the profits of a trade, the trade debts which fall due to the taxpayer during the year are credited and allowance is made for bad debts (cf per Latham C.J. (diss), Carden's case (supra at 123)), and partly to note that there appears to be no universal solvent for determining the punctum temporis when revenue transactions are brought to account or income is derived. As was pointed out in the Australian Gas Light Co case (supra), these so-called "tests" are mere signposts and:

Helpful as these tests may be as signposts, each of

them has been conceived in and applied in the varied

and contrasting circumstances. As signposts they

indicate that invariably something more than

provisions of goods or services by the taxpayer is

required. (p 4805).

34. Mrs Branson sought to counter Mr Robinson's submission by placing a very different construction on the cases relied on by Mr Robinson. Henderson's case (supra), according to Mrs Branson, simply states that "work in progress which has never been billed is not to be taken into account for taxation purposes". With respect to Mrs Branson, the case goes further than that. At the very least, it is authority for the proposition that in ascertaining the income in the case of a large partnership carrying on a practice of the type considered in that case, only fees which have matured into recoverable debts are to be included. From this it followed that a mere estimate on the value of "work in progress" for which payment cannot be demanded does not form part of the net earnings. Indeed, if one thing emerges from the decision of Barwick C.J. (with whom McTiernan and Menzies JJ. agreed) it is that professionals are not automatically to be assessed on a receipts basis. Davies J. put it felicitously in F.C. of T. v Dunn 89 ATC 4141:

The matter depends upon the nature and incidence of

the income earning enterprise, upon current

accounting principles and practice and upon current

perceptions in the field in which the taxpayer gains

his income. These matters may differ from time to

time. There may be relevant differences between

different occupations, between different periods and

between different taxpayers in the same field of

occupation. (at p 4151)

35. Doing the best I can, it seems to me that where goods are sold - including land - on terms that payment is to be effected at a later date, the statement by Menzies J. in Rowe (supra) resolves the issue against the taxpayer:

In a system of annual accounting, ordinary business

considerations would indicate that what becomes

owing to a company for trading stock sold during a

year should, in some way, be brought into account to

balance the reduction of trading stock which the

transaction effects. Any other method of accounting

would lead to a misrepresentation of the trader's

financial position. (supra at p 448)

36. I have therefore concluded that, if Venture were permitted to include in its assessable income for the 1985 year only those sales for which payment had been received in that year, this would constitute a "misrepresentation of (its) financial position". Stripped of its own peculiar features, this case is, in reality, no different from that of a Department store which permits account customers 30 days before payment becomes due. Can it be argued that sales effected between 25 June and the end of the financial year are not to be brought into account in that year because it left the store with "no dispositive powers over the goods", or because there is no "debt due" until the following year? Once the case is reduced to these basic terms, the answer becomes at once obvious; cf Rowe (supra) at 448). Indeed, I am satisfied that on a finding that the land in the instant case ceased to be Venture's trading stock on and after 25 June 1985, that is really the end of the applicant's case. It is only due to Mr Robinson's plausibility and because - like Rich J in Farnsworth (supra) - I found the facts in this case "interesting", that I have spent so much time in analysing both the facts and the argument. I am also grateful to Mrs Branson, whose commonsense approach to the issue has brought the matter back down to earth.

37. Since the point of "derivation" was the only issue argued before me, it follows that the Tribunal affirms the objection decision under review.
