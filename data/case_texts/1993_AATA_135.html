Australian Doctors Fund Limited and Department of Treasury and Commissioner of Taxation [1993] AATA 135; (1993) 30 ALD 265 (30 April 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

AUSTRALIAN DOCTORS FUND LIMITED v. DEPARTMENT OF TREASURY

and COMMISSIONER OF

TAXATION

Nos. N92/102 and N92/526 and N92/537

AAT No. 8679

Number of pages - 8

[1993] AATA 135; (1993) 30 ALD 265

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

B.J. McMahon(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Freedom of Information - denial of access - exemptions claimed - conclusive certificates - documents relating to taxation of contributions to Fund - correspondence between and advice to Ministers and Treasurer - whether documents disclose deliberative process - whether documents contain purely factual material - whether reasonable grounds exist for claim that disclosure contrary to public interest

Freecom of Information Act 1982 (Cth) ss 36(1), (3), (5), 58(5), 58C(2)(a)

Re Howard and Treasurer of Commonwealth of Australia 7 ALD 626 at 637

Re Fewster and Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet 11 ALN N266

Re Rae and Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet 12 ALD 589

Re Macphee and Department of the Treasury 11 AAR 166

Department of Industrial Relations v Burchill [1991] FCA 616; 105 ALR 327

HEARING

SYDNEY, 14 April 1993

30:4:1993

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr MJ Neil QC with Mr S Gaegler

Solicitor for the Applicant: Tress Cocks Maddox

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr C Erskine

Solicitor for the Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The decision under review in so far as it relates to the letter of 21 January 1991 is set aside. The remainder of the decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

B.J. McMAHON On 24 September 1991 the applicant wrote to the Freedom of Information officer at the Treasury, seeking access to ".... all correspondence and documentation relating to taxation matters concerning the Fund. In particular any letters or directives to the Treasury from politicians concerning the taxation of contributions to the Fund" After consultation with the office of the Commissioner of Taxation and with the office of the Minister for Community Services and Health, 18 documents were identified as falling within the terms of the request. Of these, 13 were made available to the applicant either wholly or partly. No review is sought of the decision to make deletions from those documents to which partial access was originally given.

2. Of the remaining 5, one was a letter dated 26 February 1991 from Mr Robinson, an officer of the Treasury described as "Adviser (Taxation)", to the Hon BL Howe, Minister for Community Services and Health. This letter was later released to the applicant and was tendered as Exhibit C in the present proceedings. It was a reply to Mr Howe's letter of 21 January 1991 addressed to the Treasurer, Mr PJ Keating, which is one of the remaining 4 documents for which exemption is claimed. The other 3 documents are letters dated respectively 14 February 1989, 7 August 1989 and 28 November 1989 from Dr N Blewett, then Minister for Community Services and Health to the Hon PJ Keating, then Treasurer.

3. The respondents claim that these 4 letters are exempt pursuant to the provisions of s 36 of the Freedom of Information Act 1982. The relevant parts are as follows -

"s36(1)Subject to this section, a document is an exempt document

if it is a document the disclosure of which under this Act :

(a) would disclose matter in the nature of, or relating to,

opinion, advice or recommendation obtained, prepared or recorded, or

consultation or deliberation that has taken place, in the course of,

or for the purposes of, the deliberative processes involved in the

functions of an agency or Minister or of the Government of the

Commonwealth; and

(b) would be contrary to the public interest.

(3) Where a Minister is satisfied, in relation to a document to

which paragraph (1)(a) applies, that the disclosure of the document

would be contrary to the public interest, he may sign a certificate

to that effect (specifying the ground of public interest in relation to

which the certificate is given) and, subject to the operation of Part

VI, such a certificate, so long as it remains in force, establishes

conclusively that the disclosure of that document would be contrary

to the public interest.

(5) This section does not apply to a document by reason only of

purely factual material contained in the document."

4. Although not a party to these proceedings in a strict sense, 2 certificates dated 26 August 1992 and 10 November 1992 were given by Mr Howe, Minister for Health, Housing and Community Services (as he was then entitled) in respect of the first 2 and second 2 letters respectively. Sub-section 36(3) does not appear to be confined to a grant of power to a Minister responsible for the agency to whom the initial request was made. The power granted by sub-section 36(3) in its terms can be exercised by any Minister. No attack was made upon the validity of the certificates in the present proceedings.

5. Several consequences flow from these certificates. Sub-section 58(5) is in these terms -

"s 58(5)Where application is or has been made to the Tribunal for

the review of a decision refusing to grant access to a document in

accordance with a request, being a document that is claimed to be an

exempt document under section 36 and in respect of which a certificate

is in force under that section, the Tribunal shall, in a case where

it is satisfied that the document is a document to which paragraph 36

(1)(a) applies, if the applicant so requests, determine the question

whether there exist reasonable grounds for the claim that the

disclosure of the document would be contrary to the public interest."

6. Section 58C(2) provides that the hearing of a proceeding involving a review of the question whether reasonable grounds existed for the claim that the disclosure of the document would be contrary to the public interest must be held in private, while evidence or information is given, or a document is produced, to the Tribunal by an officer of an agency, or during a submission which is being made to the Tribunal in relation to the claim. In response to the mandatory nature of the words of paragraph 58C(2)(a), I directed that the door of the hearing room be locked during the relevant part of the proceedings. However, I exercised the discretion granted by s 58C(3) and gave directions as to the persons who might be present during that hearing. As the applicant's counsel indicated that he wished to cross-examine a witness to be called by the respondent, and as counsel for the respondent helpfully offered to present his submissions in a general manner, I directed that the applicant's senior and junior counsel and its solicitor could be present during the restricted part of the hearing. No member or officer of the applicant and no member of the public was present.

7. I will attempt in these reasons to follow the procedure adopted by Davies J in Re Howard and Treasurer of Commonwealth of Australia 7 ALD 626 at 637. His Honour there said -

"Because of the requirement for privacy, it is not permissible for

the Tribunal to set out in its public reasons for decision, any of

the evidence or information given to or submissions made to the Tribunal

by or on behalf of the agency in relation to the claim that the

disclosure of the documents would be contrary to the public interest.

That restriction does not, however, preclude my describing in these

reasons for decision the general nature of the issue as it appears

from the Treasurer's certificate or as appears from the material

lodged with the Tribunal in the documents submitted by the agency

pursuant to s 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975

(Cth) (the AAT Act). Nor does the prohibition preclude my setting out
the

substance of the submissions made to the Tribunal by Mr Howard.

The above and other provisions necessarily affect the formulation of

reasons and the obligation imposed on the Tribunal by s 43(2) and

(2B) of the AAT Act, that is, to include in its reasons for decision its

findings on material questions of fact and a reference to the

material on which those findings are based. Clearly, in so far as the

Tribunal's findings of fact are based upon exempt matter or a

document made exempt by a certificate under s 36(3), the Tribunal may not

infringe the exemption - see ss 58(2) and 63(1). Thus, although in

this review I have inspected the documents in issue and have taken

the contents of those documents into account in arriving at my decision,

it is necessary that I avoid in my reasons for decision any

disclosure of their contents."

8. In relation to all 4 documents it is necessary to establish firstly whether any or all of the them fall within the terms of s 36(1)(a). Unless this is first established, the exemption claim must fail. The giving of a certificate under sub-section 36(3) does not of itself suffice to qualify a document under paragraph 36(1)(a).

9. In my view the fourth document, the letter from Mr Howe dated 21 January 1991, is different in kind from the other 3 and does not comply with the requirements of s 36. It is a letter containing 4 paragraphs. The first 3 contain purely factual material and therefore by virtue of sub-section (5), fall outside the terms of this section. Exhibit C, the reply to the fourth document, refers to Mr Howe's "comments relating to the statement made at the Australian Doctors Fund/Australian Medical Association Summit on general practice, that contributions to the Fund are deductible". This gives an indication of the factual material contained in the letter. The last paragraph consists of one sentence in which the author makes an innocuous suggestion to which no exception could be taken. The only fact that distinguishes this letter from many others that could easily cover the same subject matter, the material not being of a secret nature, is that it is from one Minister addressed to another Minister.

10. As was said in Re Fewster and Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet 11 ALN N266 at N271 -

"...Parliament has not gone on to provide, as it might well have

done, had it been so minded, that documents containing confidential

communications between Ministers or between senior public servants

and Ministers are also exempt, as a class, from disclosure under the

Act. Rather, the question whether such communications should be exempt

has been left to be determined having regard to the contents of each

document, in the light of the public interest test posed by s

36(1)(b): see Lianos at 494-5. The need to ensure candour and frankness
in

the expression of advice etc and to maintain confidentiality, where

appropriate, are left, in my view, as facets of the public interest

to be weighed and evaluated in each case with other competing

considerations. They are relevant but not determinative

considerations: see Re Brennan and Law Society of Australian Capital

Territory (No. 2) (1985) 8 ALD 10 at 21; cf Re Lianos at 496."

11. The letter from Mr Howe can not be regarded as part of the deliberative processes involved in the functions of the Government of the Commonwealth. To elevate it to this status would be to confer exemption upon almost any communication between Ministers. It has repeatedly been said that this is not the function of the Act, and of s 36 in particular. The proposition was rejected, for example, in Re Rae and Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet 12 ALD 589 at 603. The fact that Mr Howe is the author of the letter does not give the letter any particular status. It is the material contained in the letter which must be considered. There is no confidential information contained within its 4 paragraphs. It was not responded to by Mr Keating personally and exemption has not been claimed for the response. It had only a minimal relevance to the deliberative processes involved in the functions of Mr Howe's own Department. In those circumstances I consider that document 4 does not fall within the terms of paragraph 36(1)(a) and that accordingly the claim for exemption in relation to that document must fail.

12. The remaining 3 documents are, in my view, are of a different character. In all 3, Dr Blewett has not only sought advice on the taxation status of donations to the applicant and has not only made comments about the applicant's activities, he has also raised material of a general nature for consideration. That material does not relate to trivial issues, but raises questions which, in my view, are appropriately raised between senior Ministers who would be expected to consult regularly, and who would have a common interest in the deliberative processes involved in the Government of the Commonwealth.

13. The documents should be looked at against the factual background emerging from evidence presented by the applicant. It was incorporated as a company limited by guarantee on 11 May 1989. On 3 March 1989 its solicitors wrote to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation enclosing a copy of the proposed Memorandum and Articles of Association and requesting a ruling that contributions made by doctors and other associated workers would be deductible under sub-section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. In March 1989 the Deputy Commissioner replied in these terms, omitting formal parts -

"INCOME TAX : AUSTRALIAN DOCTORS' FUND LIMITED

Reference is made to your letter of 13 March 1989 in which advice

was sought concerning contributions made to the abovenamed

organisation.

As you are no doubt ware, any decisions given in advance cannot be

binding on the Commissioner and at the time of making an assessment

the law as it is then enacted and interpreted must be applied to the

established facts.

At the outset it is pointed out that in determining the character of

an organisation not only do we have to have regard to the objects set

out in the constituent document but also we have to look at the

actual activities being carried out. For this reason it is not possible
to

give a firm decision until the company has been operating for some

time, say twelve months.

Subject to the foregoing, you are advised that if the company

operates strictly within its stated objects, it may be anticipated that

contributions made to the fund will qualify as allowable deductions

under sub-section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act provided

that the contributor establishes a nexus between the subscription and the

carrying on of his business.

If it is ultimately found that the Fund is operating predominantly

for political purposes, contributions will not qualify as a deduction."

14. On 25 January 1990 a further letter was received from the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, which omitting formal parts, was in the following terms-

"Reference is made to your letter of 13 March 1989 and the

preliminary response from this office dated 30 March 1989. As noted

in our response it was necessary to examine the actual activities as

well as the objects of the funds before giving a firm decision.

The issue of deductibility of contributions to the Australian Doctors

Fund Limited has now been reconsidered, and, for the reasons outlined

below, contributions by medical practitioners or medical practices

will not be deductible under the general deduction provisions.

Close examination indicates that it would be very difficult for a

medical practitioner or incorporated medical practice company to

establish the necessary link between the derivation of income from

the provision of medical services and contributions that might be made to

the fund.

If, by reference to the fund's objects and known activities, it could

be established to the Commissioner's satisfaction that the fund was

primarily directed to representing doctors in matters closely

connected with the derivation of the income, deductibility of

contributions would not be in question. Representational activities

in pursuit of higher fees and better working conditions, the

development of ethical standards and the promotion of professional

development, for example, would argue in favour of deductibility.

However, on the basis of the available evidence, the Commissioner

could not be so satisfied. The Memorandum of Association of the

Australian Doctors' Fund Limited contains the following objects -

(a) to maintain, improve, advance and promote health care for all

Australians and ensure that there is freedom of choice of health

care;

(b) to research, develop, establish and promote health care

policies for the benefits of all Australians; and

(c) to promote community support for medical research.

While those objects are expressed to be for the purpose of maintaining

the economic viability of medical practice, the impression is that

they are related primarily to health care policy issues, public

attitudes and the general interaction between the community and the

medical profession. In the circumstances, there would only be the

remotest connection between the objects of the fund and the

derivation of income of individual doctors or medical practices.

Further evidence of the aims of the fund is contained in a brochure

titled "Australian Doctors' Fund - Striving for quality and freedom

in health care in Australia]" Under the heading "The Challenge", the

brochure says the fund will provide an opportunity "to turn back the

tide of increasing intervention by Governments in the rights of

people to choose their health care". It characterises the fund as being

"about restoring high quality health care, a proper balance between

private and public health, in other words, the right to choose". It

also says the fund "will give the medical profession a capacity to

act, persuade, lobby, campaign, initiate, educate and protect". Thus

expressed, the fund could not be said to be acting on behalf of

medical practitioners in relation to their income earning activities.

Apart from the quite clear inferences in its written constituent

document as to the fund"s concerns about freedom of choice and the

promotion of health care policies, media reports of the launching of

the fund in July 1989 suggest a somewhat political flavour. Dr Bruce

Shepherd, chairman of the fund, was quoted in "The Age" of 7 July

1989 as saying the group (i.e. the fund) would act as a "catalyst or

supply ship" to help the A.M.A. and other medical groups fight for the

greater privatisation of the health care system. The "Australian" of

the same date mentioned possible legal challenges to health

legislation that prevents people taking out private insurance to

cover medical services, political lobbying on health issues, and public

education campaigns. A somewhat similiar report appeared in the

"West Australian" of 10 July 1989."

15. Further negotiations took place between the applicant and the Commissioner in an endeavour to obtain the desired tax status. I was informed that these negotiations are still continuing and that proposals for re-arranging the financial structure of the organisation were under consideration. The applicant believed that the correspondence for which exemption is sought was responsible for precipitating the second letter from the Deputy Commissioner.

16. Senior counsel for the applicant argued firstly that the 3 letters from Dr Blewett did not form part of the "deliberative processes" of Government, because they were not related to the formulation of policy, or at least to the proper discharge of the functions of office of that Minister. As I have said, having read the letters, I am of the view that they contributed in a significant way to a debate, then current, in suggesting appropriate policy involving health workers and tax obligations. As such, they also appeared to me to be related to the functions of Dr Blewett's then office. The Tribunal has taken a broad approach to the concept of deliberative processes. In Fewster the Tribunal said at N268 and N269 -

"(11) In a carefully prepared written submission which he

supplemented by oral submissions at the hearing, Mr Bayne submitted

(as he has done on many previous occasions before this Tribunal) that

the expression "deliberative processes" in s 36(1)(a) should be read

as the equivalent of "policy-forming processes" (of the agency or of

government). In support of this construction he relied upon the

decision of Beaumont J in Harris v Australian Broadcasting Corp and

Other [1983] FCA 242; (1983) 5 ALD 545; 50 ALR 551 at 559-61, affirmed on appeal by

the Full Court [1984] FCA 8; (1984) 51 ALR 581, and the decision of Keely J in

Public Service Board v Scrivanich (1985) 8 ALD 444. Mr Byrne (sic)

submitted that the Tribunal's decisions adopting a broader

construction of the words "deliberative processes" should not be

followed (see in particular Re Waterford and Department of Treasury

(No.2) [1984] AATA 67; (1984) 5 ALD 588 at 606; affirmed by a Tribunal presided over by

the President, Davies J, in Re Murtagh and Commissioner of Taxation

[1984] AATA 249; (1984) 6 ALD 112 at 118-19; see also the decision of the Tribunal,

constituted by Morling J, in Re Toohey and Department of Prime

Minister and Cabinet (1985) 9 ALN N94).

(12) However, for the reasons given by me in Re Waterford (No. 2), I do

not understand the decision of Beaumont J in Harris as saying that

the "deliberative processes" referred to in s 36(1)(a) are limited to

policy matters the subject of deliberative processes. Neither do I

understand the Full Court to have so held on the appeal. The

observations made to that effect in Waterford (No. 2) were not dissented

from by the President in Murtagh. Furthermore, in Kavvadias v

Ombudsman (No. 2) [1984] FCA 179; (1984) 6 ALD 198 at 209-212 Sheppard J also concluded

that Beaumont J had not expressed "a final view on the meaning of the

expression" (deliberative processes). His Honour expressed a

preference for the wider construction of the word "deliberative" and

added that he "would not have imported any qualification into the

meaning of it based upon the need for there to be as part of the

deliberative process some aspect of policy-forming". His Honour was

content, however, to leave the question open for future decision. In

the light of this conflict of opinion amongst the Federal Court

judges who have considered the issue, the brief reference to the point in

Scrivanich does not seem to me, with the greatest respect, to carry

the matter to the point of finality so far as this Tribunal is

concerned - particularly as Keely J refers only to the views expressed

by Beaumont J in Harris and makes no reference to the observations of

Sheppard J in Kavvadias or of the President in Murtagh.

(13) In the circumstances, in the absence of any definitive

decision to the contrary by the Federal Court, I see no reason to

depart from the view which I expressed in Waterford (No. 2) at 606 as

follows : "The action of deliberating, in common understanding,

involves the weighing up or evaluation of the competing arguments or

considerations that may have a bearing upon one's course of action.

In short, the deliberative processes involved in the functions of an

agency are its thinking processes - the processes of reflection, eg,

upon the wisdom and expediency of a proposal, a particular decision

or a course of action. Deliberations on policy matters undoubtedly come

within this broad description. Only to the extent that a document

may disclose matter in the nature of or relating to deliberative

prosesses does s 36(1)(a) come into play."

17. I adopt the above views. The fact that the processes took place outside the cabinet room seemed to me irrelevant. It is not unreasonable to suppose that deliberative processes are continuous processes as between senior ministers and when the subject of those deliberations is sufficiently weighty, then those processes may be regarded as part of the functions of the Government of the Commonwealth.

18. The next issue to determine therefore is whether there exist reasonable grounds for the claim in the 2 certificates that disclosure of the remaining 3 documents would be contrary to the public interest. I am not to consider whether public interest considerations in favour of disclosure outweigh public interest considerations in favour of non-disclosure. I am not to consider whether, in issuing the certificates, the Minister displayed an undue level of sensitivity. I am not to consider whether the certificates should or should not have been issued. I am to consider only the question posed by sub-section 58(5).

19. The approach which the Tribunal should take in these circumstances was set out in Rae at pages 593 to 595 and was authoritatively reiterated by a former President of this Tribunal in Re Macphee and Department of the Treasury 11 AAR 166 at 173 and 174-

"I have come to the view that in approaching cases where the

applicant requests a review of a decision to issue a conclusive

certificate the question for the Tribunal is whether reasonable

grounds exist to support the certificate. If reasonable grounds do

exist to support the claim then that is the end of the matter despite

the fact that there may be other grounds relied upon, the

reasonableness of which may be doubtful. Reasonable grounds are

those based on reason. It is not possible to place an exhaustive
definition

upon what may constitute reasonableness: see Wegan Constructions Pty

Ltd v Wodonga Sewerage Authority [1978] VicRp 6; (1977) 36 LGRA 147 at 149. It is

obviously a matter to be determined by the Tribunal on the facts as

they are found as to whether or not there exist reasonable grounds

for the claim. It is not enough to show that some other reasonable

person could have concluded that other grounds could not have supported
the

issue of the certificate. What may be the conclusion of one

reasonable person does not of itself exclude all other conclusions

from the realms of reasonable possibilities : see Re W (An Infant)

(1971) AC 682 at 700. For examples of what may constitute reasonable

grounds for other purposes see R v Rymer (1877) 2 QBD 136 at 140

and Adams v Thrift (1915) 2 Ch 21.

It follows from what I have said that the Tribunal should not

attempt to balance the grounds supporting the certificate against

grounds supporting the contention that the issue of the certificate

is unreasonable. This style of approach is akin to full merits review.

Neither should the Tribunal approach the issue of whether there are

reasonable grounds for the issuing of a certificate on the basis that

such grounds can be presumed to be in existence unless it can be

shown that they are in fact grounds no reasonable man could consider

reasonable or they are grounds no reasonable man could rely on in

reaching a decision to issue a certificate. The words of s 58(5) are

clear - the Tribunal must simply determine if reasonable grounds

exist. As I have said reasonable grounds are those based on reason."

20. An earlier president of this Tribunal, Davies J, also endorsed the approach taken in Rae. In Department of Industrial Relations v Burchill [1991] FCA 616; 105 ALR 327 at 330 His Honour said -

"I need not elaborate on what is the import of the term

"reasonable grounds". In Re Porter and Department of Community

Services and Health (1988) 14 ALD 403 at 405-6, Deputy President RK

Todd said :

"To be 'reasonable', it is requisite only that they be not fanciful,

imaginary or contrived, but rather that they be reasonable; that is

to say based on reason, namely agreeable to reason, not irrational,

absurd or ridiculous: see Re Actors' Equity Association of Australia

and Australia Broadcasting Tribunal (No. 2) (1985) 7 ALD 584; 3 AAR 1;

Attorney-General's Department v Cockroft (1986) 12 ALD 468. It

follows that it is a heavy thing for the tribunal to reject a

certificated claim."

The words of Deputy President Todd adequately convey the concept. No

doubt, Deputy President Todd had in mind cases such as East v

Repatriation Commission [1987] FCA 242; (1987) 74 ALR 518, in which Jenkinson, Neaves

and Wilcox JJ adopted similar expressions in relation to the term of

"reasonable hypothesis" in s 120(3) of the Veterans' Entitlements Act

1986 (Cth).

The issue for the Administrative Appeals Tribunal was thus whether

the view expressed by the conclusive certificate was reasonably open. To

adapt the words of Starke J in FCT v Broken Hill South Ltd (1941) 65

CLR 150 at 155 :

"This court (the Administrative Appeals Tribunal) has no authority to

decide whether the finding (certificate) is correct, but only whether

there is any material upon which the tribunal (certifier) could

reasonably so find : American Thread Co v Joyce (1912) 6 Tax Cas 1;

Currie v Inland Revenue Commissioners (1921) 2 KB 332.""

21. Both certificates are in identical terms -

"For the purpose of sub-section 36(3) of the Freedom of

Information Act 1982 ("the Act"), I BRIAN HOWE, Minister of State for

Health, Housing and Community Services, having examined each of the

documents listed in the attached Schedule, and satisfied myself that

the said documents to which paragraph 36(1)(a) of the Act applies,

CERTIFY that -

the disclosure of the said documents or parts of documents, as

indicated in the Schedule, would be contrary to the public interest

on the grounds that -

- the documents are internal working documents containing high level

opinions or advice which are of a preliminary or deliberative nature

which may not be reflected in the subsequent decision;

- release of the documents may create a misleading impression of the

decision making process and thereby erode public confidence in the

administration of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936; and

- the necessary confidentiality applying to correspondence between

Ministers on matters of policy would be breached."

22. The Minister is bound by sub-section 36(3) to specify the ground of public interest in relation to which the certificate is given. Three grounds are set out in general terms. Sub-section 58(5) does not, however, confine the Tribunal to those stated grounds. The Tribunal must inquire whether there are any reasonable grounds for the claim. In the light of the correspondence with the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation to which I have referred, and in the light of the terms of the remaining 3 documents, I consider that the second of the stated grounds and to a lesser extent, the third of the stated grounds are reasonable in the circumstances.

23. It is against the public interest to impugn the integrity of the administration of the Income Tax Assessment Act. The public interest requires universal acceptance of the proposition that liability to taxation and entitlement to deductions will be decided only in accordance with the law. It is in the public interest that there be total confidence that such decisions are not influenced by extraneous factors, and most particularly not by political factors. There is no evidence before me given either publicly or privately that in fact any improper influence was brought to bear upon the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation in the present circumstances. Nevertheless, it might be mistakenly thought, if the 3 documents were released, that such influence may have played some part in the formulation of the final decision.

24. One may well argue that it is just as much in the public interest to release the documents, knowing that sensible and informed persons would not come to such a conclusion when the contents of the documents were made known to them. It is not, however, my task to balance these facets of public interest. I am to determine only whether the ground to which I have referred is a reasonable ground. In the sense in which that term was explained in Burchill, there can be no doubt, in my view, that the ground is made out. Accordingly it is not necessary to to consider the first or third stated ground.

25. The public interest in maintaining confidence in the integrity of the taxation administration system is of particular importance in the present circumstances because of the evidence that negotiations are still continuing between the applicant and the Deputy Commissioner. Thus although the documents themselves are between 3 and 4 years old they are still of relevance and the public interest in their non-disclosure has not diminished. Whether there is as the applicant submitted "an overriding public interest in facilitating the accountability of government by exposing decision making to public scrutiny" is not something with which I am entitled to concern myself. The ground relied upon by the Minister is a reasonable ground and I must therefore find that disclosure would be contrary to the public interest.

26. The decision under review in so far as it relates to the letter of 21 January 1991 is set aside. The remainder of the decision under review is affirmed.
