TT88/6 and Commissioner of Taxation [1988] AATA 340; (1988) 20 ATR 3023; 88 ATC 1084 (25 November 1988)

Last Updated: 7 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Withholding Tax - failure of borrower to deduct and remit due to ignorance of the law - whether payment in default constituted a "penalty" - whether payment in default an allowable deduction - when liability "incurred".

INCOME TAX - Allowable Deduction – failure of borrower to deduct and remit withholding tax to the Commissioner failure due to ignorance - whether payment in default a "penalty" - whether payment in default allowable as a deduction - whether payment in default a borrowing expense whether an expense of discharging a mortgage - whether liability "incurred" upon failure to remit or upon payment.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss. 51, 51(1), 67, 67A, 128A-128R, 221YJ-221YRA, 221YY;

Banking Act 1959 - s39B(1)(b);

Tax Administration Act 1953-1974 - ss. 14B, 14C;

Banking (Foreign Exchange Regulations).

Mayne Nickless Limited v FC of T 84 ATC 4458;

Commissioner of Taxes (S.A.) v The Executor Trustee & Agency

Company of South Australia Ltd. - Carden's Case [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108;

Herald and Weekly Times Ltd. v. F.C. of T. [1932] HCA 56; (1932) 48 CLR 113.

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. TT88/6

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re :

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

25 November 1988 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

  1. The Applicant in these proceedings is a married woman who (inter alia) carried on business in partnership with her husband. Both spouses were professional persons but of different disciplines. Neither was concerned in a profession related to the law or accountancy. Neither spouse was familiar with the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") let alone with the 17 sections of the Act ranging from s128A to s128R which at 30 June 1982 comprised Division 11A "Dividends and Interest paid to Non-residents and to certain other persons" - of Part III of the Act or with the 16 sections of the Act ranging from s221YJ (through s221YRA) to s221YY which together comprised Division IV - "Collection of Withholding Tax" - of Part VI of the Act.
  2. In 1979 the partnership purchased its first rental property and financed the purchase with moneys borrowed locally from a mortgagee. During 1981 the couple considered the purchase of another property and, in this instance, they set out to investigate the possibility of borrowing from overseas. Conscious of Government interest in regulating the movement of moneys internationally, they approached both the Reserve Bank and, as required by that Bank in accordance with s39B(1)(b) of the Banking Act 1959, the Commissioner of Taxation seeking approval which would allow them to borrow $US45,000 from Overseas Trust Bank Limited, Hong Kong ("the Hong Kong Bank"), to enable them to purchase a unit at Surfers Paradise for a total sum of $A65,000. They disclosed a proposed cost of borrowing as "at an interest rate of 18% per annum with no other charges" and, as security for borrowing, "Nil - personal loan only". There was no express reference to the purpose to which the Surfers Paradise property was to be put but, having regard to the place of residence of the spouses and to their professional commitments, it might reasonably have been supposed that the property was to be let. There was nothing in the application which made reference to the existing property held and let by the partners. By letter of 20 August 1981 the Commissioner sought certain evidence from the Applicants in support of the application. In due course the required information was provided. On 1 December 1981 the Commissioner advised the Applicants that their application under s14B of the Taxation Administration Act 1953-1974 for the issue of a tax clearance certificate under s14C of that Act was approved. Two days later the Reserve Bank advised the partners that authority had been given on standard terms pursuant to the Banking (Foreign Exchange) Regulations for the funds to be brought into Australia.
  3. The moneys were duly brought into Australia and applied as proposed. Thereafter the partners paid interest in accordance with the arrangements with the Hong Kong Bank. The arrangements proved to be successful with the result that in May 1984 they sought leave to borrow a further $A20,000 from the Hong Kong Bank at a proposed interest rate of 12 1/2% per annum to "be paid half-yearly" to Hong Kong" to enable them to pay out the mortgage on the first property, upon which there was then a liability to pay 15% per annum interest. The applications were approved. The moneys were taken up; and interest was paid to the Hong Kong Bank in accordance with the arrangements made under the personal loan.
  4. Commencing with the year of income ended 30 June 1982 the partnership claimed, and was allowed, a deduction against partnership income in respect of all the interest payments it made. Those returns of income were not all placed in evidence but it is at least clear that in the return of income to 30 June 1985 that the amounts of interest paid and the identity of the local mortgagee and of "Overseas Trust Bank Hong Kong" were disclosed to the Commissioner.
  5. At the commencement of April 1985 the partners were still happily unaware of provisions of the Act relating to withholding tax. I accept that no-one, whether with the Reserve Bank or in the service of the Commissioner of Taxation, had said anything which would have drawn their attention to the relevant provisions of the Act. So it was that they were, to say the least, surprised when they received a letter from the office of the Commissioner, in the following terms:-

"From a review of the partnership returns of income, it has become evident that the provisions of Division 11A of the Income Tax Assessment Act, which relate to dividend (sic) and interest paid to non-residents of Australia, operate to render the interest paid by the partnership, of which you are a member, to the Overseas Trust Bank Ltd. of Hong Kong liable to withholding tax.

Section 221YL (2A) of the above Act stipulates that where interest is payable by a person (in the section referred to as the borrower') to another person who is shown as having an address outside Australia, the borrower shall, before or at the time when the interest is paid by the borrower, make a deduction from the interest of an amount determined in accordance with the regulations. Section 54ZGA of the Income Tax Regulations provides that the deduction to be made from interest is ten per centum of the interest.

To assist you in complying with the above provisions, a deduction form and a remittance form are enclosed. A pamphlet containing notes on withholding tax, also, is forwarded for your information.

Particulars of the amounts to be deducted and remitted to this office are set out below.

Year Ended 30 June Gross Interest Withholding Tax

1982 $ 4161.46 $ 416.14

  1. 6770.50 677.05
  2. 6763.71 676.37

Total withholding tax payable $ 1769.56"

  1. The demand prompted a letter of protest stating (inter alia) :-

"We ... find it hard to believe that such a requirement of withholding tax was not made known to us until now, some four years later since your first approval of our overseas borrowing in 1981. ... Your Office is the sole authority on taxation matters and it is incredible that such requirement of withholding tax was not mentioned in your letter of approval to us."

  1. The protest resulted in a letter from the Commissioner of 5 September 1985 which said:-

"I refer to your letter dated 23 April 1985 and apologise for the delay in responding.

The question of your partnership's liability to pay withholding tax has been fully reviewed and it is confirmed that the full amount stated in my letter of 3 April 1985 is correctly due and payable under the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act.

Further you are advised that the Act automatically imposes additional tax for late payment of any tax which is not remitted to the Australian Taxation Office within twenty-one days after the end of the month in which a liability to withhold tax occurs. For your information such a penalty is calculated at the rate of 10% per annum up until 13 February 1983 and 20% per annum thereafter.

In view of the circumstances of your case, however, it has been decided to grant full remission of penalty providing all tax currently due and payable is paid within six months of the date of this letter and all future tax withheld is forwarded to this office within the time prescribed.

Appropriate deduction and remittance forms are enclosed and it is requested that you quote the following number, 40 923 538, on all payments or correspondence relating to this matter."

8. When that reply was received the partners made enquiry of the Hong Kong Bank as to whether it was liable to pay withholding tax and whether it would be prepared to pay the 10% withholding tax. Not surprisingly, in the style of bureaucrats everywhere, the Hong Kong Bank advised that:-

"We have carefully considered the three points mentioned in your letter"

but that

"It's most regreted to advise that pursuant to the Hong Kong Association of Bank's regulation and under normal banking practice, all banks in Hong Kong are not liable to pay Withholding Tax on the loan interest received from any Australian citizen...

In the circumstance we regret to inform that we are not in the position to pay the 10% Withholding Tax for the past or in the future as this is prohibited under Hong Kong Associations of Bank regulations."

8. Notwithstanding the expression of regrets, the Hong Kong Bank proceeded to give notice that it would require full repayment of the loan "plus interest" on or before the current expiry date for the loan: December 29, 1985. To meet the demand of the Bank arrangements were made for a loan from an individual in Hong Kong. The arrangements for that borrowing took into account the prospect that the partners would have an obligation to withhold tax as required by the Act. The matter was then taken up with the Commonwealth Ombudsman and, in due course, an undated letter of advice was received from the Office of the Ombudsman, stating (inter alia):-

"I take it from the material you have provided that you are not disputing that withholding tax was properly payable to the Taxation Office. What is at issue is the requirement placed on you to pay amounts which you had not deducted from the interest paid to the lender because you were unaware of the provisions of s221YL(2A) of the Income Tax Assessment Act.

It is not clear whether in arranging your overseas borrowings you had professional advice. What is clear however is that you were aware of the foreign exchange control provisions of both the Banking Act and the Taxation Administration Act. In these circumstances I do not find it surprising that the Taxation Office would not feel it necessary to specifically advise you of your further obligations under the Income Tax Assessment Act. The certificates issued by the Taxation Office in terms of s14C of the Taxation Administration Act after all merely certified in essence that in the opinion of the Commissioner, the arrangements you entered into were not associated with tax evasion or avoidance. I think it is fair to say that the responsibility rests with the person entering into any arrangement to find out what legal requirements may be imposed on him by virtue of the arrangement. While I accept without question your ignorance of the liability to deduct withholding tax from the interest payments paid overseas, it nevertheless remains clear that you were required to do so and the responsibility for the payment of the tax remains yours, at least in the first instance."

Three points arising from the letter should be noted:-

* the assumption that liability was not in dispute;

* the absence of reference to some borrowers being exempt from liability; and

* an implicit assumption that persons directed by the Reserve Bank to the Commissioner of Taxation for the purpose of seeking a clearance certificate are independently aware of the law relating to withholding tax.

The letter went on to say the Ombudsman considered that the Taxation Office should have followed up on the question of withholding tax much earlier than it did but that he could find no basis for recommending waiver of the obligation to pay the tax. He acknowledged that the Commissioner had, on his own initiative, fully remitted additional tax for late payment. He recommended that legal advice should be sought as to the disclaimer by the Hong Kong Bank of responsibility for the tax. The fact that he did not mention anything about the possibility of the partnership having been entitled to an exemption from liability to pay the tax is hardly surprising. It is hardly to be expected of the Ombudsman and his staff that they should be expert in all facets of all the legislation of the Commonwealth.

  1. The end result of all the foregoing was that, during the year of income ended 30 June 1986, the partnership gave way to the claims of the Commissioner and paid $2,818 to the Commissioner, made up as follows:-

Year Ended 30 June 1982 $416.14

  1. $677.05
  2. $676.37

1985 $1,048.86

$2,818.42

The amount so paid was claimed as a deduction by the partnership in its return of income to 30 June 1986. The claim of the partnership was made pursuant to s51 (1) of the Act. The claim was disallowed and, upon objection by the Applicant, the decision of the Commissioner to disallow the partnership claim was confirmed. The matter now comes before this Tribunal for independent review.

  1. The partners complain of failure on the part of those in public service, who claim familiarity with and expertise in the requirements of the Commonwealth as to payment of interest to overseas lenders, to bring to the attention of the borrowers their obligations as to withholding tax. The complaint was not unreasonable. Having regard to the very few individuals who borrow overseas, it would not have been difficult for either the Reserve Bank or the Commissioner in their standard documentation to have made sufficient reference to withholding tax to put the borrowers on notice. Not everyone engages in international transactions of such sophistication and magnitude as to have professional advisers - lawyers, accountants and merchant bankers - at hand.
  2. Secondly, the partners complain of delay on the part of the Commissioner in bringing forward his claims to collect withholding tax from the partnership. The Ombudsman prima facie thought the delay on the part of the Commissioner unreasonable, but I rather doubt it. In this regard the partnership was a victim of circumstance. Had the credit rating of the borrowers been less satisfactory and such as to call for the provision of security and all the documentation which usually attends such borrowings, it is likely that the loan documentation would have brought the fact of withholding tax to the attention of the partners. In the circumstances that did not happen. As the Australian Governmental authorities had not brought the fact of withholding tax to the attention of the partners either, the result was that, coupled with lack of knowledge and expertise on the part of the Applicants as to the finer and less common points of our taxation system, all contributed to the present problem. Finally, the failure of the Commissioner to check at an earlier date meant that the magnitude of the problem which was brought to the attention of the partners was greater than it might have been. But, as will be seen, even then lack of understanding on the part of the partners and, it seems, within the office of the Commissioner, still left problems which need never have arisen.
  3. Before passing to those problems, it is appropriate to briefly consider one further matter of complaint. It was argued that after so long a delay it was not fair that the Commissioner should be allowed to "amend" the tax assessments of the Applicant. In that respect the sense of the partners at being harshly dealt with is understandable, but it is not soundly based. In the first place, the perception of a long delay is not well-founded. The delay was as much as 32 months and, at the time demand for withholding tax was made was as little as perhaps 8 months from last return. Even then, the arrangements continued so that thereafter liability was increased by $1,048 in a total of the $2,818 paid. Secondly, in a strict sense, the moneys called on to be paid by way of withholding tax had nothing to do with the tax liability of either of the partners. Their personal tax liability was a function of their individual taxable incomes as assessed. Those assessments were not at any stage amended. What the partnership was being called on to do was to pay tax due by somebody else - the Hong Kong Bank. The payment which was made was made with money of the partnership, but it was a payment of tax levied on the Hong Kong Bank. The explanation for that is to be found in a consideration of the substantive issues.
  4. The argument for the Commissioner in summary form was put as follows:-
    • (a) s.128B (5) made the Hong Kong Bank liable to pay income tax on the interest it was to derive. That tax is what is referred to in these reasons as "withholding tax";
    • (B) S.128C(3) provided that if any withholding tax remained unpaid at the expiration of sixty days after the time when it became due and payable, additional tax was due and payable at the rate of 20% per annum on the amount unpaid computed from the expiration of that period;
    • (c) s.221YL(2A) obliged the partnership, as the person liable to pay interest, to make a deduction from that interest sufficient to provide for the payment of withholding tax;
    • (d) s221YN(1) obliged persons who had made such deductions to pay the amount deducted to the Commissioner within twenty-one days after the end of the month in which the deduction was made;
    • (e) s221YQ(1)(b) provides that persons (such as the partnership) who fail to make a deduction in accordance with s221YL are "liable, in addition to any other penalty" to pay to the Commissioner an amount equal to any unpaid withholding tax payable by the payee-taxpayer (the Hong Kong Bank) pursuant to s128B(5) and any unpaid additional tax thereon payable pursuant to s128C(3);
    • (f) the moneys so paid by the partnership did not constitute an allowable deduction to the partnership because the expenditure was not an expense of borrowing for the purposes of s67, or an expense of discharging a mortgage for the purposes of s67A of the Act;
    • (g) no deduction is allowable pursuant to s51(1) of the Act because the outgoing was not incurred in the course of deriving assessable income but only by reason of failure to comply with provisions of the Act, and furthermore, that being a "penalty" is not allowable as an income tax deduction?
    • (h) that even if any amount might have been allowed as a deduction pursuant to s51 of the Act, the deduction is not to be allowed in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1986 because no liability was "incurred" in that year. The argument is that the liability on the part of the partnership to pay the withholding tax and additional tax was in all respects complete for the purposes of s221YQ when default was made by the Hong Kong Bank in effecting payment of withholding tax at the expiration of twenty-one days after the end of the month in which the income to which the tax relates was derived as provided for in s128C(1). Therefore, it was argued the liability was "incurred" on those dates, and only on those dates.
  5. Consideration of the argument requires that some other provisions ought also to be taken into account. In subsequent references to provisions of the Act I shall record only so much of each provision as is material to the present purpose.
  6. Material provisions of ss128B, 128C and 128G as they stood when the initial loan was taken up provided as follows:-

"128B(2) ... this section applies to income that

(a) is derived, ... by a non-resident; and

(b) consists of interest that--

(i) is paid to the non-resident by a person to whom this section applies ... ; or

(ii) ........................."

"128B (5) A person who derives income to which this section applies that consists of interest is... liable to pay income tax upon that income at the rate declared by the Parliment in respect of income to which this sub-section applies."

"128C(1) Withholding tax is due and payable by the person liable to pay the tax at the expiration of twenty-one days after the end of the month in which the income to which the tax relates was derived by him or of such further period as the Commissioner, in special circumstances allows.

(2) Withholding tax, when it becomes due and payable, is a debt due to the Queen on behalf of the Commonwealth and payable to the Commissioner.

(3) ... if any withholding tax remains unpaid at the expiration of sixty days after the time when it became due and payable, additional tax is due and payable at the rate of .. per centum per annum on the amount unpaid, computed from the expiration of that period." (The rate was 10% per annum to 13 February 1983 and 20% per annum thereafter.)

"128G(1) This section applies to interest in respect of a loan where--

(a) the loan was raised outside Australia;

(b) .....................................

(c) the Commissioner has issued a certificate under section 128H in respect of the loan.

(2) Tax is not payable, and shall be deemed not to have been payable, in accordance with this Division in respect of interest to which this section applies."

  1. The issue of a certificate under s128H required, first, that the applicant "apply to the Commissioner in writing for the issue of such a certificate" [s128H (1)]. If the partnership had made such an application and the application been refused the decision of the Commissioner could have been independently reviewed by this Tribunal pursuant to the provisions of s128P of the Act. Whether a certificate should have been granted is to be determined by reference to s128H (2). Whether it should have been granted in the circumstances of this case was not argued. It seems that neither the partners nor the Commissioner's representative were aware of the possibility of such an application having been successfully made. Nor did the Ombudsman advert to the possibility. While recognizing that, had the matter been fully tested by argument, the facts might have taken on a different complexion, it nonetheless does seem appropriate to comment on what appears to have probably been the situation. S128H(2) provided:

"Where an application is made in accordance with sub-section (1) and the Commissioner is satisfied that

(a) the applicant was an Australian entity throughout the period of 60 days that ended

(i) ........................; or

(ii) in any other case - on the sixtieth day before the day on which the contract for the making of the loan was made;

(b) the loan moneys have been employed, and are intended to be employed, only for a qualifying use or for making moneys available for a qualifying use;

(c) ................................ ; and

(d) ................................

the Commissioner shall issue to the applicant a certificate containing particulars of the loan and stating that the loan complies with this sub-section, but otherwise he shall refuse the application."

  1. In order to determine whether the proposed and, later, actual use of the initial moneys constituted a "qualifying use" requires that regard should be had to S128A(8). It provides:-

"For the purposes of this Division--

(a) a reference to a qualifying use, in relation to loan moneys, shall be read as a reference to use of those moneys for the purposes of an enterprise at a time when the enterprise is or was ... an enterprise owned by an Australian entity...; and

(b) where any loan moneys have been put to a qualifying use, the qualifying use shall be deemed to continue until

(i) the enterprise ceased to be an enterprise of a kind referred to in paragraph (a); or

(ii) the moneys are repaid to, or otherwise received back by, the person by whom they were made available to the entity that has used them for the purposes of the enterprise, or the moneys are used otherwise than for the purposes of the enterprise,

and no longer."

  1. S128A(7) provided:-

"A reference in this Division to the use of moneys for the purposes of an enterprise shall be read as not including use of those moneys in the course of carrying on an enterprise

(a) by way of providing capital for another enterprise; or

(b) by way of the making of loans."

Neither exclusion would appear to have any relevance.

  1. Since, unless the contrary intention appears, s128A(1) defined "entity" to include "the partners in a partnership"; and "enterprise" to mean "a business or other industrial or commercial undertaking", it seems highly probable that, had the Applicants known of the existence of the withholding tax provision and, in particular, of the possibility of gaining a certificate under s128H of the Act, application would have been made and granted and there would have been no further problem in the matter.
  2. With effect from 20 May 1983 the criteria for the issue of exemption certificates changed. Those changes would have applied in relation to the second borrowing. I do not refer to them in detail because, had the partnership been aware of the withholding tax provisions in relation to the first loan, no problem should have arisen in relation to the later loan.
  3. I accept that, according to the norms of Australian law as expressed in the Act, the Hong Kong Bank was liable to pay withholding tax and additional tax thereon in the event of non-payment. I accept, as there was no dispute about the matter that the amounts for which the Hong Kong Bank was liable amounted to $416.14, $677.05 and $676.37 and $1,048.86 in each of the years ended 30 June 1982 to 1985 respectively. I find that the partnership, by reason of not having applied for and received a s128H certificate, was liable to deduct withholding tax when effecting payment of interest; and to make payment of the tax withheld to the Commissioner within twenty-one days of the close of the month in which payment was effected; and that, not having deducted withholding tax, the partnership became liable to pay to the Commissioner all of the amounts for which the Hong Kong Bank was liable by reason of the interest payments by the partnership. I find that the Commissioner did not claim additional tax against the partnership and that, during the year of income ended 30 June 1986, the partnership paid to the Commissioner $2,818.42 being the withholding tax for which the Hong Kong Bank was liable in relation to the years of income ended 30 June 1982 to 1985 inclusive. I do not accept the submission for the Commissioner that the payments so made constituted a "penalty" in the sense provided for in Mayne Nickless Limited v FC of T (84 ATC 4458). There was no prosecution. There was no conviction. And no penalty was imposed upon conviction. The effect of s221YQ(1) was to provide that, even if the partnership had suffered a penalty upon conviction or was liable to prosecution and thereby to the possibility of conviction and penalty, it would nonetheless be liable to pay the unpaid withholding tax and any unpaid additional tax which might have been payable. The obligations of the partnership in that regard had no penal character. So much becomes clear upon a consideration of s221YQ(2) which provides:

"Where a person has paid to the Commissioner an amount payable by virtue of paragraph (1)(a), that person may recover an amount equal to that amount from the person liable to pay the withholding tax to which that first-mentioned amount relates."

  1. If the Hong Kong Bank had taken a different view of its obligations under Australian tax law, it might have responded favourably to the request from the partnership that it pay the tax. But it did not do so and it is hardly likely that it will either change its mind or suffer the partnership to secure judgment against it should it pursue its claim to recovery by force of s221YQ(2) of the Act. That being so, as a practical matter it is for the partnership to seek such solace as it can by way of claiming a tax deduction for the loss it has suffered.
  2. I agree with the submissions for the Commissioner that neither s67 nor s67A are relevant. However, so far as s51 is concerned, I reject the submission that the amount paid to the Commissioner is not deductible by reason of the outgoing being "private" in character as a result of it being a "penalty" imposed upon the partners for breach of legal duty. Furthermore, I am satisfied that there was a "loss or outgoing" and that it was incurred "in gaining or producing the assessable income, or (was) necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income" for the partnership. For the partners it came to be an additional expense incurred in the course of their income-earning activities. The circumstance that the loss was occasioned by ignorance of the law did not make it any the less a "loss or outgoing incurred" in those income-producing activities. It was as much a "loss or outgoing" as it would have been had it been occasioned by an error of calculation or a loss of funds in the course of transmission. That being so, I am satisfied there was an entitlement to a deduction pursuant to the provisions of s51(1) of the Act. What remains to be determined is whether the deduction was wholly allowable, or allowable in part only, or not allowable at all in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1986: the year in which the expenditure was met.
  3. The argument for the Commissioner was based upon a contention that, by force of 128C(2):-

"Withholding tax, when it becomes due and payable, is a debt due to the Queen on behalf of the Commonwealth and payable to the Commissioner."

and that that date was fixed by S128C(1), which provides:-

"Withholding tax is due and payable by the person liable to pay the tax at the expiration of 21 days after the end of the month in which the income to which the tax relates was derived by him ..."

The contention is that no part of the money paid to the Commissioner represented a liability "incurred" on any date during the year of income ended 30 June 1986. I reject the submission. As Dixon, J said in Commissioner of Taxes (SA) v The Executor Trustee & Agency Company of South Australia Ltd Carden's Case C(1938) [1938] HCA 69; 63 CLR 108], when discussing whether an assessment should have been based upon an "accrual" or "cash receipts" basis:-

"In the present case we are concerned with rival methods of accounting directed to the same purpose, namely, the purpose of ascertaining the true income. Unless in the statute itself some definite direction is discoverable, I think that the admissability of the method which in fact has been pursued must depend upon its actual appropriateness. In other words, the enquiry should be whether in the circumstances of the case it is calculated to give a substantially correct reflex of the taxpayer's true income."

  1. In the circumstances of the present case I am satisfied that it was appropriate to have regard to a "cash receipts" basis and for that purpose to allow the deduction in the year in which the payment was actually effected. As Gavin Duffy, C.J. and Dixon, J. had said earlier in Herald and Weekly Times Ltd v FC of T [(1932) [1932] HCA 56; 48 CLR 113 at p118] when considering the deductibility of payments made by a newspaper publisher in satisfaction of defamation claims:-

"No point is made of the fact that the publication took place in a former year, and properly so. The continuity of the enterprise requires that the expenditure should be attributed to the year in which it was actually defrayed."

  1. Accordingly, the order of the Tribunal will be that the deduction claimed by the partnership was an allowable deduction and that, accordingly, the taxable income of the Applicant for the year of income ended 30 June 1986 will be reduced by $1,409.
