Mildred Fitzpatrick and Commissioner of Superannuation [1995] AATA 163 (16 June 1995)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

MILDRED FITZPATRICK v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V94/734

AAT No. 10233

Number of pages - 17

Superannuation

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 2 May 1995

16:6:1995

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr Moore

Solicitors for the Applicant: Maddock Lonie and Chisholm

Advocate for the Respondent: Ms McDougall

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

B.M. FORREST, L.S. RODOPOULOS AND C.G. WOODARD The applicant, Mrs Mildred Mary Fitzpatrick, applied for a review of a decision of a Delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation dated 2 December 1992, which affirmed an earlier decision dated 13 January 1991, that the applicant does not meet the definition of "spouse" as contained in sub-s. 3(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act") and therefore is not entitled to a spouse pension.

2. The Tribunal received in evidence the documents lodged by the respondent pursuant to s. 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (T1-T41 inclusive) and a photograph album of the applicant's which was tendered during the course of the hearing. Oral evidence was given by the applicant, her son Matthew Fitzpatrick and her niece Annette Mary Kelly. Mr Moore of counsel appeared for the applicant and Ms McDougall for the respondent.

BACKGROUND

3. The applicant was born on 17 April 1920 and the late Alan Charles Wilson was born on 1 April 1924. Mr Wilson commenced as a contributor under the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act") on 31 October 1972 by virtue of his employment at the Department of Defence. Following a severe coronary occlusion and extended sick leave, Mr Wilson retired on the ground of invalidity and became a pensioner under the 1922 Act on 1 October 1975.

4. The applicant had been married previously to Mr Fitzpatrick and had divorced in approximately 1970. There were six children of the marriage.

5. The applicant and Mr Wilson met approximately two years prior to marrying. At this time the applicant was living with her son, Matthew Fitzpatrick, in a unit in Highett which had been the family home for many years. Mr Wilson was living by himself in a unit also in Highett. Mr Wilson and the applicant married on 2 October 1982 and following their marriage lived together in Mr Wilson's Highett home for approximately six weeks before holidaying in Sydney. After this holiday the applicant returned to live with her son at her previous residence in Highett and Mr Wilson returned to his own home. Mr Wilson lived there until he sold the property in 1984 and bought a unit in Aspendale where he lived until his death in 1991. The applicant lived in her Highett home until she moved at a later date to a house in Frankston purchased by her son.

6. Mr Wilson wrote a letter to the Commissioner for Superannuation, enclosing his Certificate of Marriage and nominating his wife as next of kin in regard to his superannuation, date stamped 7 August 1984 (T6/30).

7. Mr Wilson died on 28 July 1991. At the date of death, the applicant and Mr Wilson were still lawfully married. The applicant's solicitors submitted an application on her behalf to the Commissioner for Superannuation for a spouse pension.

8. The issue therefore is whether the applicant is entitled to a spouse pension under the 1976 Act. The only element of entitlement in contention is whether the applicant meets the definition of spouse in the 1976 Act, in force at the time of Mr Wilson's death. Sub-section 3(1) provides:

"3. (1) In this Act, unless the contrary intention appears:

...

"spouse", in relation to a person who has died and was, at the time

of his death, an eligible employee or a retirement pensioner, means-

(a) a person who was legally married to the deceased person at the

time of the person's death and who, at that time, was living with

the person on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis;

(b) a person who was legally married to the deceased person at the

time of the person's death but who was not living with the person on

a permanent and bona fide domestic basis at that time, and who, in

the opinion of the Commissioner, was wholly or substantially

dependent upon the deceased person at that time;

..."

THE EVIDENCE

9. The applicant provided the respondent with a number of Statutory Declarations in support of her application. A Statutory Declaration made on 21 October 1991 read in part:

"2. My husband and I resided together for several months after the

date of our marriage, however we then separated and resided in our

own respective residences.

...

5. We always did our weekly shopping together for food and grocery

items. I would pay for the majority of these items and we would

consume the food together generally at my home.

6. Alan Wilson always dined at my premises on Tuesdays and Thursday

and frequently on a Friday.

7. I spent most of the weekends at Alan Wilsons (sic) home. On

Sunday night we would always dine at my sisters (sic) premises

together.

8. Alan Wilson would always take me out to dinner every Saturday

night. He regarded this as his contribution towards the general

food expenses.

9. In all family gatherings which we had including birthdays and

Christmas, Alan Wilson was always included. He was always regarded

as my husband."

(T13/44-46)

...

(T26/66-69)

12. In relation to the separation and the reasons for it, the applicant said that Mr Wilson was very mean with his money and that she was not used to being told what she should do. He would buy poor quality food and was very frugal in his habits. She described the relationship while she lived in Highett as good; there was no argument leading to the separation - it was only because of the food situation that she returned to her son. She said that the separation was not very good for the relationship for a couple of weeks but that after a time they decided to live separately but to continue the relationship. The applicant described that she was then able to buy better cuts of meat and good fruit and vegetables and that eventually her husband also stopped buying bargain goods such as big bags of old fruit. She felt that he learnt it was better to buy fresh food and had changed to a point where he was buying better food for himself. However, despite this change the applicant said that they did not think to move in together again as the relationship was working very well. They shared a lot of common interests, such as going to the movies, dining out, participation in a Scottish dancing group and travel. There was no discussion about the two of them resuming cohabitation when Mr Wilson moved to the Aspendale flat.

13. The applicant said that she stayed every weekend with Mr Wilson at his home and that about two Thursdays a month they went out for dinner. On some Thursdays and nearly every Saturday they liked to eat out, usually at hotels. Every Sunday night they dined with the applicant's sister at her home. They jointly attended family gatherings such as weddings and christenings and went on holidays to Sydney, Canberra, Queensland and Echuca, quite often with the Moorabbin Pipe Band, as they were both on the committee. They stayed together when they travelled and the applicant indicated that she had loved Mr Wilson and assumed he had loved her. They maintained a sexual relationship throughout the marriage.

14. Mr Wilson had been accepted by her family as her husband and she was accepted by his children. A number of photographs were exhibited that showed Mr Wilson with the applicant at various functions and family events. The applicant had decided to keep the name Fitzpatrick as her son and other children called her by that name and she was accustomed to it.

15. Quite often her husband and she would go shopping together but this was not always the case. Sometimes on Thursday afternoons, Saturday mornings or Tuesday evening they would do the shopping. On the weekend the applicant said she would help him with housework.

16. Mr Wilson's health was poor but the applicant said that he never complained. He developed pain in his arms and legs and suffered with angina but despite these problems his death was sudden. Mr Wilson had been in hospital and come to her on discharge to stay with the applicant and her son Matthew when he was ill. He paid her money to look after him. The applicant said that this was for approximately twelve weeks. He returned to his home in Aspendale some time before he died.

17. The applicant also gave evidence in relation to the financial arrangements between herself and her husband. She said that he paid for meals and bought her presents, such as a wedding ring, earrings, engagement ring, brooch and watch. He also bought her clothing. The Tribunal was told that he gave her money and would say that it was a loan but that he never asked for it back. He would give her money to pay bills such as Myercard and Bankcard, giving her $2000 for a Myer account at one time. He always gave her birthday, Christmas and anniversary presents and would sometimes give her money toward maintaining her car.

18. When the applicant lived at Highett, initially with her son and for some twelve to sixteen months by herself, she paid the rent and household expenses. When she moved to her son's home in Frankston, her son provided her with accommodation and food and paid the household expenses in return for her doing the housekeeping. Mr Wilson did not contribute toward the rent and, when asked, the applicant did not think that he should have. The applicant said that she felt he paid his share, a comment which seemed to be directed toward the fact that he had paid for their meals when they ate out together. Her son did the maintenance work for her car and she paid for the petrol. The applicant and Mr Wilson did not have any jointly owned property. Mr Wilson's unit in Aspendale was in his son's name. Apparently he did not leave a will. The applicant said that she had taken action to recover some furniture from Mr Wilson's home. She had not contributed to its purchase.

The couple spent the weekends with each other, attended family gatherings together and spent some time together mid week. The family members refer to the fact that Mr Wilson had been very frugal and managed his money well, one describing him as being very much consumed about saving money and not spending it without good reason. Barry McInnes said that:

(T26/78)

THE ISSUES

31. In order for the applicant to be entitled to a spouse pension the Tribunal has to be satisfied that at the time of his death she was living with Mr Wilson on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis or was wholly or substantially dependent upon him.

A. WHETHER THE APPLICANT WAS LIVING WITH THE DECEASED ON A PERMANENT AND BONA

FIDE DOMESTIC BASIS.

32. There are a number of cases which deal with definitions similar to that contained in s. 3 of the 1976 Act. However, they do not arise in the context of superannuation legislation and as such must be treated with some degree of caution.

33. In Staunton Smith v Secretary, Department of Social Security [1991] FCA 513; 32 FCR 164 the Federal Court considered the cancellation by the Department of a Sole Parent Pension when the wife returned to live in her estranged husband's home with her down syndrome child some years after separation. In this context the Court had to decide whether the applicant was living "separately and apart" from her husband; the reverse of the question asked here. Despite the different issue, the Court's general comments on these type of cases may be noted:

"...The responsibility of the fact-finding Tribunal is to have

regard to all of the material facts of each case... The Tribunal

will make its determination whether a particular man and woman are

not living separately and apart only after assessing the totality of

the evidence and other material that is before it."

(p.170)

34. The court held that the Tribunal should have commented on the parties' social relationship, their sexual relationship, the manner in which they held themselves out to the public and their friends and their subjective views. The court went on to say that:

"... in every case it will be necessary to have regard to the

particular circumstances of the people whose lives and lifestyles

will be affected by the decision of the Department; it is wholly

inappropriate to fall back on standards, conventions or

'role-models'..."

(p.175)

35. Aligned with this view that decision makers must look at the totality of a relationship in assessing its conformity to a legislative definition, it is now accepted that the financial support that a husband gives his wife, whilst being an incident of the state of living together on a bona fide domestic basis is far from being the only one. The preferred approach is to look at all facets of the inter-personal relationship of the two persons: Lambe and Department of Social Security [1981] FCA 171; 4 ALD 362; Re Lambe and Department of Social Security 3 ALN N44; Re Tang and Director-General of Social Services 3 ALN N49.

36. In Re Lambe and Department of Social Security (supra), the Tribunal, in looking at whether the applicant was living with a man as his wife on a bona fide domestic basis although not legally married to him, saw the words "domestic basis" as requiring that there must be a household in which the relationship between the parties subsists, but also went on to express a view that one must look at the overall indicia of the relationship:

"...This will involve consideration of the inter-relationship of the

parties and any children in the household; whether that relationship

contains any of the indicia of a family unit; and the way in which

the parties present their relationship to the outside world. As

was indicated in Waterford's case, the question of financial aid

provided to a woman will be an important consideration but it is

only one of a number of relevant matters which need to be taken into

account if the question posed by Parliament is to be answered.

48 Before a woman can be said to be living with a man 'as his

wife', there must, in our view, be elements both of permanence and

of exclusiveness in the relationship... as these elements are of the

essence of a marriage relationship. But within those broad

confines, it is surely a notorious fact that marriage, in present

day society, allows considerable scope to the parties to develop

their relationship as they see fit, without damaging the fundamental

integrity of that relationship as a marriage. What might be called

the traditional concept of marriage - a marriage in which the

husband is the bread winner and the wife the homemaker caring for

her husband and children - is far from being a comprehensive

description of marriage as it is accepted in today's society. It is

unnecessary and it would be unwise to attempt any description of the

range of relationships acceptable within and recognisable as a

marriage, but it seems reasonably clear that those relationships

extend at one end of the spectrum to the more traditional

relationships of the husband as breadwinner and the wife and

children as dependents, through relationships in which a high degree

of financial independence is enjoyed by both parties, to

relationships in which the wife is the breadwinner and the husband

and children are her dependents."

(p.N76-N77)

37. Whilst this decision appears to broaden the concept of a "domestic" relationship, it does this in the context of widening the scope of acceptable financial arrangements and not in terms of the necessity or otherwise for a common household. It must be noted that Re Lambe is dealing with relationships where the couple are not legally married but seek to show that they live together on a bona fide domestic basis, which differs from a circumstance such as the present where the couple are married but do not occupy one household.

38. Thus the cases take the view that one must look at the relationship in its entirety and as such the facts of being legally married but of not sharing a household or being financially independent would not of themselves be conclusive as to living in a permanent domestic relationship or otherwise, but would be factors taken in to account when assessing the relationship as a whole.

39. However, whilst the cases do urge that one looks at the relationship in its entirety, an emphasis is placed upon the concept of a shared household. In Re RC and Director-General of Social Services 3 ALD 334 the then President of the Tribunal, Davies J said:

"...Increasingly, the right to quite idiosyncratic arrangements in

marriage and marriage-like relationships is being both demanded and

accepted. Necessarily, it is becoming difficult to generalise about

distinctive elements that characterise such relationships. But,

nevertheless, this is the test which the statute requires us to

apply."

He goes on to say,

"...The second element requires that the woman live with the man on

a bona fide domestic basis, that is to say, in a common household.

Such a household will often be one in which the man is the principal

income earner. But, increasingly these days, women are earning

income which may be greater or less than that of the male. The test

looks to a common household to which both the man and the woman both

contribute in their own ways."

(p.348)

40. His Honour refers to Tulk v Tulk where Cussen J referred to elements of "marital intercourse, the dwelling under the same roof, society and protection, support, recognition in public and in private, correspondence during separation" and that the "weight of each of these elements varies with the health, position in life, and all the other circumstances of the parties." He also referred to Todd's case where it was said that "marriage involves many elements some or all of which may be present in a particular marriage - elements such as dwelling under the same roof, sexual intercourse, mutual society and protection, recognition of the existence of the marriage by both spouses in public and private relationships."

41. In Furmage and Social Security Commission (1978) 2 NZAR 75 the Supreme Court of New Zealand considered the cancellation of a domestic purposes benefit, one of the grounds of which was that the applicant and a man were living together on a domestic basis as husband and wife. The relevant legislation said that the decision maker, in its discretion, could regard as husband and wife, any man and woman who, not being legally married, are living together on a domestic basis as husband and wife. In this instance the woman was separated from her husband and had custody of the children. The man involved was initially married but had later left his wife and moved into a one bedroom flat. Both places of residence were used by both parties for the purpose of continuing the relationship.

42. In outlining the difficulties with such legislative provisions, Barker J referred to another New Zealand case, Millet v Millet (1924) NZLR 381, where Salmon J said:

"If on the one hand, mere sexual intercourse is not enough, and if

on the other hand, residence together in a common matrimonial home

is not necessary, what is the true test of cohabitation?"

43. In Furmage (supra) his Honour distinguished the case before him from that of Millet by reference to the additional words "on a domestic basis" . He held that these required a living together under the same roof on a basis of some permanence. He said:

"The word 'domestic' is variously defined in dictionary definitions

placed before me by counsel as 'having the character or position of

an inmate of a house', 'intimate, familiar, at home', or 'of

belonging to the home, house or household,' 'pertaining to one's

place of residence', 'concerning or relating to home or family.'"

(p.79)

44. His Honour took the view that the legislature, in including the words "domestic basis" was stressing the need to be living under the one roof; going out of its way to add these limiting words to an otherwise fairly broad and flexible concept.

45. In Roy and Sturgeon (1986) 11 NSLR 454, Supreme Court (NSW) Powell J said:

"...while I accept that the concept of a man and woman living

together as man and wife does not necessarily imply that the two

persons in question are always living together under the same roof

(see eg, Re Fagan (at 464;822)) it does not necessarily follow that

a man and woman, each of whom maintains a separate home, are to be

regarded as living together because they are accustomed regularly to

spend time together, and even sleep together, in the home of one...

With respect (in referring to Weston's case) it seems to me that to

attempt to dissect the phrase "living together as a husband and wife

on a bona fide domestic basis" into discrete "elements" and then to

test the factors of a particular case by reference to a set of a

priori rules in order to establish whether a particular "element"

is, or is not, present, is to ignore the fact that, just as human

personalities and needs vary markedly, so, too, will the various

aspects of their relationship which lead one to hold that a man and

wife are living together as husband and wife on a bona fide domestic

basis vary from case to case...it seems to me that each case will

involve the Court making a value judgement having regard to a

variety of factors relating to the particular relationship, those

factors including but not limited to the following:

1. the duration of the relationship;

2. the nature and extent of the common residence;

3. whether or not a sexual relationship existed;

4. the degree of financial interdependence, and any arrangements for

support, between or by the parties;

5. the ownership, use and acquisition of property;

6. the procreation of children;

7. the care and support of children;

8. the performance of household duties;

9. the degree of mutual commitment and mutual support;

10. the reputation and "public" aspects of the relationship."

(p.458-459)

46. It must be borne in mind that these cases deal with instances where the parties are not legally married and as such a greater emphasis may have been placed on the need for a common household than an instance such as the present where the couple are in fact legally married.

47. Turning to the facts in the present matter the Tribunal acknowledges that the applicant and Mr Wilson had for some years experienced a supportive and loving relationship, enjoying each other's companionship. The question before the Tribunal though is whether this relationship was such that it could be described as the applicant and Mr Wilson living together on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis.

48. There are a number of elements of the relationship which point to its permanency and the fact that it was bona fide. The couple enjoyed a relationship of some length which could be said to have withstood the test of time; an exclusive sexual relationship existed between the parties; there was some evidence of a limited performance of household duties by the applicant for Mr Wilson, namely the cooking of some meals and some help with domestic work on the weekend; a mutual commitment to and support of each other existed; Mr Wilson came to live with the applicant while convalescing; and there was an acceptance by family and friends of the relationship.

49. However, the applicant and Mr Wilson did not share a common residence. Whilst they spent some time at each other's homes this was limited to the weekend. There could not be said to be two common households between which the couple travelled. The applicant maintained a separate residence, initially with her son, for some time on her own, and then again with her son. She prepared meals for her son and herself and did housework for him. There was a limited time spent by Mr Wilson at the applicant's home and the applicant only spent the weekends at Mr Wilson's home.

50. The Tribunal concurs with the view of his Honour in Furmage that the use of the word "domestic" emphasises that the sharing of a common household is an important factor. However, we reject the respondent's contention that if there be no common household, it follows as a matter of course that the couple cannot be said to live together on a permanent bona fide domestic basis. As previously outlined, the Tribunal must look at the relationship as a whole and all facets of it in deciding this issue.

51. The Tribunal finds that there was no common household and that, in addition, there was little, if any, other domestic arrangements in place between the couple. The applicant spent most of the week at her own home preparing her own meals for herself and her son. She did all the household tasks for herself and presumably Mr Wilson did his own during the week. Whilst they spent the weekend together, for other days the applicant and Mr Wilson made their own arrangements, apart from the occasional meal together on a Tuesday or Thursday. There was some shopping done together but this seems to have been on a very limited basis, just as the applicant's domestic chores at Mr Wilson's home appear to have been limited to some help with housework.

52. Similarly, whilst financial interdependence is no longer a crucial aspect in the assessment of domestic relationships, that is, a factor upon which a case may rise or fall alone, it nonetheless remains a significant issue. In this instance there was little if any financial interdependence and support between the applicant and Mr Wilson. Whilst there was some discrepancy in the evidence given by the applicant on this issue at different times, the Tribunal is satisfied that the parties retained a financial independence from one another within the marriage. The parties did not acquire any property together, they lived in separate residences and maintained these independently, they owned and maintained their own vehicles and the applicant did not leave a will. There was only one joint bank account, of which the applicant was unaware.

53. Whilst the applicant and Mr Wilson recognised each other before their families and friends as each other's husband and wife respectively, it is significant, in the opinion of the Tribunal, that the applicant did not do so to the Department of Social Security. The applicant described herself as divorced and for the duration of the marriage was paid age pension at the single rate. The explanation given to the Tribunal that she had been confused and meant that she was divorced from her first husband is inadequate. It does not explain why a payment continued to be paid to her at the single rate for the duration of her marriage to Mr Wilson. For social security purposes the applicant described herself as a single woman; inferentially she had formed a subjective view of her relationship with Mr Wilson. It is also noted that when she requested a Bereavement Allowance the applicant advised the Department of Social Security that she had separated from her husband soon after her marriage.

54. Whilst many of the cases point to the raising of children and engaging in activities as a family unit as a significant factor to be taken in to account, we accept that given the age of the applicant and Mr Wilson at the time of marriage, that this would be far less significant in their circumstances. On this point we note the evidence that the couple attended family occasions with each other, functioning as a unit.

55. On the totality of the evidence, we are not satisfied that the applicant and Mr Wilson lived with each other on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis at the time of Mr Wilson's death. Whilst the Tribunal understands that there are a broad range of relationships which develop between people today, it is nonetheless bound by the legislation which indicates that the relationship must be of a domestic basis. Given what we have said in relation to the lack of a common household, the limit of financial support each gave to the other, the domestic arrangements, and the applicant's subjective view of her position, we do not accept the submission made on behalf of the applicant that she was a "spouse" in accordance with para. 3(1)(a).

56. We turn now to the second element of the definition: para. 3(1)(b).

B. WAS THE APPLICANT WHOLLY OR SUBSTANTIALLY DEPENDENT ON THE DECEASED.

57. The issue of dependency has arisen in a number of cases. In Re Schlatter and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority 8 ALD 133 the Tribunal looked at para. (b) of the definition of "widow" in the Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Act 1973. "Widow" was defined in s 3(1) of the Act as including a "dependent female" of the member and "dependent female" was defined as a woman who was, at the time of his death, wholly or partly dependent on the member.

58. In its reasons for decision the Tribunal found that dependency is a financial concept as distinct from an emotional one and that it relates to the provision of the necessities of life, that is, the lower order needs of food, shelter and clothing. The Tribunal looked at a number of factors that could be taken in to account, but it emphasised that they were not conclusive one way or another. In summary, the Tribunal said:

"...therefore, dependency is a financial concept which exists

if the claimant satisfies the Authority (or on appeal a Tribunal)

that as at the date of death the deceased was either paying money

or providing support to the claimant for the basic necessities of

life or lower order needs; alternatively, was under a legal

obligation to provide such support to the claimant pursuant to

a maintenance order which probably would have been effectively

enforced if not paid voluntarily....

It would also seem from the cases previously referred to

that, in considering whether the above factors exist, the

Tribunal must look at the whole relationship between the parties

as it existed at the date of death taking into account both past

events and future probabilities which would include matters such

as any financial arrangements, written or verbal agreements,

provisions in a will and all matters which may affect the

financial affairs between the parties and not merely limited

to the payment of money but includes provision of support other

than by payment of money..."

(para. 87, 88)

59. Whilst these comments from Re Schlatter are helpful, the view of "substantially dependent" expounded in the reasons as meaning something not trivial, menial or nominal has been overturned by subsequent decisions. In Secretary, Department of Social Security and Wetter [1993] FCA 17; 112 ALR 151 the Federal Court considered the term "dependent child" in the context of the sole parent's pension wherein it is defined as "a child who is being wholly or substantially maintained by the person" and commented:

"In the present context the word "substantially" appears in

contrast to the word "wholly" but forms a phrase with it. If

"substantially" bore the meaning suggested by the Tribunal,

namely, something more than merely incidental, there would have

been no need at all for the word "wholly" to have appeared. It

is the word "wholly" that gives context here to the word

"substantially". In the context, in my view, the word means

something less than "wholly" but more than merely

"insubstantial' or "insignificant" and is appropriately

paraphrased by the words "in the main" or " as to the greater

part". I am reinforced in my view by the adoption of the same

view by the Full Court of this court in Commissioner for

Superannuation and Scott in a similar context."

(p.159)

60. In Commissioner of Superannuation and Scott 13 FCR 404 the Federal Court considered the phrase "wholly or substantially dependent" in the definition of spouse in s 3 of the Act. Fisher and Spender JJ. held that the Tribunal at first instance had erred in law in directing itself that "substantially meant something more than trivial minimal or nominal." In their opinion the expression meant that that person must be "essentially or in the main dependent upon another person". (p.408)

61. Their Honours went on to express a concern that concepts relevant to the Family Law Act together with the practices and decisions of that court should not necessarily determine conclusively the question of dependence and whether such was substantial. They pointed to the fact that the 1976 Act provides benefits for employees on their retirement and after their death for spouses and children which to some extent the particular employee has purchased by his contributions. In this way it differs greatly from the concept of Social Security legislation and the rights of spouses and children under the Family Law Act when there is a breakdown of the marriage relationship. Their Honours added:

"It is our opinion that the matter could be determined as a

matter of fact without being virtually confined to the Family

Law Act 1975 and decisions of the Family Court. In that

determination, the need for support is at least a relevant matter

as is whether on the facts there has been an irrevocable

abandonment of an entitlement to such support. Furthermore in

assessing need we would have thought that it would be proper

to disregard charity and pension payments which have been made

because the need was not otherwise satisfied."

(p.411)

62. On remission from the Federal Court the Tribunal in Re Scott and Commissioner for Superannuation 19 ALD 208 found that the applicant was dependent upon the deceased in respect of all the basic necessities of life except accommodation. She was dependent upon him for the payment of rates and for maintenance on the family home although no doubt there was very little prospect that she would ever recover those amounts from him. That did not mean that she was not dependent upon him, only that he was not discharging his obligations.

63. In Re Coffey and Commissioner for Superannuation 19 ALD 274 the Tribunal said that dependency was a factual and financial concept relating to the lower order needs of food, shelter and minimal clothing, to be determined at the time of death. Factors relevant were the payment of money or provision of support by the deceased and any legal obligation to support the claimant.

64. In Re Saint-James and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority 3 ALN N59 the Tribunal had to consider whether the widow of the deceased was a "dependent female". In that case the Tribunal said that they were not satisfied at any stage that the applicant was financially dependent on the deceased for her subsistence and support. His financial contribution was no more than was needed to pay the increased living expenses for food and the like which the applicant incurred in providing for two persons rather than just for herself.

65. In the evidence before the Tribunal in this instance there is some discrepancy as to exactly what Mr Wilson did and did not pay for in the course of the relationship. In her earlier statutory declaration the applicant said that the couple did their weekly shopping together, that she would pay and they would consume the food together. She referred to Mr Wilson paying for her restaurant meals as being his contribution toward the general food expenses. However, she sought to resile from this statement in a later Declaration where she said that she would always be with her husband when she did the grocery shopping and that she had never paid for his groceries.

66. The Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant and Mr Wilson retained their financial independence within the marriage. The parties did not jointly own any property or possessions and the only joint bank account that existed was that which was used by Mr Wilson to deposit the married rate aged pension, of which the applicant said she knew nothing. Indeed, the reason proffered to the Tribunal for their separation was that the applicant and Mr Wilson had very different views on managing finances and that this was what ultimately led them to live separately. Presumably the applicant wished to retain her financial independence and to dispose of her income in the way she saw fit.

67. Whilst there was some evidence that the applicant and Mr Wilson did some shopping together, it appeared that the applicant paid for these groceries, Mr Wilson contributing his share to food expenses by paying for the meals that the couple ate when they went out on the weekend and on holidays. If Mr Wilson contributed to the purchases this was only to the extent of covering the applicant's increased expenditure from having him present for some meals.

68. The applicant was in receipt of a single rate aged pension which, along with the board she received from her son, enabled her to pay the rent, buy groceries and cover the other household expenses when she lived at her Highett home. At a later time when she lived at her son's house at Frankston she received $150 a week from him for food and household expenses and in return she cooked his meals, did his housekeeping and it appears ran some errands for his business.

69. The only evidence of Mr Wilson providing for the applicant financially is that he paid for her restaurant meals and for holidays. In addition it appears that he provided gifts and on the odd occasion had given her some money to pay her credit card bill. It could not be said that Mr Wilson provided the applicant's lower order needs or that she was dependent on him to do so.

70. Whilst there is some authority for disregarding welfare payments and the charity of family members when assessing an applicant's need for support: Re Scott, this is not relevant here where the applicant was receiving the age pension, which is not a welfare payment akin to a supporting parent's benefit as in Scott. Nor can it be said that she was forced to rely on the charity of her relatives because of her husband's abandonment of her needs. The evidence was that for a significant amount of time the applicant was able to provide her own necessities and that at a later stage she was given support from her son in return for housekeeping.

71. It follows in our opinion that the applicant was not wholly or substantially dependent upon Mr Wilson at the time of his death and therefore was not a "spouse" within the definition in para. 3(1)(b).

72. The decision under review is affirmed.
