Re Ian John Innes v Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1988] FCA 404; 20 FCR 161 (22 November 1988)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: IAN JOHN INNES

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

No. QLD G2337 of 1987

Income Tax

[1988] FCA 404; 20 FCR 161

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

QUEENSLAND DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Pincus J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - appeal dismissed for default and non-appearance - whether power to set aside order.

Federal Court Rules: O.52A r.14, O.35 r.7

HEARING

BRISBANE

22:11:1988

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr F.L. Harrison QC with Mr D.O.J. North

Solicitors for the Applicant: Deacon & Milani

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr P.E. Hack

Solicitors for the Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The Court's orders of 17 May 1988, including its order for costs, be set aside under O.35 r.7(2);

The applicant give discovery on or before 6 December 1988;

Inspection take place on or before 13 December 1988;

The matter be relisted for further directions on Thursday, 15 December 1988 at 10.00 a.m.;

The costs of today's application be taxed and paid by the appliant to the respondent.

NOTE: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

In this matter an application is made to set aside an order I made on 17 May 1988. The applicant has had an income tax appeal pending in this Court since November 1987, when I made certain interlocutory orders and adjourned the matter for further directions on 23 March 1988. I then made further interlocutory orders and adjourned the matter for review to 17 May 1988.

2. On that date, there was no appearance on behalf of the applicant and the appeal was struck out with costs. The subsequent history of the matter is that nothing was done to prosecute the appeal on the applicant's side until very recently. Apparently, the applicant did not, nor did his various solicitors, inquire as to what was happening in the appeal, and there is no suggestion that any attempt was made to comply with the orders which I had made in March 1988.

3. The case looks like one in which an appeal was instituted without any serious intention of prosecuting it unless all else failed - perhaps not even then. Mr Harrison QC, who has appeared with Mr North for the applicant today, assures me that his instructions are that the appeal is now to be prosecuted without, as I understand it, abandoning the attempt to settle it in the meantime.

4. The point taken on behalf of the Commissioner by Mr Hack is that nothing can be done by the applicant now except to appeal against the order I made in May, seeking an extension of time to do so. The Federal Court Rules to which Mr Hack refers me are O.52A r.14 and O.35 r.7. Order 52A r.14 allows, in circumstances of this sort, an order dismissing proceedings for want of prosecution; an order fixing a time for the doing of acts and ordering that upon non-compliance, proceedings stand dismissed; or any other order as may seem just.

5. The argument which Mr Hack advances is based upon O.52A r.14, sub-r.3, which says:

"An order under paragraph (1)(b) may be varied at

any time before the proceeding stands dismissed for

want of prosecution, and in special circumstances

may be varied or revoked after that time."

Mr Hack says that the special provision for revocation of orders made under paragraph (1)(b) (the self-executing orders) is in contrast to the absence of any such provision for revocation of orders made under (1)(a) or (1)(c). In short, he says that in a tax appeal, if the proceeding is dismissed for want of prosecution or any other order is made because of the applicant's default or lack of diligence, then the only remedy the applicant has is to appeal.

6. Although I can see the force of what Mr Hack says, in my opinion the contention is incorrect. I have the view that O.35 r.7, which gives general power to vary or set aside judgments or orders, even after entry, where the order is made in the absence of a party, applies to tax appeals. The only possible exception is that it may be that, as to orders of the kind mentioned in O.52A r.14(1)(b), O.35 r.7 is implicitly excluded. It is a familiar notion that courts are free to set aside orders made in the absence of one side. See, for example, the decision of the High Court in The Owners of the S.S. Kalibia v. Wilson [1910] HCA 77; (1910) 11 CLR 689 at p 694, per Griffith C.J. It seems to me improbable that those who framed these Rules intended that, if a tax appeal is dismissed for non-appearance or other default on the part of the applicant, then nothing can be done about it other than to appeal. It may be that such a step is taken by the Court because, for example, of a misunderstanding on the part of the respondent or the Court, or some accident preventing the applicant or his solicitor from appearing or complying with the orders of the Court.

7. In short, I think there is jurisdiction to set aside the order which I made in May; despite the view I have that the appeal was not then being prosecuted seriously, I think I should do that, to give the applicant the opportunity now to pursue the matter.

8. The Court, where parties do not desire to prosecute a tax appeal immediately but wish to have time to negotiate, has power to adjourn the matter for a sufficient time to enable negotiations to take place. The practice, as I understand it, is rather against simply letting a great collection of appeals accumulate undealt with, in the hope that one day they might go away.

9. The orders will therefore be:

1. that the Court's orders of 17 May 1988, including its order

for costs, be set aside, under O.35 r.7(2);

2. that the applicant give discovery on or before 6 December

1988;

3. that inspection take place on or before 13 December 1988; and 4. that the matter be relisted for further directions at 10 a.m.

on Thursday, 15 December 1988.

10. The question of costs arises. Mr Harrison has made no submission about it. It is not a matter of routine that the costs of such an application should be paid by the party in default; and as Mr Harrison points out, there has been some degree of default on the part of the respondent, also. In the whole of the circumstances, however, it seems to me fair that the applicant should pay the costs. It will therefore be ordered that the costs of the application before me today be taxed and paid by the applicant to the respondent.
