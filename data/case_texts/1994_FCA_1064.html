DECISION

BLACK CJ This appeal raises once again questions about the effect of the purported registration under state law of a branch of an organisation of employees registered under federal law. In the present case the questions arise in the context of an amalgamation that took place in May 1992 under Division 7 of Part IX of the Industrial Relations Act 1988 (Cth) ("the Act") between the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Employees Association ("the ATAEA") and two other registered organisations of employees to form the Media Entertainment and Arts Alliance. The issues and the relevant facts are set out in the reasons for judgment of the primary judge, Ryan J (reported at (1992) 39 FCR 1), and in the separate reasons for judgment of Northrop J, Wilcox J and Gray J which I have had the advantage of reading.

2. I agree with Gray J that s.26(1) of the Industrial Arbitration Act 1916 (Qld) ("the 1916 Queensland Act") in its terms permitted the registration of a branch of an organisation registered under the Conciliation and Arbitration Act 1904 (Cth) and I join Gray J in agreeing with Wilcox J that the Court should conclude that what was purportedly registered on 18 March 1917 under the 1916 Queensland Act was the Queensland Branch of the ATAEA. For the reasons given by Gray J I too reject the argument that the purported registration on 18 March 1917 had the effect of causing the ATAEA, as distinct from its Queensland branch, to be registered.

3. In determining what follows from these conclusions, I adhere to the view I expressed in Frizziero v Rice (1992) 36 FCR 449 at 452-455 that whilst registration under state law of a branch of a federally registered organisation does not of itself necessarily involve any inconsistency with federal law so as to attract the operation of s.109 of the Constitution, there is a direct inconsistency when state law permits the registration and thus the incorporation of the branch of a federally registered organisation qua branch. In my view, therefore, the 1916 Queensland Act could not have brought about the result that the Queensland branch of the ATAEA became a separate legal entity, a body corporate distinct from the corporation created under federal law when the ATAEA was registered as an organisation of employees under the Conciliation and Arbitration Act 1904. The Queensland branch of the ATAEA thus retained its character as a branch of the federally registered organisation.

4. It then remains to consider whether, in determining the extent of the inconsistency, it is possible to sever the provisions for incorporation from the provisions for registration. In Frizziero v Rice I concluded that the provisions of the Industrial Code 1920 (SA) relating to incorporation could not be severed from those relating to registration and that the provisions of the Industrial Code insofar as they permitted the registration of the branch of a federally registered organisation were also invalid. I considered that the provisions were completely interdependent and inseparable. Although there are differences between the Industrial Code considered in Frizziero v Rice and the 1916 Queensland Act I consider that the provisions for registration and those relating to incorporation in that Queensland Act have essentially the same interdependent and inseparable relationship.

5. Like the South Australian Industrial Code, the 1916 Queensland Act proceeded on the footing that the bodies that were to play an important part in the scheme of industrial conciliation and arbitration provided for by the Act should have the legal character of a corporation established under that legislation. Registration was not merely a form of recognition. The inseparability of registration from incorporation is emphasised by the language of s.37(1) of the 1916 Queensland Act:

"37 (1) Every industrial union shall, upon and during

registration, become and be, for the purposes of this Act, a body

corporate by its registered name, having perpetual succession and

a common seal."

6. An industrial union registered under the 1916 Queensland Act could, in its capacity as a body separate from its members, make applications to the Court of Industrial Arbitration established under Part II, take part in the work of Industrial Boards established under Part IV and make industrial agreements under Part V. An industrial union was to be quite distinct from its members who, for example, would be bound by an industrial agreement entered into by a union and filed under Part V: see s.54(1). It was also contemplated that industrial unions registered under the Act might hold property (see s.30(3)(ii) and s.76) and might sue members for fees and dues payable to the union under its rules (s.79). Industrial unions were liable to penalties for contravention of the Act (s.74) and the provision for the imposition of penalties against an employee who was a member of an industrial union involved there being an industrial union against which the penalty could be enforced (see ss.75(1) and (2)).

7. In my view, for the same reasons as I concluded that severance was impossible in the case of the legislation considered in Frizziero v Rice it is not possible to sever the provisions for incorporation in the 1916 Queensland Act from the provisions for registration.

8. Counsel for the appellants argued that the question of severance should be considered in the light of Queensland legislation to the same effect as s.15A of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 (Cth). However, the first such provision in Queensland was not made until the Acts Interpretation Act 1954 (Qld) was enacted: (see now s.9, and see s.4 of Act as originally enacted). This was of course well after the events of 1917 that are relevant to this appeal and the provisions therefore have no bearing upon the present question: see University of Wollongong v Metwally [1984] HCA 74; (1984) 158 CLR 447.

9. I agree with Wilcox J that the Queensland Branch of the ATAEA was incapable of amalgamating, independently of the larger organisation, with the Australian Workers Union of Employees or any other body. It remained part of the federal organisation, and assets held in its name were assets of the federal organisation. Upon amalgamation of the federal organisation the assets became those of the Alliance by reason of s.253R of the Act.

10. I should refer briefly to the jurisdiction of the Court to make the orders sought by the appellants. Section 50(1)(a) of the Industrial Relations Act confers jurisdiction on the Court with respect to matters arising under the Act in relation to which applications may be made to it under the Act. Sections 253X(2) and 253ZC(1) were relied upon as authorising the application made to the primary judge.

11. Section 253X provides:

"(1) The amalgamated organisation must take such steps as are

necessary to ensure that the amalgamation, and the operation

of this Subdivision in relation to the amalgamation, are

fully effective.

(2) The Court may, on the application of an interested person,

make such orders as it considers appropriate to ensure that

subsection (1) is given effect to."

12. Section 253ZC provides:

"(1) Where any difficulty arises in relation to the application

of this Subdivision to a particular matter, the Court may,

on the application of an interested person, make such order

as it considers proper to resolve the difficulty.

(2) An order made under subsection (1) has effect despite

anything contained in this Act or in any other Commonwealth

law or any State or Territory law."

13. The subdivision to which s.253X(1) and s.253ZC(1) each refer is Subdivision F of Division 7 of Part IX of the Act. Part IX is the part dealing with registered organisations and Division 7 concerns the amalgamation of such organisations. Division 7 is divided into seven subdivisions, each of them making detailed provision for particular aspects of the amalgamation process, a process that reflects (amongst others) the object specified in s.3(j) of the Act "to encourage and facilitate the amalgamation of organisations". Division 7 commences with s.233 which recites, in subsection (a), that it is the intention of the Parliament that in the application to Division 7 of the objects of the Act, particular regard should be had to the attainment of the objects mentioned in paragraphs 3(h), (j) and (k).

14. Included within Subdivision F is s.253R(1), noted earlier, which provides that on the day of amalgamation all assets and liabilities of a de-registered organisation cease to be assets and liabilities of that organisation and become assets and liabilities of the amalgamated organisation.

15. To make an amalgamation, and the operation of Subdivision F in relation to it, fully effective in a practical sense it would clearly be necessary for a newly-created amalgamated organisation to take control of any assets that had become its property upon amalgamation. To take this example further, I think it clear that s.253X(1) imposes an obligation on the amalgamated organisation to get in such assets. If the amalgamated organisation failed to do so then s.253X(2) would empower the Court, on the application of an interested person, to require it to do so.

16. On one view, s.233X(2) might be limited in operation to a situation in which the amalgamated organisation was recalcitrant in carrying out its obligation to ensure that the amalgamation was fully effective. On that view the power of the Court to "make such orders as it considers appropriate to ensure that subsection (1) is given effect to" would not extend to ordering, for example, the handing over of assets belonging to the amalgamated organisation and wrongfully held by others, but would be confined to ordering the amalgamated organisation to act. If the organisation was faithfully doing what s.253X(1) required it to do, but was being frustrated in doing so by the refusal of others to co-operate it could nevertheless, on this view, receive no assistance from the Court under s.253X(2).

17. A broader view of the scope of s.253X is that the focus is not upon the amalgamated organisation, although it certainly has a duty to ensure that the amalgamation is fully effective, but upon the object of ensuring that the amalgamation and the operation of Subdivision F in relation to the amalgamation are in fact fully effective in a practical sense. In my judgment, the language of the section readily permits this broader view and it is the view to be preferred. The end of a fully effective amalgamation is what Division 7 and whole of Subdivision F are aimed at. The broader view advances this object, and also the specific statutory object of facilitating the amalgamation of organisations (see s.3(j)) to which object attention is directly drawn by s.233, the section with which Division 7 beings. Moreover, it advances the intention of the Parliament, expressed in s.233(b) that the application of the Act in relation to amalgamations should be, amongst other things, practical. I conclude therefore that the Court does have jurisdiction to make the orders sought by the appellants, it being clear that at least the President of the Alliance and the Alliance itself are interested persons within the meaning of s.253X(2).

18. This conclusion about the scope of s.253X makes it unnecessary for me to express any view about the uncertainties that would seem to lurk within the apparently broad confines of s.253ZC and about the sense in which "difficulty" is used in that section.

19. In my view the appeal should be allowed and the order made on 19 October 1992, dismissing the application, should be set aside. I agree with the declarations and orders proposed by Wilcox J.

NORTHROP J On 18 March 1917, the Queensland Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Union of Employees ("the State Union") was registered as an industrial union of employees under The Industrial Arbitration Act of 1916 (Qld) ("1916 State Act"). Under subsection 26(1) of the 1916 State Act, on such registration the State Union was to be an industrial union "until such registration is duly cancelled". Under s31 of the 1916 State Act, a certificate was issued to the State Union. That section provides:

"31. The registrar shall issue to each industrial union

registered under this Act a certificate of registration in the

prescribed form, which certificate shall, until proof of

cancellation, be conclusive evidence of the registration of the

union therein mentioned, and that it has complied with the

prescribed conditions to entitle it to be registered."

2. For the purposes of this appeal it can be accepted that the registration has not been cancelled and so the certificate of registration of the State Union constitutes conclusive evidence of the registration of the State Union and that it complied with the prescribed conditions which entitled it to be registered. Under s36 of the 1916 State Act, the Court of Industrial Arbitration established under that Act had power, on certain grounds, to order that the registration of the State Union be cancelled. For the purposes of this appeal it can be accepted that no such order has been made. Section 37 of the 1916 State Act is set out in full:

"37.(1.) Every industrial union shall, upon and during

registration, become and be, for the purposes of this Act, a body

corporate by its registered name, having perpetual succession and

a common seal.

(2.) There shall be inserted in the registered name of

every industrial union the words "union of employees," and also

the locality in which the majority of its members reside or

exercise their calling."

3. For the purposes of this appeal, therefore, it must be accepted that at all material times the State Union was a corporation separate and distinct from its members. This statement will be considered in more detail later in these reasons.

4. The Media, Entertainment and Arts Alliance ("the Alliance") is an organisation of employees under the Industrial Relations Act 1988 ("Commonwealth Act"). It is a corporation separate and distinct from its members. It came into being as the Alliance on 18 May 1992 following the amalgamation of three organisations of employees one of which was the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Employees Association ("the ATAEA"). The ATAEA had first become registered as an organisation of employees on 10 February 1910 under earlier Commonwealth legislation under a different name but for present purposes it can be referred to as the ATAEA. In February 1917 the Queensland Branch of the ATAEA successfully sought registration as an industrial union under the 1916 State Act. The similarity of names of the ATAEA and the State Union illustrates the close relationship between them. It is true to say, however, each corporation performed the functions for which each had become incorporated. The ATAEA performed its functions in the Commonwealth industrial tribunals. The State Union performed its functions in the Queensland State tribunals. Neither had a right to appear in the tribunals in which the other appeared in order to perform the functions for which each had become incorporated. It is true to say that confusion and absence of clear lines of control developed between the natural persons being members of the ATAEA, whether attached to the Queensland Branch of the ATAEA or not, and natural persons being members of the State Union. In most cases, the natural persons were members of the ATAEA attached to its Queensland Branch as well as being members of the State Union. These persons can be described as persons having dual membership.

5. Despite this uncertainty behind the veils of the corporate personalities of the ATAEA and the State Union, the functions of each corporation were performed by each in its respective area. It may be that the natural persons did not always perform their obligations in conformity with the requirements of the Commonwealth Act and the 1916 State Act in its original form or as later enacted. Nevertheless, natural persons were directing the affairs of the two corporate bodies. In reality, this is the genesis of the present litigation.

6. Part IX of the Commonwealth Act is headed "Registered Organisations". Division 7 of Part IX is headed "Amalgamation of organisations". Division 7 contains detailed provisions providing for the amalgamation of two or more organisations to create one organisation. Subdivision F of Division 7 is headed "Amalgamation taking effect". Subdivision F comprises sections 253Q to 253ZC. The application giving rise to this appeal was commenced under s253ZC. That section is set out:

"253ZC(1) Where any difficulty arises in relation to the

application of this Subdivision to a particular matter, the Court

may, on the application of an interested person, make such order

as it considers proper to resolve the difficulty.

253ZC(2) An order made under subsection (1) has effect despite

anything contained in this Act or in any other Commonwealth law or

any State or Territory law."

7. In that section, "the Court" is the Federal Court of Australia.

8. It is noted that the section refers to "the application of this Subdivision". That is Subdivision F. That Subdivision concerns the amalgamation which has been approved in conformity with the earlier Subdivisions of Division 7 and which has to be consummated. The amalgamation that has been approved for the purposes of Division 7 takes effect in accordance with s253Q. A presidential member must fix a day, called the amalgamation day, on which the amalgamation is to take effect. In substance, on the amalgamation day the amalgamated organisation becomes registered as the organisation registered in conformity with the Commonwealth Act. In the present case, that is the Alliance. Here the amalgamation date was 18 May 1992. On that day, by reason of subsection 253Q(3), the persons who immediately before that day were members of the ATAEA or of the other two organisations involved in the amalgamation became members of the Alliance and the three organisations became deregistered. Section 253R is important and is set out:

"253R(1) On the amalgamation day, all assets and liabilities of

a de-registered organisation cease to be assets and liabilities of

that organisation and become assets and liabilities of the

amalgamated organisation.

(2) For all purposes and in all proceedings, an asset or

liability of a de-registered organisation existing immediately

before the amalgamation day is taken to have become an asset or

liability of the amalgamated organisation on that day."

9. With the exception of subsection 253W(1), no reference need be made to the other provisions of Subdivision F at this stage. That subsection is set out:

"253W(1) This Subdivision applies, and must be given effect to,

despite anything in:

(a) any other Commonwealth, State or Territory law; or

(b) any contract, deed, undertaking, agreement or other

instrument."

10. Leading up to the amalgamation day, being 18 May 1992, disputation arose between those natural persons controlling the affairs of the ATAEA and the Queensland Branch of the ATAEA on the one hand and those natural persons controlling the affairs of the State Union on the other. In February 1992, those controlling the State Union caused an application to be filed by the State Union under Queensland State legislation seeking amalgamation with another State union, the Australian Workers Union of Employees ("the AWU") pursuant to the provisions of the State legislation. In April 1992 the Federal Council of the ATAEA directed those persons, being the respondents Herbert White, Arthur Westbrook, James Beattie, Macquarie Hayward, William Morris and Edward Estern (hereafter referred to as "the natural person respondents") to cease taking steps to implement the proposed amalgamation of the State Union and the AWU and to take steps to bring about the amalgamation of the State Union with corresponding state unions associated with the other two organisations proposing to amalgamate to form the Alliance. The natural person respondents ignored the direction. After the amalgamation day, the management committee of the Alliance directed the natural person respondents to take all steps to give effect to the federal amalgamation insofar as they related to the State Union. The natural person respondents ignored that direction. Apparently, nobody has taken legal proceedings against the natural person respondents under s209 of the Commonwealth Act seeking orders that they perform or observe the rules of the Alliance, or the ATAEA, by carrying out the directions mentioned.

11. On 5 June 1992, the appellants made an application to the Court for orders under s253X and s253ZC of the Commonwealth Act. Section 253ZC has been set out earlier in these reasons. Section 253X provides as follows:

"253X(1) The amalgamated organisation must take such steps as

are necessary to ensure that the amalgamation, and the operation

of this Subdivision in relation to the amalgamation, are fully

effective.

(2) The Court may, on the application of an interested

person, make such orders as it considers appropriate to ensure

that subsection (1) is given effect to."

12. It is difficult to see why the application sought orders under s253X. The Alliance is the amalgamated organisation. No orders are being sought against it. In fact it was named as an applicant. The other two named applicants are members of the Alliance. They had been members of the ATAEA. These three applicants are the appellants in this appeal. The respondents named to the application were the natural person respondents to this appeal, being members of the ATAEA and the State Union, and the State Union. A large number of orders were sought against the respondents including orders restraining them from funding the proposed amalgamation between the State Union and the AWU, a declaration that the former Queensland Branch of the ATAEA "is one and the same as" the State Union, a declaration that all the assets and all interests in any property held in the name of the State Union are the assets of the Alliance, consequential orders, including an order directing the respondents to cause those assets to be transferred to the Alliance, and a declaration that all members of the State Union are members of the Alliance.

13. The application came on for hearing before the Court constituted by Ryan J. By that time additional respondents had been added. On 19 October 1992, the Court dismissed the application. The appellants have appealed from that judgment. The orders sought by the amended notice of appeal are in substance those contained in the application, but now, having regard to what has happened, an additional order is sought declaring that the purported amalgamation between the State Union and the AWU is void. At the hearing of the appeal, there was a joint appearance on behalf of the natural person respondents and the State Union. The same counsel appeared on behalf of Mr Dolbel and the AWU. There was a joint appearance on behalf of the Industrial Registrar of the State of Queensland and the Minister for Employment, Training and Industrial Relations for the State of Queensland. The respondents supported the judgment of the trial Judge.

14. The resolution of this appeal has been made difficult by reason of a number of factors. The powers conferred on the Court by s253ZC of the Commonwealth Act are novel. As far as I know, this is the first case where the Court has had to consider the nature of those powers. The Court was not referred to any authority of this Court where the section had been considered. There is no doubt that the Court has jurisdiction to hear and determine an application under s253ZC, see paragraph 50(1)(a) of the Commonwealth Act. It was only after the Court raised this matter that submissions were directed to the issue.

15. Directions had been given that the parties file written submissions before the commencement of the hearing of the appeal. Because this had been done, no summary or outline of argument in conformity with the Federal Court practice note No 1 had been provided by the appellants. The written submissions went straight into a complex mire of facts. Nowhere was there a brief statement of each contention of law or fact on which counsel wished to rely. During the course of submissions, it appeared that the chief submission or contention of the appellants was that, in reality, the State Union was the ATAEA, or Queensland Branch of the ATAEA, that the State Union had no separate legal identity and that any property "owned" by it was owned by the ATAEA, that any awards to which it was a party under Queensland State legislation were awards of the ATAEA or the Queensland Branch of the ATAEA, that the members of the State Union, by reason of such membership, were members of the ATAEA and the Queensland Branch of ATAEA and that upon the amalgamation day the State Union became part of the Alliance with the result that thereafter the relationship which had existed between the State Union and the ATAEA continued to exist between the State Union and the Alliance. A summary of argument would have compelled the formulation of propositions on behalf of the appellants to assist the Court in the understanding of the issues.

16. Two days had been allocated for the hearing of the appeal. Because of the difficulties arising from the matters mentioned in the preceding paragraph, great pressures arose in the presentation of submissions. In the event, the parties were given leave to make further submissions in writing. The final submissions on behalf of the appellants were not received until 12 October 1993, even though the hearing of the appeal was completed on 4 August 1993. The written submissions drew attention to the real issues raised by this appeal and were of great assistance.

17. For ease of reference, s253ZC is set out again:

"253ZC(1) Where any difficulty arises in relation to the

application of this Subdivision to a particular matter, the Court

may, on the application of an interested person, make such orders

as it considers proper to resolve the difficulty.

253ZC(2) An order made under subsection (1) has effect despite

anything contained in this Act or in any other Commonwealth law or

any State or Territory law."

18. It is to be noted that the exercise of the power to make an order is dependent upon the existence of a difficulty arising "in relation to the application of" Subdivision F of Division 7 of Part IX of the Commonwealth Act "to a particular matter". The Subdivision contains provisions giving effect to amalgamations, but the power is to be exercised with respect to a particular "matter". The section does not refer to "a particular amalgamation", but to "a particular matter". This must refer to a particular matter arising in a particular amalgamation. The word "matter", in this sense, must relate to a "thing" or "subject matter" contained in a section within the subdivision. At the request of the Court, counsel for the appellants supplied to the Court a list of difficulties which he contended came within the first part of subsection 253ZC(1). This list, in essence, expanded each item or subject matter contained in the earlier sections of the Subdivision. Included among the difficulties so listed were difficulties as to the identification of members of a proposed de-registering organisation immediately before the amalgamation day, who by subsection 253Q(3), became members of the amalgamated organisation. It is not immediately clear that questions relating to that matter are difficulties. The Commonwealth Act contains detailed provisions directed to determining who are members of an organisation, see Division 9 of Part IX of the Commonwealth Act. That Division, which consists of sections 261 to 267, is headed "Membership of organisations". There is much to be said for the view that questions relating to membership of an organisation should be considered by the Court pursuant to procedures in conformity with Division 9 of Part IX. Under those procedures, each person affected must be given a chance to be heard. No such provision applies to "difficulties" under Division 7 and, certainly, all persons who may be affected by the orders sought in this appeal have not been given the opportunity to be heard. Indeed, there is no evidence in this appeal directed to the particular circumstances of any of those persons. Further, reference is made to s253S which varies the period of notice required by s264 in relation to a resignation from membership of a proposed de-registering organisation. The effect of subsection 253ZC(2) will be considered later.

19. The subject matter of s253R is the transfer of assets from a de-registering organisation to the amalgamated organisation. The terms of that section have been set out earlier in these reasons. Section 253T relates to orders or awards of the Industrial Relations Commission, and the effect of amalgamation on those. Other sections relate to the effect of amalgamation on other matters, for example, s253U on instruments and s253V on pending proceedings. Sections 253Y, 253Z, 253ZA and 253ZB provide for the processes by which the transfer of property is to be effected. It is in this context that subsection 253W(1), which has been set out earlier in these reasons, applies to ensure the Subdivision has effect notwithstanding other laws. In this regard, the provisions of subsection 253ZC(2) have the same effect with respect to orders made under subsection 253ZC(1). The wording of subsection 253W(1) and 253ZC(2) have similar effect, the one on the provisions of the Subdivision, the other on orders made under subsection 253ZC(1). An illustration of a difficulty would be if, for some reason, the procedural provisions of sections 253Y, 253Z, 253ZA or 253ZB were being ignored by the persons required to perform functions. In the present case, the appellants are seeking orders determining whether the assets of the State Union were the assets of the ATAEA on the amalgamation day and thus, under s253R, became the assets of the Alliance. None of the assets have been identified. There has been no allegation of any non-compliance with any of the provisions of sections 253Y, 253Z, 253ZA or 253ZB. It is difficult to see how the issue sought to be raised by the appellant constitutes a difficulty in relation to the application of Subdivision 7.

20. These matters were not argued before the Court and I am prepared, for the purposes of this appeal only, to assume that the Court has jurisdiction or power to deal with the appeal.

21. The second part of subsection 253ZC empowers the Court "to make such order as it considers proper to resolve the difficulty". Having regard to R v Commonwealth Court of Conciliation and Arbitration Ex parte: Barrett [1945] HCA 50; (1945) 70 CLR 141, this constitutes a valid grant of judicial power. 23. It is very easy, these days, to forget the nature and legal basis of organisations under the Commonwealth Act. So much publicity is given to the actions by, and pronouncements made on behalf of, organisations of employees, usually by reference to the description of a "trade union" and to the activities and pronouncements made on behalf of the Australian Council of Trade Unions, the association of trade unions, mainly organisations of employees under the Commonwealth Act, that organisations of employees have taken on the appearance of a political group in the Government of Australia. As a result of this perception, the nature of organisations is often overlooked. From time to time, it is necessary to restate the true nature of organisations in order to resolve issues arising in connection with organisations, their powers and their relationships with their members. This is such an occasion.

24. In Australia, industrial relations are regulated under Commonwealth law and under State Law. Trade unions developed from guilds which essentially were associations of craftsmen deciding matters in general meetings. The need for groups of this kind arose from the necessity of persons having a common interest to combine together to protect and further their common interests. The history of guilds and trade unions is turbulent and actions by members involved many breaches of criminal law. Legislation was enacted in Great Britain and in the Colonies and States of Australia to regulate the activities of trade unions, but, at the present time, this type of legislation does not play a major part in industrial legislation.

25. The Commonwealth and States introduced legislation permitting another type of association to enable industrial relations to be regulated. The States were not limited in the type of legislation that could be introduced to achieve this purpose. Some States, by legislation, set up tribunals to regulate industrial relations between employers and employees and as part of that regulation provided for the incorporation of State unions or industrial associations. The incorporated bodies so set up were given, by legislation in some of the States, the exclusive right to appear before the State tribunals to represent the interests of their members, and possibly persons entitled to be members. The State of Queensland adopted this course. The 1916 State Act is an illustration of this type of State legislation. Other States, Victoria being an example, did not adopt that course.

26. The Commonwealth had a limited legislative power in this regard, namely under s51(XXXV) of the Constitution, to make laws for the peace, order and good Government of the Commonwealth with respect to:

"xxxv Conciliation and arbitration for the prevention and

settlement of industrial disputes extending beyond the

limits of any one State."

Reference should be made also to the incidental power in placitum (xxxix).

27. The Conciliation and Arbitration Act 1904 was enacted by the Commonwealth pursuant to this power. That Act, the predecessor of the Industrial Relations Act 1988, contained provisions relating to the registration of associations of employees and of employers. Upon registration, these associations became organisations. Many authorities of the High Court, the Australian Industrial Court and the Federal Court of Australia have considered the nature of organisations under the Commonwealth Act and the Conciliation and Arbitration Act. These authorities have established the clear principle that organisations are a corporation separate and distinct from its members. A helpful summary of the development of this legal concept is given in Wiseman v Professional Radio and Electronics Institute of Australasia [1978] FCA 31; (1978) 35 FLR 24 per Evatt and Northrop JJ at pp38-40. This is a long passage but it is useful to repeat it:

"The purposes of the registration of organizations under the Act,

stated broadly, are to facilitate the creation and settlement of

disputes arising from industrial relationships between groups or

classes of persons ascertained by reference to the conditions of

eligibility prescribed by the rules of organizations which, upon

registration, become corporate bodies separate and distinct from

their members: generally see Jumbunna Coal Mine, No Liability v

Victorian Coal Miners' Association (1908) 6 CLR 309; The Queen v

Dunlop Rubber Australia Ltd; Ex parte Federated Miscellaneous

Workers' Union of Australia [1957] HCA 19; (1957) 97 CLR 71; Williams v Hursey

[1959] HCA 51; (1959) 103 CLR 30; and The Queen v Clarkson; Ex parte Victorian

Employers' Federation [1973] HCA 57; (1973) 131 CLR 100.

By the Jumbunna case (1908) 6 CLR 309 it was established that

legislative provisions providing for the creation of

representative bodies both of employers and employees came within

the powers conferred by s51 placitum (xxxv) of the Constitution,

aided where necessary by the incidental power contained in

placitum (xxxix). Thus organizations are instruments for the more

effective exercise of the constitutional power and the legislature

has power to make laws by which organizations can be "made and

unmade at the will of Parliament" and by which organizations can

be "moulded, re-fashioned or abolished": see Australian

Commonwealth Shipping Boad v Federated Seamen's Union of

Australasia [1925] HCA 27; (1925) 36 CLR 442 at p453 per Isaacs J. The

legislature thus is empowered to make laws relating to the method

by which organizations are to be controlled, their affairs are to

be regulated and elections to committees of management within

organizations are to be conducted. In this respect, reference may

be made to what was said by the High Court comprising Dixon,

McTiernan, Williams, Webb, Fullagar and Kitto JJ in Federated

Ironworkers' Association of Australia v Commonwealth (1951) 84 CLR

265 at pp 282-283: "We think it follows from the principles to

which we have already referred that the organizations registered

and incorporated under the Conciliation and Arbitration Act may be

regulated under the incidental power with a view to ensuring that

the election of officers is duly carried out. The federal power

to provide for the formation, registration and incorporation of

such organizations depends upon the necessity or the desirability

of large classes of men being represented. Representation

involves much more than the establishment of a corporation, that

is to say, of a legal power capable of being made a party to legal

proceedings and of being the object of legal rights and duties.

It involves the representation of the men by a committee of

management or other officers who control the actions of the

corporate body. The authentic expression of the will of the

members in appointing officers must be incidental to the subject

matter of the constitutional power. It arises directly out of the

considerations which afforded the constitutional justification for

the provisions which are now Divns 1 and 2 of Pt VI of the

Conciliation and Arbitration Act 1904-1951 and Schedule B (see

particularly Schedule B(1)(a))."

Despite the views expressed in the Jumbunna case (1908) 6 CLR 309,

there remained a lingering belief that to constitute an industrial

dispute within the meaning of s51 placitum (xxxv) of the

Constitution, an industrial disagreement must exist between

definite employers and definite or ascertainable employees,

members of the organisation assuming to represent them: see R v

Commonwealth Court of Conciliation and Arbitration; Ex parte

William Holyman and Sons Ltd [1914] HCA 36; (1914) 18 CLR 273. A practical

illustration of the application of that old doctrine can be seen

in Pearce v W.D. Peacock and Co Ltd [1917] HCA 28; (1917) 23 CLR 199. The

doctrine applied in the William Holyman case [1914] HCA 36; (1914) 18 CLR 273 was

overruled by Burwood Cinema Ltd v Australian Theatrical and

Amusement Employees' Association [1925] HCA 7; (1925) 35 CLR 528. Today the

position is that an organization acts in an independent capacity

and not merely as an agent for its members. It is a party

principal and, apart from the question of whether a dispute is

real or not, the acts and conduct of individual members of the

organization are immaterial: generally see the Dunlop Rubber case

(1957) 97 CLR at pp80-85, per Dixon CJ, Webb, Fullagar, Kitto and

Taylor JJ."

28. Another useful passage appears in Imlach v Daley and Others [1985] FCA 132; (1985) 7 FCR 457 per Evatt and Northrop JJ at pp 462-3:

"It is clear law that branches of an organisation are not separate

bodies distinct from the organisation. Branches are not legal

corporations within an organisation. A reading of the judgment of

Fullagar J in Williams v Hursey [1959] HCA 51; (1959) 103 CLR 30 makes that

proposition abundantly clear. The Act contains provisions

enabling associations of employees or of employers to be

registered as organisations. Upon registration as an

organisation, the association becomes a body corporate separate

and distinct from its members. For the purposes of settling

industrial disputes, an organisation of employees stands in the

place of the group of employees who are eligible to be members of

the organisation under its eligibility rule. The organisation is

a party principal in these disputes and under the Act awards made

by the Commission in settlement of disputes are binding on

employers with respect to the members of the organisation and, in

appropriate cases, persons eligible to be members but who are not

members of the organisation. It must not be forgotten that the

rationale for organisations and the constitutional basis for their

regulation by Commonwealth legislation is the power contained in

placitum (XXXV) of s51 of the Constitution, namely the power to

make laws with respect to "conciliation and arbitration for the

prevention and settlement of industrial disputes existing beyond

the limits of any one State"; generally see Jumbunna Coal Mine NL

v Victorian Coal Miners' Association (1908) 6 CLR 309 (the

Jumbunna case), Williams v Hursey (supra), R v Dunlop Rubber

Australia Ltd; Ex parte Federated Miscellaneous Workers' Union of

Australia [1957] HCA 19; (1957) 97 CLR 71 (the Dunlop Rubber case) and R v

Clarkson; Ex parte Victorian Employers Federation (1973) 131 CLR

100 (the VEF case).

In industrial jurisprudence, an organisation need not be divided

into branches. The existence of an organisation as a corporate

body is essential. Branches are established for the purpose of

internal management. Branches are not essential for the existence

of organisations. Organisations are creatures of the Act.

Branches are formed or created by the rules of an organisation or

pursuant to powers contained in the rules. There is no doubt some

of the provisions of the Act recognise the existence of branches

within organisations, but that does not mean that every

organisation must have branches. As was said by Fullagar J in

Williams v Hursey at 54-55 in reference to a branch of an

organisation of employees:

"It has no separate identity - no existence apart from the

registered organisation, of which it is an integral and

inseverable part. Its members are merely a section of the

total membership of the federation - locally organised for

the sake of convenience, but in no respect independent of

the federation, and in all respects subject to the control

of the federation. The branches are permitted within limits

to make rules of their own, but the rules which they make

derive authority from the rules of the federation."

It is equally clear law that an organisation, provided it complies

with the requirements of the Act, the regulations and its rules,

is able to mould its internal structures as it thinks fit. It is

not for the court to mould those structures to a form which the

court considers desirable. The position is made clear by the

following passage from the judgment of Dean J in the Municipal

Officers' Association of Australia v Lancaster [1981] FCA 151; (1981) 54 FLR 129

at 164-165, a passage approved and applied in Wright v McLeod

(1983) 6 IR 203; 51 ALR 483:

"The constraints and restrictions imposed, by positive and

negative requirements of the Act and Regulations, upon the

freedom of the members of an organization to select, for

themselves, the rules which they consider appropriate for

their particular organization, are real and significant. It

cannot, however, be too strongly stressed that, subject to

those constraints and restrictions, the content of the rules

of a registered organization is primarily a matter for the

members (Watson v Australian Workers' Union (1967) 10 FLR

347 at 361; Cassidy v Amalgamated Postal Workers' Union of

Australia (1967) 11 FLR 124 at 126-127; Wiseman v

Professional Radio and Electronics Institute of Australasia

[1978] FCA 31; (1978) 35 FLR 24; Re Airline Hostesses' Association (1980)

[1980] FCA 110; 48 FLR 214). This Court has no authority generally to

supervise the content of the rules or to require that the

rules comply with what those constituting the court might

see as preferable, desirable or ideal. To put the matter

differently, it is for the members, or those entrusted by

the members in that regard, to decide the content of the

rules. The function of this Court is to determine, in

accordance with ordinary judicial procedure, whether some

provision or provisions of the rules adopted by, or on

behalf of, the members can properly be described not merely

as undesirable but as oppressive, unreasonable or unjust.""

29. The Commonwealth Act contains many provisions relating to the method of control of organisations by its members. Sections 208 and 209 of the Commonwealth Act, corresponding to the earlier provisions of sections 140 and 141 of the Conciliation and Arbitration Act, which in turn were similar to the sections considered in Barrett above, provide a very simple but effective method by which members can test the powers and the exercise of powers of organisations and committees of organisations including branches of organisations. It is true to say that the Courts have taken a broad view of the powers of an organisation and have left it to the controlling bodies to determine what the organisation should do, but, nevertheless, organisations, being corporations, are subject to the laws of the Commonwealth and of the States when performing their activities.

30. Organisations have a unique position with respect to the regulation of industrial relations under the Commonwealth legislation. They are able to create industrial disputes. They have the exclusive right to appear in the Commonwealth Industrial Relations Commission with respect to those disputes. Under State legislation providing for registration of trade unions, normally the State registered union has the exclusive right to appear in the State industrial tribunals. The existence of these different structures has led to difficulties, particularly where all or some of the members of a branch of an organisation are members of a State registered union. An illustration of this type of problem appears in Moore v Doyle (1967) 15 FLR 59. A Committee, constituted by J.B. Sweeney J, and known as the Committee of Inquiry on Co-ordinated Industrial Organisations, was appointed to investigate and recommend solutions to difficulties arising from this problem. The terms of reference of the Committee are set out in Imlack v Daley, above, at p467:

"The Committee will inquire into -

a. The desirability of a system or organisation and/or

registration of employee and employer organisations which

would enable the one body to represent in terms of legal

personality, structure, organisation and otherwise its

members in both Australian and State arbitration systems.

b. Means by which such a system if desirable may be achieved.

c. The systems of registration of organisations of employees

and employers under the Australian Conciliation and

Arbitration Act and of trade or industrial unions and

associations under statutes providing for conciliation and

arbitration in the States and the effects of such systems on

the operations of these bodies and their branches.

d. Problems and difficulties arising from the fact of

registration under one or more of such systems and the

operation thereunder of the same group of members of

employer and employee organisations and steps appropriate to

be taken.

The Committee will conduct its inquiries with a view to

recommending such action as is necessary and desirable in the

light of its inquiry so as to enable the efficient functioning of

employer and employee organisations under systems of Australian

and State conciliation and arbitration and the efficient

functioning of such systems."

The Committee made its recommendations but they have not been implemented fully. The problem remains. The orders sought by the appellants cannot resolve the problem.

There is no suggestion that any member of the ATAEA commenced proceedings under s141 of the Conciliation and Arbitration Act or s209 of the Commonwealth Act for orders that members of the ATAEA attached to the Queensland Branch of the ATAEA perform or observe the rules of the ATAEA insofar as they might have affected the ATAEA or the State Union. Applications under these sections could not have been brought against any person who was not a member of the ATAEA. This issue does not arise in this appeal, but it does highlight the nature of the matters raised by the appellants in this appeal.

34. Two facts are clear. The ATAEA is a corporate body separate and distinct from its members. The State Union is a corporate body separate and distinct from its members. There can be no basis in law to support the view that somehow the State Union is an emanation or adjunct of the ATAEA being forced to follow the ATAEA as if it, the State Union, were the ATAEA. The assets of the ATAEA are not the assets of the State Union. The assets of the State Union are not the assets of the ATAEA. The activities of the two separate corporate bodies, of necessity, are directed by natural persons acting in conformity with the rules of the respective corporate bodies.

35. At the beginning of these reasons, the opinion was expressed that the State Union was at all material times a body corporate. This arises from the grant of the certificate referred to in s31 of the 1916 State Act and the fact that its registration has not been cancelled. This conclusion is in conformity with the established principles applied in relation to similar legislation as illustrated by s122 of the Corporations Law, see for example, Oakes v Turquand and Harding (1867) 2 LRHL 325. There, a submission had been made that the validity of the registration of a corporation could be challenged in proceedings in which that issue arose as collateral to the main issue. On this issue, Lord Chelmsford LC said at p354:

"The first of them strikes at the root of the company's existence,

for it asserts that there was no memorandum of association

subscribed by seven persons, and, consequently, that there never

was any incorporated company. This, as I understand, is founded

upon an alleged variance between the prospectus and the memorandum

of association, which is made the ground of a separate objection.

The short answer to this objection is found in the Companies Act,

1862, which, in the 6th section, provides that any seven or more

persons may, by subscribing their names to a memorandum of

association, and otherwise complying with the requisitions of the

Act in respect of registration, form an incorporated company.

And, by the 18th section, upon the registration of the memorandum

of association, andc, the registrar shall certify under his hand

that the company is incorporated, and a certificate of the

incorporation of the company given by the registrar shall be

conclusive evidence that all the requisitions of the Act in

respect of registration have been complied with. I think the

certificate prevents all recurrence to prior matters essential to

registration, amongst which is the subscription of a memorandum of

association by seven person, and that it is conclusive in this

case, that all previous requisites had been complied with."

38. In many ways, a corporation constituted under the Commonwealth Act or the Queensland State Act is a corporation for the purposes specified in the relevant legislation. It is true that the rules of the corporation must state the objects of the corporation, but at the same time those objects must be within the purposes of the legislation or incidental to those purposes. One thing is clear, the objects of the corporation cannot include an object to engage in profit making enterprises to obtain financial benefits for the members of the corporation in their capacity as members. The members do not hold shares. They have no rights of property arising from being members which can be converted to personal financial gain. To this extent, there is a similarity between corporations of this type and corporations incorporated for charitable purposes. In conformity with this concept, an industrial corporation is created for the purpose of standing in the place of its members and persons entitled to become members in pursuing industrial benefits for those members. The corporation holds property in its own right. Its affairs are controlled by its members in conformity with the provisions of the rules of the corporations. Difficulties may arise where such a corporation ceases to be incorporated. This is illustrated by a reference to Bacon v O'Dea [1990] FCA 406; (1989) 25 FCR 495. In that case, special legislation applied. Even so, difficult legal problems arose concerning property which had been held by the corporation which become unincorporated. Another illustration, in relation to the creation of industrial disputes, appears in R v Evatt ex parte The Master Builders' Association of New South Wales [1974] HCA 56; (1974) 132 CLR 150. See also Dobinson v Crabb [1990] HCA 34; (1990) 170 CLR 218. These last two cases involved the construction and application of s143 and in particular subsection 143(6) of the Conciliation and Arbitration Act. The equivalent provisions are now contained in s298 of the Commonwealth Act. The problems arising on deregistration do not arise in this appeal, but they do illustrate the care which is needed to be applied to determine the true analysis of the appellants' contentions.

In his written reasons in reply, counsel for the appellants said:

"5. In the common law jurisdictions there are two (at least)

types of corporation; corporations sole (with which we need

not deal) and corporations aggregate. A corporation

aggregate required two (2) or more incorporators bound

legal entity or identity was given to an association. In

the case of a company that association was formed by the

obtained an identity separate and distinct from the

to have their separate personalities but, when acting as the

association, are the company. But the corporation is always

an association (see s298 and Dobinson v Crabb, Bacon v

O'Dea). Upon incorporation of a company there is not in

40. All these submissions are rejected. The written submissions, as set out, note a distinction without a difference. The effect of the companies legislation is that, upon the certificate being given, the subscribers to the company's memorandum, together with such other persons as from time to time become members of the company, become members of an incorporated body. Under the industrial legislation, upon registration the association becomes incorporated. The association is merely an aggregate of persons including, where the association is an association of employers, corporate bodies. The membership of the corporation comprises the original members and other persons who from time to time become members of the corporation. If an organisation becomes registered under a State Act, the newly created corporation is separate and distinct from the organisation. If a company or a corporation becomes "deregistered", the relevant legislation prescribes what happens. The authorities just mentioned illustrate this. It is helpful to refer to Bacon v O'Dea which involved the construction and application of s143(6) of the Conciliation and Arbitration Act. The passage in the judgment of the Court, Northrop, Gallop and Pincus JJ, at pages 507 to 511 under the heading "Nature of the Branch's right", should be read in full.

There is a suggestion at pp435-6 that the PSA, upon registration, "acquired limited corporate status" under the relevant State legislation. This view appears to be inconsistent with the strong views expressed in Williams v Hursey, an authority not referred to in the reasons. In Cawthorne, King CJ said at p436:

"One type of body qualified for registration is therefore an

association consisting of not less than twenty employees. An

association does not cease to be an association when it is

incorporated under the Associations Incorporation Act. A

corporate personality is superimposed on the pre-existing

association, but the association continues its existence as an

association. After incorporation under that Act the PSA remained,

as it still remains, an association of not less than twenty

employees. It is therefore entitled to apply for registration

under the Industrial Conciliation and Arbitration Act unless the

word "association" is to be read down so as to mean an association

which has not been incorporated under any other Act, that is to

say an unincorporated association.

I do not see why the meaning of the word should be so restricted.

There is, to my mind, nothing inconceivable in the notion of a

body drawing corporate status from two or more statutory sources.

Such a body would have the capacity and powers conferred by both

statutes and would be subject to the obligations imposed by both.

Mr Bleby, for FCU, argued that the provisions of the Industrial

Conciliation and Arbitration Act relating to incorporation

contemplate that the applicant for registration is an

unincorporated association. They are, I have no doubt, designed

to confer corporate status on an unincorporated applicant

association, but they are also capable of application to an

association already incorporated under another statute. The

effect of those provisions, if applied to an association already

incorporated under the Associations Incorporation Act, is to

provide an additional legal source of corporate status for the

association. The association, upon registration, would be

incorporated under both Acts, have the powers and capacities

conferred by both, and be subject to the obligations imposed by

both. So far as I can see, there is no conflict between the two

statutes, and the mere possibility of such a conflict arising in

the future is not a sufficient ground for restricting the meaning

of "association" to exclude an already incorporated association."

None of the opinions expressed any view on the nature of an association when incorporated even if some invalidity had occurred in the registration process.

44. Relying on the passage quoted, counsel for the appellants submitted that the ATAEA or its Queensland Branch was in fact and in law the State Union. This submission is rejected. In Cawthorne, the Court was not considering the effect of incorporation. It was not concerned to consider the effect of corporation, the distinction between the incorporators and the new corporate body nor the method of control of the new corporate body. In my opinion, Cawthorne does not assist in the resolution of the issues raised in this appeal.

45. For the purposes of this appeal, it can be assumed, without deciding, that the ATAEA, either directly or through its Queensland Branch, caused the State Union to be registered as an industrial union under the 1916 State Act. Similarly it can be assumed, without deciding, that under its rules the ATAEA had power to do what it did. It must be accepted that, upon registration and the issue of the certificate of registration, the State Union became a corporation, but that does not mean that the State Union became the ATAEA masquerading under the name of the State Union and controlling the State Union through its Queensland Branch. Upon registration, the State Union was to be controlled by its members in conformity with its rules. The question of whether those members have performed the duties and exercised the powers imposed and conferred upon them by those rules does not arise for consideration in these proceedings.

46. For the purposes of this appeal, it is not necessary to refer to the provisions of the Queensland State legislation which have, from time to time, replaced the provisions of the 1916 State Act. For all practical purposes, the provisions are sufficiently similar. The relevant provisions have been continued under the Industrial Conciliation and Arbitration Act 1932, the Industrial Conciliation and Arbitration Act 1961 and the Industrial Relations Act 1990 (Qld).

47. Subsection 37(1) of the 1916 State Act, which is set out earlier in these reasons, provides that an industrial union, as defined, shall "upon and during registration, become and be, for the purposes of this Act, a body corporate by its registered name, having perpetual succession and a common seal". A registration may be brought to an end in conformity with the provisions of s36. Until that occurs, there can be no doubt that the registered union is a body corporate for all purposes, Williams v Hursey above. The effect of the provisions contained in s37 of the 1916 State Act, and the subsequent legislation, is discussed at length by Pincus J in Sharpe v Goodhew (1990) 96 ALR 251, strangely, apparently, not reported in the Federal Court Reports. Commencing on p265 under the heading "Saving of registration", his Honour discusses the relevant Queensland legislation, comparable Commonwealth legislation and a number of authorities. I agree with the opinions expressed in that passage. His Honour's conclusions appear at pp268-9:

"Following Shelberg's case then, and reading "industrial union" in

the way I have indicated, I hold that s73 has a curative effect,

the registration, not having been cancelled, is taken to be valid.

Although when it was done the registration was unauthorised by the

then Act, the only means of correcting the register is by an

application under s73, which gives a discretion. Were such an

application made in the present case, it would surely fail because

the deficiency - that there was no Queensland branch of the

federal union in 1917 - was remedied, in a practical sense, many

years ago, in 1930 or 1931. But no application for cancellation

has been made.

I therefore hold that 1917 registration is presently deemed to be

valid."

48. The opinion that the State Union upon registration became a legal person, as a corporation, separate and distinct from those causing its incorporation, as well as its members from time to time, is consistent with legal principle as expressed by Fullagar J in Williams v Hursey, above, at 527, by Ryan J in Frizzerio v Rice (1992) 36 FCR 449 at 464, by Fitzerald J in Re Ferris (1982) IR 432 at 434, and by Wenstall ACJ in Allingham (above) at p245.

49. These principles are consistent with similar principles applied with respect to companies registered under company law legislation. This is illustrated by the famous case of Salomon v A Salomon and Co (1897) AC 22. The relevant provision considered there was s18 of Companies Act 1862 (UK). The substance of that section, in its context with s6, appears in the extract from the opinion of Lord Chelmsford LC in Oakes v Turquand, set out earlier in these reasons. The trial judge had held that the company was an "alias" of the appellant, the person instrumental in obtaining the registration of the company, who, after incorporation, carried on his business through the company as his agent and thus he was bound to indemnify the agent with respect to its liabilities. At pp30-31 Lord Halsbury LC said:

"I am simply here dealing with the provisions of the statute, and

it seems to me to be essential to the artificial creation that the

law should recognise only that artificial existence - quite apart

from the motives or conduct of individual corporators. In saying

this, I do not at all mean to suggest that if it could be

established that this provision of the statute to which I am

adverting had not been complied with, you could not go behind the

certificate of incorporation to shew that a fraud had been

committed upon the officer entrusted with the duty of giving the

certificate, and that by some proceeding in the nature of scire

facias you could not prove the fact that the company had no real

legal existence. But short of such proof it seems to me

impossible to dispute that once the company is legally

incorporated it must be treated like any other independent person

with its rights and liabilities appropriate to itself, and that

the motives of those who took part in the promotion of the company

are absolutely irrelevant in discussing what those rights and

liabilities are.

I will for the sake of argument assume the proposition that the

Court of Appeal lays down - that the formation of the company was

a mere scheme to enable Aron Salomon to carry on business in the

name of the company. I am wholly unable to follow the proposition

that this was contrary to the true intent and meaning of the

Companies Act. I can only find the true intent and meaning of the

Act from the Act itself; and the Act appears to me to give a

company a legal existence with, as I have said, rights and

liabilities of its own, whatever may have been the ideas or

schemes of those who brought it into existence."

50. These principles have been applied ever since. The legislation may vary but a reference to s123 of the Corporations Law illustrates the general position. Part 2.2 of the Law relates to registration of companies. Where an application for registration has been made in accordance with s118, the Commission must register the company. A certificate is to be issued under s121 and s122 states the effect of the certificate. Section 123 provides that, on and from the day specified in the certificate under s121 as the date of commencement of the company's registration, "the subscribers of the company's memorandum, together with such other persons as from time to time become members of the company, are an incorporated company of the name stated in the memorandum". As was said by Mason CJ, Brennan, Dawson, Toohey and Gaudron JJ in Australian Securities Commission v Marlborough Gold Mines Limited [1993] HCA 15; (1993) 177 CLR 485 at 493 in relation to incorporation under the Corporations Law:

"The company acquires its corporate personality upon and by reason

of registration by the Commission (s123(1))" (emphasis included).

51. Under s124, the subscribers shall be deemed to have agreed to become members and, on the company's incorporation, each becomes a member of the company. The subscribers have no special legal relationship with the company. The company is not their agent. The subscribers are not masquerading under the name of the company.

52. In the present case, neither the ATAEA nor the Alliance has been or has become the industrial union known by its registered name as the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Union of Employers. On this view, no question of inconsistency between Commonwealth law and State law arises. The ATAEA and the Alliance are corporations created under Commonwealth legislation to perform functions pursuant to Commonwealth legislation. The State Union is a corporation created under State legislation to perform functions pursuant to State legislation. The corporations perform different functions with respect to different subject matters.

53. The relationship between the State Union and its members are regulated by the rules of the State Union. The activities of the State Union are directed by the members of the State Union acting in conformity with its rules. Neither the ATAEA nor its Queensland Branch have any right to direct the members, whether officers or not, of the State Union. It follows that the amalgamation which resulted in the creation of the Alliance could not, of itself, involve the State Union becoming part of the Alliance.

54. The appeal should be dismissed.

The issues arising for consideration and the relevant facts and statutory provisions need not be repeated as they are set out in those reasons or in the reasons for judgment of the primary judge, Ryan J (reported in (1992) 39 FCR 1). It is therefore possible for me to state summarily the conclusions to which I have come.

2. I agree with Wilcox J that the documents lodged in support of the application for registration in 1917 establish that that application sought the registration of the entity called the Queensland Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Employees Association ("the ATAEA"); it did not seek the registration of a new industrial association constituted by the persons who happened to be the members of the Queensland Branch of the ATAEA at that time. The evidence does not show that those members ever formed any such new association or that they ever considered a motion that such a new association should be formed. Nor was there any evidence that all the persons listed as members of the Queensland Branch attended the meeting that resolved to apply for registration under the Queensland Industrial Arbitration Act 1916 (the "Queensland Act"). I agree with Wilcox J that, on the evidence, none of the persons concerned with the 1917 registration ever suggested that a new entity had been created and had applied for registration under the Queensland Act.

3. In my opinion the Queensland Branch of ATAEA, as an "industrial association" and a "branch of a trade union" within the meaning of s.26(1) and (6) of the Queensland Act, was registered under the Queensland Act in 1917 and remained part of the ATAEA.

4. I agree with the reasons given by Wilcox J for concluding that the branch, notwithstanding its registration under the Queensland Act, remained part of the ATAEA and bound by the federal rules of ATAEA (leaving aside, for consideration later in these reasons, the provision in the Queensland Act for incorporation).

5. I agree with the reasons given by Gray J for rejecting the argument that the legal consequence of what occurred in 1917 was that the ATAEA, as distinct from its Queensland Branch, became registered under the Queensland Act.

6. I also agree with his reasons for concluding that the Queensland Act was inoperative to the extent of its inconsistency with the Conciliation and Arbitration Act (Cth) in relation to the Queensland branch of the ATAEA.

7. I am unable to uphold the submission that the words appearing in s.37(1) of the Queensland Act make it clear that registration and incorporation are not severable concepts. I do not accept that incorporation is an integral part of the Queensland legislation that cannot be severed; nor do I consider that that is the discernible intention of the Queensland legislature. I agree with the reasons given by Gray J for concluding that the provisions in the Queensland Act for registration and for incorporation, which are in separate sections, are severable. My opinion is not based upon any "argument of convenience". I agree that the rights referred to by Gray J as being conferred by the Queensland Act upon a union registered under that Act, can be exercised by the branch as an industrial union, without the need for incorporation.

8. In my opinion, the appeal should be allowed and the order made on 19 October 1992, dismissing the application, should be set aside. I agree with the declarations (a), (b) and (c) proposed by Wilcox J but would not make the declarations (d) and (e) proposed by him. I agree with the other orders proposed by Wilcox J.

WILCOX J The issue underlying this litigation is whether or not the entity known as Queensland Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Union of Employees ("ATAUE") is part of an organisation constituted under Commonwealth law that was previously known as the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Employees Association ("ATAEA") and, since an amalgamation that took effect on 18 May 1992, is now part of the Media, Entertainment and Arts Alliance ("the Alliance"). The appellants, Victoria Jane McJannet and Charles Henry Livingstone, are officers of the Alliance. They contend that ATAUE was always part of ATAEA, the federal organisation, and is now part of the Alliance. Their contention is opposed by the first respondents, Herbert White, Arthur Westbrook, James Beattie, Macquarie Hayward, William Morris and Edward Eastern, who are officers of ATAUE, ATAUE itself (which is the second respondent) and the Australian Workers Union of Employees ("AWUE"), the fifth respondents. ATAUE has purported to enter into an amalgamation with AWUE under Queensland law. These three respondents are supported in their submissions by the third respondent, the Queensland Industrial Registrar, and the sixth respondent, the Queensland Minister for Employment, Training and Industrial Relations.

2. The primary Judge (Ryan J) answered the underlying question adversely to the appellants. He dismissed their application for a series of declarations and orders that would have prevented ATAUE and AWUE consummating their then proposed State-level amalgamation and required the transfer of ATAUE's assets to the Alliance. Since his Honour's order, the ATAUE-AWUE amalgamation has been effected. As a result of this development, the appellants seek from this Court relief that differs a little from that sought at first instance.

3. The relevant facts were summarised by Ryan J in his reasons for judgment, now reported at (1992) 39 FCR 1. In a judgment I have had the advantage of reading, Northrop J sets out the legal context in which the case arises. I do not propose to re-cover either ground. I will refer only to such facts and legal matters as are necessary to explain my view of the case.

The reasoning of the primary Judge

4. The first step taken by Ryan J in his reasons for judgment, after setting out the facts, was to consider s.26 of the Queensland Industrial Arbitration Act 1916 ("the 1916 Queensland Act"). This was the Act under which ATAUE was purportedly registered in 1917. The Act has long been repealed but ATAUE's registration (if valid) continued under subsequent Acts. The current statute is the Industrial Relations Act 1990 (Qld). Notwithstanding legislative changes, it is common ground that the validity of ATAUE's registration at the time of its purported amalgamation with AWUE depends upon the validity of its registration under the 1916 Queensland Act.

5. Ryan J quoted s.26 at 15-16; but the section looms so large in the case that it is worth repeating the relevant parts:

"26(1) The registrar may, on application made as

prescribed, register as an industrial union

under this Act any industrial association or

trade union of employees.

On such registration the industrial association

or trade union shall be an industrial union

until such registration is duly cancelled.

(2) Such application shall be in the form

prescribed, and shall be signed by the

secretary and the president or chairman of the

association or union.

Notice of such application shall be published

as prescribed.

(3) Upon an application to be registered as an

industrial union, the registrar may require

such evidence as to him seems fit, either oral

or on affidavit -

(a) Of the authority of the person signing the form of

application;

(b) That the persons on whose behalf the application is

made should not in the public interest or for other

good reason join an industrial union which has already

been registered;

(c) That the rules of the applicant association or union

and their administration provide reasonable facilities

for the admission of new members, and do not impose

unreasonable conditions upon the continuance of their

membership, and are not in any other way tyrannical or

oppressive;

(d) That the registration of the applicants will not

unjustly affect any other industrial union;

(e) That the application is bona fide in the interests of

employees, and not in the interests of an employer or

employers.

(4) Within the prescribed time and in the prescribed

manner any industrial union or employer may, by notice

to the registrar, oppose such application.

(5) The registrar shall fix a day for considering any

objections on any of the above grounds to the granting

of the application, and shall notify the same as

prescribed.

(6) No branch of a trade union shall be registered unless

it is a bona fide branch of sufficient importance to

be registered separately.

(7) ...

(8) ..."

6. Ryan J pointed out that, although s.26(1) authorised the registration of "any industrial association or trade union of employees", the Act did not define either term. He went on, at 16, to express opinions that the words "trade union" connoted a trade union registered under the Trade Union Act 1916 and "industrial association" "comprehends all other associations of employees having as their objects the protection and advancement of the industrial interests and conditions of those employees". A "trade union" could be an "industrial association". "So understood", he said, "an 'industrial association' within the meaning of s.26 of the Queensland Act could be ... a trade union not registered under the Trade Union Act 1916, including an organisation registered under the Conciliation and Arbitration Act 1904". In other words, an "industrial association" might be constituted under federal (not Queensland) law. ATAEA was registered under the (federal) Conciliation and Arbitration Act from the time of its formation (1910) until the replacement of that Act by the Industrial Relations Act 1988. From that time until the amalgamation that formed the Alliance, ATAEA was registered under the Industrial Relations Act. The amalgamated body remains registered under that Act.

7. Ryan J went on to say, still at 16:

"The members of a trade union in that wider sense,

including the members of a federally-registered

organisation, gathered for administrative purposes in

a branch, could also have been regarded collectively

as an industrial association registrable under the

Queensland Act."

8. Ryan J then referred to some decided cases. They included Frizziero v Rice (1992) 36 FCR 449, a Full Court decision to which I will return, and Sharpe v Goodhew (1990) 33 IR 238, a decision of Pincus J. In the latter case at 245, Pincus J said that:

"although apart from subs.(6) a mere branch would not

have been registrable, that provision made a branch

registrable, whether or not the trade union of which

it was a branch was a purely Queensland trade union".

Pincus J held that the section permitted the registration of a branch of a federal industrial organisation. He emphasised that s.26 did not permit the registration of mere names; there must be "an organised existing body of persons". He said:

"It is true that a branch of an organisation is not a

legal entity and is merely a collection of people,

but the expression 'bona fide branch' connotes that

the collection of people is an organised one, a unity

in a practical sense."

9. Ryan J expressed general agreement with the analysis of s.26 made by Pincus J. He then referred to a passage in the judgment of Fullagar J (with whom Dixon CJ and Kitto J agreed) in Williams v Hursey [1959] HCA 51; (1959) 103 CLR 30 at 59. That passage was important to the argument before us, so I will set it out:

"... whatever the rules may do or fail to do, they

have, in my opinion no application to the present

cases for the reason that the 'Hobart Branch' of the

federation is not an 'unincorporated society,

fellowship, club or association'. It has no separate

identity - no existence apart from the registered

organisation, of which it is an integral and

inseverable part. Its members are merely a section

of the total membership of the federation - locally

organised for the sake of convenience, but in no

respect independent of the federation, and in all

respects subject to the control of the federation.

The branches are permitted within limits to make

rules of their own, but the rules which they make

derive authority from the rules of the federation.

It is contemplated that a branch may have 'property

and moneys', but, if r.15 of the Hobart Branch rules

means that the beneficial ownership thereof is in the

members of the branch, it is inconsistent with

r.10(A) of the rules of the federation which makes

the 'fund and property' of the organisation and its

branches subject to the 'care, superintendence,

management and administration of the governing body

of the Organisation for the carrying out of the

objects of the Organisation'. The position is

precisely analogous to that which was held by this

Court to subsist in Hall v Job [1952] HCA 57; (1952) 86 CLR 639 and

there is a passage in the judgment in that case

which, if we read 'Federation' or 'Organisation' for

'Institution' and 'Branch' for 'Lodge', is exactly

applicable to the present case."

10. In a passage at 18 that is critical to his reasoning Ryan J then said:

"It is only by construing s.26 of the Queensland Act

as extending the facility of obtaining registration

to the members of a branch of a federally-registered

organisation that the legal impossibility indicated

in the passage just cited from Williams v Hursey is

avoided. Likewise, by construing s.26 as extending

the facility of obtaining registration to the members

of a branch of an unincorporated association, being a

trade union registered under the Trade Union Act 1916

or an industrial association other than a

federally-registered organisation, the corresponding

impossibility implicit in the passage from Hall v Job

... at 649 is avoided." (Original emphasis)

11. His Honour treated Pincus J, in Sharpe v Goodhew, as using the word "branch" "as a compendious reference to 'members of a branch'". He also referred to the decision of Wanstall ACJ in Allingham v Australian Workers' Union (1972) QdR 217; 21 FLR 228. The latter decision, he said at 20, "supports my conclusion that it is legally impossible for an industrial union registered under the Queensland Act to be a branch of a federally-registered organisation". Accordingly, he held that the entity registered in 1917 was a new body, different from both ATAEA and its Queensland branch, comprised of the individuals who happened to be members of the Queensland branch at that time.

12. Ryan J made only a passing reference to s.37 of the 1916 Queensland Act. But this section has played a major part in the submissions put to us. So it is desirable that I set it out immediately:

"37.(1) Every industrial union shall, upon and during registration,

become and be, for the purposes of this Act, a body

corporate by its registered name, having perpetual

succession and a common seal.

(2) There shall be inserted in the registered name of every

industrial union the words 'union of employees,' and also

the locality in which the majority of its members reside or

exercise their calling."

Analysis of this reasoning

13. As it seems to me, Ryan J's reasoning may be summarised in this way:

(i) Section 26 of the 1916 Queensland Act permitted the

registration of an "industrial association" subject

to compliance with the formal requirements of the

Act;

(ii) These requirements were satisfied in 1917 in

respect of the association that was the subject of

the application;

(iii) Therefore that association was validly registered

under the Queensland Act.

(iv) The association that was registered could only have

been:

(a) the Queensland branch of the federal

organisation; or

(b) the members of the Queensland branch

acting as a group of individuals;

(v) It was legally impossible for the branch to be

validly registered - see Williams v Hursey;

(vi) Therefore, the association that was registered must

have been the members of the branch acting as a

group of individuals;

(vii) It follows that the association registered under

the Queensland Act is an entirely different entity

from the organisation registered under the

Commonwealth Act. This is so notwithstanding that,

for many years, all members of the federal

organisation (ATAEA) attached to the Queensland

branch were also treated as members (and the only

members) of the State registered entity (ATAUE).

14. Northrop J expresses general agreement with the reasoning of Ryan J; although he holds or assumes that the association registered in 1917 was the Queensland branch of the federal organisation, not the members of that branch as individuals. I differ with diffidence from these two experienced industrial lawyers. But I think Ryan J's analysis is wrong. I cannot see that Williams v Hursey has the effect he attributes to it. I will return to that point. There is a more fundamental matter. Assuming, for the moment and the sake of the analysis, that Williams v Hursey does have the attributed effect, this only means that any attempt to register a branch of a federal organisation under a State Act is misconceived and ineffective. It does not mean that the attempt resulted in something else being registered. If, as a matter of fact, the branch was the association presented for registration, but could not validly be registered, there would be no valid registration at all.

15. With respect to my colleagues, it seems to me erroneous to start with an assumption that there was a valid registration under the Queensland Act and then to ask what association could validly have been registered. The appropriate course, surely, is to examine the evidence and determine, as a matter of fact, what association was presented for registration. If that association could validly be registered, then (everything else being in order) the registration was valid. If the association presented for registration could not validly be registered, the purported registration was invalid. Whether the registration was valid or not, the association presented for registration retained its original identity. If it was a part of some larger organisation, it remained so.

16. When the case is approached in this way, the answer becomes clear.

The application for registration

17. The evidence shows that, on 14 February 1917, Talbot Sewell, the Secretary of the Queensland Branch of ATAEA, wrote a letter to Mr JJ McGee, the Queensland Industrial Registrar. He wrote on ATAEA's letterhead, which made reference to the organisation's registration under the Conciliation and Arbitration Act. Mr Sewell said that he enclosed forms 1 and 2 "for application for registration of the Queensland Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Employees Association under the Queensland Industrial Act (sic) of 1916". He also said he enclosed some other documents. He mentioned that "the Association" is registered as "an Industrial organisation of employees" under the Commonwealth Act.

18. The letter bears a stamp, "Industrial Registrar Queensland 16 Feb 1917" and an endorsement in Mr Sewell's handwriting "Memo and application returned 20/2/17". It seems that the Industrial Registrar thought the application deficient in some respect. The evidence does not reveal the perceived deficiency. But it does show that Mr Sewell then submitted a fresh application. This application was signed by himself and Mr F W Argus, in their capacities as Secretary and President, respectively, of "the Queensland Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Industrial Association". They sought registration of "the said Industrial Association as an Industrial Union of Employees" under the Queensland Act. According to a date stamp, this application was received by the Industrial Registrar on 3 March 1917.

19. It will be noted that the name stated in this document is not precisely the same as that of the federal organisation. The word "Employees" was omitted and "Industrial" substituted. At 3 Ryan J quoted the terms of this application. But the reproduction in his printed reasons masks something revealed by the original document: the majority of its words came from a printed application form, containing some blank spaces and bracketed alternatives. It seems that the document was a standard form produced by the government for completion by persons applying for registration of an association under the Act. The form contained a blank space for insertion of the name of the relevant association. Immediately after this space, the words "Industrial Association" were printed. Following them, in brackets, were the words "or Trade Union". The person who completed the subject form wrote in the blank space the words "Queensland Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Employees As", and then struck out "Employees As" and the bracketed words "or Trade Union". Apparently, this person acted on the belief that the name had to contain either the words "Industrial Association" or "Trade Union" (the categories of organisations mentioned in s.26(1) of the Queensland Act) and selected the former. The printed words in the form suggest that this belief was held in government circles. It may explain the return of the original application, which presumably showed the correct name of the federal organisation, without either "Industrial Association" or "Trade Union". The Industrial Registrar apparently thought this unacceptable.

20. If the Industrial Registrar did insist on the words "Industrial Association" or "Trade Union", it seems to me that he was wrong; there was nothing in the 1916 Queensland Act that required an association registered under s.26 to incorporate either formula in its name. Be that as it may, and whatever the reason for the inclusion of the words "Industrial Association" in the stated name, the inclusion creates no factual problem. The documents that accompanied the application form clearly show that the association presented for registration was the Queensland branch of the federal organisation.

21. The first accompanying document was an affidavit sworn by Messrs Sewell and Argus. In that affidavit they deposed to several matters. The first of them was:

"That, to the best of our belief, there is no other

existing Industrial Association (or Trade Union) of

Employees, whether registered or not registered, the

name of which is identical with the proposed name, or

so nearly resembles the name as to cause confusion."

This statement would have been untrue if the applicant for registration was not the federal organisation, ATAEA, or part of it. The federal organisation bore a name almost identical to that of the applicant. And the statement would have been untrue to the knowledge of Mr Sewell and Mr Argus. They were, at the time, the Secretary and President of the Queensland branch of ATAEA.

22. The next document, which was annexure "A" to the application and described by the printed form as "List of members of the applicant Industrial Association (or Trade Union) of Employees", was a list of names headed "List of Members of the Q'land Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Association". This document was followed by annexure "B", which was described in the affidavit as "A list of officers of the Industrial Association of Employees". The annexure was headed: "A list of officers of the Q'land Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Employees Association". The names of a number of office bearers (including Mr Argus as President and Mr Sewell as Secretary) were set out.

23. Annexure "C" to the application referred to "the rules of the Industrial Association (or Trade Union) of Employees". As Ryan J found, those rules comprised a copy of the printed ATAEA rules, as lodged with the federal Industrial Registrar on 19 August 1916, with a handwritten alteration to one provision concerning member contributions. The submitted set of rules bore the title "Rules of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Employees' Association". It was divided into two sections, "Federal Rules" and "State Rules", each with a separate Index. Rule 10 of the "Federal Rules" provided for a Federal Council with power, amongst other things, to appoint a Committee of Management, to hear and decide any appeal from a branch or a member, to determine any matter referred to Federal Council by the Committee of Management or a branch, to settle disputes between branches and to reverse the decision of any branch if it thought fit to do so. The rules contained a form of application for membership (quoted by Ryan J at 5) headed "Australian Theatrical and Amusement Employees' Association". It had a sub-heading: "Registered under the Commonwealth Conciliation and Arbitration Act, 1904 and 1910 on February 10, 1910". The form required each applicant for membership to "apply to become a Member of the above Association, and agree to abide by its Rules". Rule 26(a), which was contained in the "Federal Rules" section, provided for the formation of a branch in each State and said that "the members of each Branch shall consist of such members of the Association as are resident within that State". Rule 26(d) required each branch to have a Committee of Management consisting of a President, Vice-President, Treasurer, Secretary, Assistant Secretary, three Trustees and not more than twelve other members. Rule 26(e) stated that "rules of the branches shall be made by the Federal Council or Committee of Management" (that is the Federal Committee of Management appointed by Federal Council under rule 10) "and may be altered, added to, amended or rescinded by either body at any time". Rule 1 of the "State Rules" gave the conduct of the business of each State branch to that branch's Committee of Management. Rule 2 directed that committee to "transact any business of the State Branch requiring immediate attention" and to report to the next State Branch meeting. Rule 32 required members to report disputes with employers to the State Branch Secretary and obliged the Secretary, failing amicable settlement and if the Committee of Management so directed, to "call a special meeting of the State Branch to deal with the matter in dispute".

24. The application described its annexure "D" as "A copy of a resolution passed in accordance with the rules by a majority of the members present at a general meeting of the Industrial Association (or Trade Union) of Employees, or by other competent authority in the Industrial Association (or Trade Union) of Employees, in favour of registration under this Act". Mr Sewell and Mr Argus annexed a copy of the following minute:

"Resolution passed in accordance with rules of the

Q'land Br. of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement

Employees Association by a majority of the members

present at a general meeting of the branch is as

follows: 'That the Q'land Branch of the Australian

Theatrical and Amusement Employees Association

register under the Q'land Arbitration Act of 1916'."

25. Annexure E to the application was a list of occupations in the theatrical industry. It was headed "List of Callings comprised in the membership or objects of the Queensland Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Employees Association".

26. Annexure F read:

"The locality or district in which the members of the

Q'land Branch of the Australian Theatrical and

Amusement Employees Association exercise their

calling is in all towns or townships throughout

Q'land."

27. If the entity that was registered in March 1917 had been a new industrial association, formed by the people who happened to be members of the Queensland branch of the federal organisation, it would have been necessary for those people to meet and resolve to form the new association. The 1916 Queensland Act did not itself create new associations. The scheme of s.26(1) was to empower the Industrial Registrar to "register as an industrial union under this Act" an existing industrial association or trade union. So, if the association proposed for registration did not already exist, it would have needed to be formed. It would have been necessary to hold a meeting of those sponsoring the formation of the association, and there carry a resolution forming the association. That resolution would then have needed to be sent to the Industrial Registrar. In this case there is no evidence of such a meeting. No formation resolution was submitted to Mr McGee. The only resolution submitted to him was that quoted above. It does not record a decision to form, or unite in, a new association. It records a decision taken at a meeting of the Queensland branch of the federal organisation, not of a group of individuals intent on forming a new association, to register under the 1916 Queensland Act a named existing entity, the Queensland branch of the federal organisation. There is no evidence that all the people listed in annexure "A" as members of the branch (and who, according to Ryan J, were the individuals comprising the new association) attended the meeting that resolved to apply for registration or assented to that resolution. This would have been necessary if they were to be members of a new association.

28. On 18 March 1917, Mr McGee issued a certificate that, on that day, "an Industrial Association (or "Trade Union") of Employees, called Queensland Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Union of Employees" was registered by that name under the Queensland Act. It will be noted that this name differed both from the true name of the branch and that shown on the application form. The explanation of Mr McGee's action, undoubtedly, was the requirement of s.37(2) that there "be inserted in the registered name of every industrial union the words 'union of employees'". It seems that Mr McGee thought this authorised him to register an industrial union in a name that differed from the true name of the industrial association or trade union presented for registration.

29. Notwithstanding the curiosity of this situation, the documents submitted in support of the application clearly establish that those concerned with the application intended to present for registration the existing entity known as the Queensland branch of the federal organisation, and not some new association constituted by the individuals who happened then to be members of that branch.

Post-registration events

30. If more evidence is required, it is provided by events subsequent to registration. As Ryan J stated at 5-6:

"until about 1979, the Queensland Branch of the ATAEA

and the ATAUE were administered as if they were one

and the same entity. There was a single form of

application for membership, one membership card was

issued, a single contribution was collected and one

election was conducted, apparently under the CAA, for

offices in both entities. General meetings and

meetings of the executive were held without drawing

any distinction between the Queensland Branch of the

ATAEA and the ATAUE."

(The "CAA" was, of course, the Commonwealth Conciliation and Arbitration Act.)

31. In other words, Ryan J found that, until long after all those involved in the registration had retired or died, everybody concerned with the matter treated the entity registered under the Queensland legislation as if it was the same entity as the Queensland branch of the federal organisation. These people included Mr P J Wallace, the Queensland Industrial Registrar in office in 1943. It appears that, on 7 March 1943, the Secretary of ATAUE gave Mr Wallace notice of a decision of "the Queensland Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Union of Employees" to delete a paragraph from its rules. On 23 March 1943, Mr Wallace wrote a letter to the Secretary in which he said:

"I would point out to you that, though Rule 34 of the

Rules of that Association refers only to the

Queensland Branch, it would appear that that Branch

has no power to alter the Rule. This could only be

done by the Australian Association.

I was advised by the person lodging the alteration

that this had been done, but I would require a

certificate in writing, or preferably an affidavit,

to that effect before registering the alteration".

The Secretary responded with an affidavit in which he deposed to a resolution passed on 9 February 1943 at a meeting in Melbourne of "the Federal Committee of Management of the said Association" making the same deletion. He enclosed a copy of a letter from the federal Industrial Registrar stating that the amendment had been filed. This information satisfied Mr Wallace. On 26 March 1943 he wrote to say that the amendment had been registered.

32. It is, to my mind, highly significant that none of the people connected with the 1917 registration ever suggested that they thereby created a new entity. The first mention of a second entity occurred in 1979. The details were recorded by Ryan J at 7-8. He there set out an extract from the minutes of a meeting of the "executive" of the composite entity (that is, that which was universally recognised as being both a branch of the federal organisation and the association registered under the Queensland legislation) held on 26 July 1979. He went on to note that, on the same day, the same executive members conducted another meeting for which separate minutes were taken. These minutes were headed: "Queensland Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Union of Employees". The sub-heading reads: "Minutes of Inaugural Meeting 26th July 1979".

33. The use of the word "inaugural" in the sub-heading shows that, even in 1979, nobody thought that two separate organisations previously existed. The minutes suggest that, even then, the participants did not wish to have two separate organisations. It seems that the "inaugural" meeting stemmed from advice (right or wrong) to the effect that the State registration necessitated the creation of a separate fund. Only gradually, as different people became involved in the administration of the two entities, did the "administrative bifurcation in 1979", as Ryan J called it at 8, turn into conflict.

34. Having regard to all this evidence, it is evident that, if the relevant question is considered as an issue of fact, it would be perverse to hold other than that the association registered in 1917 was the Queensland branch of the federal organisation. The respondents' argument

35. Counsel for the respondents did not suggest that the Court should hold, as a fact, that the association presented for registration in 1917 was other than the Queensland branch of the federal organisation. Without formally conceding that the factual position was as I have stated, they sought to avoid the effect of such a conclusion. In their oral submissions counsel for the first, second and fifth respondents expressed the matter in this way:

"The possibilities really are two: it is within

power or without power for the federal body to

establish a union under the State Act. If it is

within power, then the consequence in our submission

is simply that the power has been exercised but the

body created is a new one, which takes its character

from the State Act. There may be control ... by the

federal body but the bodies are distinct."

In their subsequent written submissions, these counsel said:

"Registration as an industrial union of employees

under the 1916 Act, however, brings into being a new

body corporate, which is separate and distinct from

the persons or bodies which caused its formation.

That is the position whether the person or body

causing the formation of the industrial union of

employees is a federally-registered organisation (or,

in some way, a 'branch' of such an organisation) or a

group of individuals."

Counsel referred to the comment of Fullagar J in Williams v Hursey at 52 that registration gave to a union:

"an independent existence as a legal person. It is

given a personality, which is distinct from that of

all or any of its members, and which continues to

subsist unchanged notwithstanding the changes which

are bound to occur from time to time in its

membership".

They said that "(t)he view that incorporation under the 1916 Act gives rise to a new legal person, separate and distinct from those causing its incorporation" is supported by some observations in Frizziero v Rice, by a dictum of Fitzgerald J in Re Ferris (1982) 2 IR 432 and by the decision in Allingham. Counsel for the third and sixth respondents put similar submissions.

36. With respect to counsel, this is, substantially, "straw man" reasoning. It attributes to their opponents an obviously untenable proposition and then elaborately demolishes it. Of course an incorporated body is an entity separate and distinct from those causing its incorporation. The principle is a fundamental of corporations law, authoritatively established at least as far back as the House of Lords decision in Salomon v A Salomon and Company Limited (1897) AC 22. The applicants do not say otherwise. Nor do they dispute that incorporation changes the legal nature of a previously unincorporated association; it becomes a legal person - that is, a person individually recognised by the law, capable of suing and being sued. Ordinarily, a newly incorporated body is a free-standing entity, separate and distinct from any other corporation. The reason is that, ordinarily, an entity presented for incorporation is not part of another entity. But what if it is? Does incorporation have the effect of hiving off the newly-incorporated part so that it is no longer part of the larger corporation? If so, how and why? And, if so, what is the implication of that result for the application of State legislation that permits the incorporation of part of an entity constituted under federal law? These are the real questions in the case.

37. In Allingham the question was whether the plaintiffs, firemen employed by the Metropolitan Fire Brigades Board and members of the United Firefighters Union of Australia, were members of one or more of what was seen as three separate bodies: the Australian Workers' Union (an industrial organisation registered and incorporated under Commonwealth law), AWUE (an entity whose history parallelled that of ATAUE and which was registered under Queensland legislation) and "The Australian Workers' Union Queensland Branch" (the Queensland branch of the federal organisation). Wanstall ACJ held that the second of these entities, the one registered under Queensland law, was in fact the Queensland branch of the federal organisation; however, the consequence of its registration (with the consequential incorporation of the registered body) was that it became "separate and distinct" from the federal organisation. His Honour expressed his findings in this way (at QdR 245; FLR 252):

"I hold that the body known as the Australian

Workers' Union (Queensland Branch), and registered as

a trade union under Queensland law, is a legal

entity, with a legal personality of its own, separate

and distinct from that of its members, and from that

of the federal organization (the first defendant) and

that it is not merely a part of the latter in the

sense in which Williams v Hursey defined the Hobart

branch of the Waterside Workers Federation. I also

hold that in fact it is the Queensland branch of the

first defendant, and functions as such. Next I hold

that the second defendant is a corporation distinct

in law from the legal personality which is the trade

union, and distinct from the first defendant, and

distinct from its own members. I agree with the

submission that the Queensland branch of the

Australian Workers' Union has two identities in State

law, one as the personality which is the registered

trade union, and the other as the corporation which

is the registered industrial union."

38. The statement that the State-registered body had a "legal personality of its own, separate and distinct from that of its members" is unexceptional. On any view of the matter, that was true. A branch of a federal organisation has an existence that is separate and distinct from that of the members attached to it. The remainder of the first sentence in the quoted passage is not clear to me. In relation to the first section of it, his Honour may have meant no more than that the entity clothed with corporate status under State law was different to that already incorporated under Commonwealth law, in the way that a part is different from the whole. If that was what he meant, there can be no quarrel with it; an arm is different to a whole body. But his Honour went on "and ... is not merely a part of the latter". The emphasis may be on the word "merely", in which case the statement is obviously correct (if the incorporation provision validly applied) because it is now something more than a mere part; it is a part that has achieved corporate status. This may be what his Honour meant; he went on to hold that the State-registered body was in fact the Queensland branch of the federal organisation. Understood in this way, it seems to me that Allingham furnishes no support for the respondents' argument in this case. As I read the judgment, Wanstall ACJ did not hold that, upon registration under the State Act, a branch of a federal organisation ceases to be part of the federal organisation and entitled to go its own way. Indeed, I think his holding that AWUE "is the Queensland branch" of the federal organisation is a decision to the contrary. If I have misunderstood his Honour, and he was of the opinion that the effect of registration was to sever the branch from the federal organisation, his opinion is unpersuasive because it fails to explain how or why this occurred or to grapple with the constitutional consequences of that conclusion.

39. The decision in Re Ferris takes the matter no further. This was an application for an inquiry in relation to elections to certain positions in the federally-registered Australian Workers' Union. At 434 Fitzgerald J said:

"Members of the federal body in Queensland are also members

of one or more distinct legal entities registered under or

continued by the Industrial Conciliation and Arbitration

Act 1961-1980(Q). There is no present need for a detailed

discussion of the situation which exists, how it arose, or

of the practical consequences."

He referred to Allingham. It does not appear that any argument was put to Fitzgerald J concerning the validity of the State registration that created what the present parties call AWUE, or the consequences of this registration.

40. In Frizziero v Rice Gray J found as a fact that the entity registered under the relevant South Australian legislation was the South Australian branch of a federal organisation, the Federated Confectioners Association of Australia ("FCAA"). He then stated a case for the opinion of a Full Court, the question being whether this registration was effective to create an incorporated body under South Australian law. The Court declined to answer the question. The majority (Keely, Sheppard and Ryan JJ) thought the validity of the registration was a question of mixed fact and law and should not be answered until all the facts were found. Black CJ would have answered the question in the negative, taking the view that the South Australian provision for incorporation of State-registered bodies was inconsistent with the application of the federal legislation and inseverable. Gray J adhered to the view he had expressed in Bailey v Krantz (1985) 13 IR 339, holding that incorporation was inconsistent with the federal legislation, but this provision was severable so the registration itself was valid.

41. Although the Court was divided in Frizziero v Rice, no member disputed that the State-registered body was part of the federal organisation at the time of the application for registration; that issue had already been resolved. But the present respondents seek comfort from the comment of Ryan J at 473 that he was "not persuaded" that what was incorporated in South Australia "could, from the moment of incorporation, properly be described as a branch of the federally-registered FCAA". He went on:

"As I understand orthodox notions of the nature of a

corporation, they require, for a corporation

aggregate, two or more members: ... The members must

be bound by identifiable rules or articles and the

corporation must possess a name effective to

distinguish it from other legal persons."

42. I do not find this reasoning persuasive. Ryan J's mistake, I respectfully suggest, was in limiting himself to orthodox notions. For one corporation to be a branch of another is certainly unusual. Pincus J pointed this out in Sharpe v Goodhew at 243, but added the comment that "a sovereign legislature is not bound to respect legal orthodoxy". I would not myself apply the word "sovereign" to any legislature within a federation, but I agree with the substance of Pincus J's comment. Provided only that it acts within its constitutional power, a State Parliament can confer corporate status on any entity it likes - a single individual, a group of individuals without rules or a common name, even an animal or an inanimate object. Within its own constitutional sphere, a Parliament is free to be as innovative as it wishes. It is surely not more circumscribed than the Emperor Caligula, who appointed his horse as Consul. Outside the industrial arena, there is at least one instance of a State Parliament enacting legislation to incorporate the local branch of an Australia-wide organisation. I refer to the Australian Red Cross Society New South Wales Division Incorporation Act 1929 (NSW) which I discussed in E v Australian Red Cross Society [1991] FCA 20; (1991) 99 ALR 601 at 628. It appears to have been quite common for corporations incorporated by Royal Charter subsequently to receive a second Charter, whose literal effect was to incorporate the grantee: see Halsburys Laws of England (4th ed) vol 9 paras 1243-1244. This legal oddity also occurred in the industrial arena in at least one Australian State. It appears that in South Australia it was commonplace for associations incorporated under the Associations Incorporation Acts to be registered under the Industrial Code, and thereby acquire a second corporate status: see The Queen v Cawthorne; ex parte Federated Clerks' Union of Australia (South Australian Branch) (1979) 22 SASR 433. In that case, at 436, King CJ commented that there was "nothing inconceivable in the notion of a body drawing corporate status from two or more statutory sources". The point is not that Cawthorne is similar to the present case, it clearly is not, but that it is a mistake to restrict oneself to orthodox notions.

43. The problem posed by the respondents' argument is illustrated by the summary of the argument made by Northrop J. His Honour says that "(u)pon registration, the State Union was to be controlled by its members in conformity with its rules". But what members? Strictly speaking, the Queensland branch of ATAEA had no members at the moment of registration. The rules provided that the members of the organisation were members of the federal organisation. They were simply attached to the branch within their State of residence. Members who moved interstate took their membership with them. They were assigned to a different branch of the same organisation. They did not have to join a different organisation. And the "State Union" had no rules other than the rules of the federal organisation which subordinated the decisions of all branches to those of the Federal Council.

44. In my opinion the answer tendered by counsel for the respondents to this first, and critical, aspect of the case should be rejected. Conformably with the findings made in all the earlier cases (Allingham, Bailey v Krantz, Sharpe v Goodhew and Frizziero v Rice), the Court should hold that what was registered under the Queensland Act in 1917 was the Queensland branch of the ATAEA. The Court should also hold that this identity was not lost by the fact of registration or incorporation, if either of those events validly occurred. To the extent that either Wanstall ACJ in Allingham or Ryan J in Frizziero v Rice suggested that this result would occur their view should not be accepted. Neither judge explained the conceptual basis for ascribing metamorphic qualities to registration or incorporation. I do not know what it might be. If that result did occur, it would plainly pose a massive problem in relation to the validity of the registration or incorporation. The effect of the State legislation would be to deprive federal organisations of their branches in those States where registration resulted in incorporation.

45. Once this conclusion is reached, the fundamental issue in the case is resolved. The association presented for registration under the Queensland Act was the Queensland branch of the federal organisation. It had no separate existence before registration. It acquired no separate existence by virtue of registration. Whether or not the registration was valid, the branch remained part of the federal organisation. Being part of a larger organisation, it was incapable of amalgamating, independently of the larger organisation, with AWUE or any other body. If the federal organisation (ATAEA), of which it was a part, amalgamated with one or more other organisations, as it did to form the Alliance, the entity the subject of the Queensland registration became part of the amalgamated body. Immediately prior to the federal amalgamation any assets held in the name of the Queensland-registered body were assets of the federal organisation. Upon amalgamation they became assets of the Alliance. In my opinion, the appellants were entitled to the relief they sought before Ryan J. Orders along those lines ought now be made, updated to deal with the purported State amalgamation that has since occurred.

46. In one sense, it is not necessary to pronounce upon the validity of the 1917 registration. The matter of validity cannot affect the identity of the entity purportedly registered. But the question of validity has been argued before a bench of five Judges. It is a question that will arise if the Alliance seeks to take advantage of the State registration in the Queensland Industrial Commission, as seems likely. It also raises issues of considerable general importance. As the reported cases demonstrate, it has been commonplace for branches of federally-registered organisations to become registered under State Acts. Under the circumstances, this Court should address the question.

Validity of the 1917 registration under Queensland law

47. The validity of the 1917 registration involves two matters: the application to the facts of the relevant State law and the question whether there is anything in Commonwealth law, or arising out of the nature of a federally-registered industrial organisation or the rules of the particular organisation, preventing that organisation taking advantage of any permission provided by the State law.

48. So far as the first matter is concerned, like Ryan J (and Wanstall ACJ in Allingham, Gray J in Bailey v Krantz, Pincus J in Sharpe v Goodhew and all members of the Court in Frizziero v Rice), I see no conceptual difficulty in State legislation permitting the registration for State purposes of an organisation constituted under Commonwealth law. This type of interaction is common in the Australian federation. A ready example is furnished by the registration under State law of Commonwealth land titles; an action specifically envisaged by Commonwealth legislation. See, for example, s.51 of the Lands Acquisition Act 1989. However, the Commonwealth may not force the States to effect registration of a Commonwealth instrumentality or interest: see Commonwealth v New South Wales [1923] HCA 34; (1923) 33 CLR 1. If the relevant State legislation excludes Commonwealth instrumentalities or interests, that is the end of the matter. The first question, then, in relation to the present problem is whether it does so.

49. There was nothing in the 1916 Queensland Act that excluded registration of a federally-constituted employees' organisation. The only stipulation of s.26 was that the entity presented for registration be an "industrial association or trade union of employees" (s.26(1)) or a significant branch of a trade union (s.26(6)). A federally-constituted organisation might readily have answered either of these descriptions. Unlike Ryan J I see no reason to confine the term "trade union" to an entity registered under the Trade Union Act 1916 (Qld). But this does not matter; in 1917 ATAEA was an "industrial association of employees" as well as a "trade union of employees", in the ordinary meaning of those words. There is no reason to doubt that its Queensland branch was both "bona fide" and "of sufficient importance to be registered separately".

50. I have already stated the terms of s.37 of the Queensland Act. Subsection (1) requires that there be a "registered name". Subsection (2) requires this name to contain the words "union of employees". The Queensland Industrial Registrar purported in 1917 to comply with this requirement. Although there had been no resolution to change the name of the federal organisation or its Queensland Branch, and although the name specified in the application of 3 March 1917 was "Queensland Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Industrial Association", Mr McGee registered the association under the name "Queensland Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Union of Employees".

51. I doubt that it was proper for Mr McGee to register the branch under a name different from its correct name. If the name of the association presented for registration did not accord with the requirements of the Act, it seems to me he should have rejected the application on that ground. He did not do so. What is the consequence of this?

52. At 25-29 Ryan J referred to the terms of the Queensland Industrial Relations (Protection from Invalidity) Act 1991. I do not think that s.5(1) of this statute overcomes the name problem. That subsection prevents challenge, in any proceedings, to the validity of certain things. They include "the legal existence or registration of an industrial organisation". But the operation of s.5(1) is limited to challenges on the grounds specified in subs.(2). Those grounds may be summarised as grounds arising out of the entwining of a State-registered organisation and a federal organisation. There is no ground that would cover the registration of an organisation under a name that complies with the command of s.37(2) but is not the organisation's true name.

53. Section 6 has potential relevance to the problem under discussion. Section 6(1) empowers the Industrial Commission to issue a certificate "declaring that a matter specified in the certificate - is valid notwithstanding the existence of an invalidity specified in the certificate". Such an invalidity may be one that results from contravention of a requirement of a "previous Act" - a term defined by s.4 so as to include any Act in existence before the Industrial Relations Act 1990 that provided for the registration of industrial unions. The 1916 Act is plainly such an Act; so the failure to effect registration under the correct name of the applicant is an invalidity that might be covered by a certificate. However, it does not appear that any certificate has been issued under s.6. The effect of what was done in 1917 must be determined independently of this legislation.

54. Leaving aside the Industrial Relations (Protection from Invalidity) Act I see two answers to concern about validity under State law. First, a misnomer is not necessarily fatal to validity. It has long been the law that a grant or devise to a corporation in a name differing from its true name will nevertheless be valid provided that the stated name is sufficient to indicate the intended grantee or devisee and to distinguish the named corporation from others: see Dr Ayray's Case [1572] EngR 105; (1614) 11 Co Rep 18b at 21a; Dean and Chapter of Bristol's Case (1603) Cary 44; Croydon Hospital v Farley [1816] EngR 293; (1816) 6 Taunt 467; and Attorney General v Corporation of Rye (1817) 1 Moore CP 267. There is no reason to apply a different rule in relation to a statutory registration. The addition of the words "union of employees" in the registration of ATAUE in 1917 does not obscure the intent of those concerned with the registration or create any difficulty in distinguishing the registered entity from others.

55. Secondly, I respectfully agree with the view expressed by Pincus J in Sharpe v Goodhew about the effect of s.73 of the Queensland Industrial Conciliation and Arbitration Act 1961. That section authorises the Queensland Industrial Commission to cancel a registration where "an industrial union has been registered erroneously or by mistake". Following a decision of the Full Court of the Supreme Court of Queensland in Federated Miscellaneous Workers' Union of Employees of Australia, Queensland Branch v Shelberg (1984) 1 Qd R 273, Pincus J held that the fact that the legislation gave the Commission a discretion to cancel an erroneous registration was an indication that the Queensland Parliament did not intend that an error should result automatically in invalidity.

56. It seems to me that there is no validity problem arising out of State law.

Validity of the 1917 registration under federal law: the fact of registration itself

57. I have already indicated my opinion that the views expressed by Fullagar J in Williams v Hursey do not undermine the validity of State registration of a branch of a federal organisation. The judges who decided Allingham, Bailey v Krantz, Sharpe v Goodhew and Frizziero v Rice obviously shared that view. But as Ryan J held to the contrary in the decision under appeal, and that holding was critical to his conclusion, I should justify my opinion. I can do so briefly because of the discussion of the point by Gray J in Bailey v Krantz at 366-370 and in Frizziero v Rice at 462-463, with which I respectfully agree. In the first passage, Gray J accepted that "a branch has no existence apart from the registered organisation of which it is part" but said that this did not preclude legislation dealing with a branch "as if it were a definable body". He pointed out that there were provisions in the then federal legislation (the Conciliation and Arbitration Act) that did so. In the second passage he observed that:

"(t)here is no difficulty, conceptual or otherwise,

in recognising the existence of a branch for purposes

such as authority to have custody of and to deal with

property of the organisation."

58. As it seems to me, a difficulty in reconciling the notion of State registration of a branch with what was said in Williams v Hursey emerges only if the effect of registration is severance of the branch from the remainder of the federal organisation. What Fullagar J was saying, as I understand him, was that a branch exists only as a part of the whole; while it may act on behalf of the organisation within the limits of the powers conferred upon it by the organisation's rules, it cannot have a life of its own independently of the organisation itself. To express the matter in terms of my physical analogy, while attached to the body an arm is a definable and useful member, but it has no function when severed from the body. If the effect of State registration is to create a new entity that is not part of the federal organisation, there is an obvious problem in reconciling the result with what was said by Fullagar J in Williams v Hursey. This is because Fullagar J applied s.109 of the Constitution. The approach taken by Ryan J would mean that State registration deprived an industrial organisation registered under federal law of one of its constituent branches and the members affiliated with that branch; State legislation would take precedence over the operation of federal legislation, the direct antithesis to the situation prescribed by s.109. If, however, and leaving incorporation aside for the moment, the effect of a State registration is that the branch remains what it previously was, part of the federal organisation, but empowered to act on behalf of members in connection with applications to relevant State tribunals, there is no problem. The local branch remains part of the federal organisation. It remains bound by the federal rules. Provided that it acts within the rules, its actions are valid and are actions of the organisation itself. I think this is what Fullagar J meant when he said in Williams v Hursey at 81 that, provided it acted within its powers, the Hobart branch "is, so to speak, the federation" (original emphasis).

Validity of the 1917 registration under federal law: the requirement of incorporation

59. As it seems to me, a problem of validity arises only because of the requirement of incorporation in s.37(1) of the Queensland Act. Section 37(1) of the Queensland Act provides that "upon and during registration" every industrial union shall "become and be, for the purposes of this Act, a body corporate by its registered name, having perpetual succession and a common seal". This language is close to that of s.136 of the Conciliation and Arbitration Act, discussed by Fullagar J in Williams v Hursey at 52. Section 136 read:

"136. Every organization registered under this Act shall

have perpetual succession and a common seal, and may

purchase take on lease hold sell lease mortgage

exchange and otherwise own possess and deal with any

real or personal property."

60. Fullagar J commented that the provision alone was "quite enough to give to a registered organisation the full character of a corporation". So it must be concluded that this is the effect of s.37(1) of the Queensland Act. If the registration was valid, upon the registration of the Queensland branch of ATAEA in 1917 that branch became a corporation with all the characteristics attaching to that status. In Frizziero v Rice Black CJ referred to aspects of the South Australian legislation, which also provided for incorporation upon registration. He thought those provisions inimical to the notion that the registered entity, the South Australian branch of FCAA, continued to be governed by the federal rules. The 1916 Queensland Act did not contain so many provisions of this nature as the South Australian legislation. Perhaps the only one was s.79 which empowered a registered industrial union to sue for fines, fees, levies and dues payable under its rules. If this provision applied, the Queensland branch could have instituted legal action in its own name, a situation inconsistent with that under the federal legislation and the organisation's rules: that any legal action would have to be instituted by the federal organisation. However, even though the Queensland Act provided only this one specific inconsistency, as Fullagar J observed (still at 52), "(t)he notion of qualified legal capacity is intelligible, but the notion of qualified legal personality is not". It cannot be doubted that the effect of incorporation of the branch (if valid) would be to enable it to do all the actions mentioned in the South Australian Act and noted by Black CJ. These actions include the acquisition and disposal of real and personal property. The power to do these things is inconsistent with the scheme of ATAEA federal rule 18 which puts the "funds and property" of the organisation under the control of the federal Committee of Management. Moreover, there is a more fundamental problem. It was expressed by Gray J in Bailey v Krantz at 376:

"A branch is part of an organisation; the question of

its continued existence lies in the hands of the

organisation. An organisation may wish to abolish

its branches, and to continue to exist as an

organisation without them. It may wish to change the

nature of its branches, for example from geographical

to occupational branches, or from branches consisting

of all members within a State to branches of larger

or smaller geographical areas. If a branch were able

to be given perpetual succession in its own right,

this power of disposition which the organisation has

over its branch would be lost. The branch could

continue to exist, notwithstanding any decision of

the organisation to disband or abolish it. Such a

result cannot be consistent with the capacities given

by the federal Act to an organisation."

See also his Honour's comments in Frizziero v Rice at 462.

61. It seems to me inescapable that the incorporation under State legislation of a branch of a federally-registered industrial organisation involves a conflict calling for resolution under s.109 of the Constitution. It may be resolved in only one way: by holding inapplicable to branches of a federally-registered organisation so much of s.37(1) of the Queensland Act as attaches to registration the consequence of incorporation.

62. In his judgment Northorp J draws attention to s.31 of the 1916 Queensland Act which provides for the issue of a certificate of registration. This certificate is said to "be conclusive evidence of the registration therein mentioned". But, of course, a certificate issued under State law cannot remedy a problem created by the supremacy of Commonwealth law over State law pursuant to s.109 of the Constitution.

Validity of the 1917 registration: severance

63. The conclusion I have reached in relation to the last topic is consistent with that of both Gray J (in Bailey v Krantz and Frizziero v Rice) and Black CJ (in Frizziero v Rice) in relation to the South Australian legislation. But their Honours parted company on the significance of that conclusion. Gray J thought that the South Australian provision providing automatic incorporation was severable from that permitting registration. On this view, the result would be similar to that envisaged by s.136A of the Conciliation and Arbitration Act, inserted in 1974 following the report of the Sweeney Committee. That section (perpetuated under the Industrial Relations Act as cl.5 of Schedule 4) read:

"136A(1) Where it is not contrary to the rules of an

organization to do so, it may participate in

the systems of conciliation and arbitration or

of wages boards or like systems established

under the law of a State, and for that purpose

a branch of an organization may become

registered under a law of a State so long as

that registration does not involve the branch

in becoming incorporated, or otherwise becoming

a legal entity, under the law of a State.

(2) Where an organization so participates, its

rules may provide that the Secretary of the

branch of the organization in the State shall

be the person to sue or to be sued under the

law of the State in respect of any acts or

omissions arising from that participation."

64. Gray J did not suggest that s.136A applied to the organisations under consideration in either Bailey v Krantz or Frizziero v Rice. In each case the registration of the State branch pre-dated the enactment of that section. He expressed his conclusion in Bailey v Krantz in this way, at 378:

"It should be noted that the section (s.136A) is

prospective in its terms; it contemplates that

organisations will adopt rules expressly permitting

the non-corporate registration of their branches

under State laws. Assuming that such non-corporate

registration already existed, by the registration of

a branch and the operation of s.109 of the

Constitution in relation to a purported grant of

incorporation, the section does not, in its terms

seek to set at nought such registration.

From all that I have said, it follows that what was

registered in South Australia in 1919 was the South

Australian branch of the union, and that this

registration was valid. Because of s.109 of the

Constitution, the South Australian Act and its

predecessors have not conferred upon that branch

incorporation. The provisions relating to

incorporation are severable from the provisions

relating to registration. There was nothing in the

federal Act, or any regulations under it, or in the

rules of the union in 1919, to prevent such

registration. No provision of the law of South

Australia has purported to undo the registration, or

to render it invalid. Nor has any provision of the

federal Act undone what was validly done in 1919."

65. Although he did not say so bluntly, Black CJ thought this reasoning missed the point; the matter of severance was not to be determined by reference to the terms of the federal Act or regulations or the content of the organisation's rules but by reference to the intention underlying the State legislation. In Frizziero v Rice the Chief Justice analysed the terms of the South Australian Industrial Code and concluded, at 455:

"In my judgment, the sections of the Code that

defined the nature of some of the powers and

attributes of an association once registered were

interdependent and inseparable from the sections that

provided for such registration. To remove s.68 (the

provision incorporating registered entities) from the

scheme of the Act, and thus to remove with it the

sections that proceeded upon the footing of

incorporation, could only produce, in my view,

provisions which the Parliament of South Australia

did not intend to enact. It is beside the point that

a scheme of registration without incorporation could

work and work well; that is not the scheme that the

South Australian Parliament chose to enact.

I therefore differ from the conclusion reached by

Gray J in Bailey v Krantz and conclude that the

inconsistency with the Conciliation and Arbitration

Act revealed by the provisions of the Code providing

for incorporation necessarily extends to the

provisions allowing for the registration of a branch

of an organisation."

66. It is not clear to me whether Gray J had the benefit of Black CJ's reasoning before he wrote his Frizziero v Rice judgment. He may not have; he made no reference to it. In relation to severance, Gray J adhered to the view he had expressed in Bailey v Krantz, with some short additional observations. He pointed out at 464 that the Victorian and Tasmanian industrial relations legislation permitted registration without incorporation and commented that:

"there is no inherent connection between registration

or recognition on the one hand and incorporation on

the other. If the purpose of registration is to

enable participation in a State system of industrial

conciliation and arbitration, and if there already

exists a corporate body which is effectively to be

registered by reference to its branch in the

appropriate State, separate incorporation of that

branch is unnecessary."

67. I agree with this comment, but the difficulty is that pointed out by Black CJ. In South Australia the legislature elected to impose a connection between registration and incorporation. I agree with Gray J that the result was unfortunate; as he said, acceptance of his view would tend "to save, rather than strike down registrations that have taken place". But this argument of convenience should not divert the Court from applying recognised legal principles. In considering whether a provision of a State Act for incorporation of a registered entity is severable from the provision for registration itself, the Court must consider the discernible intention of the State legislature. The federal legislation is irrelevant. When the South Australian legislation is examined, the view of Black CJ is, to my mind and with respect, plainly correct. And it applies equally to the Queensland legislation relevant to this case. Counsel for the first, second and fifth respondents expressed the position well when they said in their written submissions:

"The words 'upon and during registration' and 'become

and be' in s.37(1) make it apparent ... that

registration and incorporation are not severable

concepts. It is plain, from the manner in which the

Act deals with the powers, duties, responsibilities

and liabilities referred to in the 1916 Act, that it

contemplates that the industrial union will be an

entity in its own right."

68. In my opinion, the provision for incorporation contained in s.37(1) of the Queensland Act prevents that Act enabling the registration of a branch of a federal organisation. The registration effected by Mr McGee on 18 March 1917 was invalid.

69. If this conclusion is correct, many State registrations are invalid. In the early part of this century many federal organisations sought registration under State law of their local branches. In several States the relevant statute provided for automatic incorporation; it is in those cases that invalidity will arise. In the absence of s.136A, this may have created a major problem; there is obviously a practical advantage in federal organisations being able to participate in proceedings before State tribunals that affect their members' interests. However, s.136A removes the problem, provided the relevant States co-operate. If the affected States are prepared to amend their legislation so as to remove the consequence of incorporation, in those cases where the registered association is a branch of a federal organisation, the branch may make a fresh application for registration, the grant of which would not conflict with federal law.

The Relief sought at first instance.

GRAY J The Media Entertainment and Arts Alliance ("the alliance") is an organisation of employees, registered pursuant to the Industrial Relations Act 1988 ("the IR Act"). The alliance came into existence on 18th May 1992, pursuant to s. 253Q of the IR Act, by way of the amalgamation of three pre-existing registered organisations of employees. One of those pre-existing organisations was the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Employees Association ("the ATAEA").

2. The alliance is an appellant in this proceeding. It is described as the third appellant. The first and second appellants, Victoria Jane McJannet and Charles Henry Livingstone are members of the alliance. Mr. Livingstone is the former federal secretary of the ATAEA; since the amalgamation, he has been the federal president of the alliance.

3. At the commencement of the proceedings, there was registered pursuant to the Industrial Relations Act 1990 (Qld.) ("the present Queensland Act") a union of employees known as the Queensland Branch of the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Union of Employees ("the ATAUE"). The six persons described as the first respondents, Herbert White, Arthur Westbrook, James Beattie, Macquarie Hayward, William Morris and Edward Eastern were officers of the ATAUE.

4. By their application filed on 5th June 1992, the appellants sought orders restraining the six individual respondents and the ATAUE from taking further steps to implement a purported amalgamation between the ATAUE and the Australian Workers Union of Employees, Queensland, another body registered as a union of employees pursuant to the present Queensland Act. The appellants also sought a declaration that the former Queensland Branch of the ATAEA is one and the same as the ATAUE and a declaration that all the assets and all interests in property held in the name of the ATAUE are assets of the alliance. Orders were sought directing the six individual respondents and the ATAUE to transfer property and account for moneys or assets and to hand over records of the ATAUE to the alliance. A declaration was also sought that all members of the ATAUE are members of the alliance.

5. The proceedings at first instance were regularised by the addition of the Industrial Registrar of the State of Queensland, who is named as the third respondent in this appeal, and Graham Dolbel and the Australian Workers Union of Employees, Queensland, who together represent the interests of the other party to the proposed amalgamation at the state level, and who are the fourth and fifth respondents to this appeal. The Minister for Employment, Training and Industrial Relations for the State of Queensland intervened and is now the sixth respondent to this appeal.

6. The relief sought at first instance by the appellants thus raises once again the difficult problems which arise as a result of people attempting to administer representative industrial bodies by availing themselves of the separate systems of registration which have been offered under federal laws and the laws of some states. The judgment below.

7. Ryan J delivered a judgment at first instance on 20th November 1992. It is reported as McJannet v. White (1992) 39 FCR 1. His Honour held that, upon registration, the ATAUE "became a separate entity from the ATAEA though bound to it by force of an identical set of rules." (p. 23). His Honour further held, at p. 25, that because "the ATAUE acquired a corporate existence separate from its members, it was, as a matter of law, unaffected by the federal amalgamation." His Honour's conclusion, at p. 30, was that the officers of the ATAUE, in their capacity as such officers, could not perform or refrain from any act which, in law, would effectuate the amalgamation by which the alliance came into existence. His Honour dismissed the application.

8. The central issue in this appeal, therefore, is whether, by virtue of the purported registration of the ATAUE under Queensland legislation, there was created a corporate body separate and distinct from the ATAEA. If there was, his Honour's conclusion was correct and the appeal should be dismissed. If not, it would be necessary to examine whether the applicants are entitled to any, and if so what, relief sought in the proceeding before Ryan J.

The jurisdiction of the Court.

9. The appellants invoked ss. 253X and 253ZC of the IR Act as founding the jurisdiction of the court to grant them the orders which they sought. Although not a great deal of argument was directed towards the question of jurisdiction, it is one which must be resolved. The Court cannot act on the basis that it is convenient for all parties to have the issues between them resolved, if it does not have jurisdiction to resolve those issues.

10. Both sections relied on are found in Subdivision F of Division 7 of Part IX of the IR Act. Part IX deals with registered organisations. Division 7 deals with amalgamation of those organisations. The division sets out the procedure by which organisations registered pursuant to the IR Act may amalgamate. Ordinarily, the process of amalgamation involves the preparation of a scheme for amalgamation, after negotiations between the representatives of two or more proposed amalgamating organisations, the approval of that scheme by the committee of management of each of those organisations, the making of an application to the Australian Industrial Relations Commission for approval for the submission of the amalgamation to ballot and the conduct of ballots of the members of the organisations. Subdivision F contains provisions dealing with the taking effect of an amalgamation after the approval of the proposed amalgamation by ballot. A designated presidential member of the Australian Industrial Relations Commission is required to fix a day on which the amalgamation is to take effect, pursuant to s. 253Q(2). That day is known as the "amalgamation day". Upon the amalgamation day, various consequences follow. One of them is the deregistration of any organisation or organisations which are to be deregistered as part of the scheme of the amalgamation; see s. 253Q(3)(c). On the amalgamation day, all assets and liabilities of a deregistered organisation cease to be assets and liabilities of that organisation and become assets and liabilities of the amalgamated organisation; see s. 253R(1).

11. The amalgamation day, on which the alliance came into existence, was 18th May 1992. On that day, a designated presidential member of the Australian Industrial Relations Commission made an order deregistering the ATAEA and another organisation which participated in the amalgamation. On that day, therefore, all assets and liabilities of the ATAEA ceased to be assets and liabilities of the ATAEA and became assets and liabilities of the alliance. Further, the alliance became obliged by s. 253X(1) to "take such steps as are necessary to ensure that the amalgamation, and the operation of this Subdivision in relation to the amalgamation, are fully effective".

12. On 3rd June 1992, the federal management committee of the alliance carried a resolution directing the six individual respondents to this proceeding to take all steps to implement the amalgamation and in particular "so as to ensure the amalgamation is fully effective" to provide to a named person all accounts of the Queensland branch of the former ATAEA, to transfer all property of that branch to the alliance, to pay any further moneys received by that branch to the Queensland branch of the alliance, to direct all employers receiving payroll deductions for subscriptions of members to pay such sums to the Queensland branch of the alliance and to discontinue certain proceedings in the Queensland Industrial Relations Commission relating to the purported amalgamation of the ATAUE with the Australian Workers Union of Employees, Queensland. The directions with respect to accounts, property and moneys were expressed in terms which made it clear that the alliance regarded the ATAUE as being the Queensland branch of the former ATAEA for the purposes of the directions. In other words, what was claimed was an entitlement by the alliance to accounts, property and moneys of the ATAUE on the basis that they were accounts, property and moneys of the former Queensland branch of the ATAEA.

13. Following the communication of this resolution, some documents and property which were conceded as belonging to the ATAEA were delivered up. The six individual respondents, however, have resisted any further compliance with the directions in the resolution, on the basis that property of the ATAUE was not property of the ATAEA.

14. Section 253X(2) provides, "the Court may, on the application of an interested person, make such orders as it considers appropriate to ensure that subsection (1) is given effect to." It will be recalled that the subs. (1) referred to is the provision requiring an amalgamated organisation to take such steps as are necessary to ensure that the amalgamation, and the operation of Subdivision F in relation to the amalgamation, are fully effective.

15. In one sense, an amalgamation will always be fully effective, once the amalgamation day has passed. The organisations which were parties to the scheme of amalgamation approved as required will have become amalgamated. There would be no point in a provision such as s. 253X(1) if "fully effective" were to be construed as meaning only fully effective in the legal sense. What must have been intended by the provision is that an amalgamated organisation must take steps to ensure that the amalgamation is fully effective in a practical sense. Thus, if there are assets of a former organisation which has been deregistered on the amalgamation day, which assets have not been placed under the control of the amalgamated organisation, the amalgamation could not be said to have been fully effective. Nor could the operation of Subdivision F, and particularly of s. 253R(1), be said to be fully effective in relation to the amalgamation in such circumstances. The amalgamated organisation therefore has a duty to pursue such assets. If it is unsuccessful, s. 253X(2) permits application to be made for orders which will make the amalgamation fully effective in a practical sense.

16. There can be no doubt that the amalgamated organisation is an interested person, within the meaning of s. 253X(2), in the making of such an application. Nor can there be any doubt that the appellant Mr. Livingstone has the requisite interest as federal president of the alliance. The question whether the appellant Ms. McJannet had a sufficient interest as a member of the alliance, or whether she held some other office which would give her such an interest, was not the subject of argument. It is unnecessary to deal with that question as the Court at first instance had before it two applicants who were clearly interested persons within the meaning of s. 253X(2).

17. The Court therefore had jurisdiction pursuant to s. 253X(2) of the IR Act to entertain the application for orders directing the six individual respondents and the ATAUE to transfer property and account for moneys or assets and to hand over records of the ATAUE to the alliance. Similarly, it seems to me that the Court had jurisdiction to entertain the application seeking to restrain further steps implementing the purported amalgamation between the ATAUE and the Australian Workers Union of Employees, Queensland. That purported amalgamation, if the ATAUE were in truth the Queensland branch of the ATAEA, would prevent the amalgamation which led to the creation of the alliance from being fully effective. If s. 253X(2) did not authorise the seeking of declaratory relief itself, that relief could be sought legitimately pursuant to s. 21 of the Federal Court of Australia Act 1976, once it is clear that the application is otherwise within the jurisdiction of the court. See McLeish v. Faure [1979] FCA 38; (1979) 40 FLR 462, especially at pp 475-476.

18. The meaning of s. 253ZC of the IR Act is by no means as easy to ascertain. It provides as follows:-

"(1) Where any difficulty arises in relation to the

application of this Subdivision to a particular

matter, the Court may, on the application of an

interested person, make such order as it considers

proper to resolve the difficulty.

(2) An order made under subsection (1) has effect

despite anything contained in this Act or in any other

Commonwealth law or any State or Territory law."

19. This section plainly cannot be construed as conferring legislative power on the Court, despite the apparent conferral by subs. (2) of the power to override legislation. To confer legislative power on a court would infringe the doctrine of separation of powers, which is implicit in the constitution: R. v. Kirby; Ex parte Boilermakers' Society of Australia [1956] HCA 10; (1956) 94 CLR 254. Thus, a "difficulty" of the kind contemplated by subs. (1) cannot be a question of inconvenience in the application of the provisions of Subdivision F. It must be a difficulty of another kind, but what kind is not clear.

20. In addition, the use of the word "matter" raises questions. It has been held that the word "matter", when used in s. 347 of the IR Act bears a meaning similar to that which was attributed to it by the High Court of Australia in Fencott v. Muller (1983) 152 CLR 570, at p 603, namely "a justiciable controversy, identifiable independently of the proceedings which are brought for its determination and encompassing all claims made within the scope of the controversy". See Poulos v. Waltons Stores (Interstate) Ltd. (1986) 15 IR 335, at pp 339-340, Thompson v. Hodder [1989] FCA 493; (1989) 21 FCR 467, at p 469, Re Polites; Ex parte Hoyts Corporation Pty. Ltd. [1991] HCA 31; (1991) 173 CLR 78, at p 93 and Bostik (Australia) Pty. Ltd. v. Gorgevski (No. 2) (1992) 36 FCR 439, at pp 445-446. Section 253ZC was enacted by the Industrial Relations Legislation Amendment Act 1990, which came into operation on 1st February 1991. The Act was passed after the judgments in Poulos and Thompson had been published. Yet it seems clear that the word "matter" in s. 253ZC bears a different meaning. Subdivision F applies to the amalgamation of organisations; it may be that the term "matter" is intended to comprehend an amalgamation. Section 253X(2) makes provision for legal proceedings; it may be that s. 253ZC is intended to apply only where a difficulty arises in such proceedings. If that be the case, the choice of language is unusual as a means of giving the Court broad discretionary powers in a proceeding already within its jurisdiction.

21. The meaning of the word "difficulty" and the meaning of the word "matter" in s. 253ZC were not the subject of argument. Being of the view that the Court derives the jurisdiction it needs to deal with this proceeding from s. 253X, I prefer to express no concluded view about the effect of s. 253ZC.

22. "Every organization registered under this Act shall

and a common seal, and may purchase take on lease hold

sell lease mortgage exchange and otherwise own possess

23. The equivalent provision, which had by then become s. 136 of the CandA Act, was held in Williams v. Hursey [1959] HCA 51; (1959) 103 CLR 30, at p 52, to "give to a registered organization the full character of a corporation."

As well as the "conclusive evidence" of its registration which was to be found in its certificate of registration, the ATAEA (along with all other organisations then registered) had the benefit of no less than two separate statutory provisions, deeming its registration to have been proper. 28. The purported registration of the ATAUE was effected on 18th March 1917. It is clear from the evidence that, at that time and prior to it, when the application for registration was made, the ATAEA was in existence and had a branch in Queensland. It must be presumed, in the absence of evidence to the contrary, that the rules of the ATAEA remained until after 18th March 1917 in a form at least substantially similar to that which they took on 16th August 1916. A number of conclusions can be drawn about the power of the Queensland branch of the ATAEU to apply for registration under Queensland law.

29. In the first place, it must be recognised that the CandA Act at that time said nothing at all about the registration of organisations, or their branches, under state law. Such registration was neither expressly permitted nor expressly forbidden. Nor did the rules of the ATAEA say anything express about the subject of registration under state law. It can be said that the objects of the ATAEA were broad enough to permit such registration, and even that object (h), with its specific reference to carrying into effect (in) any way considered necessary or advisable any provisions of any state statute relating to industrial disputes and arbitration, provided some positive encouragement. The specific reference to branch general meetings is of significance in the light of the manner in which approval was given for the application for registration under Queensland law. Also of significance is the express power of the federal council to reverse the decision of any branch, which would have enabled the federal council to deprive the application for registration of its necessary authority, had it been felt to be improper.

30. The conclusion must be reached, therefore, that, under the rules of the ATAEA, there was power in the Queensland branch of the ATAEA to make application for registration under Queensland law, and no barrier to the exercise of that power is to be found in the CandA Act. It is necessary to turn to the provisions of the relevant Queensland legislation, in order to ascertain whether they contemplated an application by a branch of an organisation registered under the CandA Act.

The Queensland Registration Provisions in 1917.

31. Part III of the Industrial Arbitration Act 1916 (Qld.) ("the 1916 Queensland Act") made provision for the registration of certain bodies. Section 26(1) provided:-

"The registrar may, on application made as prescribed,

register as an industrial union under this Act any

industrial association or trade union of employees.

On such registration the industrial association or

trade union shall be an industrial union until such

registration is duly cancelled."

32. A question which arises immediately is whether the words of this section were appropriate to include the registration of a branch of an organisation registered under the CandA Act. The learned trial judge (at p. 16) pointed out that no definition appears in the 1916 Queensland Act of either of the expressions "industrial association" or "trade union". His Honour expressed the view that the phrase "trade union" connoted a trade union registered under the Trade Union Act 1916 (Qld.) and "industrial association" comprehended all other associations of employees having as their objects the protection and advancement of the industrial interests and conditions of those employees. His Honour recognised that, when used in that sense, "industrial association" was capable of including a "trade union" in the ordinary meaning of that term and was therefore intended to comprehend a trade union not registered under the Trade Union Act 1916 (Qld.), including an organisation registered under the CandA Act.

33. Whilst it is of no particular moment, I disagree with his Honour's reasoning, whilst agreeing with his conclusion on this point. I see no warrant for giving to the phrase "trade union" in s. 26(1) of the 1916 Queensland Act any meaning other than its ordinary meaning. The Oxford English Dictionary defines "trade union" as "an association of the workers in any trade or in allied trades for the protection and furtherance of their interests in regard to wages, hours, and conditions of labour, and for the provision, from their common funds, of pecuniary assistance to the members during strikes, sickness, unemployment, old age, etc." The dictionary quotes uses of the phrase from 1835 onwards, which indicate that it was in ordinary use in 1916. Readers of the passage in Albion Quarrying Co. Pty. Ltd. v. Associated Quarries Pty. Ltd. [1945] VicLawRp 1; (1945) VLR 1, at p 21, to which the learned trial judge referred, will recognise that Herring CJ there used the words of the Oxford Dictionary definition in setting out what he regarded as the popular meaning of the phrase "trade union".

34. In my view, the phrase "trade union" in the 1916 Queensland Act is to be given its ordinary meaning. The phrase "industrial association" indicates an intention by the legislature to capture an even broader class of associations. It includes, for instance, associations of workers not confined to a particular trade or allied trades and, perhaps associations of employees which did not have rules for the provision from common funds of pecuniary assistance to their members. The intention was to enable the registration of the broadest class of bodies representing the industrial interests of employees.

35. The question remains whether the words were appropriate to allow registration of a branch of an organisation registered under the CandA Act. One of the issues which arose in Williams v. Hursey [1959] HCA 51; (1959) 103 CLR 30 was whether it was possible for a claim to be brought in the Supreme Court of Tasmania against a body described as "the Hobart Branch of the Waterside Workers' Federation". The claim was brought in reliance on a rule of the Supreme Court of Tasmania which permitted "any unincorporated society, fellowship, club or association" to be sued in its name. The Waterside Workers' Federation of Australia was an organisation, registered pursuant to the CandA Act. The issue, therefore, was whether its Hobart branch could be described as an unincorporated society, fellowship, club or association. The High Court held that it could not. See the judgment of Fullagar J at pp. 51 and 53-55. His Honour there examined the nature of a branch. It is unnecessary for me to go in detail to the passage in the present context.

36. It follows from Williams v. Hursey that the word "association" in the legislation of a state, if it be used in its ordinary and natural meaning, is inapt to include a branch of an organisation registered under the CandA Act. The same is probably true of the phrase "trade union". There is no difficulty in both expressions comprehending the organisation itself; it is the non-existence of a branch as any kind of legal entity which makes the expressions inapt to apply to it.

37. On the other hand, there is no principle that legislation cannot refer to a branch. Indeed, the IR Act does so on many occasions. It refers expressly to "a branch" and to "branches". A branch of an organisation is something recognisable, which can be described in words. There is no particular reason why appropriate words in the legislation of a state cannot apply to a branch, as a matter of expression.

38. In my view, the words of s. 26(1) of the 1916 Queensland Act did so apply. The crucial factor in forming this view is the words of s. 21(6), which are:-

"No branch of a trade union shall be registered unless

it is a bona fides branch of sufficient importance to

be registered separately."

39. Unless the words "trade union" in s. 26(1) were intended to apply to a branch, subs. (6) would be entirely meaningless.

40. I am therefore of the view that s. 26(1) of the 1916 Queensland Act, in its terms, permitted the registration of a branch of an organisation registered pursuant to the CandA Act. This was also the view which I took in Callaghan v. Federated Clerks Union of Australia (1987) 22 IR 346, at pp 348-349. It is also the view which was taken, by implication, by Moynihan J in Federated Clerks Union of Australia, North Queensland Branch v. Industrial Conciliation and Arbitration Commission (1988) 29 IR 160.

The nature of a branch

41. It was suggested during argument in this appeal that a branch of an organisation registered under the CandA Act was inherently incapable of being registered under state legislation. The argument was based on what I believe to have been a misunderstanding of what Fullagar J said in Williams v. Hursey, referring to the Hobart Branch of the Waterside Workers Federation of Australia. At pp 53-54, his Honour said:

"It is plain that the "Hobart Branch" has no

corporate character - no separate legal existence as a

juristic person."

At pp. 54-55, his Honour said:

"It has no separate identity - no existence apart from

the registered organization, of which it is an

integral and inseverable part. Its members are merely

a section of the total membership of the federation -

locally organized for the sake of convenience, but in

no respect independent of the federation, and in all

respects subject to the control of the federation.

The branches are permitted within limits to make rules

of their own, but the rules which they make derive

authority from the rules of the federation. It is

contemplated that a branch may have "property and

moneys", but, if r. 15 of the Hobart branch rules

means that the beneficial ownership thereof is in the

members of the branch, it is inconsistent with r.

10(A) of the rules of the federation which makes the

"fund and property" of the organization and its

branches subject to the "care, superintendence,

management and administration of the governing body of

the Organization for the carrying out of the objects

of the Organization".

42. The situation is perhaps reminiscent of the complaint of a child that she had been taken to watch the regiment march but all she had seen was a whole lot of soldiers.

43. As I have said, a branch is identifiable. The rules of the organisation of which it is part may give it, through its decision making bodies, powers and functions. Within the scope of its authority, it may act without reference to other branches or the decision making bodies of the organisation itself. It may therefore pursue policies and perform acts which have not received the specific approval of the decision making bodies of the organisation. To say that these policies and acts are policies and acts of the organisation is not to deny that they are things done by the branch. As subsequent legislation recognises, the rules of an organisation may give to a branch "autonomy" in some respects. See now cl. 4 of Schedule 4 to the IR Act.

44. Given that many organisations have been constituted traditionally as federations or unions of branches consisting of their members living and working in the various states, there is nothing remarkable about those branches, within the authority given to them under the rules of the organisations, seeking to pursue improvements in the wages and conditions of their members by reliance on state legislation. If, in order to pursue that end, a branch finds that it is necessary to become registered, there is no particular reason why it may not do so. The placing of a name of a branch on a register cannot of itself involve any derogation from the scheme of the CandA Act or the IR Act or any interference with the conceptual nature of an organisation. A branch is, after all identifiable, and can be given a name. The writing of this name in a register does not itself involve any change in the nature of the branch or anything inconsistent with that nature.

What was registered on 18th March 1917?

45. I have had the advantage of reading in draft form the reasons for judgment of Wilcox J in this appeal. His Honour has set out under the headings "The Application for Registration" and "Post-Registration Events" his conclusion that what was registered on 18th March 1917 under the 1916 Queensland Act was the Queensland branch of the ATAEA and the facts and reasoning upon which that conclusion is based. I respectfully agree with the conclusion, with his Honour's reasoning and with his Honour's statement of the facts.

46. It should not be regarded as surprising that the view should have been taken within the ATAEA and by the registrar appointed under the 1916 Queensland Act that registration of a branch was an appropriate course. It was a frequent occurrence for branches of organisations registered under the CandA Act to become registered under the legislation of various states at and about that time. It should not be expected that the legal subtleties involved in such actions would be apparent to those persons. Indeed, it would have taken a lawyer of remarkable prescience to advise that such registration led to any legal difficulties. It is only necessary to compare the judgments in Waterside Workers' Federation of Australia v. Burgess Bros. Ltd. [1916] HCA 2; (1916) 21 CLR 129 with those in Williams v. Hursey to see the development of legal theory relating to the branches of organisations registered under the CandA Act at the highest judicial level.

47. One argument put in the present case, although somewhat faintly, was that the effect of the registration on 18th March 1917 was to cause the ATAEA, as distinct from its Queensland branch, to become registered under the 1916 Queensland Act. As I understand this argument, it is not an argument that that was what occurred in fact, but an argument that that was the legal consequence of what occurred. Again, the argument was based on a misunderstanding of what was said by Fullagar J in Williams v. Hursey, at pp 81-82:-

"But in truth, as has been said, the branch has in law

no existence separate from that of the federation. It

is merely an aggregate of members which is an integral

part or section of the whole federation, having that

degree of autonomy which is permitted to it by or

under the constitution of the federation. It

represents the federation in the port of Hobart. In

and for the port of Hobart it is, so to speak, the

federation. It is forbidden by rr. 2 and 4 of Pt. II

of the federal rules to strike or to take any step to

"enforce any wages, hours, or conditions of labour"

without prior approval of the governing body of the

federation. But, subject to those limitations, and

perhaps one or two others, it is set up and organized

in the port of Hobart to do in that port whatever the

federation may do in any Australian port. Because it

has that character and those functions, it seems to me

that acts of the branch within its local limits are

prima facie acts of the federation itself."

48. It is true that, having in no sense an independent existence, a branch of an organisation cannot act otherwise than as the organisation itself. It need not, however, act as the entire organisation. Given that a branch may be subject to rules of the organisation which permit it to make decisions affecting itself, those decisions affect the organisation only in the sense of affecting the branch. Nothing said by Fullagar J leads to the conclusion that, if a branch acts expressly in the name of the branch, it is to be treated as having acted in the name of the organisation as a whole.

The effect of registration.

49. Section 37(1) of the 1916 Queensland Act provided as follows:-

"Every industrial union shall, upon and during

registration, become and be, for the purposes of this

Act, a body corporate by its registered name, having

perpetual succession and a common seal."

The similarities between this provision and that which was held in Williams v. Hursey to confer full corporate personality will be apparent.

50. Once it is accepted as a matter of fact that the Queensland branch of the ATAEA applied for registration and was registered under the 1916 Queensland Act, one of three possible legal consequences must follow. The first is that the registration was perfectly valid and s. 37(1) of the 1916 Queensland Act created from what was registered a new legal entity, separate from the ATAEA. This is the view which commended itself to Ryan J at first instance. It is also in conformity with the views expressed by his Honour (with the support of Sheppard J) in Frizziero v. Rice (1992) 36 FCR 449, especially at pp 473-476. Pincus J reached a similar conclusion, based on different reasoning in Sharpe v. Goodhew (1990) 33 IR 238, at pp 242-243. In his reasons for judgment in the present appeal, which I have had the privilege of reading in draft form, Northrop J has reached a similar conclusion by what I understand to be yet another process of reasoning.

51. The second possibility is that the registration was totally invalid, and nothing was ever registered, much less created as a separate entity. This view rests on the proposition that, if s. 37(1) of the 1916 Queensland Act operated to incorporate the branch, there would be an inconsistency between that provision and the CandA Act, within the meaning of s. 109 of the Constitution. Because incorporation is an inseparable consequence of registration, the state legislation could not operate with respect to a branch of an organisation registered under the CandA Act. This view coincides with the assumption which underlay the report of Mr. Justice J.B. Sweeney, who acted as a committee of inquiry into the question of dual registration of industrial bodies. It was the view which appealed to Black CJ in Frizziero v. Rice, at pp 452-455. It is the view expressed by Wilcox J in the present appeal.

52. The third possibility also rests on the proposition that s. 37(1) of the 1916 Queensland Act could not operate with respect to the Queensland branch of the ATAEA, because it was inconsistent with the CandA Act. On this view, however, incorporation is severable from registration. The registration remains valid and the 1916 Queensland Act was inoperative to the extent of any inconsistency with the CandA Act, in relation to the Queensland branch of the ATAEA. This was the view which I expressed in Bailey v. Krantz (1985) 13 IR 339, especially at pp 376-377. I followed the same view in Callaghan v. Federated Clerks Union of Australia (1987) 22 IR 346, at pp 347-349. Moynihan J followed it in Federated Clerks Union of Australia, North Queensland Branch v. Industrial Conciliation and Arbitration Commission (1988) 29 IR 160. I adhered to that view in Frizziero and Rice at pp 461-464.

53. I respectfully agree with Wilcox J in his reasons for judgment in the present appeal that the first possibility is untenable. It is arrived at by reasoning backwards from the assumption of a valid registration with valid incorporation under the state legislation to the notional creation of an association, separate from the ATAEA, which is taken to have applied for registration. The notional creation of an association is not only inconsistent with the facts. It also rests upon the mistaken view that the state legislation can accomplish in two steps what it cannot accomplish in one. If the state legislation cannot take a branch of an organisation registered under the federal legislation and incorporate it directly, then it cannot take such a branch, convert it notionally into a separate association and then incorporate that association.

54. Once it is accepted that, as a matter of fact, the Queensland branch of the ATAEA applied for registration and was registered, a question of inconsistency arises inevitably. Pincus J in Sharpe v. Goodhew accepted that this question arose and held that there was no inconsistency; incorporation of a branch of a federally registered organisation was a legal possibility. I am unable to agree with that proposition. I have already stated my reasons for that view fully in Bailey v. Krantz and in Frizziero v. Rice and can see no point in repeating them.

55. To the extent to which the argument in favour of the first possibility rests upon the conclusive effect of a certificate of registration or of any provision under which incorporation is granted, or deemed to be effective, the argument must fail. It does not avoid any question of inconsistency. Plainly, if incorporation cannot be conferred initially because of inconsistency with federal legislation, it cannot be conferred retrospectively for the same reason.

56. Nor can it be argued, as the respondents attempted to do, that it is simply enough to point to the creation of a corporation. The notion of a corporation without corporators goes beyond anything presently known to our legal system. A corporation is not created in a vacuum. The privilege of incorporation is granted, in this case by statute, to a specified body, namely the industrial association or trade union which applied for it. If that industrial association or trade union was one which, by reason of the existence of federal legislation, could not receive a grant of incorporation, then the state legislation purporting to grant incorporation was inconsistent with that federal legislation. A recognition of that inconsistency underlies the views expressed by Ryan J at first instance, and by Northrop J in this appeal. Their Honours take the view that, because incorporation of a branch would be impossible, it must have been something other than the Queensland branch of the ATAEA which was registered. The only expression of a view that there is no inconsistency between incorporation of a branch by state legislation and the CandA Act is that of Pincus J in Sharpe v. Goodhew. For the reasons which I expressed in Frizziero v. Rice at pp 459-464, I regard that view as untenable.

57. Once that conclusion is reached, it is necessary to confront the issue of severance. In Bailey v. Krantz and again in Frizziero v. Rice, I took the view that provisions for incorporation were severable from provisions for registration, so that it was possible for a branch of an organisation registered under the CandA Act to become registered under South Australian legislation. The contrary view, expressed by the learned Chief Justice in Frizziero v. Rice, is that incorporation is such an integral part of the scheme of the South Australian legislation that it cannot be severed. The result of that view is that the purported registration of the branch must fall, because of the inconsistency between a provision for incorporation of a branch and the CandA Act.

58. Having reconsidered in this appeal the view which I expressed in Bailey v. Krantz and Frizziero v. Rice, in the light of the arguments put in this appeal, I remain of the view that incorporation and registration are severable. In the case of the 1916 Queensland Act, the provisions are found in separate sections, separated by a number of other provisions. There is no inherent connection between the placing of a name on a register and the granting of incorporation. No conceptual difficulty results from regarding the Queensland branch of the ATAEA as having become an industrial union under s. 26(1) for the 1916 Queensland Act, without the incorporation purportedly conferred by s. 37. The real purpose of becoming an industrial union was no doubt to give to that branch the right to refer an application to the Court of Industrial Arbitration given by s. 7(1), the right of nomination of members of industrial boards given by s. 39(5), the right of entering into binding agreements with employers or associations of employers given by s. 51 and the right of nominating members of conciliation committees given by s. 58(1) of the 1916 Queensland Act. All of these functions could be carried out by the branch as an industrial union, without the need for incorporation. It might also be mentioned that they could be carried out without the imposition of a new name, requiring the insertion of the words "union of employees", as required by s. 37(2), this being inconsistent with the provisions of the CandA Act relating to the changing of the name of an organisation. In similar fashion, as legislation developed at both the state and federal levels, the state could not force upon the branch as an industrial union rules which had not been adopted in accordance with the requirements of federal legislation, members who were not eligible to join the federal organisation and elections separate from those conducted under the CandA Act and the IR Act. All of these things were inconsistent with the federal legislation. All of them are to be regarded as severable, they being incidental to the primary objects of registration, which were concerned with the ability of the branch to represent its members in the state system of industrial conciliation and arbitration.

59. Manifestly inconsistent with the IR Act was any attempt to amalgamate the branch with any other entity registered under state legislation. This must be so whether or not the provisions relating to incorporation are regarded as severable from those related to registration. That is to say, whether or not the Queensland branch of the ATAEA became registered on 18th March 1917 under the 1916 Queensland Act, no legislation of the State of Queensland could effect an amalgamation between that branch and the Australian Workers Union of Employees, Queensland.

Conclusion

60. In my view, the Queensland branch of the ATAEA became registered validly on 18th March 1917 and has remained registered. It did not become incorporated. Its rules, including those relating to eligibility for membership, its elections for officers and its assets all remained governed by the rules of the ATAEA and are now governed by the rules of the alliance, since the amalgamation. Any assets held by the branch were assets of the ATAEA and are now assets of the alliance. The purported amalgamation of the ATAUE and the Australian Workers Union of Employees, Queensland is a nullity.

61. I agree with the declarations (a), (b) and (c) which Wilcox J proposes. I disagree with the declarations (d) and (e) which his Honour also proposes. I agree otherwise with the orders which his Honour proposes.
