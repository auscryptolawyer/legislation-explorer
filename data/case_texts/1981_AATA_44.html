Re Heather Margaret Watts and the Commissioner of Superannuation [1981] AATA 44 (5 June 1981)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: HEATHER MARGARET WATTS

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR

SUPERANNUATION

No. V80/53

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Mr R.K. Todd (Senior Member)

Mr L.G. Oxby (Member)

Dr A.H. Marsh (Member)

CATCHWORDS

superannuation _ determination of retirement benefits _ medical examination _ whether failure to furnish full information _ whether furnishing of false information _ issue of benefit classification certificate _ nature and extent of duty to disclose information _ nature of relationship between a contributor and the fund _ physical or mental condition specified _ whether incapacity caused by or connected with conditions in certificate

Superannuation Act 1976 s.16(2)(3)(4)(11), s.66(2)

HEARING

CANBERRA

5:6:1981

ORDER

The Tribunal decides to affirm the decision of the Acting Commissioner for Superannuation:

(a) to issue pursuant to sub-section 16(11) of the Superannuation Act 1976 a benefit classification certificate in respect of the applicant specifying the conditions 'history of emotional lability, history of headaches (migrainous and otherwise) and dizziness';

(b) to form the opinion that pursuant to paragraph (c) of sub-section 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 the incapacity that was the ground for the applicant's retirement was caused, or substantially contributed to, by the physical or mental condition specified in the benefit classification certificate, or by a condition connected with such conditions.

DECISION

1. This application for review concerns the applicability in the proved circumstances of the provisions of s.16(11) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act").

2. Section 16 of the 1976 Act provides for the conduct of medical examinations. By s.16(2) a person who proposes to become, or becomes, an eligible employee shall undergo such medical examination or examinations by an approved medical practitioner or practitioners as the Commissioner requires. Section 16(3) provides that a report or reports of the result or results of the medical examination or examinations shall be furnished to the Commissioner. Section 16(4) provides:

'16(4) The Commissioner shall consider the report or reports, and such other matters (if any) as the Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of the opinion that the person is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the report or reports, to continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age, the Commissioner shall issue a benefit classification certificate to that effect, being a certificate in which the relevant condition or conditions is or are specified.' The abovementioned provisions for the issue of benefit classification certificates catered for the situation of persons becoming contributors to the Superannuation Fund whose physical or mental condition at the point when he or she was medically examined prevented their unqualified entry as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund. It will be recalled that prior to 1 July 1976, broadly speaking such a person became a contributor to the Provident Account. Under the new system of benefit classification certificates specific provision was made in relation to the case of a person failing to make full disclosure of necessary information at the time when he or she was medically examined. This was effected by s.16(11), which is as follows:

'16(11) Where _

(a) a person has ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of death or retirement on the ground of invalidity;

(b) his period of contributory service is less than 20 years and, at the time he ceased to be an eligible employee, he had not attained his maximum retiring age;

(c) a benefit classification certificate was not in force in respect of him at the time he ceased to be an eligible employee or a benefit classification certificate was in force in respect of him at that time but there was not specified in the certificate the physical or mental condition or conditions which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, caused, or substantially contributed to, the death or the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement on the ground of invalidity; and

(d) the Commissioner is satisfied _

(i) that, at or in connexion with a medical examination which the person was required to undergo under this section, the person failed to furnish any information required to be furnished by him or furnished false information; and

(ii)that, if the person had not failed to furnish that information or had not furnished false information, there would have been in force in respect of the person at the time he ceased to be an eligible employee a benefit classification certificate in which the physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in paragraph (c), or a physical or mental condition connected with that condition or those conditions, would have been specified,

the Commissioner shall issue a benefit classification certificate in which the condition or conditions referred to in paragraph (c) is or are specified and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have been in force in respect of that person at the time of his death or retirement.'

4. The respondent claims that the provisions of s.16(11), and in turn the provisions of s.66(2) of the 1976 Act are applicable in the circumstances of the present applicant. The relevant portion of s.66(2) is that which relates to the formation by the Commissioner of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for the person's retirement 'was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition connected with such a condition or such conditions'. If such an opinion is formed, the benefit entitlement of the retiree is quite limited.

5. The applicant is now aged 25. In February of 1977 she entered the Commonwealth Public Service as a Clerk Class 1. On 29 April of that year she was medically examined for the purpose of determining if the state of her health was such as to permit her to become a permanent officer and a contributor to the Superannuation Fund. This examination was completed in a routine manner and Miss Watts was duly appointed and became a contributor to the Fund. As subsequent events have led the respondent to conclude that the answers which the applicant gave to questions asked of her in this examination were either false or incomplete, this initial medical examination is therefore central to the determination of the application for review.

6. Prior to undergoing a physical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO") a prospective entrant to the Public Service is presented with a questionnaire entitled 'Applicant's Statement of Personal Medical History'. The applicant is asked to answer the questions set out in this questionnaire, and then further answers are given to questions asked by the CMO in relation to specific aspects of the entrant's health and medical history. In written answers to the following questions in the Statement the applicant gave the following answers to the questions set out below:

'Are you now suffering or have you ever suffered from any of the following disabilities:

(1)(a) Nervous fatigue or neurasthenia? No

(f) Persistent headaches? No

(g) Dizziness or turns? No

(h) Attack of unconsciousness or weakness?No

(i) Migraine? No

(11)(b)Any condition requiring glasses, corneal or contact lenses to be worn constantly? Yes

(12)(a) Have you had any operations, surgical treatments or accidents? Yes

(b) Have you had any complaints, illness or injury not mentioned above? No

(c)Have you been absent from school or work through illness over the past two years? (If so, please state the duration.) Yes

(d)Are you in good health now? Yes

(13)(a)Date you last received medical or surgical treatment and for what condition? Nov 75 Appendix OP

(b)Are you aware of any circumstances regarding your health which will interfere with the satisfactory discharge of the duties of the position for which you are now applying? No"

The questionnaire then required details of any 'Yes' answers to be furnished, save in relation to question 12(d). The applicant set out the following: 'Question Year

No. Occurred

11(b) 1972 Short sighted Tending to Improve

12(a) 1975 Appendix removed

12(c) 1977 1 day through minor accident.'

The CMO noted against the last entry the words 'Fell off horse'. The respondent alleges that in respect of the answers set out above the applicant furnished false information or failed to furnish information that was required to be furnished. We now turn to the subsequent history of the matter.

7. Miss Watts took up a position as an Arrears Recovery Officer in the Department of Veterans' Affairs. As standard procedure she was engaged for six months on a probationary basis during which time her conduct, diligence and efficiency were monitored with a view to deciding if her permanent appointment would be confirmed. Unfortunately during this period Miss Watts was unable to work on numerous occasions due to illness. At the end of her probationary period she was adjudged to have taken excessive sick leave and she was sent for a further medical examination on 14 October 1977. The purpose of this examination was to determine the reasons for this excessive sick leave and to decide whether her permanent employment should be confirmed. The examining Doctor, Dr Genn, was apparently satisfied that the medical certificates furnished by the applicant justified her absences from work and passed her fit to continue in her employment. The applicant's probationary period was extended for a further six months. At the conclusion of this further period the applicant was made a permanent officer with retrospective effect to the day upon which she commenced her duties.

8. Unhappily, the applicant's poor health continued and she was frequently absent from work. She was required to undergo a further medical examination by Dr Wong, a CMO. This medical examination took place on 24 November 1978. Dr Wong's evidence was that Miss Watts' sick leave records showed that the absences were predominantly due to migraine headaches, abdominal pain and vomiting. Being unable to detect an organic cause of such symptoms, Dr Wong concluded that the applicant's complaints were psychologically based, and he diagnosed the applicant's condition as neurasthenia. From the evidence of Dr Wong and of Dr James (to whom we refer later) it appears that the word 'neurasthenia' is a rather antiquated and unspecific medical term. It would not, in our view, be unfair to call it a 'catch-all' diagnosis for various nervous disorders. Dr Wong quoted us the definition contained in the International Classification of Diseases as:

'Neurotic disorder characterized by fatigue, irritability, headache, depression, insomnia, difficulty in concentration, and lack of capacity for enjoyment. It may follow or accompany an infection or exhaustion or arise from continued emotional stress.'

Acting on this diagnosis, Dr Wong recommended the applicant for retirement. Despite the fact that the ultimate recommendation of the Department of Health was for redeployment (of which more later) the applicant was in fact retired.

9. Because the applicant had been a contributor for less than 20 years the Commissioner following his 'standard practice' (Exhibit T5, p.26) requested the CMO Melbourne to investigate the possibility of the applicant having failed to furnish full information. Miss Watts was approached for an authority to divulge medical records from the Doctors who had treated her. Having gained this authority Dr Wong concluded in a memorandum to the Commissioner dated 17 March 1980 (Exhibit T5, p.33):

'In my opinion, there is enough evidence from the medical report from Dr. B. James practice (since taken over by a Dr. V. Lim, who made the report) that relevant and significant information was withheld if the original Form S6/PSB9A contained no declarations from Miss Watts regarding headaches (migrainous or otherwise), emotional lability, weakness, dizziness, and accident or injury.'

10. Acting on this advice the Commissioner issued, pursuant to s.16(11), a Benefit Classification Certificate specifying the following:

'History of emotional lability, History of headaches (Migrainous and otherwise), Dizziness.'

In addition it was concluded that neurasthenia, the ground for the applicant's retirement, was caused or substantially contributed to by the conditions specified in the benefit classification certificate. This course of action was confirmed as appropriate by Dr B.J. Hackett, the Senior CMO in Melbourne. It is these two decisions which the applicant seeks to have reviewed.

11. The questions before the Tribunal are: Did the applicant furnish false information, or fail to furnish information required to be furnished to the CMO in her initial medical examination? If so, was the true state of her medical history such as would have led to the issue of a benefit classification certificate specifying the physical or mental conditions which caused or substantially contributed to the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement on the ground of invalidity, or a condition connected therewith?

12. We now have to consider, with the benefit of hindsight, the applicant's medical history prior to her medical examination in April 1977. That history indicates that the applicant did not reveal the whole of that history, which may be set out as follows. Save as expressly mentioned, the consultations referred to were held with Dr James, the applicant's general practitioner.

(i) In 1972 she complained of headaches, thought at the time to be due to an eye defect and a degree of sinusitis.

(ii) In October 1974 she was involved in a car accident and was treated in hospital and her head, neck, ribs and left knee X-rayed.

(iii)In December 1974 and January 1975 she attended complaining of headaches and neck pains, which Dr James considered normal following a car accident.

(iv) In September 1975 she attended complaining of stomach pains.

(v) In October 1975, after a couple of further visits, she had an appendicectomy.

(vi) In February 1976 she attended complaining of headaches and dizzy turns. These were thought to be 'migraine-like' attacks (although she was not so informed) and she was treated accordingly. There was also some enlargement of the thyroid gland but this was found to be normal.

(vii)In April 1976 she went to the doctor at the University which she was then attending. She said that she was feeling rundown, that she thought she had a virus and that she was having difficulty swallowing. The doctor sent her to Dr P. Taft, whom she saw the next day. In evidence, Dr Taft said that the referring doctor in turn had said that the symptoms of which she had complained were a lack of drive, of some shakiness, of some emotional lability, of a consciousness of swallowing not being as easy as normal and of a feeling that she had a swelling in the neck. We would doubt that the word 'lability' was her own, but it is no doubt an accurate summation of the symptoms which she had described. To Dr Taft the applicant used the words 'lacking in drive, shakiness, being more emotional than usual, experiencing difficulty in swallowing and a swelling in the neck'. Dr Taft's investigations concluded that there was no thyroid abnormality and he appears to have been satisfied that the applicant's symptoms were all functional rather than organic. They were, he said, common physical manifestations of an anxiety state.

(viii) In December 1976 the applicant saw her general practitioner in relation to a minor chest complaint.

13. In the Reasons for Decision being handed down today in Re G.A.J. Hare and the Commissioner for Superannuation (No. V80/5) the Tribunal has discussed the extent of the duty of disclosure that rested upon an applicant undergoing a medical examination for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act"). Much of what was there said is relevant in the present application and is here repeated. The statutory provisions contained in the 1976 Act, which are set out above, are clearer than was the case in relation to the 1922 Act, where the duty of disclosure had effectively to be implied from but one provision, namely that portion of s.5 of the 1922 Act which prohibited contribution to the Superannuation Fund under Part III of that Act or to the Provident Account unless the person in question had undergone a medical examination. But it is still necessary to discuss some general aspects of the principles involved.

14. In attempting to assess the nature and extent of the obligation of disclosure that rested on the applicant in the present context, we were referred to statements contained in text books as to the duty of disclosure in relation to contracts of life insurance. The principles that have been evolved in relation to life insurance that are relevant in the present context are those relating to disclosure of material facts. They are clearly summarised in Ivamy, Personal Accident, Life and other Insurances (1973) p.73:

'As in other branches of insurance the insured is under a duty to disclose to the insurer all material facts and not make any material misrepresentations.

If the insured fails in such a duty, the insurer can avoid liability.

If, however, there is a 'basis clause' in the proposal form, the insurer may avoid liability even if the fact which is not disclosed or which is misrepresented is not a material one."

A 'basis' clause is one whereby the accuracy of the answers given by a person who makes a proposal is made the basis of the contract, as to which see Babatsikos v. Car Owners' Mutual Insurance Co Ltd 1970 VR 297. But the matter is not as simple as it may seem at first sight. The principles developed in relation to life insurance are principles based upon the law of contract, for the person who has made the proposal and the insurer who accepts the proposal enter into a contract of insurance, normally evidenced by a policy. The lack of a contractual basis to the relationship in question at least renders inapplicable those rules which there apply in cases where there is a 'basis' clause. And the rules as to materiality must also be approached with care. Subject to some additional comments that we make later, the rules as to materiality are rules that have been evolved as part of the law of contract. We do not see how it can be said that the rights conferred and obligations imposed on a person who became affected by the provisions of the 1922 Act or of the 1976 Act can be said to arise out of contract. They are conferred and imposed, respectively, by statute. To the extent that any duty of disclosure can be identified, it must be identified by implication from the provisions of the statute. And in the case of the present applicant that means the 1976 Act.

15. Section 16(2) of the 1976 Act requires a person to undergo such medical examination as the Commissioner requires, and it is in reliance upon such an examination, and such other matters as he considers relevant, that the Commissioner forms the opinion that is requisite if he is, pursuant to s.16(4), to issue a benefit classification certificate. The provisions of s.16(11)(d) make it clear, at least by implication, that there is an obligation to furnish information because s.16(11)(d)(i) speaks of information 'required' to be furnished by him. Likewise, if it is necessary to be said, the same provision attaches consequences to the furnishing of false information.

16. As we see it, a medical examination does not consist merely of a physical examination. Blakiston's Gould Medical Dictionary, 4th ed. gives as the first meaning of 'examination' the words: 'Investigation or inspection for the purpose of diagnosis'. The notion of investigation embraces, in our opinion, the asking of such questions as the examining doctor considers relevant for the purpose of the examination, which in this case is not so much for the purpose of diagnosis as of a general examination for the purpose of considering whether the person is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the medical report, to continue to be an eligible employee until he or she attains his or her maximum retiring age. When such questions were asked, they demanded full and fair answers. Should an applicant be in doubt as to whether some possibly relevant fact should be disclosed, we think it plain that the doubt has to be, and has always to be, resolved in favour of disclosure. An applicant is seeking a valuable right, no doubt a right involving mutual rights and obligations, but one which, while it would involve his own contributions, could also ultimately involve the interests of the Australian taxpaying community. The answering of the questions demands, and at the relevant time in this case demanded, the utmost good faith, and in this sense it is true that some analogy may be drawn with the principles applicable to contracts of life insurance. The words of Viscount Dunedin in Glicksman v. Lancashire and General Assurance Co 1927 AC 139 at p.143 are not, for the reasons already stated, directly relevant, but they may be adopted as a useful exposition for present purposes:

'There is the duty of no concealment of any consideration which would affect the mind of the ordinary prudent man in accepting the risk.'

17. A point to note in the circumstances is that it may be difficult to spell out any duty of disclosure beyond the confines set by the questions asked. In insurance cases it is said that the form of questions asked may render the proposer's duty more strict, in that it might direct his or her attention to the materiality of a fact or class of facts, but that it 'is more likely, however, that the questions asked will limit the duty of disclosure, in that, if questions are asked on particular subjects and the answers to them are warranted, it may be inferred that the insurer has waived his right to information, either on the same matters but outside the scope of the questions, or on kindred matters to the subject of the questions'; MacGillivray & Parkington on Insurance Law, 6th ed., 1975, paragraph 736. In this respect it is appropriate to note that there would appear to be a number of deficiencies in the questions asked in the form that was presented to the applicant. Some of the questions seek disclosure of symptoms, e.g. 'recurrent pains in the chest'. Others seek diagnoses, e.g. 'goitre or thyroid disease'. We consider that it would have at least been desirable to ask a question such as 'Have you lost any time from work, or have you consulted a doctor, because of any illness or injury?" The question could be related to a specified period of time. It would give the examining doctor the opportunity to have enquiries made, if necessary, and would not be dependant upon the examinee's recollection of symptoms or conveyed diagnoses, or indeed upon some self-diagnosis, perhaps the most unreliable touchstone of all. These comments are particularly apposite, we suggest, in the difficult area of nervous disorder, wherein there is so much difficulty of definition.

18. We conclude that at a medical examination conducted pursuant to s.16(2) of the 1976 Act for the purpose of a report being made for the purposes of s.16(3) of the same Act of the result of the medical examination, the prospective employee was obliged to answer all questions put to him fully and truthfully having regard to the knowledge which might fairly have been attributed to him of his then present condition and his past medical history. In this context we would however adopt as apposite for present purposes what Fletcher Moulton LJ said in Joel v. Law Union and Crown Insurance Company 1908

2 KB 863 at pp.884-5:

'But in my opinion there is a point here which often is not sufficiently kept in mind. The duty is a duty to disclose, and you cannot disclose what you do not know. The obligation to disclose, therefore, necessarily depends on the knowledge you possess. I must not be misunderstood. Your opinion of the materiality of that knowledge is of no moment. If a reasonable man would have recognized that it was material to disclose the knowledge in question, it is no excuse that you did not recognize it to be so. But the question always is, Was the knowledge you possessed such that you ought to have disclosed it? Let me take an example. I will suppose that a man has, as is the case with most of us, occasionally had a headache. It may be that a particular one of those headaches would have told a brain specialist of hidden mischief. But to the man it was an ordinary headache undistinguishable from the rest. Now no reasonable man would deem it material to tell an insurance company of all the casual headaches he had had in his life, and, if he knew no more as to this particular headache than that it was an ordinary casual headache, there would be no breach of his duty towards the insurance company in not disclosing it. He possessed no knowledge that it was incumbent to him to disclose, because he knew of nothing which a reasonable man would deem material or of a character to influence the insurers in their action. It was what he did not know which would have been of that character, but he cannot be held liable for non-disclosure in respect of facts which he did not know.'

What Fletcher Moulton LJ said above is entirely consistent with one of the conclusions that should be drawn from s.16(11), namely that what has to be revealed is something that has come to the mind of the person. It must be information. A person may be informed by his doctor that he has diabetes, and he then possesses that information. The person may have a very sore toe. He is informed of that fact by reason of the pain that is registered. But one way or another the pain has come to his notice. It is something 'of which (the person) is apprised or told' (see Shorter Oxford Dictionary). The section clearly requires this. This disposes of any question of the duty of disclosure being absolute, with the consequence that if it should be later found that there was some pre-existing condition that had not previously manifested itself at all, then nevertheless the provisions of s.184(5) would operate to permit the issue of a benefit classification certificate accordingly. It seems clear to us that if there were such an absolute duty it would have been expected that the legislation would have taken an entirely different form, but in any event our conclusion is based simply upon the meaning of the word 'information'.

19. In what has gone before we have discussed the question of the source of the obligation to make full disclosure. On one view the cases on insurance law may have gone beyond the ordinary rules of mistake and misrepresentation that apply in the law of contract. The rules are commonly stated as an application of the principle that, a contract of insurance being a contract that involves uberrima fides, full disclosure is required of such material facts as are known to the assured. As Lord Mansfield said in a famous passage in Carter v. Boehm (1766) 3 Burr 1905 at 1909: 'Insurance is a contract upon speculation. The special facts, upon which the contingent chance is to be computed, lie more commonly in the knowledge of the insured only: the underwriter trusts to his representation, and proceeds upon confidence that he does not keep back any circumstance in his knowledge, to mislead the underwriter into a belief that the circumstance does not exist, and to induce him to estimate the risque as if it did not exist. The keeping back such a circumstance is a fraud, and therefore the policy is void. Although the suppression should happen through mistake, without any fraudulent intention; yet still the underwriter is deceived, and the policy is void; because the risque run is really different from the risque understood and intended to be run at the time of the agreement ... The governing principle is applicable to all contracts and dealings. Good faith forbids either party by concealing what he privately knows, to draw the other into a bargain, from his ignorance of that fact, and his believing the contrary..."

20. These considerations have led us to consider the basis of the doctrine of uberrima fides. The Courts have evolved rules of conduct in this area which are commonly expressed as contractual rules, but the basis of them may deserve further identification. In a previous edition of Cheshire & Fifoot's Law of Contract, namely the 4th ed., 1956, the learned authors confessed to some difficulty in deciding at what stage contracts uberrimae fidei should be discussed, but considered that 'they seem to fall most naturally under the general heading of fraud. They are cases in which one party requires confidence in the other, and a misuse of that confidence may properly be regarded as a further example of constructive fraud.' Constructive fraud, as the authors point out, was an equitable doctrine to be distinguished from the term fraud at common law. It 'embraces cases in which there has been no dishonesty. It is directed against conduct which, though free perhaps from moral turpitude or from actual evil design, may tend to the deception of third persons, or to the abuse of a confidential relationship, or to the injury of the public interest.' Reference is then made to Story, Equity Jurisprudence, 2nd English ed., s.216. We would refer also to s.218 of that work which states as follows:

'But by far the most comprehensive class of cases of undue concealment arises from some peculiar relation, or fiduciary character between the parties. Among this class of cases are to be found

those which arise from the relation of client and solicitor, principal and agent, principal and surety, landlord and tenant, parent and child, guardian and ward, ancestor and heir, husband and wife, trustee and cestui que trust, executors or administrators and creditors, legatees or distributees, appointer and appointee under powers, and partner and part-owners. In these, and the like cases, the law, in order to prevent undue advantage, from the unlimited confidence, affection, or sense of duty, which the relation naturally creates, requires the utmost degree of good faith (uberrima fides) in all transactions between the parties. If there is any misrepresentation, or any concealment of a material fact, or any just suspicion or artifice or undue influence, courts of equity will interpose, and pronounce the transaction void, and, as far as possible, restore the parties to their original rights.'

21. We do not rely upon the point for the purpose of coming to our decision as to how the relevant duty of disclosure should be formulated, but from the foregoing it appears that there are grounds for asserting that a duty of disclosure may be capable of being discerned from the nature of the relationship existing between two parties to a transaction, that relationship not necessarily being of a contractual nature. If this is so, the relationship between an applicant in relation to the Superannuation Fund and those who have responsibility for the administration of the Fund would in our opinion be enough to enable the discernment from that relationship of the duty of disclosure which we have decided is to be implied from the terms of the relevant legislation.

22. We now turn to the matters in respect of which it may be suggested that the disclosures made by the applicant at her medical examination may have been less than sufficient, bearing in mind that it is not any failure to furnish information, or any furnishing of false information, that entitles the Commissioner to issue a benefit classification certificate, but only such failure to furnish, or such furnishing of false information, as would have led to a benefit classification certificate being in force in respect of the person (see s.16(11)(d)(ii)), that is to say by operation of s.16(4).

23. First, it may be suggested that the headaches which subsequently gave the applicant much trouble, although not necessarily connected with her neurasthenia, may have been so connected, and that she answered 'No' to a question about headaches although as indicated above she had had headaches prior to being medically examined in February 1977. The history of headaches must be divided into at least two parts. Prior to 1976 there is at least a clear possibility that her headaches were all associated with some other cause, such as eye defects or the car accident. If these had been all, and had been disclosed, we could not conclude that a benefit classification certificate would have been in force, nor could it be assumed, had a benefit classification certificate been in force specifying headaches, that that condition would have had any relation to the neurasthenia that constituted the incapacity that became the ground for retirement. Are, however, the headaches which occurred in February and April 1976, in combination with other symptoms, a different matter? In February the applicant complained to Dr James of headaches and 'dizzy turns'. The difficulty in relation to headaches is again in the form of the question asked, which enquired as to 'persistent' headaches. All in all we do not consider that the applicant can be held to have made inadequate disclosure in relation to headaches.

24. In April the applicant complained to the University doctor and to Dr Taft of the various matters set out in paragraph 13(vii) above. She complained of shakiness, of being lacking in drive, of being more emotional than usual (referred to by the University doctor as 'emotional lability'), and of swallowing difficulties and of a swollen neck. Dr Taft thought that these symptoms, as they presented to him, were functional in origin. The complaint made in February 1976 of 'dizzy turns' is also highly relevant. We consider that in view of the failure of the applicant to disclose these matters the following answers given by her were deficient in the terms of the standards which we have enunciated:

(i) The answer 'No' to the question relating to 'nervous fatigue'. We consider that while neither the applicant nor her various doctors may have used this term, a question in this form related quite clearly to the problems of which the applicant complained in April 1976.

(ii) The answer 'No' to the question relating to 'dizziness or turns', even though in her evidence the applicant said that the dizziness was confined to a single episode. 'Dizziness' was a complaint specifically made to Dr James in February 1976.

(iii) The answer 'Nov 75' to the question 'Date you last received medical or surgical treatment and for what condition?'. In this regard we are clearly of the opinion that the applicant should have disclosed her visits to doctors in February, April and December 1976.

25. As to the events of April 1976 she said that the applicant did not regard herself as having had medical treatment. This is we consider a good example of what we have said as to the extent of the duty placed upon the person being examined. Where the question of disclosure or non-disclosure is a nice one, seemingly demanding a close analysis of the words used in the question to determine whether a particular matter should be revealed, a situation has arrived in which the doubt should be resolved in favour of disclosure. This much at least the duty of utmost good faith demands. In our opinion that duty was not here discharged.

26. There being no doubt that the applicant's answers were inadequate, the question remains whether, had the applicant furnished information as to her condition in February and April 1976 we should be satisfied that a benefit classification certificate specifying some form of nervous disorder would have been in force.

27. There is undoubtedly a cogent case to be made for the applicant that such a certificate would not have issued. The events of February and April 1976 could be seen as only part of a number of circumstances that had affected her physical and mental condition. She had had an appendicectomy in October 1975 and had been subjected to the strains of University examinations with which the tribunal is entitled to feel very familiar and to which we refer again below. There are grounds for concluding from the evidence that had the matters in question been disclosed and reference made to her own medical practitioners the reports which they furnished would have been to the effect that her problems were associated with her operation and with the University situation. It is likely that Dr James would have denied any symptoms of nervous disorder or of neurasthenia, or that the headaches were persistent (see transcript pp.48 and 51A).

28. Despite these considerations however we have come to the conclusion that the decision of the Commissioner that a certificate would have issued was correct. We do not overlook the fact that the applicant's troubles in February and April 1976 may have been related to the strains of her situation at University. She had had to carry a subject over to a supplementary examination with the usual result that she had not had a proper summer vacation. She had apparently gone back to University in a jaded condition and a number of symptoms had appeared. That they were significant however is supported by her subsequent history. The neurasthenia that caused her eventual incapacity no doubt took a grosser form, but we consider that it had its clear parallel in the emotional lability, shakiness and dizziness that were manifested in February through April 1976, and which should in our opinion have been mentioned by the applicant in April 1977. The incapacity that was the ground for the applicant's retirement was caused by a condition represented by a number of symptoms, functional and not organic in nature, which may be loosely grouped together under the term neurasthenia. Emotional lability, shakiness and dizziness, the symptoms that presented in 1976 were, taken together, essentially a representation of the same condition. The question in our opinion is whether on the balance of probabilities the disclosure by the applicant of the 1976 symptoms would have led to their being specified in a benefit classification certificate. It may be that the conditions specified might have been expressed differently, so called 'nervous disorders' being an area notoriously hard to define in medical terms. But however expressed they would have embraced the symptoms mentioned and, as it turned out, would have been a reflection of her later history. There is of course a strong element of speculation in the enquiry, not least because the enquiry presupposes a situation in which the applicant was accepted as a contributor but in respect of whom a benefit classification certificate may or may not have issued, whereas if in fact the applicant had made the disclosures which we have found were requisite deferment or even rejection might have been considered. We consider that we must accept as an assumption what in fact occurred, namely that the applicant was accepted. So approaching the matter, the question is whether a certificate specifying the conditions under discussion would have issued, and the test to be applied can only be based on the balance of probabilities. Whether disclosure would have led to further enquiries, or would have led directly to the specification of such conditions in a benefit classification certificate, we are in fact satisfied on the balance of probabilities that such specification would have followed. We are equally satisfied that the applicant had evinced in 1976 a predisposition to display the symptomatology which was, in what we have described as a grosser form, ultimately her downfall in relation to the Commonwealth Public Service. In fact the case demonstrates in a fairly classic form the need for the statutory provisions in question.

29. We do not mean to be critical of the applicant's honesty or veracity in any way. She presented to us as a pleasant person with a good mind and an attractive manner. We do not suggest that she furnished false information. We consider that she had persuaded herself that she did not have to furnish the information that it seems to us that she should have furnished, but she did not appreciate that in the circumstances it was her duty, when in doubt, to disclose. Indeed, another difficulty which we have with the questionnaire is that it did not, at least in the form then in use, convey to the person required to answer it the character or extent of the duty of disclosure, or the serious consequences of a non-disclosure.

30. There remain several matters of concern to the Tribunal regarding the applicant's retirement. The first and most serious of these is the fact that on the applicant's evidence (which the Tribunal sees no reason to reject) the applicant was given to believe that what was in fact her retirement was a temporary period of 12 months convalescence after which time she could return to work, given an improvement in her health. When she presented to the personnel section to arrange this she was given forms to complete which actually had the effect of retiring her from the Public Service. During the twelve months which followed the applicant laboured under the misapprehensions first that she would be able to return to work, and secondly that she would be entitled to Superannuation payments for that period even though she believed she had not retired. No doubt those concerned were somewhat exasperated by the applicant's poor attendance record, but it is a matter of concern to the Tribunal that such a drastic measure as retirement could be visited upon her without the consequences being explained to her in the clearest of terms.

31. Another allied matter is the fact that after the applicant was examined by Dr Wong, although he recommended that she be retired on grounds of invalidity he also recommended that she 'may in due course, partially or completely recover and become fit for some form of Commonwealth employment' (document T5, p.23). More importantly, Dr McGuinness, the Senior CMO who made the Department of Health's ultimate recommendation recommended that 'In view of her age ... she be given a trial in another position'. Despite this recommendation no such redeployment appears, on the face of the documents before the Tribunal, to have been contemplated.

32. These problems underlie the ironies that emerge from our consideration of the results of any decision made by the Tribunal. Neither option open to the Tribunal, that is the affirmation on the one hand, or the setting aside on the other, of the decision of the respondent, would appear to be in the best interests of a person of the applicant's age and apparent abilities. The two possible outcomes of the Tribunal's decision are that the applicant may, subject to re-examination of her fitness, live on a pension from the age of 25 onwards, or be forced into a tight labour market having been retired from the Commonwealth Public Service on health grounds which in itself might detract from her employability. It seems to us that in all the circumstances redeployment as recommended may well have been the preferable course, especially as the applicant appeared quite healthy and self assured at the hearing and gave evidence of being in much better health. Be that as it may however, the Tribunal's jurisdiction does not extend to making such a recommendation.

33. A further irony is that if the Tribunal were to set aside the respondent's decision the path would be clear for the applicant to recommence duties via the avenue of s.74 and s.75 of the Act. For the reasons stated however the Tribunal is unable to come to that conclusion. We wish to make it plain however that in affirming the decision of the Commissioner our decision is an affirmation of that decision in terms of the Superannuation Act. It does not represent an affirmation of what, at the personnel level, was in our opinion some very unsatisfactory administration of the employment rights of a human being.
