Cahill and Commissioner for Superannuation [1988] AATA 656 (16 September 1988)

Cahill and Commissioner for Superannuation [1988] AATA 656 (16 September 1988)

Last Updated: 3 July 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Superannuation - whether "final annual rate of salary" of applicant includes higher duties allowance (HDA) and workers compensation payments - whether HDA component of applicant's sick pay to be included in "notional salary" for purpose of determining applicant's final annual rate of salary - whether workers' compensation payments are "salary"

Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act 1971 ss.27, 45 and 52

Superannuation Act 1976 ss.3, 5, 46 and 47

Superannuation (Salary) Regulations (Statutory Rules 1978 No 282) regs 4, 5, 6, 12 and 13

Introduction

  1. This is an application for review of a decision of the respondent made on 23 January 1985, which was confirmed under sub-section 154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") on 14 November 1986, to the effect that the applicant's "final annual rate of salary", in terms of the definition in sub-section 3(1) of the Act, was $13,444.
  2. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged by the respondent pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and numbered T1 to T21, together with other documents lodged at the hearing, to some of which it will be necessary to refer. Evidence was given at the hearing by the applicant, who was represented by Mr Cavanough of counsel. The respondent was represented by Mr Eadie, of the Australian Government Retirement Benefits office.

The Facts

3. The facts in this matter are not in dispute, and are now set out as we find them to be, having considered the whole of the evidence. The respondent chose not to cross-examine Mr Cahill, and the Tribunal has no reason not to regard him as a witness of truth. He was horn at Lilydale on 1 December 1960, and his family moved to Shepparton in about 1973. He was one of six brothers. His father has been an invalid pensioner for a number of years and his mother died in 1979. As a result of his mother's long illness, the family was in financial difficulties, and although Mr Cahill qualified for university entrance in 1978 it was financially necessary for him to enter the work-force.

  1. After working for a period as a clerk in a local business, and running a scrap metal business of his own, Mr Cahill was appointed a base grade class 1 clerk in the Australian Public Service in May 1980. He took up a position in the correspondence section of the Department of Veterans' Affairs ("the Department").
  2. After some 12 months he was appointed to a position ("the acting position") which had been advertised in the following terms:

"TEMPORARY TRANSFER : CLERK CLASS 2/3, $12,272-13,807, POS. NO. 840, ENTITLEMENT SECTION

Applications are invited from staff for temporary transfer to the above vacancy for the period 4 May 1981 to 3 August 1981.

DUTIES: (Summary Writer) - Prepare summaries of evidence for use by Repatriation Review Tribunals in considering applications for review."

Although the acting position was described as available only until 3 August 1981, Mr Cahill in fact continued after that date to carry out the duties of that position and to receive a salary including a higher duties allowance ("HDA") so that he was paid at the rate applicable to the acting position until 7 January 1982, the commencement of the period of absence referred to in paragraph 7 below. The following extracts from his examination-in-chief set out his understanding of his status in respect of the acting position, and the manner in which he arrived at that understanding:

"And did you continue to carry out those duties beyond August 1981? ---Yes, I did.

And do you remember on what basis you continued to carry out those duties beyond August 1981? ---The same basis that I always carried them out on. That was my job, and that was it.

Yes. That is what you were directed to do? ---Yes.

Yes. And are you aware - - -? ---The question was never - never came up, that, you know, it is August now you are due to finish this job. And the question was never raised with me at any time that, you know, there is a set tenancy for this job.

Yes? And as of this date, or some other date, you will be required to go back to other duties.

Yes? The issue was just never raised.

Yes. Well, how did you regard yourself in terms of time in relation to that job? You know, what was the nature of your appointment as you saw it at the time? --- It was an indefinite temporary transfer---

An indefinite temporary transfer? --- which is department-speak for a non-classified position that you know is going to always be there, and that is going to be your job. It just has not been classified by establishment section yet, and therefore it cannot be gazetted.

All right? ---But in the meantime, you are going to be in that job, and that is it.

Yes. Now, are you aware that something was done to formalise the occupancy of that job position? Yes, I was. The position was I am not sure when, I think it might have been around about October of - what year are we talking about?

1981, I believe?---1981. The job was advertised as coming up for gazettal.

Yes? --- And I did not even notice it at first, but then I realised the position number was the same as the position number I was acting in.

Yes? --- And it caused some concern to me, and I said to my boss I was - - -

What was the name of your boss? ---Karen Strickland. I said, ‘What's the go here? They are advertising my job?', and she said, 'It's just establishments. It's coming up. It's going to be gazetted'.

Yes? --- And I said, 'Well, you know, are they trying to get rid of me, or what?', and she said, 'No, it's all right. If you don't actually get your own position number you just get another job, and you're right there. But it's a matter that this is one job that will come up. It's come up in an establishments review and it is going to be classified, so they've got to call for applicants for the job'

Yes? So she said I had to re-apply for the job, too."

And later Mr Cahill said:

"I knew I had security at the 2-3 level at the Department of Veterans' Affairs. All the indications that were ever given to me there were: all right, this job is up, it is advertised, but if you do not actually get this position number because someone else has got seniority, or whatever reason, you know, there is heaps of other acting 2-3 jobs, we will just slot you into one of them. There was - the issue of you are going to be demoted, was never raised."

6. Mr Cahill said that he became concerned about certain aspects of the Department's dealings with veterans, and this concern preyed on his mind. He became a compulsive eater, and his weight went from 13 to 18 stone. (In 1988, at the hearing before the Tribunal, he presented with a slim build appropriate to his age and height.) In late 1981 he had to take time off because of the illness and suicide of one of his brothers, who was a paranoid schizophrenic. On his return he continued to find the work distressing, especially as he was occasionally required to deal with files relating to schizophrenic veterans. His work output was not good. He had been offered a transfer to a base grade position in another department, but he did not accept it, because he saw himself as having security in the Department at class 2/3.

  1. He saw an officer of the Public Service Board who had told him he would try to arrange a transfer at class 2/3; and he said that he would accept a base grade transfer if it was located at Shepparton, where he could live with his family. After an incident at work which he found particularly distressing, his doctor told him he should not go back to work at the Department, and he did not return after 6 January 1982. From that date he was on sick leave without pay.
  2. He asked the personnel office for the appropriate forms to apply for compensation and superannuation, and was under the impression that he had received and completed them, but it transpired later that he was not given the appropriate superannuation documentation, and it was not until 27 October 1983 that he finally applied for superannuation benefits. He returned to Shepparton, where he still lives with his father and two of his brothers, and purchased a house. On the basis of a report by Dr Freed, a psychiatrist, he was retired from the Public Service with effect from 6 October 1983 pursuant to section 19 of the Commonwealth Employees (Redeployment and Retirement) Act 1979.
  3. On 18 March 1983 a delegate of the Commissioner for Employees' Compensation determined that Mr Cahill was entitled to compensation under the Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act 1971 ("the Compensation Act") in respect of aggravation of a pre-existing disorder. No copy of that determination was in the papers before the Tribunal, and the Tribunal in this respect relies on a statement in the respondent's Reasons for Decision. A subsequent determination made on 3 May 1983 set out the rates at which compensation was payable to Mr Cahill from 7 January 1982. Those rates were expressed to be calculated pursuant to section 45 of the Compensation Act which section provides for compensation for total incapacity. Clearly Mr Cahill had been found to be totally incapacitated for work in terms of the Compensation Act.
  4. In respect of the first 26 weeks of his absence from work, i.e. from 7 January 1982 to 7 July 1982, Mr Cahill was paid at the rate of $14,250 per annum, pursuant to sub-section 45(2A) of the Compensation Act, i.e. at the rate of the salary which he had been receiving immediately before 7 January 1982 in respect of the acting position. From 8 July 1982 to 22 February 1983, compensation was paid at the fixed rate prescribed by sub-section 45(2) of the Compensation Act, which provision does not (relevantly) relate to previous salary. That rate was, as appears from both the regulations made under the Compensation Act and the relevant determination, $119.30 per week before 1 November 1982 and $129.45 per week thereafter. (The respective annual rates derived from those prescribed figures are $6203.60 and $6731.40, not $6223.50 and $6753, the amounts which appear on the agreed chronology which was Exhibit 1.)
  5. For reasons which were not explained to the Tribunal, Mr Cahill was paid sick pay in respect of the four weeks from 12 May 1983 to 8 June 1983. He received two weeks of full pay and two weeks of half pay, calculated at a rate of $16,080, which was the appropriate rate of salary (including HDA), payable on and after 12 August 1982, in respect of the acting position. It is to be assumed that those four weeks constituted his remaining entitlement to sick pay. The sick pay was the only payment which he received from the Commonwealth after the termination of compensation on 22 February 1983. Both the compensation and the sick pay were paid out of the budget of the Department of Veterans' Affairs.
  6. With effect from 6 October 1983 the relevant annual rate for a base grade clerk was increased to $13,444, and for a clerk class 2/3 to $16,771.
  7. Following the late application for superannuation benefits referred to in paragraph 8 supra, Mr Cahill was paid a pension pursuant to the Act with effect from 7 October 1983. He applied for backdating of that pension to January 1982 and received payment of what was described in a letter from the respondent dated 26 March 1985 as "a compensatory allowance under sub-section 90(3) of the Public Service Act" which was said to be calculated on the basis of "pension which would have been payable from 2 July 1982 to 6 October 1983". That expression was not further explained in that letter or in other materials before the Tribunal. It may have been intended to mean "pension which would have been payable had you lodged an application for superannuation benefits immediately on retirement". In the course of considering that application, questions arose as to the rate at which Mr Cahill's pension should be paid, which led to the making of the decision under review, namely, a decision that his "final annual rate of salary" in terms of the definition in sub-section 3(1) of the Act was $13,444, being his appropriate rate as a base grade clerk.

The legislation

17. The effect of other definitions in sub-section 3(1) of the Act, not here extracted, is that 6 October 1983 was Mr Cahill's "last day of service", which is defined as the day on which he ceased to be an "eligible employee", which is defined, in his case, as a "permanent employee", which is relevantly defined. The "anniversary of his birth last preceding his last day of service" was 1 December 1982. His "final basic contribution" was the contribution payable by him under the Act on the fortnightly contribution day last preceding 6 October 1983. The amount of that contribution was calculated, pursuant to section 46 of the Act, on the basis of the salary payable to him, or deemed by section 47 to have been payable to him, on 1 December 1982.

The issues

18. By virtue of section 47 of the Act, if on 1 December 1982 Mr Cahill's annual rate of salary was less than the highest annual rate of salary payable to him on any day during the previous 12 months, then his annual rate of salary on 1 December 1982 is deemed, for the purposes of section 46, to be that highest rate. (That provision is subject to any effect of his having made an election under sub-section 47(2), which it is not suggested that he did.) If a rate was deemed by section 47 to apply for the purposes of section 46, and that rate was higher than the rate ascertained according to paragraph (a) of sub-section 3(1), then his "final annual rate of salary" would fall to be determined under paragraph (b) of the definition in sub-section 3(1), rather than paragraph (a).

  1. Similarly, by virtue of sub-paragraph (c)(i) of the definition of "final annual rate of salary" in sub-section 3(1), if at any time after 1 December 1982 Mr Cahill's annual rate of salary was higher than that ascertained according to paragraph (a) or paragraph (b) of the definition, then that rate becomes his "final annual rate of salary". (On the basis that he made no election under section 47 of the Act, sub-paragraph (c)(ii) has no application to this matter.)
  2. It thus becomes necessary to consider three different periods in which to ascertain Mr Cahill's "final annual rate of salary" in respect of each of the three paragraphs of the definition:

paragraph (a): the period is a single day, namely his last day of service, 6 October 1983;

paragraph (b): the period from 1 December

1981 to 1 December 1982;

paragraph (c): the period from 1 December

1982 to 6 October 1983.

His "final annual rate of salary" is, in effect, the highest rate of salary which he received in any of those periods. The matter falls to be determined in accordance with the Act and the Regulations. As the Full Court of the Federal Court pointed out in Eastman v Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 74 ALR 221 at 229:

"Reading the Act and the regulations together, the following scheme emerges:

(a) if a payment falls within the ordinary concept of salary it will be regarded as salary for our purposes unless excluded under the regulations;

(b) If a payment does not fall within the concept of salary within ordinary concepts it will be regarded as salary for present purposes only if so treated under the regulations."

"Final annual rate of salary" : paragraph (a)

  1. Paragraph (a) of the definition of "final annual rate of salary" looks to the "annual rate of salary on the last day of service" of the employee, that day being, in Mr Cahill's case, 6 October 1983. On that date, it appears that he was on leave of absence without pay. Accordingly, he was not receiving any payment which fell within "the ordinary concept of salary" and it becomes necessary to look to the Regulations. Regulation 13 of the Regulations provides that, where an employee is on leave of absence without pay, his annual rate of salary "on a particular day during the period of leave of absence" is to be the amount per annum of his "notional salary" on that day; in this case, 6 October 1983.
  2. Regulation 12(b)(i) defines the "relevant day" for the purpose of the ascertainment of "notional salary" as "the day ... immediately preceding the day on which the leave of absence commenced". The "leave of absence" there referred to must be the period of leave of absence without pay to which regulations 12(b) and 13(3) relate. The period of leave of absence without pay in which 6 October 1983 falls, commenced on 9 June 1983, the day after the completion of the period of sick leave with pay which is described in paragraph 11 supra. "The day ... immediately preceding the day on which the leave of absence commenced" was thus 8 June 1983, which becomes the "relevant day" for the purposes of Regulation 12(b).
  3. The formula for the ascertainment of "notional salary" in Regulation 12(b) distinguishes between the "actual annual rate of salary" of the employee, on the relevant day, the "actual salary" of the employee on that day and the "salary" of the employee on that day. "Salary" and "annual rate of salary" are defined, by reference to the Regulations, in section 5 of the Act. The intended meaning of "actual" is nowhere defined.
  4. That being so, it is to be assumed that the use of the word "actual" is intended to distinguish between the artificial constructs "salary" and "annual rate of salary", deriving from section 5 and the Regulations, on the one hand; and the amount or rate at which the moneys actually being paid to the employee on the day before the commencement of the leave without pay were actually being paid or calculated, on the other. The former amount or rate would, by virtue of the Act and Regulations, exclude any allowance excluded by the operation of Regulations 4, 5 and 6 (as to which see paragraphs 26ff. infra); the latter amount or rate would include them. It is difficult to attribute any other effect to the repeated use of the word "actual". We note that Regulation 12(b) was amended by the Superannuation (Salary) Regulations (Amendment) 1986 with effect from 15 April 1986, and that in the amended Regulations the word "actual" does not appear. It is not in issue that this matter should be decided in terms of the relevant legislation as it existed at the date of Mr Cahill's retirement (as to which see section 50 of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901). There is no material before us from which we could draw any conclusion as to the reason for the amendment or as to any relevance which it might have to the interpretation of the Regulations in the form in which we are concerned. On 8 June 1983, Mr Cahill's "actual annual rate of salary" (in respect of which he was being paid sick pay at half rates) was $16,080, including the HDA.
  5. At this point it is necessary to turn to the effect of section 5 and Regulations 4, 5 and 6 on the calculation of the artificial constructs "salary" and "annual rate of salary" so far as the HDA component is concerned.

Whether HDA component is "salary"

26. By virtue of regulations 4(a) and 5(1) the HDA would be treated as salary if "payable on a regular basis" in terms of regulation 6(1). As the Full Court of the Federal Court said in Eastman (supra) at 230:

"It is always a question of fact whether a higher duties allowance is payable with a sufficient degree of regularity and uniformity to justify its description as salary in the ordinary sense. By singling out for treatment as salary allowances only if they are paid on a regular basis and by defining precisely what is 'regular', Parliament must be taken to be saying, and to have intended to say, that allowances not paid on such a regular basis are not to be treated as salary."

27. The HDA was paid to Mr Cahill "in respect of duties or work performed" by him, in terms of regulation 6(1), from 4 May 1981 to 7 January 1982, being a period of eight months only. That being so, he does not meet the requirements of paragraph (a) of that regulation. Mr Cavanough submitted that the period from 7 January 1982 to 7 July 1982, during which Mr Cahill received compensation pursuant to sub-section 45(2A) of the Compensation Act, added to the previous six months, gave a total period of 12 months for the purposes of Regulation 6(1)(a). However, it cannot be said that during the period when he was in receipt of compensation payments, Mr Cahill was performing "duties or work". Regulation 6(1)(a) requires that the "employee has performed for a continuous period of 12 months duties or work in respect of which. an allowance ... has been paid". For this, if for no other reason, that submission must fail.

28. A certificate prepared on what is apparently a standard printed form, dated 29 September 1983 and signed by a person described as a "Person Authorised by the Commissioner for Superannuation" was in the papers before the Tribunal, and read:

"(1) I certify that the abovenamed eligible employee has performed continuously for a period of twelve months and one day duties or work in respect of the performance of which an Allowance of a prescribed kind under Regulation 5(1) was payable to him."

An alternative provision appearing on the printed form, but crossed out, read as follows:

"(2) I am of the opinion that there is a likelihood that the abovenamed eligible employee will perform, for a total continuous period of not less than twelve months (including any period before the date of this certificate that is continuous with the period commencing on the date of this certificate) duties or work in respect of the performance of which an Allowance of a prescribed kind under Regulation 5(1) -

* has been, and will continue to be from the date of this certificate payable to him.

* will, from / / , be payable to him. "

The form was headed "Certificate in Respect of an Allowance" and drafted so that either one of provisions (1) and (2) would be deleted. Provision (1) is clearly intended to provide information in terms of paragraph (a) of Regulation 6(1), and provision (2) to provide a certificate in terms of paragraph (b) and Regulation 6(2).

29. It is clear from the evidence which has been set out above that the certificate, while apparently intended to provide proof of compliance by Mr Cahill with paragraph (a) of Regulation 6(1), is incorrect. Further, the printed form is inappropriately drafted. While the operation of paragraph (a) of Regulation 6(1) depends on a matter of ascertainable fact, which occurred in the past, the operation of paragraph (b) depends on a certificate of an opinion by an authorised person as to likely facts in the future. It is confusing and unsatisfactory for the two to be dealt with in the alternative on the one form. The person who signed the certificate may well have intended to assist Mr Cahill. Whatever the merits of that intention, the signature of the certificate was ineffective so far as paragraph (a) of Regulation 6(1), which turns on past facts, was concerned. The signing of the certificate did not render true, for the purposes of the Regulations, a statement which was untrue. Completion of the alternative provision, however, while it might have assisted Mr Cahill, would have involved a statement of an opinion which could not have been formed on the basis of the facts existing at the date of the certificate, 29 September 1983. By that date, Mr Cahill had not been performing duties as an employee of the Commonwealth for well over a year.

  1. It will be noted that regulation 6 does not provide for the granting of a retrospective certificate. Such a certificate might be to the effect that, at the date of retirement of the employee, the signatory had been of the opinion that, absent the cause of the retirement, there was a likelihood that the employee would have completed 12 months on higher duties.
  2. The Tribunal does not know whether the provisions of Regulation 6 are routinely brought to the attention of Commonwealth employees, being members of the superannuation scheme established under the Act, who are appointed to temporary positions entitling them to payment of HDA. The members of the Tribunal are well aware, of their own knowledge, that Mr Cahill's situation, described in paragraph 5 supra, is by no means unusual in the Australian Public Service. Many Commonwealth employees serve for long periods performing the duties of acting positions and receiving HDA accordingly. In a situation where an employee has every prospect of continuing for over a year to perform such duties, and receive the allowance, it seems manifestly unjust that the right to superannuation on that basis should depend on the arbitrary granting of a certificate which may only be granted prospectively and cannot be granted retrospectively.
  3. The Tribunal would strongly recommend that the regulation be amended to provide for the giving of a retrospective certificate of the kind described above. In the absence of such an amendment, the Tribunal would, equally strongly, recommend that, if it is not currently the case, every Commonwealth employee appointed to such a position, should on such appointment, be formally and expressly notified of the provisions of regulation 6 in order that, in appropriate circumstances, the employee, if a member of the superannuation scheme established under the Act, may request, from the appropriate authorised person, a certificate in terms of the regulation. Whether that person would be prepared to give such a certificate is another matter.
  4. On the evidence, Mr Cahill does not comply with either paragraph of Regulation 6(1). Accordingly, his HDA is not to be taken to have been "payable on a regular basis" in terms of Regulation 5(1) and accordingly, by virtue of Regulation 5 and sub-section 5(1) of the Act, is not included in his "salary" for the purposes of the Act. And it follows, by virtue of sub-section 5(2) of the Act, that the HDA is not included in his "annual rate of salary" for the purposes of the Act.
  5. However, sub-section 5(2) is expressed to be subject to sub-section 5(3) permitting the annual rate of salary to be specified by the Regulations. As explained in paragraph 22 supra, Regulation 13(3) and Regulation 12(b) prescribe a "notional salary" to constitute the "annual rate of salary" of an eligible employee on leave without pay. Mr Cahill was on leave without pay on 6 October 1983. It should be noted that in Eastman (supra) the Court was not concerned with Regulations 12 and 13.
  6. At this point we are in a position, after the necessary digression contained in paragraphs 25 to 33 supra, to ascertain Mr Cahill's "notional salary" on 6 October 1983, the amount per annum of which becomes his "annual rate of salary" on that date.
  7. This is most readily achieved by preparing a paraphrase of Regulation 12(b) incorporating the relevant information, now ascertained or ascertainable, which relates to Mr Cahill. The paraphrase reads:

Mr Cahill's 'notional salary' on 6 October 1983 is the salary (as defined in the Act and Regulations) that would be payable to him on 6 October 1983 if -

(i) on 8 June 1983 he had commenced to perform duties or work in respect of which the salary (as defined in the Act and Regulations) was payable at an annual rate equal to $16,080, the actual annual rate at which his sick pay was being calculated on 8 June 1983; and

(ii) he continued until 6 October 1983 to perform those duties or work.

However, his notional salary does not include an allowance (except for an allowance which he was actually receiving on 8 June 1983) that would be included in the salary (as defined in the Act and Regulations) on 6 October 1983 in respect of those duties or work.

37. It does appear from the foregoing that in the calculation prescribed by Regulation 12(b) the actual overrides the artificial: and the HDA, which was included in the calculation of the sick pay which Mr Cahill was receiving on 8 June 1983 must be included in his "notional salary", and thus in his "annual rate of salary" by virtue of Regulation 13(3) as at 6 October 1983. We would refer to the decision of Dixon CJ, Kitto and Taylor JJ in Graham v Baker [1961] HCA 48; (1961) 106 CLR 340, to the effect that payments of sick pay to which the plaintiff was entitled under an industrial agreement should be taken into account in assessing damages for personal injuries payable to a plaintiff who was compulsorily retired because of such injuries: on the basis that the sick pay "constituted wages in every sense of the word".

38. We have not been unmindful of the fact that on 8 June 1983 Mr Cahill was receiving sick pay at half rates. That is not, in our view, a matter which should affect the calculation with which we are presently concerned. The incidental distribution, over four weeks, of half and full rates of sick pay is a mere accident, which should not affect a decision of such significance as the determination of "final annual rate of salary".

39. We find therefore, that Mr Cahill's "annual rate of salary" on 6 October 1983, the date to be considered under paragraph (a) of the definition of "final annual rate of salary" in sub-section 3(1) of the Act, was the annual rate applicable to the acting position, including the HDA. On that date the relevant rate for a clerk class 2/3 was $16,771. That is the highest annual rate of salary which it has been suggested that Mr Cahill was receiving, actually or notionally, at any relevant time, and accordingly that finding should conclude this matter (see paragraph 20 supra). However, in view of the complexity of the matter, and the detailed manner in which it was argued for both parties, it seems to us appropriate that we consider the periods which are relevant to paragraphs (b) and (c) of the definition.

"Final annual rate of salary": Paragraph (b)

40. The relevant period for the purposes of paragraph (b) of the definition of "final annual rate of salary" in sub-section 3(1) of the Act is the period from 1 December 1981 to 1 December 1982. For the reasons summarised in paragraph 33 supra, the HDA which Mr Cahill received while performing duties from 1 December to 6 January 1982 is not included in his "salary" for the purposes of the Act. From 7 January 1982 onwards, he was receiving payments under the Compensation Act, which from 7 January 1982 to 7 July 1982 were calculated according to sub-section 45(2A) of that Act, at the rate of $14,250, which was then the rate applicable to the acting position. From 8 July to 1 December 1982, he was paid at the considerably lower rate prescribed by sub-section 45(2) of the Compensation Act. Thus, if payments under the Compensation Act are "salary" for the purposes of the Act, then Mr Cahill's highest annual rate of salary in the period from 1 December 1981 to 1 December 1982 was $14,250.

Whether compensation payments are "salary"

  1. The Tribunal was referred to a number of authorities on the meaning of "salary". In Commissioner for Superannuation v Carpenter [1983] FCA 148; (1983) 48 ALR 230 the Full Court of the Federal Court found that "salary and wages" in sub-section 5(1) "covers everything within the ordinary meaning of salary and wages" (page 232; and see page 238). The annual rate of salary was to be determined, according to sub-section 5(2) "by examining the employee's terms of employment as at a given day and calculating the amount of salary he would have earned in the following year on the assumption that no changes occurred in those terms of employment" (Bowen CJ and Woodward J at page 234). In that case the salary of the respondent was prescribed by an industrial award.
  2. In Commissioner for Government Transport v Kesby [1972] HCA 64; (1972) 127 CLR 374 Gibbs J (as he then was) with whom Menzies, Walsh and Mason JJ agreed, said at page 388:

"The question of what is the salary in a particular case is purely one of fact. It does not simply depend on the terminology used to describe the amount payable ... a payment referred to as a 'shift penalty' or as 'overtime' could be said to constitute part of the 'salary' of bus drivers if in fact it was an amount payable to bus drivers generally as remuneration for the services that bus drivers generally are regularly required to perform."

  1. We have already referred in a different context to the decision of Dixon CJ, Kitto and Taylor JJ in Graham v Baker [1961] HCA 48; (1961) 106 CLR 340, to the effect that payments of sick pay to which the plaintiff was entitled under an industrial agreement should be taken into account in assessing damages for personal injuries payable to a plaintiff who was compulsory retired because of such injuries: on the basis that the sick pay "constituted wages in every sense of the word". In that case, at page 345, their Honours referred to authorities which established "that wages, in every sense of the word may become payable according to the terms of a contract of employment even though the employee is not called upon to, and does not, in fact, render service".
  2. It thus becomes necessary to consider whether the provisions of the Compensation Act form part of the terms of employment of an employee of the Commonwealth. Sub-section 27(1) of that Act reads:

"27.(1) If personal injury arising out of or in the course of the employment of an employee by the Commonwealth is caused to the employee, the Commonwealth is, subject to this Act, liable to pay compensation in respect of that injury in accordance with this Act."

The relevant terms of employment in Carpenter derived from an industrial award: in Graham v Balcer from what was described as "an industrial agreement". In Carpenter at least, and possibly in Graham v Baker, those terms of employment may be assumed to derive their binding force from statute rather than from contract. The entitlements of a Commonwealth employee under the Compensation Act, deriving directly from statute, might seem, in principle, to be equally part of the terms of employment of that employee.

45. This appears to have been the view of Murphy J in T.C. Whittle Pty Ltd v T. & G. Mutual Life Society Ltd (1977) 18 ALR 431, briefly expressed at page 443. His Honour said:

"In my opinion the workers' compensation benefits were also subject to rise and fall. Although they arose under statute, they were a condition of employment in the sense of the provisions."

Barwick CJ, with whom Mason J agreed said (at page 442):

"The change in the amount of compensation payable under the Workers Compensation Act raises quite different problems to those attendant on the other listed changes. Granted my interpretation of the expression 'conditions of employment', the changes in the Workers Compensation Act do not, in my opinion, qualify as changes in the conditions of employment of any of the classifications of employees used in the computation of the average wage. They are, of course, changes in the conditions which are consequential upon the employment of workers: but the obligation to pay workers' compensation does not, in my opinion, form part of the contract of employment. ... In my opinion, the increase of the amounts of compensation under the Workers Compensation Act is not a change in conditions of employment within the operation of the clause." (Emphasis added.)

However that conclusion depends, as the emphasised passage indicates, on an interpretation of the meaning of the expression "conditions of employment" in a particular clause in a particular building contract, the meaning of which was in issue in that case. At page 438 His Honour said:

"The expression 'conditions of employment' is capable, in my opinion, of two possible meanings, apart from the particular context of the clause. It could mean the conditions dictated by statute or award which are consequential upon the employment of a workman: the conditions under which employment may take place. If that meaning were adopted, the consequential condition need not be a term express or implied of the contract of employment itself. On the other hand, the expression may include only the conditions on which, as between employer and employee, the employee works; in other words, the expression relates only to the terms of the contract of employment express or implied. When those terms are so largely regulated by industrial award, it is perhaps not so easy to regard them as part of the contract of employment: but, in my opinion, that is a proper use to be made of the terms of the industrial award, at least in so far as they fix wages and other employee benefits.

The clause, in my opinion, provides a context which settles the choice to be made between these possibilities. As I have pointed out, it is both central and fundamental to the understanding and operation of the clause that the average wage is a notional concept, its amount being constructed from the financial specifications of a series of industrial awards and of statutes regulating the terms of industrial employment. These are relevantly the conditions of employment current at the date of the closing of tenders. The relevant alterations in conditions of employment must, in my opinion, be of a like character. That means, in my opinion, the change to be allowed must be a change in the obligations of the employer towards the employee in the employment or a change in the entitlement of the employee to action by the employer in the employment."

Accordingly, His Honour's conclusion at page 442 is not necessarily of relevance to the question which we have to decide. Indeed, the precision of definition in that passage is such as to suggest that had His Honour considered that the first possible meaning of "conditions of employment" to which he referred at page 438 was the meaning intended by the building contract, his conclusion would have been that the provisions of the Workers Compensation Act were part of the relevant "conditions of employment".

46. It must not be overlooked that the terms of employment of employees of the Commonwealth, are, in any event, largely governed by statute, namely the Public Service Act 1922. We note that in Fowell v Ioannou (1982) 45 ALR 491, Woodward J at 509 said that a public service employee:

"was employed pursuant to a contract of employment, the basic terms of which were constituted by a code, made up of a body of legislative, regulatory and arbitrated provisions, having the Public Service Act at its heart."

47. The Compensation Act is concerned exclusively with employees who are employed by the Commonwealth or by a "prescribed authority of the Commonwealth" (see section 7 of the Compensation Act). "Prescribed authority of the Commonwealth" is defined in sub-section 5(1) of the Compensation Act as being necessarily either a body created by Commonwealth or Territory legislation or one in which the Commonwealth has a controlling interest. Thus there is a specificity in the application of that Act which would tend towards supporting the view that its terms are part of the conditions of employment for people who are employees of the Commonwealth or (effectively) of bodies under the control of the Commonwealth. Further, in this case the compensation payments, like the sick leave payments, which Mr Cahill received, were paid directly out of the budget of the Department in which he was employed, namely the Department of Veterans' Affairs, and not by an insurer or other independent body. Thus the position of Mr Cahill was significantly different in this regard from that of the employees in the private sector whose conditions of employment were considered by the High Court in T. & G. Whittle Pty Ltd.

  1. We note Mr Eadie's submission that the fact that the Compensation Act regulates the entitlements of persons who are not employed by the Commonwealth is an indication that that Act is not properly characterised as part of the conditions of employment of officers of the Public Service. We are unable to see any force in this argument. The Compensation Act affects many thousands of employees; the fact that it affects more than one employer does not appear to us to be significant in the present context. Further, if that fact were to be thought significant, the close relationship with the Commonwealth, described in the previous paragraph, of the other employers whose employees are protected by the Compensation Act, would appear to reduce its significance to a very substantial extent.
  2. On the other hand, Mr Eadie drew the attention of the Tribunal to the apparent distinction drawn between "compensation" and "salary" in sub-sections 52(1), (5), (9) and (10) (read as a whole) of the Compensation Act. However, we consider that that usage in the statute cannot be said to be decisive of a question which turns, as the Federal Court said in Carpenter, on "the ordinary meaning of salary and wages".
  3. On balance, and not without some hesitation, we have formed the view that the authorities to which we have referred lead to the conclusion that the payments received by Mr Cahill under the Compensation Act were paid in accordance with the conditions of his employment and are therefore "salary" for the purposes of section 5(1) of the Act and thus for the purposes of the ascertainment of his "final annual rate of salary" in terms of the definition in sub-section 3(1).
  4. Mr Eadie submitted that, should we reach that conclusion, we would then be required to consider whether the "HDA component" included in the compensation payments which Mr Cahill received from 7 January 1982 to 7 July 1982 forms part of his salary for those purposes.
  5. However, sub-sections 45(2A) and (2B), by which the compensation payments were calculated, make no reference to allowances. The amount payable in a week referred to in sub-section 45(2A), to an employee "employed by the Commonwealth under conditions of employment providing for sick leave payments" as Mr Cahill was, is, in effect, the amount of" the sick leave payment that he would receive in respect of that week if, during that week, he were entitled to, and were granted, sick leave on full pay". That definition, which appears in sub-section 45(2B), effectively describes the amount of Mr Cahill's full pay in the period from 7 January 1982 to 7 July 1982. It should be noted that when he received sick pay between 12 May 1983 and 8 June 1983, it was paid at the rate payable in respect of the acting position.
  6. We do not think that it is possible to build into sub-section 45(2B) of the Compensation Act a separation of the components by which the amount prescribed by that sub-section was calculated. Given that we have found that that amount, being a payment of compensation pursuant to the conditions of employment of the applicant, falls within the ordinary concept of salary, we do not consider that it is divisible in such a way that "any part" of it "under the regulations, is not to be treated as salary" in terms of sub-section 5(1) of the Act.
  7. Mr Cavanough drew the attention of the Tribunal to clause 20 of the Public Service Arbitrator's Determination No 32 of 1956, a copy of which was before the Tribunal. That clause relates to payment of allowances during paid leave (such as sick leave), and thus recognises that, for the purposes of payment during leave, an allowance is a separable component of the moneys received by the employee from the Commonwealth. As he pointed out, there is no similar recognition in the Compensation Act of an allowance as a separable component of payments calculated according to sub-section 45(2A). Section 45 contains no provision similar to sub-section 52(10).

55. Accordingly, we find that Mr Cahill's highest annual rate of salary in the period from 1 December 1981 to 1 December 1982 was $14,250, being the rate at which he was paid from 7 January 1982 to 7 July 1982, calculated at the rate appropriate to the acting position. "Final annual rate of salary" : Paragraph (c)

56. In the period from 1 December 1982 to 6 October 1983, Mr Cahill was paid compensation at an annual rate first of $6203.60 and later of $6731.40 (see paragraph 10 supra) until 22 February 1983. Thereafter he received no payment from the Commonwealth save for four weeks of sick pay. The highest rate at which he was paid in that period was $16,080, the full rate of his sick pay, which was calculated at the appropriate rate of salary in respect of the acting position. We have already referred twice to the decision in Graham v Baker, which establishes that sick pay is "salary" according to ordinary concepts, and is thus (see Carpenter) salary for the purposes of sub-section 5(1) of the Act. However, that sub-section, as has been said, is expressly subject to the Regulations. The effect of Regulations 4, 5 and 6 is that, for the reasons set out in paragraphs 26-29 and 33 supra, the HDA component of the sick pay, recognised as separable by the Public Service Arbitrator's Determination No 32 of 1956 which is referred to in paragraph 54 supra, does not form part of the "salary" constituted by the sick pay. Regulations 12 and 13 have no application to a period of leave with pay.

57. Accordingly, in the period from 1 December 1982 to 6 October 1983 (disregarding, for present purposes, the last day of that period, which in itself constitutes the relevant period for the purposes of paragraph (a)), Mr Cahill was not paid a higher rate of salary than in the periods referable to paragraphs (a) and (b) of the definition of "final annual rate of salary" in sub-section 3(1) of the Act. Paragraph (c) of that definition is thus not relevant to our decision.

Conclusion

58. For the reasons given, we find that Mr Cahill's "final annual rate of salary", in terms of the definition of that expression in sub-section 3(1) of the Act, is $16,771, ascertained in accordance with paragraph (a) of the definition. The decision under review will be set aside, and the matter remitted to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with an appropriate direction.
