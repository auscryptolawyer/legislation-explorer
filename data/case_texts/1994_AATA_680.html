Dickens and Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] AATA 680 (23 November 1994)

Last Updated: 22 September 2008

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE - Application for extention of time. Deliberate decision not to make an application to review at the time. Application now made following Federal Court decisions. Case not a strong one.

MR ALLEN: This is an application pursuant to subsection 29(7) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 to extend the time in which to make an application to review a decision by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation notified to the applicant on or about 13th day of May 1993. The said decision read:



I, Ronald Charge Whithear, delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation (the commissioner) under subsection 154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (the Act) confirm the decision taken by a delegate of the commissioner on 17 August 1990 under subsection 157(1) not to direct that the election made by Mrs Dickens on 9 January 1989 under subsection 137(1) be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that subsection of the Act.



The period allowed under subsection (1) section 137 of the said Superannuation Act 1976 is 21 days. Pursuant to subsection (2) of section 29 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, the applicant had 28 days from the day the decision of the delegate was notified to her to lodge an application for review with the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. Principles which will guide a court, or this tribunal, in exercising its discretion whether to extend time or not was set out in the well known case of Hunter Valley Developments v Cohen, [1984] FCA 176; 3 FCR 344. There is nothing in the later case of Comcare v Ahern [1993] FCA 498; 119 ALR 85 which detracts from these principles.



At the outset and as was pointed out in Hunter Valley Developments Supra, following Lucic v Nolan, 45 ALR 411 at 416:



It is a prima facie rule that proceedings commenced out of time will not be entertained. Further, although it is not a pre-condition to the exercise of the discretion to extend time an acceptable explanation for delay should be given, see Ahern Supra at page 88. It remains that such an explanation is a relevant matter to be considered.



In this matter, the application for extension of time states:



Prior to becoming aware of the decision of Commissioner for Superannuation v Robert Boardman, unreported, 17 June 1994 in the Federal Court, the applicant was of the opinion based on legal advice that her prospects of successfully pursuing the appeal were not good and the applicant was reluctant to expend a lot of time and money in prosecuting an appeal for that reason. The decision in Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman is now reported, see [1994] FCA 1159; 123 ALR 239. That case must be read in conjunction with Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation, [1994] FCA 1063; 19 AAR 450, a decision of the Full Court of the Federal Court.



dicon 23.11.94 2



It is clear from the application to extend time and the submissions made by the applicant’s solicitor, that the applicant took legal advice following the decision of the delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation dated 12 May 1993. Following that advice, she made a conscious decision not to seek further review by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. Now, pursuant to an application lodged on 20 September 1994, some 15 months out of time, and since becoming aware of a decision of the Federal Court handed down on 17 June 1994, she seeks the exercise of a discretion on her behalf.



To my mind, the matter is covered by the decision of his Honour, McHugh J, in Gallow v Dawson, [1990] HCA 30; 93 ALR 479. At page 480, His Honour said:



The grant of an extension of time under this rule is not automatic. The object of the rule is to ensure that those rules which fix times for doing Acts do not become instruments of injustice. The discretion to extend time is given for the sole purpose of enabling the court, or justice, to do justice between the parties. This means that the discretion can only be exercised in favour of an applicant upon proof that strict compliance with the rules will work an injustice upon the applicant.



In order to determine whether the rules will work an injustice, it is necessary to have regard to the history of the proceedings, the conduct of the parties, the nature of the litigation and the consequences for the parties of the grant or refusal of the application for extension of time, see Avery v No. 2 Public Service Appeal Board 1973, 2 New Zealand Law Reports, 86 at 92. Jess v Scott, 12 FCR 187 at 1945. When the application is for an extension of time in which to file an appeal, it is always necessary to consider the prospects of the applicant succeeding in the appeal, see Burns v Gregg (1967), VLR 871 at 872, Hughes v National Trustees Executors and Agency Company of Australia Limited (1978) VLR 257 at 2634. Michelson v Michelson, 24 ALR 522 at 524.



It is also necessary to bear in mind in such an application that upon the expiry of time for appealing, the respondent has a vested right to retain the judgment unless the application is granted. Deleneous v Heinegar, 36 ALJR 2000 at 201.



It follows that before the applicant can succeed in this application, there must be material upon which I can be satisfied that to refuse the application would constitute an injustice. As the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council pointed out in Ratnam v Kumarasami (1965) 1 Weekly Law Reports 8 at 12:



dicon 23.11.94 3



The rules of court must prima facie be obeyed and in order to justify a court in extending the time during which some step in procedure requires to be taken, there must be some material upon which the court can exercise its discretion.



In the present case, the applicant sought to explain her delay in seeking an extension of time by claiming that her decision to appeal depended on:



The careful assessment of the possibilities of my claim which needed reference, research involving a lot of work and time as it would to a non professional.



The claim was made without furnishing any details as to the time spent in research, or the nature of the research upon which the applicant was engaged, or when it was that she decided to appeal. Significantly, the applicant did not assert that she had failed to lodge her appeal because she was unaware of the 21 day time limit for lodging a notice of appeal. Indeed, the terms of her affidavit suggest that she made a conscious decision not to appeal until she had satisfied herself that she could succeed in an appeal.



I doubt that I would have considered the applicant’s explanation for the delay to be a sufficient reason for enlarging the time for lodging her appeal from the 21 days required by the rules to more than 16 months which would be required if this application were to succeed even if I had thought that the applicant had real prospects of succeeding in her proposed appeal.



A case would need to be exceptional before a court would enlarge by many months the time for lodging an appeal simply because the applicant had refrained from appealing until he or she had researched the issues involved. In Hughes, McInerney J, pointed out at 263:



One object affixing time under court rules is to achieve a time table for the conduct of litigation in order to achieve finality of judicial determinations. When the time for appealing has expired, the litigation is at an end. The successful party is entitled to the benefit of the judgment in his or her favour. At that stage, the successful party has a vested right to retain the judgment. It would make a mockery of order 70 rule 3 if months after the time for appealing has expired, the unsuccessful party could obtain an extension of time on the ground that he or she had delayed appealing because that person wanted to research the issues involved. Lack of legal knowledge is a misfortune, not a privilege.



I would only add in this case that the matter is even stronger, in that the applicant took legal advice and as stated above, as a result of that legal



dicon 4



advice made a conscious decision not to take the matter further. It is clear from the passage cited that the litigant who being aware of their rights to seek further review, i.e, their appeal rights, but does not act within time due to deliberate choice bears a heavy onus in seeking any review authority to exercise discretion to extend time in their favour. I would add that in this matter, the respondent commissioner is entitled following the failure of an applicant to make an application for review within time to regard the mater as closed and adjust his records and books of account accordingly.



There is no evidence that the applicant continued to make the respondent aware that she contested the finality of the decision, compare Hunter Valley Developments Supra at page 349. In the course of considering my decision, I perused the determination of the commissioner’s delegate of 12 May 1994. I agree with the submission by Mr Whithear for the respondent that the applicant’s case is now no better or not worse than it would have been in June 1993. The question of prejudice to the respondent was raised, but I find relative to this application there has been no prejudice to the respondent, that is, the period of 15 months has not occasioned prejudice sufficient of itself to deny the application to extend time, except to the extent referred to above relating to the finality of litigation and the fact that the respondent was entitled to treat matters at an end.



The period between the resignation and the re-employment and application to make a late election, may well have led to prejudice in the presentation of the respondent’s case in the sense discussed by his Honour, Von Doussa J, in Winshuttle v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 93 Australian Tax Cases 4992 at 5003, but that would be a matter to be taken into account in weighing the evidence in a substantive hearing, if any. That having been said, the strength or otherwise of the applicant’s case is a relevant matter.



Having perused the impugned decision I can only say that the applicant’s case is not a strong one and in many ways does not differ from several other cases in this tribunal determined after the decisions in Boardman v Clarke Supra and in which applicants have not been successful. In other words, I do not regard the applicant as having real prospects of succeeding in her proposed application to review. Taking all the above factors into account, I find that the discretion to extend time should not be exercised in the applicant’s favour and that will be the decision of the tribunal.
