ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Allowable Deductions - rent on lease of broodmare to produce foal with a view to sale at a profit. Whether expenses deductible.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - S.51(1).

Browne v. Dunn (1893) 6 R.67.

Applicant COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

No. NT86/9663-67

AAT Decision No 4877

Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

20 January 1989

Sydney

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT86/9663-67

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re :

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

DECISION

Tribunal : Peter M. Roach. (Senior Member)

Date : 20 January 1989

Place : Sydney

Decision : The Tribunal decides to vary the determinations of the Commissioner upon the objections of the Applicant under review and to reduce the taxable income of the Applicant by $21,360; $14,302; $10,524; $6,549; and $7,273 for the years of income ended 30 June 1978, 1979, 1980, 1983 and 1984 respectively.

(Sgd.) PETER M. ROACH,

Senior Member.

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) Nos. NT86/9663-67

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re :

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

REASONS FOR DECISION

20 January 1989 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. The Applicant in these references is a medical practitioner. I shall refer to him as "Dr. Jim". He has been a medical practitioner for some thirty years and, for the past twenty years of that period, he has practised as a specialist in private practice in a major city. As will appear from the figures to be referred to hereafter, in the eight years to 1985 he did not derive from professional practice the high level of income supposedly derived by all medical practitioners. From his late teens he had a recreational interest in the "Sport of Kings": thoroughbred horseracing. During 1987 one of his professional colleagues brought to his attention publicity material published by a horse stud. The stud was one of many which at the time were promoting the idea that investors could profit from leasing broodmares to breed their own progeny with a view to selling the foals at a profit at yearling sales. The promoters would provide fully for the maintenance of the mares and their foals throughout the term of the lease - for a price. That price would enable the promoters to profit from their ownership of horses, whether stallions or broodmares, and from the provision of their services as stud masters and their resources for agistment.

  1. Dr. Jim responded favourably to the promotions. Having some professional understanding of the principles of genetics and having done some reading in relation to horse breeding - not sufficient on the evidence before me to have established him with any significant measure of expertise - he participated in the selection of two mares, one already in foal to a reputable stallion and the other proposed to be so mated to another stallion shortly.
  2. On 21 July 1977 Dr. Jim entered into an agreement in writing to lease two broodmares, Ml already in foal and M2 yet to be served for a period from 1 July 1977 to 30 June 1978 or until the birth of the broodmare's foal (whichever shall be the later) or until such later date as the parties may agree. The lessor was to remain owner of the mares but was to manage them and use his best endeavours to ensure delivery of live foals. The lessor was to be generally responsible for the management and care of the mare for the term of the lease and for her progeny "until it attains the age of eighteen months or is in the reasonable opinion of the lessor capable of being submitted for sale at any recognized commercial market or by private treaty whichever time shall first occur".
  3. The mare Ml dropped her foal in September 1977. As at that point I accept that it was the intention of Dr. Jim to sell her at the yearling sales in April 1979. I also accept that, at that point of time, his aim was, despite the acknowledged risks, that she would he sold at a profit. Twelve months later he was receiving promising reports from the stud, such as one which quoted "our bloodstock consultant" as having furnished a report:-

"A big filly with good strong legs and 'racehorse'

written all over her."

The consultant concluded that she was a

"'Good-legged filly with a big future well capable of

being in the class of the horses of the same breeding lines which had been successful."

However the sale did not eventuate in that way. It is probable that she was offered for sale at the yearling sales but with a reserve price set such as was not likely to attract a buyer. Early in the year of income ended 30 June 1980 Dr. Jim sold filly Ml to his wife for $3,500. I accept that his wife had an interest in horses although what the nature of that interest was not explained. Nor was it explored in cross-examination. Nor was the reason for sale to Mrs. Jim explained. However, some indication of the quality of the horse can be had from the circumstances that, some few months after purchase, Mrs. Jim sold filly Ml to another commercial promoter of racehorses for $10,000.

5. In November 1978 M2 dropped her foal - a filly. I accept that in July 1977 Dr. Jim had intended an early sale of the promised foal from both Ml and M2. But I am not persuaded that that remained his intention after April 1979. He kept the filly M2 and raced her - unsuccessfully - from October 1980 to March 1982. She later dropped a colt (1983) and a filly (1984). Both were raced and neither was successful. Due to fertility-problems the M2 filly was not mated again.

  1. However, before those matters eventuated, in July 1978, Dr. Jim entered into a further lease to again lease the mare M2 then in foal under the previous lease, for a term of twelve months from 1 July 1978 "or until the birth of the broodmare's foal (whichever shall be the later)" on the basis that M2 would be served by a different sire. The entire "rent" of $12,000 was payable in advance upon the signing of the agreement. It seems that no-one was troubled by the circumstance that the preceding lease had not yet run its course. Although, in January 1979, a veterinarian had certified that as a result of a manual examination of pregnancy he was of opinion that the mare was then pregnant, on further examination in August 1979 she was found to be not in foal. A claim was made on behalf of the Applicant on his insurers for the insured sum of $3,000 and arrangements were made for a replacement foal to be provided. The Applicant selected the filly identified as "filly M3" which was foaled in October 1979. The filly was held until sold in the first half of the calendar year 1981, the proceeds of sale of $13,000 being returned by the Applicant as assessable income.
  2. Then by a further lease of July 1979, a new mare (M3) was leased. She dropped a filly in December 1980. She raced but with little success from April 1983 to November 1985. She then went to stud. Her first foal (1987) had to be put down following an accident; a second was nearly due at time of hearing (November 1988); and it was then proposed to mate her again.
  3. The Applicant's evidence was that, after his initial experience of yearling sales at which the market had been tested he decided that, before any further attempt to sell in that way, the horses should be proved by racing with a view to enhancing their market value. So it was that, after the sale of filly Ml to Mrs. Jim, filly M2 was raced during the period October 1980 to March 1982 but did not prove to be a success. After she dropped foals in the latter part of the 1983 and 1984 years she was not again put to the stud because of multiple fertility problems. She was given away to a property owner in the country. Then filly M3 was sold without being raced. By the latter part of the 1982 calendar year filly M4 was ready to be put to the test of racing and she was raced with a modest measure of success in the period April 1983 to November 1985. The evidence was that out of twenty-three starts she had 5 wins in proving races, 5 seconds, 5 thirds. However, the returns to the Applicant from this, the only "successful" racing programme, were very small: $900. Because of syndication with a friend, that represented a half of net proceeds of racing. That was returned as income, but it was not taxed as only the net loss for the year was disallowed.
  4. The income tax returns of Dr. Jim for the years of income ended 30 June 1978 to 30 June 1985 were placed in evidence but those for the later years of income were not. The omission to do so is a matter of some concern because, in the absence of returns, there was no evidence to advance to explain figures which appeared in a summary of horse breeding activities which indicated that, at 1 July 1987, Dr. Jim had only one horse on hand. Given that the original mares had been leased; that filly Ml and filly M3 had been sold; that filly M2 was given away; that filly 7 - the 1987 progeny of filly M4 - had been "put down"; that filly M4 was still on hand and about to foal; it would seem that the appropriate inference was that filly 5 and filly 6 were no longer on hand. An explanation for that consistent with the summary is that one of those two horses was sold, seemingly for $700, during the year of income ended 30 June 1987, and that the other constituted one of two losses by death which, according to the summary, were experienced in the year of income ended 30 June 1986. No evidence-in-chief was advanced to explain the matter. Nor was the question gone into in cross-examination.

10. Consideration of the documents which were placed in evidence raises many questions, but there was nothing in the cross-examination of Dr. Jim which persuaded me that his evidence was not truthful. Nor was his evidence so improbable that it is unacceptable in the sense of being so improbably true as to not carry conviction and persuade, whether the subject of cross-examination or not [cf. Browne v. Dunn (1893) 6 R.67]. In relation to horses, profit may be sought in many ways. Venturing moneys by betting and competing for prize money may not be considered to generate assessable income for the few who succeed, or allowable deductions for the many who fail. But it is not necessarily so with other investments. I instance as practices which may generate assessable income, the putting to stud of a stallion; the management and training of horses; and the breeding of horses with a view to sale at a profit.

  1. That being so, despite the many difficulties and the several apparent anomalies in what is before me, I am persuaded that his evidence is to be accepted. The essence of that evidence was that his purpose in entering into the leasing programme in July 1977 was to breed his own horses with a view to sale at a profit; that initially it was not his intention to race the progeny at all; that such racing as was done was done to prove-up the market value of the progeny; and that, in continuing with the programme in later years, the intention of Dr. Jim remained to produce animals capable of being sold at a profit. I accept that his interest in racing was a pleasurable recreational interest; that he was an insignificant punter; that, although it was in his own words a "special thrill to watch his own horses racing", racing his own horses was not at any time an objective of his particular interest in or involvement in the breeding programme. I accept his evidence that he was prepared to syndicate his horses for racing purposes in order to confine the substantial expense involved - even though foregoing in part the prospect (such as it was) of racing for profit - at the same time preserving the advantage of those who had leased to"5 him: retaining ownership of the horse with the prospect of profit upon its sale.
  2. Essentially the argument for the Commissioner was that the nature and scale of the activities of Dr. Jim was too small and insignificant, and too lacking in commercial characteristics to be considered as a "business" and that, therefore, the expenses incurred were not to be allowed. In my view, despite finding some support in decided cases, both before the Taxation Boards of Review and this Tribunal for that approach, it is too narrow. Whether fairly described as a "business" or not, I am of opinion that if a taxpayer had sold the initial progeny in circumstances such as this at an overall profit and the evidence before me had persuaded me - as the evidence has persuaded me in this case - that the mares had been leased with a view to producing progeny to be reared until ready for sale at yearling sales or later and with a view to those sales being at a profit, I would have concluded that the profit was assessable and that, to the extent the expenses had not already been allowed, those expenses would be taken into account in assessing that profit. In this case it is not appropriate to use that particular method of calculating quantum of assessable income because, in his returns of income over the years, Dr. Jim has consistently brought his stock to account in livestock accounts. One result was that, in the year of income ended 30 June 1981, by reason of the sale of filly M3 at $13,000, Dr. Jim disclosed the derivation of a profit of $7,674 on his breeding activities for that year. Not surprisingly, as it was returned as assessable income, it was assessed as augmenting his taxable income.

THE JURISDICTION OF THE TRIBUNAL

13. Not all of the possible issues which need to be resolved between the Commissioner and the Tribunal are before the Tribunal for review. The first objection which is now before the Tribunal was one lodged in June 1979 to the Commissioner's disallowance of the claimed loss in the 1978 year. Objections to the similar disallowances for the 1979 and 1980 years were made in due course in August 1980 and May 1980. In October 1981 Dr. Jim lodged his return of income to 30 June 1981 disclosing (inter alia) a profit on Horse Breeding Account of $7,674. The notice of assessment relating to that year of income was not placed in evidence before me but, from endorsements of the Commissioner appearing on that return, it seems probable that the assessment issued on 7 October 1982. That happened to be the day upon which the Commissioner gave notice of his decision to disallow the objections in relation to the three earlier years. In the face of that, the prudent course for the then advisers to Dr. Jim to have followed would have been to have objected to the assessment of the 1981 profit as assessable income, but that was not done. It cannot be objected to now.

  1. In 1982 a further loss was claimed and disallowed by the Commissioner. I was informed that it was objected to but, upon the objections then disallowed, no action was taken to request that the disallowance be referred for independent review. No effective request can now be made.
  2. That mistake was not made in relation to the years of income ended 30 June 1983 and 1984. In both cases objections were lodged and, on the Commissioner deciding to disallow the objections, requests were made that the matters be referred for independent review. The latter such request was made on 7 January 1986 - over three years since the first request for independent review had been made and over six years since the original objection had been lodged. The Commissioner finally complied with those outstanding requests for reference on 30 October 1986 and the Tribunal was able to bring the matters to hearing during 1988.
  3. I was informed that, for reasons unknown, either no objections or no requests for reference had been made in relation to the Commissioner's action in disallowing claims to deductions in the years of income since that ended 30 June 1984. Dr. Jim was not cross-examined on the matter and I draw no inference against him on that account.
  4. It may be that Dr. Jim is a commercially astute and fiscally shrewd medical practitioner who found in this commercial breeding programme a way of gaining community financial support for his recreational pursuits. But, having heard his evidence-in-chief and the cross-examination, I am not persuaded that it is so. It is more probable that, like many of his fellows, he is financially unsophisticated and an investing optimist.
  5. Be that as it may I am persuaded, on the balance of probabilities, that the investment was directed to the generation of assessable income which in amount would be sufficient to generate a profit. That being so, I am satisfied that the requirements of the positive limbs of s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") are satisfied. The question which could then be considered is whether the outlays sought to be deducted involved expenditure "of capital, or of a capital nature" - whether the outlays should be considered not so much as the cost of leasing the mares, but rather as the price of the foals. No such contention was advanced in argument or considered in evidence. That being so, I consider that it is not appropriate that I should consider invoking such an argument against the Applicant.
  6. For the foregoing reasons I am satisfied that the Applicant is entitled to the deductions he has claimed in relation to horse-breeding activities. To give effect to that finding the Tribunal will direct that the taxable income of the Applicant be reduced by $21,360; $14,302; $10,524; $6,549; and $7,273 for the years of income ended 30 June 1978,1979,1980, 1983 and 1984 respectively.

20. In so finding, I record that:-

(a) I have taken into account as assessable income sums of $10, $10, $3,500 and $960 in the years of income ended 30 June 1978, 1979, 1980 and 1984;

(b) I have no jurisdiction in relation to the assessments for the years of income ended 30 June 1981, 1982, 1985 or any later year; and

(c) I note that, upon the view I have formed, the assessment of $13,000 as assessable income in the year of income ended 30 June 1981 was correct.
