Re Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v the Myer Emporium Limited [1985] FCA 376; 8 FCR 136 (8 October 1985)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

And: THE MYER EMPORIUM LIMITED

No. VG 86 of 1985

Income Tax

[1985] FCA 376; 8 FCR 136

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

VICTORIAN DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Fox(1), Lockhart(2) and Jenkinson(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income tax: loan made by company to subsidiary - assignment by company of right to receive interest from subsidiary - whether sum received in consideration of assignment constituted 'income' - whether profit-making scheme - whether assignment fell within s.260 Income Tax Assessment Act.

Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 s.260; sub-secn.25(1); para 26(a)

Commissioner of Taxation v. Phillips [1936] HCA 11; (1936) 55 C.L.R. 144 cons.

Commissioner of Inland Revenue v. Paget (1938) 2 K.B. 25 cons.

Shepherd v. The Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 70; (1965) 113 C.L.R. 385 cons.

W.P.Keighery Pty. Ltd. v. Commissioner of Taxation [1957] HCA 2; (1957) 100 C.L.R. 66 cons.

Newton v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1958) A.C. 450 cons.

Mullins v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1976) 135 C.L.R. 467 cons.

Cridland v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1977] HCA 61; (1977) 140 C.L.R. 330 cons.

Slutzkin v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 140 C.L.R. 314 cons.

Income Tax - Assessable income - Assignment of right to receive interest instalments - Profits of a business or realisation of a capital asset - Sale of property acquired for purposes of resale - Whether "profit" - Whether profit-making scheme - Arrangements void against Commissioner - Whether antecedent transaction - Whether ordinary business dealing - "Choice" principle - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), ss 25(1), 26(a), 260. Held: (1) That where a taxpayer assigned its right to receive instalments of interest falling due over the next seven years on a loan made by it a few days earlier, the sum received in consideration for the assignment was a capital receipt. So held because:

(a) the consideration was not interest or equivalent to, or in substitution

for, interest;

(b) the transaction was not one in the ordinary course of, or an incident

of, the carrying on of the taxpayer's business or course of dealing;

(c) (per Fox J): the contractual right to receive the interest
instalments

was an asset which did not form part of the taxpayer's stock in trade.

(2) No part of the consideration was a "profit" within the meaning of s

26(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. So held because:

(a) (per Fox J): the consideration was paid as a recompense for deprivation

of the interest over the period of the loan;

(b) (per Lockhart J): the consideration was the present value of the

taxpayer's rights to interest over the period of the loan, and there could

be no element of profit in that receipt;

(e) (per Jenkinson J): the cost to the taxpayer of what it sold included

the contractual obligation it incurred to lend the principal moneys for

seven years, and there was no evidence to suggest that the consideration

exceeded the value of that obligation.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Becker [1952] HCA 77; (1952) 87 CLR 456, applied.

(3) (per Fox and Lockhart JJ): There was no profit-making undertaking or

scheme within the meaning of s 26(a) of the Act.

(4) The assignment was not rendered void for tax purposes by s 260 of the Act because the loan agreement and the assignment did not involve a relevant alteration in the incidence of tax, there being no antecedent transaction for which the loan agreement and/or assignment could be said to have been substituted.

Mullens v. Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) [1976] HCA 47; (1976) 135 CLR 290; Slutzkin v. Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) (1977) 140 CLR 314, applied.

(5) (per Fox J): The transaction was outside the scope of s 260 for the further reasons that:

(a) it was capable of being explained by reference to "ordinary business

dealings", (b) the taxpayer entered into a scheme which made use of the
choice offered

by s 80 of the Act, in that the scheme was possible only because

the assignee was able to offset the receipt of the assigned interest

against unrecouped prior years' losses.

Newton v. Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) (1958) AC 450; W P Keighery Pty Ltd

v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1957] HCA 2; (1957) 100 CLR 66, applied.

DECISION

This is an appeal from a decision of the Victorian Supreme Court, constituted by Murphy J., in which he allowed an appeal by the present respondent from an income tax assessment for the year ending 30 June 1981.

2. The income brought to tax, the assessability of which is in question in these proceedings, was $45,370,000. This was the amount received by the taxpayer, The Myer Emporium Limited (Myer), in the relevant tax year under a deed of assignment dated 9 March 1981 in which it assigned to Citicorp Canberra Pty.Limited (Citicorp) its right to receive interest payments under a loan agreement dated 6 March 1981 made with a subsidiary, Margosa Limited (later to be renamed Myer Finance Limited).

3. The capital amount of the loan was $80,000,000. The loan itself was not assigned and was re-payable to Myer after a period of 7 1/4 years. The selection of a period in excess of 7 years avoided the possible application, in relation to the assignment, of s.102B of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 ("the Act"). The assignment has been treated on both sides as a valid legal assignment of a legal chose in action, although so to treat it is inconsistent with the argument, to which I refer later, that it should be regarded as an assignment of "moneys as they fall due". Notice of the assignment was duly given to Margosa. The assignee has the right to claim interest directly, in its own name.

5. It appears that in the tax year in question Myer, which was the holding company for over fifty subsidiaries, reorganised much of the business structure of the group. A principal reason for doing this was a proposed change in the taxation laws affecting unit trusts. The essence of the intended change was stated by the Treasurer on 11 July 1980 as follows:

"The Government has therefore decided that all income of

any public unit trust established after 11 July, 1980 as

a result of a re-organisation of a company group will be

subject to tax in the hands of trustees at the company

tax rate."

6. A proposal concerning the changes to be made in the Myer Group was presented in a long and detailed report by Hill Samuel Limited, merchant bankers, which was submitted in August 1980, and was accompanied by reports from Arthur Anderson & Co., chartered accountants, and Freehill Hollingdale & Page, solicitors. An opinion by counsel dealing, among other matters, with the issue now in question was attached to the latter report. So far as concerns the present matter, the scheme suggested in the report was, in substance, carried out. Margosa was a shelf company registered in Canberra, which was acquired for the purposes of the scheme.

7. The sum of $80 million resulted for the most part from the sale by Myer of shares in subsidiaries to a newly formed company, Myer Shopping Centres Limited, which for group efficiency purposes was to own all the real estate of the Myer Group. An additional amount, of about $7.6m. was raised by Myer to make up the $80m. There were doubtless a series of book entries and possibly some exchanges of cheques but the money for the purchase by Myer Shopping Centres from Myer was obtained, so the evidence indicates, by the former getting a short-term bank loan. 9. The cheques to be drawn by Margosa amounted in all to $80 million. The cheques drawn in favour of Myer amounted to the sum to be paid out by it to Myer Shopping Centres Pty.Ltd.

10. Prior to the re-arrangement Myer had itself acted in the role of a financier for the Group, by raising outside finance when necessary. It still does so to some extent (presumably for Myer Finance Limited), but loans to subsidiaries are now made by Myer Finance. Myer was and is a public company with its shares traded on the stock exchanges. It had been in the ordinary course of its business to lend moneys to subsidiaries. Neither Myer nor any of its subsidiaries has made any assignment of the type effected by the deed, other than the one in question.

11. The total amount of interest payable under the deed, apart from the initial payment of $82,192, was $72,500,000, and $45,370,000 represented a discounting of this amount, taking half yearly rests, at 16 per cent. The discount rate, as for the assignment arrangements in general, was a matter of commercial negotiation. It was argued at one stage that the amount of $45.37m. was a prepayment of interest, but this is not so in any sense. Under the loan agreement, the borrower has an option of early payment, and it is said (applying a formula in the Schedule) that if it had exercised the option immediately after the assignment, the amount payable would have been $45.37m. The option was not exercised, and, as I have said, the discount rate used in calculating the amount of $45.37m. was arrived at by negotiation, between parties at arms length.

12. The Commissioner claims tax on the whole $45,370,000, and has not made an alternative claim, except that on one footing, namely that if s.260 of the Act is applied, he submits, as an alternative, that $5 million is assessable. The bases upon which his case is put are (a) that the amount was income according to ordinary concepts and as such comes within sub-secn.25(1) of the Act, (b) that it was assessable income under para.26(a) of the Act (as it then stood) and (c) that s.260 applies to make the receipt taxable. The onus of negativing liability is of course on the taxpayer.

13. It is not submitted that the principal amount of the loan was other than a capital asset.

14. In relation to the claim that the amount in question was income according to ordinary concepts, it is submitted that it represents the sale price of property acquired for re-sale, and, it is added, in the course of trade or business. This way of putting the matter would not necessarily make the receipt one of income under sub-secn.25(1), but it is in any event a tortured description. What was "acquired" was the future periodical produce of a loan, and it was created rather than "acquired". What was sold was a present right to the whole of the produce, - a different commodity. It is idle to talk of the whole receipt as a profit, and no less amount has been identified.

15. It is put that while remaining lender and entitled in due course to repayment of the amount lent, Myer received part of the interest on the loan. However, what was assigned was not interest (as it became due), but the right to receive interest under and in accordance with the loan agreement. This is not simply a verbal distinction. To use an analogy, there is in the law of torts an important distinction between loss of earnings, and loss of earning capacity. Moreover, a lump sum containing an element of damages for loss of earnings over a period is not regarded as taxable (see, Atlas Tiles Limited v. Briers [1978] HCA 37; (1978) 144 CLR 202; Cullen v. Trappell [1980] HCA 10; (1980) 146 CLR 1). The amount of interest payable over the entire term was discounted, or from the taxpayer's point of view, capitalised. The sum received was not an aggregate of interest to fall due (see Commissioner of Taxation v. Phillips [1936] HCA 11; (1936) 55 CLR 144 especially per Dixon and Evatt JJ. at p 156). That the assignee was merely receiving a right was evident to it, and it insisted on guarantees from the related companies. Citicorp was not going to risk a position in which the ability of the debtor to pay was reduced, or nullified, and it was left without recourse to guarantors. In this connection it can be mentioned that while all the ordinary shares in Myer Finance are held by Myer, the preference shares are "widely held".

16. It is not correct to treat the sum received as being in substitution for interest, because it has its own independent character. It did not derive from some accidental or extraordinary circumstance, but a positive commercial dealing with a third party.

17. It does not necessarily follow that because the assignment was of a right to future interest the consideration received was not income. The assignor might, for example, have carried on a business of conducting that type of operation.

18. That such an assignment, for a lump sum, is in general productive of a non-income receipt is in accordance with principle, as illustrated by many cases. We were referred, in particular, to Commissioner of Inland Revenue v. Paget (1938) 2 KB 25. There the taxpayer had sold coupons for interest on government bonds, and the proceeds were held not to be taxable, under Case IV of Schedule D or Schedule C of the English legislation. At p.35 Sir Wilfrid Greene M.R. said:

"The purchase price received by Miss Paget was not

income arising from the bonds at all."

19. At pp.44, 45 Lord Romer said:

"In these circumstances, the only question to be decided

is whether the proceeds of sale of a right to receive

income in the future can be treated as income for the

purpose of the Income Tax Acts. The question thus

broadly stated plainly admits of but one answer; and

that answer must be in the negative. The proceeds of

the sale for a lump sum of an annuity, for instance, are

capital in the hands of the vendor and not income. And

this is true even when the subject of the sale is not

the annuity for its whole duration but the right to be

paid the annuity for a number of years or even for one

year."

20. Having in mind other observations in their Lordship's judgments it may be that the decision (so far as now relevant) should be regarded as turning to some extent on the English legislation and I do not for present purposes place particular reliance upon it. Some of the interest had already fallen due, but there had been default, and an offer by the issuers of compromise arrangements. In Thomson v. Moyse (1961) AC 967 at p 997 Lord Radcliffe without disapproval stated his view of the relevant part of the case:

"The decision was that the sum so received by her was

not her income at all for the purposes of the Income Tax

Acts."

21. Part of the joint judgment of Dixon and Evatt JJ. in Phillips' case which I have already referred to is relevant and I now set it out:

"It is true that to treat a sum of money as income

because it is computed or measured by reference to loss

of future income is an erroneous method of reasoning

(cf. Californian Oil Products Ltd. (in Liquidation) v.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1934) 52 C.L.R., at

pp 46,49,51.; Van den Berghs Ltd. v. Clark (1935) AC

at p 442). It is erroneous because, for example, the

right to future income may be an asset of a capital

nature and the sum measured by reference to the loss of

the future income may be a capital payment made to

replace that right. Or, again, the computation may be

done for the purpose of ascertaining what capitalized

equivalent should be paid for the future income."

22. What was sold in this case was an asset, which did not form part of the stock in trade of Myer. The transaction was not one in the ordinary course of Myer's dealings and was not recurrent. It is one thing to say that Myer was concerned with obtaining funds and lending then to subsidiaries; it is another to say that this embraced the discounting of loans to subsidiaries. Except in so far as it was part of a re-arrangement planned to include income tax advantages, profit-making was not part of the purpose. His Honour was of the view that the purpose of the sale was to provide working capital for the Group. This was certainly so, but his Honour's conclusion has been criticized before us on the footing that this was not the whole purpose, that what Myer was looking to was an income tax advantage. The fact is, however, that the need for ready money as working capital arose from the re-arrangement, and the re-arrangement involved much wider purposes than tax benefits. On receipt, the money was in fact invested through a Myer subsidiary on the short-term money market. In due course, it was to be lent to subsidiaries.

23. An assignment of royalties, as they become due under a licence agreement, is doubtless different from an assignment of interest due under a loan agreement, but there is not such a difference in principle as negatives the relevance in the present case of the decision in Shepherd v. The Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 70; (1965) 113 CLR 385. There was in that case quite some discussion as to the legal nature of the assignment. The assignment (held to be equitable) was of ninety per cent only of the royalties payable under the agreement. Kitto J., using the familiar analogy, described the situation as follows:

"The tree, though not the fruit, existed at the date of

the assignment as a proprietary right of the appellant

of which he was competent to dispose; and he assigned

ninety per centum of the tree." (p.396)

It was held that royalties received in a particular year after the assignment were assessable to the assignee, and not to the assignor. The assignment had been by way of gift, but there can be little doubt, on the reasoning of the majority of the Court, that even if there had been consideration the amount received would not have been taxable.

24. A deal of cross-examination at the hearing before the judge was directed to the form of the published Myer accounts for the year ending 31 July 1981. They contained the consolidated accounts of the Group as well as those of Myer. Myer did not take the full amount of $45.37 million into account as a profit in the year of receipt. Rather it treated a proportionate part ($3,889,000), calculated from March, as "deferred income" under the heading "operating profit", in both sets of accounts. The balance ($45,370,000 - $3,889,000) was described as "deferred income" in the section explanatory of the means of financing "net assets employed".

25. It was sought to show in argument that the amount of $45.37 million was thus treated (in the Company's own accounts) as income in the income tax sense for the year in question.

26. The explanation given in evidence was to the effect that it would have been misleading to have brought the whole amount in as a credit in the year of receipt because it related to a 7 1/4 year period. There was an "amortisation", which it was said complied with accountancy principles concerning "matching".

27. In an attachment to its income tax return Myer stated the position as follows:

"ASSIGNMENT OF INCOME

On 9 March 1981 the company assigned absolutely its

right and title to receive interest income arising from

a loan of $80 million due 30/6/88 for a capital sum of

$45,370,000 received in a lump sum on that date. The

term of the assignment is for the remaining period of

the loan (7 years 83 days). For book purposes the

consideration is being amortised in the profit and loss

account and amounts are being transferred to the profit

and loss account on an actuarial basis. In the year

ended 31 July 1981 the amount transferred to the profit

statement was $3,889,167 (schedule 2).

The consideration received is of a capital nature and

accordingly the amortisation of $3,889,167 is excluded

from taxable income (refer schedule 1)."

28. There was the added factor that there was a liability of

Myer Finance to pay out $10m. by way of interest annually. Although this was a matter directly affecting Myer Finance only it could be considered in relation to the appropriate form of Myer's accounts, as well as the consolidated accounts.

29. It seems to me that the Commissioner can gain no comfort from the form of the accounts, or the descriptions used therein. It is not at all a case where there has been the selection of a system of accrual accounting, as opposed to one based on receipts, so that the taxpayer can be seen to have made an election on a basic matter. The description given to an item by the taxpayer could hardly amount to a material concession, but in the present case the taxpayer's accounts follow an accepted pattern, and are not suggestive of a treatment by the taxpayer of the receipt as one of income, in the taxation sense, and certainly not as one in the one tax year (cf. Arthur Murray (N.S.W.) Pty.Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 58; (1965) 114 CLR 314 at pp 318,9). The situation is rather the reverse of that in Thorogood's case (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Thorogood [1927] HCA 36; (1927) 40 CLR 454).

30. I am therefore of the opinion that the amount is not assessable income within the meaning of sub-secn.25(1).

31. It is submitted, in the alternative, that the case comes within both limbs of para.26(a), and that the whole of the $45.37 million was "profit". I am unable to see that according to any principle of law or of accounting this sum could be regarded as profit (see per Kitto J. in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Becker [1952] HCA 77; (1952) 87 CLR 456 at p 467). On any view, it was paid as recompense for deprivation of the interest on $80 million over 7 years and more. It could possibly be argued that the discounting of its right to interest was beneficial to Myer and productive of a profit, but this has not been claimed and the amount of any such benefit has not been quantified. There was not, nor was there intended to be, a profit-making scheme relative to the amount in question (see per Gibbs J. in XCO Pty.Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1971] HCA 37; (1971) 124 CLR 343 at 350). This part of the case must fail.

32. I turn to the submission concerning s.260 of the Act. The argument is that the section applies to nullify, as against the Commissioner, the assignment to Citicorp.

33. In my view, it fails on a number of grounds.

34. The assignment was part of an arrangement, detailed in the documents, which related to what was called an "interest stream", and the discounting by Myer of the interest that became payable to it in the course of the stream. It was intended that thereby Myer would receive interest as capital and not as income. The first comment to be made is that there was not a risk of liability to tax until the loan agreement was made, and it and the assignment were steps in a pre-conceived plan. There was no "antecedent transaction" (see per Barwick C.J. in Mullens v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1976] HCA 47; (1976) 135 CLR 290 at p 302; Brambles Holdings Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1977] HCA 54; (1977) 138 CLR 467 at p 470,1; Cridland v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1977] HCA 61; (1977) 140 CLR 330 at p 338).

35. It is said that if the assignment is disregarded the amount of $45.37 million would then represent a prepayment of interest, by Margosa to Myer. The validity of this conclusion is open to question. It would seem to me to create another transaction out of what was done. Margosa would have received a loan of $80 million and immediately repaid $45.37 million. To treat this amount as a prepayment of interest, as is submitted, would be to ignore the fact that it represented a discounting of the total interest payable over the period of the loan. It is true that the loan agreement provided for the payment of a closely corresponding amount at the option of the borrower, but no such option was exercised, and no intention to do such a thing can be presumed. The correspondence in amounts only existed, of course, for a very short time. The "pre-payment" would, in accordance with the loan agreement, leave the final payment, due on 30 June 1988, still to be paid.

36. It seems to me that the discounting of a right to income from a taxpayer's assets when working funds are required, is a transaction well capable of being explained by reference to "ordinary business dealing" to quote in part the phrase which had its origin in Newton v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1958) AC 450 (see Slutzkin v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 140 CLR 314, at p 325). Sections 102B and 102C of the Act are a recognition of dealings of that nature. In most cases this course would probably not result in any diminution in overall income tax receipts. The borrower (payer) would probably have a deduction for his payment, while the assignee (payee) would normally be liable to pay tax on his receipt. This latter would doubtless be taken into account in arriving at the value of the assigned property (i.e. the discount rate).

37. In the present case the special feature, which made the operation feasible, was the accumulated tax losses of the assignee, Citicorp. These made it desirable, or acceptable, that within the statutory period it receive income to offset the losses (s.80 of the Act). If it had had to pay tax on the interest, without any offset under s.80, the purchase price for the right to interest would undoubtedly have been much lower than it was. It can fairly be said that on ultimate analysis the scheme was making use of the "choice" offered by s.80, to use the test applied in W.P. Keighery Pty.Ltd. v. Commissioner of Taxation [1957] HCA 2; (1957) 100 CLR 66 (see Cridland (supra), and Slutzkin (supra)).

38. I set out two paragraphs of the report of Hill Samuel appearing under the heading "The Citicorp Proposal".

"2. Financial Benefits

The financial benefits of the Citicorp proposal can be

illustrated by considering the example of an interest

stream of $10m p.a. for 7 years. Under this proposal,

Myer would give up $70m over the seven year period;

however, after-tax Myer would give up only approximately

$38m. In return for giving up this amount over seven

years, Myer will receive a lump sum payment of

approximately $42m. Thus, Myer will receive a greater

sum than it gives up and will receive that greater sum

at an earlier date. This amounts to a negative cost of

financing. The net present value of this benefit and

the interest savings would be approximately $9m after

tax.

3. Interest Rate

The amount of $42 million is arrived at by applying a

discount rate of 16% p.a. to the income stream of $10m

per annum. Under normal circumstances this would be a

very high rate of interest especially considering the

request for bank security. A normal rate of interest on

this security would be under 13% p.a. However, the

facility which is being offered by Citicorp is unique in

our experience.

If Citicorp were liable to pay income tax on its receipt

of the income stream, its return would be negative. It

has been indicated to us that this transaction is

possible only because Citicorp is relying upon

accumulated tax losses and that if Myer were to utilise

the total amount offered of $17.5m p.a. for seven years,

these tax losses would be virtually exhausted. For this

reason depending upon the amount of income which Myer

decides to sell, this may be the last transaction of its

kind. Citicorp is well aware that its tax losses are a

valuable resource and that there are very considerable

benefits to the other party. Thus, we believe that

there would be little prospect of a significant

reduction in the interest rate."

39. While the arrangement was so planned as to minimise taxation liability, this is not in itself offensive to s.260 (Mullens and Cridland, supra) and in any event there were other, primary purposes, namely the funding of Margosa and the funding of Myer. It was desired to do this without problems arising for Myer under its debenture trust deed, and this meant that it had probably to sell an asset. This was what was done. To treat this as an avoidance under s.260 would go well beyond the purview of that section.

40. In dealing with the problem as to what was exposed if s.260 were applied, the appellant submitted, in the alternative, that this should be regarded as $5 million (part of the $45.37 million received by Myer), as being the interest for the first six months. This is to add one reconstruction to another, and in my view is impermissible. The fact is that an application of s.260 would not expose taxable income.

41. In my view the appeal should be dismissed, with costs.

I have had the advantage of reading the reasons for judgment of Fox J. I need not refer to the facts in any detail as they appear fully from his Honour's statement of them. The case concerns the question whether the amount of $45,370,000.00 received by The Myer Emporium Limited ("the taxpayer") from Citicorp Canberra Pty. Limited ("Citicorp") pursuant to an agreement of assignment dated 9 March 1981 during the financial year ended 30 June 1981 formed part of the taxpayer's assessable income for that year. Although the amount of tax involved is large and the facts are complicated, the case is essentially one of applying well known principles of income tax law and may be dealt with fairly briefly.

2. The Commissioner's case was argued on three grounds: first, that the amount of $45.37m was income according to ordinary concepts and therefore was assessable income under sub-s. 25(1) of the Income Tax Social Services Contribution Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"); second, that it was assessable income by virtue of para. 26(a) of the Act, as it then was; and third, that s. 260 applied to annihilate the assignment from the taxpayer to Citicorp and to render the receipt taxable as income of the taxpayer.

Sub-section 25(1)

3. Central to the question whether the receipt by the taxpayer of the $45.37m from Citicorp was income or capital is the written agreement of assignment of 9 March 1981. The primary question to which that agreement gives rise is whether it was an agreement which operated as a present assignment of a legal chose in action or as an agreement to assign moneys as they fell due in the future. If the former, it supports the notion of the receipt in the taxpayer's hands being capital and, if the latter, it more readily, though not necessarily, attracts an income flavour to the receipt. Obviously the characteristics of the receipt must be ascertained from all the relevant circumstances including the rather involved events relating to the restructuring of The Myer Group and from the nature of the business of the taxpayer itself; but Citicorp's role as assignee of the taxpayer's entitlement to interest from Margosa Limited ("Margosa") was admittedly at arm's length and the only relevant transaction to which Citicorp was a party was the assignment agreement itself.

4. In my opinion the agreement operated to presently assign all the taxpayer's entitlement to interest payments from Margosa. It is true that the assignment speaks of its subject matter as "the moneys due or to become due as the initial payments ..." (clause 1) and that there are certain other provisions in the agreement which, viewed in isolation from the residue, would lend some support to the interpretation that it was an agreement to assign interest payments as and when they fell due. But, considered as a whole, the agreement presently assigned a legal chose in action.

5. Counsel for the Commissioner argued that the receipt by the taxpayer of the $45.37m was income in the ordinary sense because it represented the sale price of property acquired by the taxpayer for the very purpose of selling it and deriving that sum in return. It was said that the money received was the product of a business transaction and the fact that there was only one transaction of that kind and of an unusual nature is not to the point. It was argued that the $45.37m was not part of the capital amount of the loan of $80m lent by the taxpayer to Margosa which will in due course be repaid in full; it was profit just as much as interest itself is profit; an inflow resulting from a chose in action created for the very purpose of sale in order to produce that inflow. What the taxpayer received from Citicorp was said to be in truth consideration for loss of profits. Reliance was placed by the Commissioner upon various authorities including London & Thames Haven Oil Wharves v. Attwooll (1967) Ch 772 especially at p 804; Inland Revenue Commissioners v. Flemming & Co. (1951) 33 TC 57 and Greyhound Racing Association v. Cooper (1936) 20 TC 373.

6. Counsel for the Commissioner emphasised that, before the assignment, Margosa could have exercised its rights under clause 4(a) of the loan agreement of 6 March 1981 between the taxpayer as lender and Margosa as borrower and prepaid the whole of the interest before the due date of 30 June 1988 and that the payment would have been an allowable deduction to Margosa and the corresponding receipt would have been income in the taxpayer's hands. If, immediately after the assignment, Margosa had exercised its rights under clause 4(a) and pre-paid the interest the payment would also have been an allowable deduction to Margosa and the corresponding receipt would have been income in Citicorp's hands. Why, so it was said, is there a difference, if in the intervening few days the taxpayer assigned its rights to interest to Citicorp and received as consideration much the same sum? I observe at this point that the argument assumes that Margosa's right to prepay interest under clause 4(a) of the loan agreement survived the assignment - a questionable proposition which turns on the interpretation of the loan agreement and the assignment agreement. Other arguments along the same general lines were advanced by counsel for the Commissioner including at least one based on the assumption that Margosa's right to prepay interest did not survive the assignment.

7. The sum of $45.37m was received by the taxpayer from a third party at arms length in a commercial transaction. The principal amount of the loan from the taxpayer to Margosa was plainly a capital asset; and the Commissioner did not contend to the contrary. The amount received by the taxpayer from Citicorp was not interest or equivalent to or in substitution for interest. The taxpayer's future entitlements to interest payments from Margosa were reduced to their present value and the rights of the taxpayer under the loan agreement were converted to a capital amount.

8. In my opinion the elaborate series of transactions of which the assignment was an integral part necessarily required that the subject matter of the assignment to Citicorp be of the taxpayer's right to interest under the loan agreement and not future payments of interest. The character of the receipt in the taxpayer's hands must be gleaned from the assignment transaction itself in the light both of the other transactions involving the restructuring of the Myer Group and of the taxpayer's business as a whole. There is no relevant nexus between the ordinary trading activities of the taxpayer and the assignment. The assignment was not made by the taxpayer in the ordinary course of or as an incident of the carrying on of its business. The nexus is between the assignment and the other transactions at or about the same time with no element of periodicity or regularity; transactions which involved many companies in the Myer Group and were entered into for the purpose of reorganising the business and structure of the Group.

9. The quality of the receipt of the $45.37m in the hands of the taxpayer was capital. I therefore reject the submissions of counsel for the Commissioner based on sub-s. 25(1).

Paragraph 26(a)

10. The Commissioner submitted that the receipt of the $45.37m fell within both limbs of para. 26(a) of the Act. It was submitted that the taxpayer employed the $80m lent to Margosa to produce a gain of $45.37m, the profit being the getting in of that sum. There were no expenses incurred by the taxpayer to off-set against the $45.37m; hence the whole of that amount was said to be brought to tax under para. 26(a).

11. This is an unreal analysis of the relevant events. In no sense could the sum received from Citicorp be regarded as profit. Before the assignment the taxpayer had a right to repayment by Margosa of the principal sum of $80m and interest thereon over a period of a little more than seven years. After the assignment the taxpayer retained its rights to repayment of the $80m but had assigned its rights to interest. What it received in substitution from Citicorp was the present value of its rights to interest over the period of the loan, a capitalisation of its entitlement to interest. There is no element of profit in that receipt. Nor was there a profit-making undertaking or scheme having any nexus with the receipt of the $45.37m. The Commissioner's argument based on para. 26(a) fails.

Section 260

12. The Commissioner argued that:-

- The scheme to avoid tax consisted of all the steps which arose from the implementation of the report of Hill Samuel Limited.

13. - One of the main purposes of the scheme was to enable the taxpayer to receive the $45.37m without borrowing it and without paying tax on it. It was essential to the efficacy of the scheme that the $45.37m should not be assessable in the hands of the taxpayer.

14. - Had Margosa on 9 March 1981, the date of the assignment, exercised the right given to it by clause 4(a) of the loan agreement to repay the whole of the interest, the amount paid by Margosa to the taxpayer would have been $45.37m or an amount substantially the same. Any such prepayment would have been received by the taxpayer as income. The assignment to Citicorp, both in purpose and effect, represented nothing but an attempt to impress upon the sum of $45.37m which came into the taxpayer's hands the character of a capital receipt and to deprive it of the character of interest. It was therefore a means for avoiding tax that otherwise would have become payable by the taxpayer. Reliance was placed upon a number of judgments of the High Court including Bell v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 34; (1953) 87 CLR 548; Hancock v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1961] HCA 90; (1961) 108 CLR 258; Mayfield v. Commissioner of Taxation [1961] HCA 57; (1961) 108 CLR 303 and Mayfield v. Commissioner of Taxation [1961] HCA 58; (1961) 108 CLR 323.

15. It is true that the various transactions recommended in the Hill Samuel report were implemented at least in part in order to render the receipt in the taxpayer's hands of $45.37m from Citicorp non-taxable. In Mullens v. The Commissioner of Taxation [1976] HCA 47; (1976) 135 CLR 290 Barwick C.J. said at p 302:

"I turn now to consider whether the transaction was

avoided by s. 260. This question must be

approached on the footing that if not struck down

the transaction between the parties would be

effective to entitle the taxpayer to the deduction

claimed. Though the section speaks of the purpose

in entering into the transaction, it can have no

relevance if, being effective, the transaction does

not alter the incidence of tax, as that expression

has come to be understood. As I have already

pointed out, there will be no relevant alteration

of the incidence of tax if the transaction, being

the actual transaction between the parties,

conforms to and satisfies a provision of the Act

even if it has taken the form in which it was

entered into by the parties in order to obtain the

benefit of that provision of the Act. It would be

otherwise if there had been some antecedent

transaction between the parties, for which the

transaction under attack was substituted in order

to obtain the benefit of the particular provision

of the Act. Section 260 is not directed to tax on

income to which the taxpayer is entitled only by

reason of the actual transaction into which the

parties have entered."

16. After citing that passage from the Chief Justice's judgment, Aickin J. said in Slutzkin v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1976) 140 CLR 314 at pp 326 and 327:

"In my opinion this is equally the position where

the actual transaction is one which stands wholly

outside the operation of the Income Tax Assessment

Act. To adopt the language of the Chief Justice to

the present case, the position may be expressed

thus:- there will be no relevant alteration of the

incidence of tax if the transaction, being the

actual transaction between the parties, does not

attract the operation of any provision of the

Income Tax Assessment Act in that the receipt in

question is not income according to the ordinary

conceptions of that term, nor is deemed to be

income by any provision of the Act."

17. In Mullen's Case Stephen J. said at p. 318:-

"The principle in W.P. Keighery Pty. Ltd. v.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation (31) is not to be

confined to cases where the Act offers to the

taxpayer a choice of alternative tax consequences

either of which he is free to choose; it was there

held that merely because the taxpayer chose, quite

deliberately, the alternative most advantageous to

it from a tax standpoint it did not thereby attract

s. 260. So, too, if no question arises of a choice

between two courses of conduct but, instead, the

Act offers certain tax benefits to taxpayers who

adopt a particular course of conduct; the adoption

of that course does not establish any purpose or

effect such as is described in s. 260. Instead, an

assessment which reflects the tax consequences of

the course of conduct which the taxpayer has in

fact adopted will then represent a due and proper

incidence of tax, there will be no relief from, or

defeating of, liability to tax and the Act will

have the very operation which the legislature

intended."

18. Aikin J. cited that passage also with approval in Slutzkin's Case and went on to say at p. 327:-

"That principle applies equally in a case of

receipts with which the Act simply does not deal,

i.e. capital receipts, save such as are for the

purpose of the Act deemed to be income. To adopt a

course which produces a result outside the scope of

the Act is not to alter the incidence of tax, or to

defeat any liability to tax or prevent the

operation of the Act, notwithstanding that such

course is adopted with full knowledge of the

provisions of the Act and with a conscious

intention that the proceeds should not fall within

the operation of the Act."

19. There is no antecedent transaction in this case. The Commissioner's reliance upon the fact that Margosa could have exercised its rights under clause 4(a) of the loan agreement to repay the whole of the interest immediately before the assignment is not to the point. Although Margosa could technically have adopted that course it was never intended that it should do so. Indeed, it would have been contrary to the pre-planned course of events. It is unreal to say that what the taxpayer received from Citicorp was in essence the same as what it could have received from Margosa. The two are fundamentally different in legal character.

20. The loan agreement itself was not an antecedent transaction in the sense envisaged in the cases to which I have referred; nor were the events which preceded it relating to the restructuring of the Myer Group whether viewed singly or as a whole.

21. There was therefore no relevant alteration of the incidence of tax involved in the loan agreement or the assignment. The Commissioner's case under s. 260 fails.

22. The appeal should be dismissed with costs.

The facts and the submissions with which this appeal is concerned are set out in the reasons for judgment of Fox J., which I have had the advantage of reading.

2. The act in the law which brought the sum of money to Myer which was treated in assessment as income was in my opinion an assignment, in consideration of payment of that sum, of property presently belonging to Myer. The property consisted of a set of contractual rights with respect to interest, payable as to $5,000,000 on the day of assignment and otherwise in the future, on the loan of $80,000,000 which had been previously made by Myer to Margosa Ltd. ("Margosa"). The indication which some expressions in that document give of an intention to assign "the moneys due or to become due as the interest payments and interest thereon and costs pursuant to the Loan Agreement" as and when derived by Myer, rather than the contractual rights presently held by Myer, is in my opinion overborne by a consideration of the whole of the document.

3. Considered alone, the assignment to Citicorp in exchange for which the money was paid to Myer was a disposition by way of sale of a capital asset, outside the courses of dealing by which Myer ordinarily gained income. If regard is had to the series of transactions of which the first step was the sale by Myer of shares in some of its subsidiaries to Myer Shopping Centre Pty. Ltd., and the assignment well-nigh the last step, the payment by Citicorp to Myer is again seen to have been received on capital account, in my opinion. Once it is recognised, as upon a consideration of all that was intended to be done by Myer and its subsidiaries it must be recognised, that neither party to the loan agreement between Myer and Margosa contemplated that interest payments, other than the first payment of $82,192, would be made by Margosa to Myer in respect of the loan, it is difficult to characterise the payment by Citicorp to Myer as one made in substitution for income foregone or lost. On the contrary, Myer ascertained the terms, including the term as to price, on which a stranger would be willing to buy a chose in action of a particular description, and then arranged with a subsidiary that the chose in action be created by an agreement between itself and the subsidiary, so that that chose could be sold on those terms.

4. The uniqueness of the transaction in the history of Myer's financial activities may no doubt be allowed some influence against a conclusion that the money was received on income account. But I doubt whether the circumstance that Myer's purpose was to treat and employ the money it planned to procure from Citicorp as working capital is relevant to the determination concerning assessability under s.25. That determination turns, I think, on the circumstance that in the course of a series of transactions on capital account a capital asset was sold. The money in question was the price received on that sale. If the asset had not been sold, it would have yielded assessable income to Myer, and it was brought into existence by Myer so that it could be sold. But to say so much of an asset is not in my opinion to show that the price obtained for the asset is received on income account.

5. In my opinion the sum paid by Citicorp to Myer is not assessable income by virtue of s.26(a), because no profit is disclosed. Whether or not "acquired" by Myer, in the sense of that sub-section, the property sold to Citicorp owed its existence to the loan agreement between Myer and Margosa. The cost to Myer of what it sold to Citicorp included the contractual obligation it incurred under that agreement to lend $80,000,000 to Margosa for a little over seven years. There is nothing in the evidence to suggest that, if that obligation were valued, the amount received by Myer from Citicorp would be found to exceed that value. The "application of a business conception to the facts" of this case discloses no profit, in my opinion. (See Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Becker [1952] HCA 77; (1952) 87 CLR 456.)

6. As to s.260, I respectfully agree with the opinion of Fox J. that no relevant risk of liability to income tax preceded the making of the loan agreement between Myer and Margosa, and that the making of that agreement cannot, in the light of the evidence as to the planning which preceded March 1981, be regarded as an "antecedent transaction" (in the sense of the reasoning of Barwick C.J. in Mullins v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1976] HCA 47; (1976) 135 CLR 290 at 302) for which the assignment to Citicorp was substituted. If that be so, and the transaction of loan to Margosa and assignment of the contractual rights to interest to Citicorp is considered, there is no "relevant alteration of the incidence of tax" disclosed. The principle which is expounded by Aickin J. in Slutzkin v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 140 CLR 314 at 325-329 applies, as that learned judge pointed out, not only in respect of "a transaction which will attract tax consequences for which the Act makes specific provision" (per Mason J. in Cridland v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1977] HCA 61; (1977) 140 CLR 330 at 339), but also "in a case with which the Act simply does not deal, i.e. capital receipts, save such as are for the purpose of the Act deemed to be income" : Slutzkin's Case, 140 C.L.R. at 327.

7. I agree that the appeal should be dismissed with costs.
