Re Grant Kingston Armstrong and Commissioner of Superannunuation [1985] AATA 5 (8 January 1985)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: GRANT KINGSTON ARMSTRONG

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUNUATION

No. Q83/133

Superannuation Act

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

The Hon. J.B.K. Williams CBE (Senior Member)

D.R.S. Craik CB, OBE (Member)

Dr. N.C. Davis A.O. (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation Act - applicant retired from Public Service on ground of invalidity - determination made that health of applicant has become so restored as to enable him to perform duties of a clerk - upon review by Commissioner decision varied to add words 'not involving bending, lifting or prolonged sitting or prolonged standing' - decision as varied affirmed.

Superannuation Act 1976 - s.75(1)

Brian Michael Stoddart and Commissioner for Superannuation, unreported decision No. A82/26 of 21 December 1982

R and Commissioner for Superannuation, unreported decision No. V82/434 of 22 November 1983

HEARING

CANBERRA

8:1:1985

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision of the respondent's delegate as varied by the respondent on 2 March 1983.

DECISION

The abovenamed applicant was previously employed in the Department of Transport as a Property Officer, Clerk Class 5.

On 1 March 1977 he was examined by a Commonwealth Medical Officer. The Commonwealth Medical Officer reported that the applicant was unfit for continued employment and should be retired from the Public Service on grounds of invalidity. He expressed the opinion that the case should be reviewed in six months' time.

On 30 March 1977 a delegate of the Public Service Board decided that the applicant should be retired from the Public Service, the decision to be effective from 17 May 1977.

Following this the applicant lodged an application for invalidity benefit, and on 12 May 1977 the applicant was advised by letter that the pension due to him under the Superannuation Act 1976 was at the rate of $6,373.00 per annum based upon a final annual salary of $12,746.00. He was also provided with a summary of the provisions of the Superannuation Act which referred to invalidity pensions.

By a letter dated 20 July 1982 the applicant was requested to present himself for medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer, Dr Barker. He was examined by Dr Barker who found him unfit to resume duty, but recommended a review of the case in one year's time. However, he recommended that the applicant be examined by a neurophysician.

The applicant was examined by a neurophysician, Dr Landy, on 4 December 1982. Dr Landy furnished a report dated 4 December 1982 wherein it is stated:

'Examination:

Straight leg raising was to 80 degrees in both

legs, he had no apparent limitation of back flexion,

there was no alteration in deep reflexes, nor any

sensory impairment. Cranial nerves were intact.

He had some old XRays with him which showed

'fishboning'

in the vertebral bodies, but no obvious

narrowing in the disc spaces.

Opinion:

I can detect no evidence of any neurological

condition on present examination.'

Following Dr Landy's report, Dr Barker was requested by the respondent to

comment on the applicant's fitness to resume work in the light of the opinion of Dr Landy. Dr Barker's reply, dated 8 February 1983, was as follows:

'Following my examination plus the assessment of

Mr Armstrong given by Dr P Landy, I feel that the

general condition of Mr Armstrong has improved.

My assessment of Mr Armstrong is that he is no

longer totally incapacitated by his disability

and I feel that he should be amenable to returning

to the Public Service either in the job which he

occupied previously as a property officer of the

Commonwealth Department of Transport or as some

other suitable position.'

Following consideration of the reports of Dr Landy and Dr Barker a delegate

of the respondent made a determination pursuant to the provisions of s.75(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976. In the determination, dated 2 March 1983, the delegate stated that he was satisfied that the health of the applicant had become so restored as to enable him to perform duties of a clerk, duties that were, in the delegate's opinion, suitable to be performed by the applicant having regard to the duties performed by the applicant immediately before his retirement on the ground of invalidity, namely, the duties of Property Officer, Clerk Class 5. He directed that the Regional Direcor of the Public Service Board, Brisbane be informed accordingly with a view to finding the applicant suitable employment.

By a letter dated 14 March 1983 the applicant was informed of this determination.

By a letter dated 14 March 1983 the Regional Director of the Public Service Board, Brisbane was requested to arrange suitable employment for the applicant on either a permanent or temporary basis.

In a letter dated 5 April 1983 from the applicant to the respondent, the applicant stated that he formally appealed against the decision to have him re-employed in the Commonwealth Public Service. He also stated that he would support his appeal with medical reports and other relevant information.

The applicant in a letter dated 7 June 1983 informed the respondent that he had an appointment with Dr Toakley, a neurosurgeon practising in Brisbane, and had asked him for a written report on his condition. He said he had been informed by Dr Toakley that the report would be forwarded to the respondent.

By a letter dated 29 June 1983 the respondent requested Dr Toakley to forward his report. In this letter Dr Toakley was asked to make particular reference to the question whether the applicant's health has become so restored as to enable him to resume his former duties.

A report dated 26 May 1983 was furnished by Dr Hopcroft, an Orthopaedic Surgeon practising in Taree, New South Wales. This report was apparently furnished at the instance of the applicant. In his report Dr Hopcroft detailed the applicant's medical history and his findings following examination of the applicant. His report concludes with the following:

'In view of this man's symptoms and clinical signs

and x-ray changes I feel he is unsuited to prolonged

desk work and that future employment prospects

should involve the ability for this patient to move

about during the course of the day with only short

periods of sitting down.'

On 7 July 1983 Dr Toakley furnished the report that had been requested from

him. In it he said that he first saw the applicant on 10 February 1976. He performed a lumbar rhizolysis on 24 February 1976, a small laminectomy for a disc hernia on 10 November 1976 and an extensive decompressive laminectomy on 2 March 1977. Dr Toakley also stated that the applicant had seen him again on 24 May 1983 concerning the direction that he return to work.

The applicant stated to him on this occasion that he was not capable of doing this in that he could not stand or sit for long periods because of pain. Dr Toakley expressed the opinion that clinically the applicant is neurologically normal, and did not appear on examination to be as bad as after his surgery, and certainly not as bad as before it. He expressed the opinion that the only way to find out whether the applicant could cope with work was to let him try it. Bending, twisting, lifting and riding in a motor vehicle could aggravate what pain he had. Dr Toakley summarised his report by stating that he could find nothing clinically to prevent the return to work as a clerk, but 'one must take note of the man's history'.

The matter was subsequently reviewed by the Acting Commissioner for Superannuation. The Acting Commissioner decided to vary the decision taken by his delegate on 2 March 1983 that he was satisfied that the health of the applicant had become so restored as to enable him to perform duties of a kind that were, in the opinion of the delegate, suitable to be performed by him, namely, those of a clerk, by adding the words 'not involving bending, lifting or prolonged sitting or prolonged standing.'

The applicant gave evidence before the Tribunal. He is thirty-six years old and resides at Coolum Beach on the Sunshine Coast. He said that he was presently unemployed. He said that he first experienced back pain in 1975 whilst working as a Property Officer with the Department of Transport. The work was of a clerical nature and involved travel around Government owned airports. He could not connect the onset of back pain with any particular incident. He consulted Dr Dooley, who treated him for several months. The treatment consisted of exercises, manipulation and traction. Dr Dooley also prescribed 'pain killers'. The treatment did not help him, and, in February 1976, he was referred by Dr Dooley to Dr Toakley, who ultimately performed three operations upon his back. He said that his back pain was continuous, usually increasing in intensity as the day progressed. He said that the pain radiates from his lower spine and travels down both buttocks. He described the pain as not being a 'tearing' pain, but, rather, a 'grind'.

In late May 1977, following his retirement from the Public Service, the applicant moved from Brisbane to Taree in New South Wales, where he entered into a business enterprise with his brother. His brother is a registered builder, who built a Squash Centre. He said that he had greater expertise in management and administration. His role was to give advice. He also invested money in the business. Upon completion of the Squash Centre he was engaged in the management of it. In this respect he attended to customers attending the Centre. The nature of the business was sporadic, there being busy periods and quiet periods. During the latter he took the opportunity to lie down and rest. His wife rendered assistance in the management of the Centre. This business continued for about three years, when it was sold.

In March 1981 the applicant moved to the Sunshine Coast, where he was again involved with his brother in a building enterprise. A set of three townhouses was built. He invested money in this enterprise and rendered assistance to his brother in the construction of the buildings. His role was to attend to administrative matters such as sorting out difficulties relating to plans and building permits. He also attended to the obtaining of supplies of building materials. He also performed light work on the site such as cleaning up rubbish. He said that his activities were limited to several hours per day.

Upon completion of the buildings he obtained a position with Mallinson Building Supplies. That involved clerical work and driving around various building sites to interview builders. The job involved a lot of sitting down and a lot of standing. This exacerbated his back pain to the extent that he found that he could not carry on with the job, and, in September 1982, he resigned.

The applicant then obtained a position at the Noosa International Tourist Resort. This involved clerical work connected with restaurant bookings, bar bookings and room service. The work was performed at night when particulars of the day's activities were fed into a computer. At the time the tourist resort was in its infancy with the result that there were opportunities during the night to rest. His employment there ceased in about May 1983.

At this time it was decided between the applicant and his wife that they should change roles. His wife obtained a position as a receptionist and he attended to household duties. In this connection there was tendered in evidence a diary compiled by the applicant recording, in considerable detail, his hour-to-hour activities, covering the period 2 January 1984 to 25 January 1984.

The applicant said that prior to his back problems he was actively engaged in sport. He was a keen water skier, and played Rugby League football. He also played competitive squash, and engaged in trail-bike riding. However, due to his back problems he was forced to abandon a number of these activities, which are now confined to surfing and scuba diving, which, he said, have a therapeutic value.

The applicant was questioned concerning his ability to perform clerical duties on the limited basis proposed in the Acting Commissioner's review. He said that he could not perform clerical duties on this limited basis because he could not sit or stand for eight hours per day, week in, week out. He said that his experience with Mallinsons demonstrated this to him. He found that he could not sit or stand for other than short periods without increasing the level of his back pain. He said that he could cope with a clerical job of low classification from an intellectual viewpoint, but it would be boring and would be difficult to cope with mentally. He would be unable to work other than short periods of time without rest.

Evidence for the applicant was also given by Mr B J Armstrong, the applicant's brother, and the builder to whom previous reference has been made. He gave evidence concerning his activities with his brother in connection with the Squash Centre in Taree, and the building of the townhouses on the Sunshine Coast. He said that he had noted a big difference in his brother. Prior to his operations he had been a very active type of person, but was now very restricted in his activities. He said that he received assistance from the applicant in the building operation, the assistance being in taking levels, cleaning up and cleaning windows. He also performed administrative work in the nature of placing orders for the purchase of materials, and the organising of the sale of the townhouses. His working activity was, however, restricted to some two or three hours per day, and he was more or less free to come and go as he pleased. He thought that the applicant would not have worked for more than twenty hours per week.

Evidence for the applicant was also given by his wife. She said that she had first known the applicant in 1974, when he was a very active person, particularly in the fields of sport. She said that his back problems commenced in 1975. She said that following the applicant's operations he was optimistic that they would prove beneficial to him. He had now changed, and had become intolerant of people. He found it hard to make friends, and finds fault with people. During the period of time that he worked with Mallinsons he was continually tired and complained of back pain.

Evidence for the applicant was given by Mr Smith, a Clinical Psychologist. He also furnished a report dated 2 May 1984. In his report Mr Smith detailed the background of the applicant, as related to him, and also detailed a number of tests conducted by him upon the applicant. He expressed the opinions that the applicant is a person of superior intellect and had bright, normal memory processes. He stated the applicant is experiencing some loss of confidence and loss of self esteem; that he has mild to moderate physical pre-occupation and concern for bodily functions and that functional overlay is likely; that he experiences episodic mild to moderate depressiveness with pessimism of outlook. His upper limb operational capacities would be adequate for average work performance. His mobility, agility and facility for rapid change in body position were adequate. He expressed the further opinion that the applicant's incapacity appears to be wholly related to the varying degree and extent of pain and discomfort which he reports he experiences during and/or following exertion. Pain being a completely subjective experience is incapable of being measured, and is not necessarily proportional to the degree of physical injury which has been suffered, and which can be heightened by functional conversion. He considered that the applicant had characteristics of physical pre-occupation which would cause him to be more sensitive to the pain experience than if these characteristics were not present. He recommended that the applicant attend the Independent Living Centre at Greenslopes Hospital to obtain advice on, and to make trial of, adjustable chairs for office and home use, as well as to investigate other assistive devices. Mr Smith gave oral evidence generally along the lines of his written report.

Dr Adam also gave evidence on the applicant's behalf. He said that he was practising in Occupational Health, a field in which he had been interested for about five years. He said he had no specialist qualifications, but that there are 'moves afoot' to provide for registration of Occupational Health as a specialty. At the present he is engaged as a company medical officer for several companies.

Dr Adam also furnished two reports, each of which is dated 6 January 1984. One contains an account of his examination of the applicant on 16 December 1983 and expresses his conclusions following that examination. The other contains a critical examination of the reports furnished by Dr Barker, Dr Toakley and Dr Hopcroft, and a criticism of the view taken by the respondent on the opinions expressed in these reports. In his report which deals with his conclusions following his examination of the applicant, he expressed the opinion that the applicant suffered a severe injury to his back which required three operations; that there had been some improvement in his condition since those operations, perhaps due to a rigid adherence to a program of exercises, and that the applicant is able to undertake some physical activity despite his problems. He addressed his mind to the question whether the level of activity which the applicant is able to perform is sufficient to permit him to resume a job on a full-time basis. He said that there was difficulty in answering this question as, at his current level of incapacity, there was very little to be found on objective clinical examination. The signs of acute injury have passed, and any neurological signs which may have been present should have been relieved by surgery. Accordingly, reliance, to a great extent, must be placed upon the patient's own account of his problems, there being no objective measure of pain. He thought the best way to determine whether or not the applicant was able to return to work would be to arrange for a return on a trial basis. However, he thought that the course of decision and appeal, and reconsideration and further appeal, with the atmosphere of confrontation so engendered, would by now have precluded such an attempt at rehabilitation. A further difficulty lay in the fact that any work which the applicant may be expected to perform has been described only in general terms, and it would be more appropriate to consider whether or not he is capable of performing a particular job. Overall, however, he did not consider that the applicant is capable of returning to work. While he may be able to work for a short time, he would anticipate a gradual deterioration which would eventually force the applicant to cease work again.

In his oral evidence, Dr Adam gave evidence generally confirmatory of his written report. He also said that there may be possible areas causing problems in the applicant's back apart from pressure on nerves; it could be in the muscles, it could be in the ligaments or in the discs. An Orthopaedic Surgeon would be the person best qualified to express a view on that aspect of the matter. On the applicant's account of his initial problems, he thought that at the time that he examined the applicant that the latter was disabled to a lesser extent than before. He said that had he been managing the applicant's case he might have suggested that he go to work to see if he could manage, because, ultimately, that is the only real test. However, successful rehabilitation depends, to a certain extent, on the attitude of all the parties involved, and he felt that the process which had gone on in this case 'would have soured the attitudes on all sides and may have made such a trial rather difficult, and, I think, almost certainly bound to fail.' He thought that the applicant would be precluded from engaging in an occupation requiring the lifting of heavy weights, and that sitting for a prolonged period would aggravate his back. It may be sufficient to get up and walk around for a time to relieve the back pain, but, at other times, the pain may be so severe that the only way to rest was to lie down. He also thought that driving a car for any prolonged period of time would cause problems. He said that the vast majority of his patients suffering from back ailments got back to work. He added that most of the people he had seen had relatively acute injuries, most of which had settled down fairly quickly. He thought that the use of specialised furniture would have no effect other than to extend, for a short time, the period of time during which the applicant could remain at work.

Evidence for the respondent was given by Dr Ryan, an Orthopaedic Surgeon. He also furnished a report dated 5 March 1984. It appears that the applicant was examined by Dr Ryan at the instance of the solicitors for the applicant, and that his report was forwarded to them. However, at the hearing Dr Ryan was called to give evidence on behalf of the respondent.

In his report Dr Ryan stated that examination of the applicant revealed forward flexion so that the hands reach the mid-shin. There was approximately 60 per cent of lateral flexion and extension. There was no spasm or scoliosis, and no specific tenderness. There was a well healed midline scar, straight leg raising was full, and there was no neurological deficit in the lower limbs. X-rays of August 1983 revealed evidence of a laminectomy at L4 and L5. There were minimal degenerative changes.

Dr Ryan expressed the opinion that the applicant's symptoms are due to disc degeneration, with no disc prolapse and no nerve root pressure. His symptoms are out of proportion to the objective findings, and he thought that functional overlay was present. He expressed the further opinion that for the future the applicant was capable of carrying out a job as a clerk not involving bending, lifting or prolonged sitting or prolonged standing.

In an addendum to his report, Dr Ryan stated that pain is a subjective experience, and if the applicant tells him he has pain then he accepts that at face value.

In his oral evidence Dr Ryan explained and elaborated upon the contents of his written report.

In his cross-examination, Dr Ryan agreed that it had not been possible, to his knowledge, to record pain reliably. He said that he had a special interest in 'pain', and had given a paper to a conference on the subject. He was referred to a report made by Dr Palmer following Computerised Tomography. This report stated that at L4/5 there are central intrusions of disc material into the end plate of L4, i.e. Schmorls nodes. There had been a previous laminectomy at this level. There was, however, no evidence of disc protrusion. At L5/S1 Schmorls nodes were again demonstrated at the end plate of L5. At the level of the disc on slice number 204 some central protrusion of the disc was demonstrated. In the cut immediately superior to this (203) there appeared to be later disc herniation into the neural foramen on the left side. Previous laminectomy also had been performed at this level. Dr Ryan said that there was nothing in this report which caused him to change any of the views he had expressed. He agreed that pain is a subjective experience. Asked whether he would expect any worsening of the applicant's condition if he were to work on a temporary basis, Dr Ryan replied (No, I think there is good evidence to show that the best treatment is work.'

Evidence for the respondent was also given by Dr Landy, to whom earlier reference has been made. In his oral evidence he confirmed generally the contents of his written report. He said that from a neurological point of view, or from the point of view of a disease of the nervous system, he could not find any abnormality of the applicant upon clinical examination, and, within his speciality, could find no cause for the applicant's back pain. He also was shown the report of Dr Palmer, to which previous reference has been made, and stated that there was nothing in that report which would cause him to have any change of opinion. In cross-examination Dr Landy agreed that his opinion was confined to matters within his speciality.

Oral evidence for the respondent was also given by Dr Toakley. He also furnished a report, to which earlier reference has been made. He gave evidence concerning the three operations that he had performed upon the applicant. He said that he had not seen the applicant since May 1977 until the applicant came to him in 1982 wanting a report on his condition, which he provided to the respondent. He was asked whether, if the applicant undertook clerical work, limited in the manner suggested by the Acting Commissioner for Superannuation, working full-time in that sort of employment would exacerbate his back condition. Dr Toakley replied as follows: 'I do not know until he does it; I do not know. It depends on the motivation of the man. He has got every motivation not to work. He has got every motivation when he goes back to work to break down and then he goes back on a pension.' Clinically he thought the applicant should be able to do the work of a clerk. He would be precluded from work involving bending and lifting, sitting and standing and bending over writing. He could see no reason why he should not be able to perform the duties of a clerk if the chair he sat on was reasonable. It would be an advantage for him to be able to move around. Questioned concerning the report of Dr Palmer, he said that there was nothing in that report which would cause him to vary the opinions he had expressed. In cross-examination he said that he thought the difficulty in returning to work lay in the applicant in that he did not think there was any incentive for him to go back to work, although he thought he was quite capable of doing so. He reiterated the opinion that he expressed earlier, that the only way of ascertaining whether the applicant was able to resume work was for him to try it.

It is plain that the applicant has, over a period of some nine years, experienced problems with his back. He has undergone three operations for his back condition. A question, which now arises for the Tribunal's decision, is the degree of pain that he may still experience, and its effect upon his capacity to engage in employment.

It is here convenient to refer to the provisions of s.75(1) of the Superannuation Act, which is in the following terms:

'75.(1) Where the Commissioner is satisfied, after

receiving the report or reports of a medical practitioner

or medical practitioners with respect to the health

of a person to whom invalidity pension is payable,

that the health of the person has become so restored

as to enable him to perform duties of a kind that are,

in the opinion of the Commissioner, suitable to be

performed by him (having regard to the duties performed

by him immediately before his retirement on the ground

of invalidity and to such other matters as the Commissioner

considers relevant), the Commissioner shall so inform

such person or authority as the Commissioner considers

appropriate with a view to that person or authority

finding suitable employment for the pensioner.'

The determination under review flows from the exercise of the powers

conferred upon the Commissioner under s.75(1).

It is to be noted that the section employs the phrase 'that the health of the person has become so restored as to enable him to perform duties of a kind that are, in the opinion of the Commissioner, suitable to be performed by him (having regard to the duties performed by him immediately before his retirement on the ground of invalidity'. That phrase has received consideration by the Tribunal in other cases. In the case of Brian Michael Stoddart and Commissioner for Superannuation, unreported decision No. A82/26 of 21 December 1982 it was said:

'The word 'restored' in the context of s.75(1)

of the Act conveys the sense of 'brought back to a healthy

or vigorous state' (see definition of 'restore' -

meaning 4(c) in The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary).

It is certainly capable, in its ordinary meaning, of

embracing circumstances in which there has been an

improvement in a person's state of health from a former

condition of invalidity. It is also capable, in our

view, of embracing cases where the underlying disability

conditions may not have changed significantly, but, on

fuller medical investigation, the nature and extent of

those disabling conditions is seen to be less than

was earlier thought to be the case. For s.75(1) to

apply, it is not necessary that the pensioner's health

must be found to have been brought back to a perfect

state. What is required is that it must be seen to be

sufficiently restored to enable the pensioner to perform

the duties said to be suitable to be performed by him.'

In the case of R and Commissioner for Superannuation, unreported decision

No. V82/434 of 22 November 1983 the Tribunal as constituted in that case accepted the meaning given to the word 'restored' by the Tribunal in Stoddart's case. We also accept that meaning.

In the circumstances of this case it may well be that the decision to retire the applicant was a premature one, having regard to the fact that the applicant was to have his third operation a few days following his examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer, which was the basis of the decision to retire him. In this respect, it is noted that the Commonwealth Medical Officer, whilst expressing the opinion that the applicant was unfit for continued employment, and that he should be retired on grounds of invalidity, nevertheless recommended a review of the case in six months' time. There was a further recommendation by the delegate of the Public Service Board, under s.34 of the Public Service Act, that the applicant be retired for six months, when a review should be had, or some earlier time if recommended by Dr Toakley.

The medical evidence put forward in this case has been summarised earlier in these reasons. The view most favourable to the applicant is that of Dr Adam. He said that he addressed his mind to the question whether the level of activity which the applicant is able to perform is sufficient to permit him to resume a job on a full-time basis. He also said that he found difficulty in answering this question as there was very little to be found upon objective clinical examination. Accordingly, he thought that a great deal of reliance must be placed upon the applicant's own assessment of his problem, there being no objective measure of pain. He also thought that the best way to determine whether or not the applicant was able to return to work was to arrange for a return on a trial basis. Overall, however, he thought that there was an atmosphere of confrontation between the applicant and the respondent which might render an attempt at rehabilitation of this kind abortive.

Dr Ryan was of the opinion that the applicant's symptoms were out of proportion to the objective findings following examination of the applicant, and thought that functional overlay was present. He thought the applicant capable of carrying out work as a clerk which did not involve bending, lifting or prolonged sitting or prolonged standing. He did not think that work would worsen the applicant's condition, if he tried it, adding that 'there is good evidence to show that the best treatment is work'.

Dr Landy was of the opinion that, from a neurological point of view, he could find no abnormality of the applicant, and, within his speciality as a neurologist, could find no cause for the applicant's back pain.

Dr Toakley thought that the applicant would be precluded from work involving bending and lifting, sitting and standing. Clinically, he thought him capable of working as a clerk if he were provided with a suitable chair and was able to move around from time to time. He added that he thought the difficulty in returning to work lay in the fact that the applicant had no incentive to go back to work, although he was capable of doing so. He expressed the further opinion that the only way of ascertaining the applicant's capacity to work was for him to try it.

Dr Hopcroft was of the opinion that the applicant was unsuited to prolonged desk work, and that any employment he undertook should enable the applicant to move about during the course of the day, with only short periods of sitting down.

Dr Barker was of the opinion that the applicant's condition has improved, that he is no longer incapacitated by his disability and that he should be able to return to a Public Service position.

It is to us apparent from the medical evidence that the applicant's claims of incapacitating pain cannot be explained by clinical examination. His claims far exceed what may be expected following examinations by a number of experienced specialist medical practitioners.

The question of the applicant's fitness for work must be considered in the context of the kind of employment proposed in the determination as varied. It is not labouring work involving strenous physical activity. On the contrary, it is clerical work within the Public Service not involving bending, lifting or prolonged sitting or prolonged standing. It appears to us that the crux of this matter lies in what was termed by Dr Adam as confrontation between the applicant and the respondent, and what was termed by Dr Toakley as a lack of motivation on the part of the applicant to return to work.

Since his retirement from the Public Service the applicant has been in receipt of a pension supplemented, from time to time, by income from working activities. He has become accustomed to a new life style following his retirement. The conclusion we draw from a consideration of all the evidence is that he is strongly opposed to, and resents, any suggestion that he should resume work in the Public Service. Some insight into the personality of the applicant may be gained from his letter dated 15 September 1983 to the Registrar of the Tribunal whereby he instituted this review, most of which is couched in very robust terms. We have not lost sight of the evidence that pain is subjective and is not measurable. We are, however, firmly of the view that whilst the applicant's condition may well be such that strenuous physical activity would exacerbate his back condition, the barrier to his performance of non-physical work of the kind proposed is his attitude of mind, wherein he is strongly opposed to the abandonment of his present living arrangements, which may occur following re-employment in the Public Service. This mental attitude has, we think, caused him to maximise and, indeed, overstate his problems. Some of the consequences of change in his living arrangements are set out on page 9 of the applicant's letter, dated 15 September 1983, to the Registrar of the Tribunal, to which earlier reference has been made. While this attitude is understandable, it is not, in our opinion, a consideration relevant for present purposes. The issue is the applicant's fitness or otherwise to undertake clerical work of the kind proposed. We believe he is fit for such work.

For the foregoing reasons, we affirm the decision as varied by the Acting Commissioner.

It appears from the evidence that some consideration was given to the question of the employment of the applicant in the area in which he resides. We think that this proposal should be pursued in order to minimise, as far as possible, disruption of his present living arrangements. We have no knowledge of the work available in his area of residence.

We would, however, recommend to the relevant authority that endeavours be made to find employment in this area.
