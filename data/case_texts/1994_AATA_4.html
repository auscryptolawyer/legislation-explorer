Re Taxation Appeals [1994] AATA 4; (1994) 27 ATR 1215 (14 January 1994)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

Nos. VT93/79, 89 and 90

AAT No. 9242

Number of pages - 20

Income Tax

[1994] AATA 4; (1994) 27 ATR 1215

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

P GERBER (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - claimed deduction for loss on disposal of shares - whether shares held as trading stock or as long term investment - whether shares able to be applicant's trading stock during his bankruptcy - whether Commissioner of Taxation empowered to issue assessment - whether "nil tax advice" an assessment - Bankruptcy Act 1966, secs 58, 116 - Goods Act 1958 (Vic), sec 27 - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, secs 28, 31, 170

Shaw v Hyett and Anor (1891) VLR 612

Wood v W and G Dean Pty Ltd [1929] HCA 44; (1930) 43 CLR 77

Martin v FCT [1953] HCA 100; (1952-1953) 90 CLR 470

Batagol v FCT [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 CLR 243

Franklin's Selfserve Pty Ltd v FCT [1970] HCA 33; (1970) 125 CLR 52

Investment and Merchant Finance Corporation Ltd v FCT [1971] HCA 35; (1971) 125 CLR 249

AC Williams v FCT [1972] HCA 48; (1972) 128 CLR 645

Curran v FCT 74 ATC 4296

Case M91 80 ATC 655

Deane and Croker v FCT 82 ATC 4112

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 31 August 1993

14:1:1994

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr P Searle

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr T Murphy

Solicitors for the Applicant: Sly And Weigall

Solicitors for the Respondent: AGS

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decisions under review.

DECISION

P. GERBER The principle issues in these applications are whether the taxpayer ("T") - a surgeon by trade - was a sharetrader in the 1988, 1989 and 1990 tax years as distinct from an investor; i.e. did various shares held by him constitute his trading stock? Indeed, was T able to be a sharetrader and did he "acquire" the shares he purchased in view of the fact that he was at all relevant times a bankrupt, having been declared as such on 3 October 1986 (the major - and petitioning creditor - being the Commissioner of Taxation, T having being "caught" when the "Curran" scheme Curran v FCT 74 ATC 4296 was finally laid to rest in John v FCT 89 ATC 4101); alternatively, can property, which should vest beneficially in the trustee in bankruptcy, constitute trading stock in the hands of the bankrupt. A further complicating question arises: can these shares constitute trading stock if T did not have dispositive power over the shares, which were bought with $400,000 borrowed from the trustee of T's family trust (the loan agreement between T and the trustee of the family trust, dated 30 June 1987, provided that the shares were to be lodged as security for the loan). For good measure, when the value of the shares fell significantly, and the lender sought additional security, T borrowed $105,000 from his wife and assigned the shares to her as security, albeit remaining, so it was said, the beneficial owner. The loan agreement is dated 11 November 1987. An additional issue was raised by Mr Searle, of learned counsel, who appeared on behalf of T and questioned whether a document, issued by the respondent on 3 April 1989, constituted a valid assessment, and if so, whether the Commissioner could issue a further notice of assessment in respect of that year - which he purportedly did on 24 March 1993.

2. Mr Searle, in his opening, pointed out that T derived, year by year, (and going back in time to a period well before the three years now before the Tribunal) a large income from his surgical practice and incurred exorbitant losses on his share and future dealings which substantially exceeded his income. Indeed, the picture which emerged in the early years is of a man best described as a compulsive gambler on the stock and future markets. Worse, he not only invested in sinking ships after shrewder investors had abandoned them, but should one of his ships be still afloat when he put his money in it, it would sink soon after with all hands on deck.

3. Looking at the 1983 return, T's medical income was (in round figures) $178,000 and his losses from his share transactions $361,000. In that year, his activities on the stock market took place partly on the US and the UK markets, his US brokers being Merrill Lynch Pierce Fenner and Smith Inc ("Merrill Lynch"); his UK brokers being Prior Harwin Securities Ltd. T also had some shares in Australian companies. The gross US dividends came to US$760; his dividends from his Australian share portfolio (predominantly mining) amounted to $1,962. (The UK investments do not appear to have attracted any dividends, just losses).

4. I was invited not to confine my attention to the three years now in dispute, but that to look at the larger historical perspective in order to try and understand what occurred in the 1988, 1989 and 1990 tax years. Turning to the Merrill Lynch statement for part of the 1983 year, a most bizarre picture emerges. For example, Merrill Lynch bought shares in Metromedia on July 26 for US$17,850 and sold them some 16 days later for US$1,275 - a loss of US$16,575. No explanation was offered for this exotic transaction. On September 14, Merrill Lynch purchased some scrip on behalf of T in Dial Corporation for $US28,875 and, although there appears to have been no sale, this transaction is recorded as a loss on September 27 of $US28,875, i.e. the whole of the purchase price. Again no explanation was offered by T. I can only assume that that company is one of those "ships" that sank without trace in the two weeks after acquisition of the scrip. Without going into further analysis of the 1983 Merrill Lynch dealings, suffice it to note that between July 1, 1982 and January 11, 1983, scrip was purchased on T's behalf in the amount of US$723,067 and sold for $US$697,240 - a loss of US$25,827. T's UK losses were even more impressive; an acquisition of shares in that year for 934,221 pounds were sold for 730,911 pounds - a loss of 230,310 pounds.

5. Turning to the 1984 tax year, T lost, overall, some $A188,079 on his share transactions, which had the same random buy and sell pattern as that revealed in Deane and Croker v FCT 82 ATC 4112, save that we are here dealing with large - and real - sums of money, and losses which - unlike Dean and Croker - are not explicable in terms of gaining a tax advantage.

6. I have no returns for the 1985 and 1986 tax years. However, the evidence does not suggest that there were any share transactions during this period.

7. Given such frenetic - and unsuccessful - buying and selling of shares, it is not surprising that T was declared bankrupt in October 1986. However, undeterred by such minor hiccup, T borrowed, as previously noted, some $400,000 from the trustee of the family trust for the purpose of buying more shares in July/September 1987 - in the words of his counsel - "and of course he purchased just prior to the October crash", to which I am tempted to add: "and of course suffered a further loss, albeit this time 'on paper'".

8. In his returns for the three years, T returned a tax loss in 1988 of $124,935, in 1989 taxable income of $14,467 and taxable income of $88,491 in 1990. In each year he treated all newly acquired shares as trading stock, a treatment which resulted in a claimed loss of some $323,126 in the 1988 tax year, and similar losses of $45,056 and $15,253 were claimed in 1989 and 1990 respectively, the shares being valued in each year at market price. In each of the three years, T claimed interest on the loan used for the acquisition of the shares. He was initially assessed as returned, and it was only after an audit that adjustments were made, year by year, disallowing the loss on shares and the interest on the loan (the latter being subsequently allowed in each of the three years after objection; no explanation was proffered for this "change of heart". One of the impugned stock returned a modest franked dividend).

9. T gave evidence that he first started using Merrill Lynch in about 1983. He explained that he was paying 60 cents in the dollar in tax at that time and unable to make any meaningful contribution to his superannuation - "I'm a manual worker and shares weren't going to be effective, and that's when I started trading in order to try and make up for these difficulties". (tr p 12). T claimed - and I have no reason to disbelieve him on that aspect - that he took a lively interest in stocks and shares at or around this time:

There were economic books and then there was just the market, how the

market worked. There was charting books. But I was mainly interested in

inflation and the economy with respect to inflation. And I became

interested in gold and gold trading as an alternative currency, and

between the two of them, I decided, having discussed it with these

people in the financial industry, to trade in an attempt to go ahead.

To support such wide reading, a list of the books which had formed T's education was tendered (exh "C").

10. Consequent upon his bankruptcy, one S was appointed trustee of T's estate on 3 October 1986, and in a letter sent to T on the same day, S pointed out, inter alia, that "(y)our property now vests in me as your Trustee in Bankruptcy and is available for division amongst your creditors by reason of sections 58 and 116 of the Bankruptcy Act". In a Business Questionnaire (exh "F"), requisitioned by the Trustee, T stated that he carried on two businesses - a medical practice since 1973 and "Investment and Share Trading approx 1977" (exh "F"). In the same response, T states that the share trading business ceased "since early 1986".

11. T claimed that the reason he borrowed $400,000 was "because I decided to resume share trading specifically to try and earn money to pay my receiver, to pay my creditors" (tr p 19) and in a reply to a requisition from the Tax Office, T stated on 23 October 1992 that "these shares were acquired for the purpose of reselling at a profit in a trading transaction. At no time was there any intention of retaining the shares as an investment for dividend income". He stated that he advised the lender (the trustee of the family trust - Calmit Pty Ltd ("Calmit") that he was an undischarged bankrupt. Exh "G" is the relevant loan agreement, dated 10 June 1987, between the lender - Calmit - and T. The loan purports to be for a period of 190 days with an option to renegotiate for a further 190 days. Interest is fixed at 22% payable in advance, and security for the loan is stated to be "lodgement of share certificates with Calmit". (exh "G")

12. Exh "H" and the "T" documents contain the various contract notes relating to the scrip T bought with the $400,000 he borrowed from the family trust. These shares were acquired in the following companies for a total outlay of $399,501:

60500 Consolidated Exploration Ltd

9100 Australian Consolidated Minerals Ltd

3500 Noranda Pacific Ltd and

1000 North Flinders Mines

13. T claimed that the above shares were bought as part of his "strategy". When asked what his strategy was, he replied: "Well, my strategy was actually to trade in gold, because gold was something I came to know and study and became very interested in. And these being gold shares, were a means of trading in gold and the fluctuations in gold, because they could be easily transferred". When asked by his counsel what time frame T was contemplating for the sale of these shares, he replied:

Well these would have been sold on the basis of the gold price because

the gold shares under normal circumstances reflect the gold price.

Another advantage of the shares is that there can be a leverage effect,

and they usually follow - as is happening at present -the shares usually

follow the increase in gold - and so I was studying the one commodity,

namely gold, and these represented my interest in gold, and there are

various support resistance levels.

What is the support resistance levels and what does that mean?---Well, a

support and a resistance level are the same thing. If you chart the

share you'll find that as the shares go up there are certain levels

where there was a lot of activity, with the share price, so called,

moved sideways. It does not go up or down. This is because a lot of

shares are changing hands, but it is not enough. There are many buyers

and sellers, but they're both matching, so that the price stays the

same. This is a resistance level on the way up, and the support level

on the way down. So that when it breaks through this resistance level,

then you can expect it to go up to the next resistance support level,

but when it's coming down, you can expect it to be held up at that

resistance level - at that support level.

Well how did these particular shares perform during August and September

of 1987?---They responded to this and they increased in accordance with

the increase in the gold price.

And how much did they increase in value?---It was substantial, it was

about a $55,000 profit on paper.

And how did that crash affect the share price of the shares that you had

purchased?---Well, severely, but not initially severely. As a result -

when they crash, and it was all in the papers and on the things - the

shares went down a little bit, but not very much, and the gold price

actually went up. And after the crash the gold price - the crash of '82

a 14 year wave has washed right over all these trading activities and

even now people - because people certainly didn't predict it and they

still can't explain it and I - even the so-called hardened long term

people were - normally could explain it and nobody predicted it, except

people who said they did in various ambiguous statements, like, "it can

go up or it can go down". And then, when the crash happened, they said:

"See, I told you it could go down". But it wasn't predicted. But after

this so-called crash, the share prices started slumping. These share

prices went down a little. Consolidated Exploration went down to $4 or

something. It had been up to $6. In May the previous year it was $4 or

something, but it had been up to $8 so it had been up to - it had

previous high. But the confusing thing for me was that the gold price

was continuing to increase, to rise. And I was determining - and it

actually brought them through a resistance level and was moving up to

the next resistance, so I thought this was a blip, or at first some

aberration, initially. (tr pp 20-21).

14. The "letter" referred to (exh "I"), dated 10 November from the Secretary of the family trust, noted that there had been a severe fall in the value of the shares lodged as security for the loan and requested "further security or reduction of the loan of $400,000". This, in turn, led to the additional borrowing by T of some $105,000 from his wife (who was a director of Calmit). To complete the factual background, it was stated that Calmit had, in turn, borrowed the $400,000 from Elders Finance. T was to add that it was his understanding - "I wasn't exactly privy to it, and it had nothing to do with me, but I understand Elders had, in fact, transferred the shares into its own holding company, or nominee company, it still recognised Calmit as ...". At this point, Mr Searle properly interrupted his client and told him not to volunteer information based merely on his "understanding". It was not made clear at this stage whether Elders Finance was informed by Calmit that the person allegedly trading in these shares was an undischarged bankrupt. The matter was to be explored further in cross-examination.

15. In response to Calmit's demand, T entered into a written agreement with his wife, dated 11 November 1987, (exh "J") the terms of which are set out below:

It is agreed that:

(1) (wife) lends $105,000.00 to T for the specified purpose of reduction

by that amount of the loan outstanding to Calmit by T.

Consolidated Exploration Ltd 60,500 shares

Australian Consolidated Minerals Ltd 9,100 shares

16. In due course, these shares were formally transferred to T's wife on 11 November 1987 for a total consideration of $104,910. I might add here that, notwithstanding his bankruptcy, T at no time made any effort to hand the scrip to his trustee.

24. T was discharged from his bankruptcy in or about October 1991. He claimed that he sold "most of the shares" he bought in the years in question in order "to reduce the debt, because the interest rates were building up and ... I was having difficulty meeting the interest rates on the loans ... and they were demanding more security on the loans. I retained a few". (tr p 25) I have no evidence what shares he continued to hold.

25. Mr Murphy took T carefully through the various share transactions with his brokers in July 1982. The first recorded acquisition consist of 12 "CLBIPICT" (Columbia Pictures?) on July 1, 1982 at a cost of US$9900; no sale is recorded save that under "(Loss)/Profit US$", this transaction is recorded as a loss of US$9900 on July 27, 1982. When it was put to T that the 12 CLBIPICT were, in fact, options which had just expired, he replied: "Could have been". T agreed that on July 26, he bought Metromedia shares at a cost of US$17,850 (already referred to in para 4 above). When asked whether he sold them on 12 August 1982 for US$1275 for a loss of US$16,998, the transcript shows that the only response was "Mm". Similar responses were elicited when T was taken through other dealings with his broker.

If we might jump down to Dial Corporation?---Mm

You sold them, or they were disposed of or ceased to exist or whatever

happened (for $US28875)?---Mm

On 27 September?---Mm

There is Homestead Mining on December 14, you purchased 4000 something

for $US210240?---Mm

...

In that page, it appears that you have bought and you have sold within

26. After further probing of T's dealings with Merrill Lynch, Mr Murphy then took the witness through some of his dealings with Prior Harwin in the UK. T appears to have opened an account with that firm in November 1982, and his first transaction consisted of what counsel referred to as a purchase of "150,000 whatever" in Britoil on November 16 1982 for 147,252 pounds which were sold on 26 November 1982 for 107,436 pounds - a loss of 39,816 pounds. Indeed, looking down the list of T's dealings with that firm, commencing with the aforementioned Britoil - there were 24 transaction in all, the last transaction recorded being on June 17, 1983 -it becomes apparent that not one transaction disclosed a profit. The opening balance was 934,221 pounds and the closing balance was 730,911 pounds - a loss of 203,309 pounds ($333,614) over a period of six months. A dispassionate observer, looking at this pattern, must surely wonder what investment "strategy" lay behind this "trading". It soon became apparent that, whatever the motive, these shares or "whatevers" were held for a few days or weeks at most before being sold. Whatever else it may have achieved, the result was hardly likely to provide for T's old age.

27. Mr Murphy asked T how this "strategy" operated on a-to-day basis:

Now, you devised this strategy of trading. In that trading with, say,

Merrill Lynch; did you give Merrill Lynch day to day instructions - buy

this, sell?---Yes and no. I was on the phone frequently. He was on the

phone frequently to me. He gave me advice, we discussed it, I accepted

it. I knew some things, he knew some things. I did take a lot of

advice.

And this is from your contact at Merrill Lynch?---Who was fed by various

departments about the trading and things.

Now, in the same way, did you take similar advice in relation to the

And were you active in that?---Yes. I made the decision to buy Britoil.

It was put to me that it was being privatised and floated.

Did you receive any advice - Australian taxation advice - in relation to

that trading in the United States and the United Kingdom?---I am not

completely sure. I think I did, but I can't remember specifically what

it was.

And you mentioned before that you faced the problem of a 60 cent in the

dollar tax rate?---Yes.

Which you attached to your professional earnings?---Yes.

Did you receive any advice to the effect that if you made trading

profits, that would also be subject to 60 cents?---Yes, I did.

So that the trading profits themselves would not provide any sort of a

tax hedge?---No, but it would have been unmanually (sic) earned income.

What was the significance of that to you? Did that have a tax

significance?---Only insofar as before .... inaudible .... it was

......inaudible ..... a second business. (tr pp 32-33)

28. T was closely cross-examined on his involvement in the Curran scheme and the sources from which Calmit derived its income (predominantly from managing the non-medical side of the practice; a different company employs T as a surgeon). This line of cross-examination was largely directed to establish that T was keenly alive to the various tax implications of his activities and desirous of reducing his tax liability where legally possible. T readily agreed that he believed that his overseas share trading losses were tax deductible.

29. T agreed that Calmit was interposed between him and Elders as the lender of the $400,000 so that his own bankruptcy need not be disclosed.

30. T claimed that his earlier investment strategy changed when he re-entered the market in 1987. Since I have attached significant importance to this change in strategy, I suppose to set out much of the relevant cross-examination. When asked what the difference was between the two strategies, he claimed:

(t)hat on the first strategy I was depending on advice from people who

were making money on each transaction.

Now, in the second strategy you just ---? I wanted to not trade so

frequently and have a definite target and trade the larger fluctuations,

not the little blips.

You in fact had a fairly large fluctuation prior to October, in the

sense I think that you had a paper profit of $55,000?---It wasn't a

large fluctuation and it was - it wasn't a - it was a - yes, it was a

gradual increase.

When you say - make sure we get our terms understood. When you say you

were going to trade on large fluctuations, in percentage terms, what

sort of fluctuations are you talking about?---Well, it depended on the -

as far as - it doesn't depend on how big the fluctuation is, it depends

on where you think it will meet the next resistance level.

Well, you have distinguished small fluctuations and large fluctuations.

Are you also distinguishing between small resistance levels and large

resistance levels?---Well, this is one of the difficulties, but I was

trying to eliminate the small resistance - in the small resistance

levels.

Now, when you purchased these shares in August of 87, had you formulated

any resistance level at which you would sell?---Yes, on gold - as far as

gold's concerned. Not the shares, because it was just the one thing I

was following. Not only individual shares.

Now, when you say that you find the gold resistance level, does that

mean that when it reached this particular resistance level, that you

would then sell all your shares in the various companies?---No.

How would you determine what shares - or did you think through a

strategy determining what shares you would sell?---Well, it would be

most of the shares.

Not all of the shares?---Not all of the shares.

Well, how would you determine those shares which would - - -?---

Well, that would depend on the price of the shares and what the shares

themselves were doing. To some extent, and I would - I wanted to keep

shares so that I could continue to trade. I may have sold all of the

shares. I may have done.

Now, can you tell - these shares that you bought in August and September

87, you bought more shares later on, didn't you?---Yes.

In the 88 year and the 89 year?---Yes.

Have you sold any of the shares which you purchased in August 1987 as of

now?---Most of them.

Do you recall when that was---If you give me my bag, I can tell you. I

think it was - well, was it October - 88 sometime I think. I've got it

in my bag.

...

Now do you recall - so that is at that date which is November of 1987,

you still clearly held your shares - did you recall when you sold the

shares?---Did I recall when I sold the shares?

Yes?---I can't recall. I would have to refer to my notes.

Well, could I put it to you that you did not sell any shares until some

time in the year ended 30 June 1990; you sold some BHP gold options, and

it looks like Western Mining ordinary shares?---Yes, that could be so.

(tr pp 41-43)

31. T claimed that the scrip purchased in July/September 1987 was held by Calmit as security, and that Calmit transferred other shares to Elders as security. He added that as a result of the October share market crash, the shares held by Elders as security for the $400,000 loan to Calmit had also declined in value and that Elders thereupon demanded additional security from Calmit, which, in turn, made the demand for additional security from T (exh "I"). The following took place in cross-examination:

How would you determine what shares - or did you think through a

strategy determining what shares you would sell?---Well, it would be

most of the shares.

32. Mr Murphy concluded his cross-examination by putting to T that the shares acquired in the 1987 tax year, which he held for some two or three years, were bought by him "to establish some wealth" for his retirement. T was vehement in his denial:

No, no. no, because I couldn't have, because the trustee would have

demanded them. They were traded to get earnings to pay the trustee.

33. The first issue to determine is whether a document - "T9" - dated 3 April 1989 constitutes an "assessment" with respect to the 1988 tax year. If it is, then the respondent had no power to issue a further notice of assessment in respect of that year unless it can be shown that there had been a failure to make a full and true disclosure of all the material facts necessary for the Commissioner to make his assessment.

34. Turning to that document, it is headed

NIL TAX ADVICE

for the year ended 30 June 1988

Below the box setting out T's name and address is the heading:

Details of your assessment:

YOUR TAXABLE INCOME IS NIL $ C

TAX ON TAXABLE INCOME A 0.00

TOTAL CREDIT FOR GROUP CERTIFICATE E 13250.00 CR

BALANCE OF THIS ASSESSMENT L 13250.00 CR

OTHER AMOUNTS PAYABLE (NOTE 13) 153529.57 DR

NET AMOUNT PAYABLE 140279.57 DR

**** DUE DATE FOR PAYMENT OF $140279.57 ****

**** AS PREVIOUSLY ADVISED ****

TOTAL AMOUNT PAYABLE $140279.57

DATE DUE AND PAYABLE SEE ABOVE

J.J.CROTTY

Deputy Commissioner of Taxation

35. I am satisfied (i) that the reference to "nil taxable income" and "tax on taxable income" being $0.00 relates to the 1988 tax year and (ii) that the references to net amount payable, total amount payable and the due date for payment of same relates to a tax liability referable to an earlier year or years of income (it will be recalled that the Commissioner had previously petitioned for T's bankruptcy). For myself, I have difficulty in seeing how sec 170 can operate in circumstances where there is no tax payable unless, of course, a taxpayer falls within the specific criteria set out in subsecs 170(2) and (3). As Kitto J noted in Batagol v FCT [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 CLR 243:

The topic of (sec 170) is the power of the Commissioner to amend

assessments, a concept which pre-supposes that an assessment is

something creating a legal obligation, so that an amendment of it must

depend upon a positive grant of power to alter that obligation.

Throughout the section, an assessment is referred to as a specific,

identifiable thing, which, unless amended ... will stand as decisive of

liability (at p 253).

Earlier, his Honour had noted that:-

Relying upon these and other indications of intention in the Act, the

Commissioner maintains that the word "assessment" is used throughout in

s.170 in a sense which makes a "nil" assessment an impossibility. (at p

251).

36. I was invited by Mr Murphy to adopt the view that the document "T9", set out above, was a mere "advice" that no tax was payable in respect of the 1988 tax year (for good measure, the notice was headed "NIL TAX ADVICE") and hence was not a "Notice of Assessment". Counsel cited numerous authorities in support for his submission. The proposition is clearly correct. A somewhat similar case arose in Case M91 80 ATC 655.Taking the facts from the headnote:

The taxpayer, a proprietary company, showed a net profit of $1,370 in

its return for the year ended 30 June 1976 and claimed against that

carry-forward losses of $7,465. On 16 August 1977, the Commissioner

issued a notice to the taxpayer stating that no tax was payable on the

income shown in the return. Subsequently, on 23 February 1978, the

Commissioner issued a notice of assessment to the taxpayer showing a

taxable income of $1,370 and a tax payable of $582.25. The adjustment

sheet showed that the deduction for past year losses had been disallowed

because the requirements of sec. 80A and 80E had not been satisfied.

The taxpayer's objection came before the Board, where it was contended

that an assessment of tax is complete when the taxable income and the

tax payable thereon has been ascertained. Accordingly, it was claimed

that the issue of the assessment notice in February 1978 was a notice of

an amended assessment and was not justified under sec. 170. The

Commissioner, on the other hand, claimed that the notice of assessment

which issued in February 1978 was not an amended assessment.

Held, assessment confirmed.

Relying on Batagol v FCT [1963] HCA 51; (1963) 109 CLR 243, the notice of 16 August

1977 was not an assessment as it did not state a taxable income nor an

amount of tax payable. The first time an assessment was completed was

upon the taxpayer being served with the notice of February 1978. That

notice was not a notice of amended assessment; it was a notice of the

first assessment made for the 1976 year.

37. The above decision is, with respect, clearly correct. In the result, I am satisfied that the document "T9" does not constitute a Notice of Assessment. The notation to "OTHER AMOUNTS PAYABLE (NOTE 13)" clearly refers to tax outstanding from previous years and cannot alter the character of the notice, which clearly states that for the year of income (1988) no amount of tax is payable. In the circumstances, I need not concern myself whether or not there had been a full and true disclosure of all the material facts.

38. I now turn to the issue whether or not the shares now in dispute can constitute "trading stock" for purposes of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). It was argued on behalf of the Crown that T, being an undischarged bankrupt during the years he purchased the relevant shares, could neither acquire title to the scrip nor effect a transfer on sale, since any property acquired after he became a bankrupt vested in the trustee of his estate as soon as the property was acquired, and was divisible amongst his creditors; cf secs 58(1)(b) and 116(1)(a) Bankruptcy Act 1966. It followed, so it was said, T could not have been in the business of trading in shares.

39. Sec 28 of the Act provides:

Subdivision B - Trading Stock

28 (1) Where a taxpayer carries on any business, the value, ascertained

under this subdivision, of all trading stock on hand at the beginning of

the year of income, and of all trading stock on hand at the end of that

year shall be taken into account in ascertaining whether or not the

taxpayer has a taxable income. (my italics)

....

(3) Where the value of all trading stock on hand at the beginning of

the year of income exceeds the value of all trading stock on hand at the

end of that year, the amount of the excess shall be an allowable

deduction.

Sec 31 provides that the value of each article of trading stock (not being livestock) to be taken into account at the end of the year of income shall be, at the option of the taxpayer, its cost price or market selling value or the price at which it can be replaced.

40. It is therefore a nice question whether an undischarged bankrupt can be a sharetrader and/or own trading stock? If the answer is "yes", it will be necessary to decide whether this applicant was, in the relevant years, trading in shares or engaged in a business of share trading.

41. In John v FCT [1989] HCA 5; (1989) 166 CLR 417, the High Court, in a joint judgment stated:

The definition (of trading stock) looks to the nature of goods that may

constitute trading stock and posits that they will constitute trading

stock if acquired for any of the specified purposes, including sale. It

presupposes that the person by whom they are produced, manufactured,

acquired or purchased is or will be engaged in trade in those goods.

But it does not render an inquiry into whether or not the person is or

will be engaged in that trade irrelevant. A single transaction does not

render a person a trader, although, of course, a single transaction may

constitute an adventure in the nature of trade. Nor, we think, is a

single item acquired for the purpose of manufacture, sale or exchange an

item of trading stock unless the purchaser is or will be engaged in

trading goods of that nature. Thus it is relevant to inquire whether

the person who acquires an item claimed to be trading stock is a trader

in the sense that he is engaged or will be engaged in trading goods of

the nature of the item acquired. It is for this reason that it is

pertinent to inquire whether Malindi (the partnership formed in 1977 in

order to buy and sell shares of which the appellant was a partner) was a

share trader or, as was put in argument, was engaged in the business of

a share trader. For the purposes of the present case the terms may be

used interchangeably, but that will not always be so. A person may be a

trader notwithstanding that his business is described in more general

terms. So much is implicit in the definition of "trading stock". A

person's business may be that of manufacturer or producer yet he may be

a trader in the goods manufactured or produced. Thus the relevant issue

is not the nature of the business carried on, but rather whether the

person is a trader in the goods which are claimed to be trading stock.

Whether or not a person is a trader seems to us to be a question of

fact, albeit that in some cases the determination of that fact may

depend on questions of impression and degree. If trading has not

commenced or if there is no discernible trading pattern, the question of

intention or purpose may be relevant in the sense that if there is an

absence of intention or purpose to engage in trade regularly, routinely

or systematically then the person may well not be a trader. A fortiori

if some contrary or inconsistent intention or purpose is present. But

if trading has commenced and the activities reveal a discernible trading

pattern, then it seems to us that the motive for undertaking the

activities or for undertaking a particular transaction cannot serve to

characterize the person engaging in those activities as a non-trader, or

as a non-trader in relation to a particular transaction. (at pp 429-430)

42. In FCT v Suttons Motors (Chullora) Wholesale Pty Ltd [1985] HCA 44; (1984-1985) 157 CLR 277, the High Court took the opportunity to considered whether, to constitute "trading stock", the goods had to be owned by the person claiming to be in business. The facts in that case were complex and need not be set out in detail for present purposes (they can be found in the decision of the primary judge reported at 82 ATC 4418). Suffice it to point out that the appellant sought to sell to retail customers motor vehicles which were held by it merely as bailee in possession with an option to purchase. The question was whether, on those facts, the vehicles satisfied the description of "trading stock". The majority (Gibbs CJ, Wilson, Deane and Dawson JJ; Brennan J dissenting) noted (at p 282):

The fact that particular goods may not have been paid for or may not be

owned by the trader does not preclude their being trading stock on hand

in relation to his business if, notwithstanding lack of payment or

ownership, they are legitimately held in the possession of the trader as

or as part of the body of stock to be sold or exchanged in the ordinary

course of the trade of that business. (my emphasis).

Mr Murphy, who very properly brought the case to my attention, sought to distinguish it on the basis that "in that case the taxpayer would acquire title before sale; here the Applicant could never acquire title".

43. With great respect, I find this attempted distinction unpersuasive. Leaving aside whether or not the appellant in Suttons Motors had title to the vehicles at the time of their disposal, it seems to me that the issue in this case is not whether T had a good title to the shares as against the world, but whether - assuming he had purported to sell them - a bona fide purchaser for value would have received good title. Put in terms of the Suttons Motors case, did T hold the shares "legitimately"? In my view, he did. The trustee in bankruptcy had access to T's tax returns and was - or should have been - aware at all relevant times that T had bought the shares in dispute and took no effective action to ensure that the shares would be transferred to him and left T in possession of them. As early as 9 October 1986, he notified T of his duties and obligations as a bankrupt, including the requirement that he, the trustee, be notified "in writing of any change in the nature of your employment, occupation, business or profession (penalty $100 or six months imprisonment)". T's response, dated 10 October 1986, stated, inter alia, that he had been a sharetrader since 1977, but had ceased to operate that business "since early 1986". The trustee also had T's 1989 tax return in which there is a reference to the taxpayer's "share trading stock". In 1990 he wrote to T, claiming that on legal advice that as trustee in bankruptcy, "he had no present entitlement to restrain dealings with your shares subject to security". This is not intended to be critical of the trustee's action - or lack of it - but turns on the legitimacy of T's possession of the shares. To answer that, one may have to pose the question: would a prospective bone fide purchaser for value be able to claim a good title to the shares by asserting that the real owner (the trustee) had precluded himself from denying T's authority to sell the shares. Such a hypothetical buyer could point to sec 27 of the Goods Act 1958 (Vic), which provides:

Sale by person not the owner

Subject to the provisions of this Part and of any express enactment,

where goods are sold by a person who is not the owner thereof and who

does not sell them under the authority or with the consent of the owner,

the buyer acquires no better title to the goods than the seller had

unless the owner of the goods is by his conduct precluded from denying

the seller's authority to sell. (my emphasis)

44. In this case, T borrowed a large amount of money and bought shares with the proceeds, and held on to the scrip in total disregard of any restraints the Bankruptcy Act or the trustee have imposed on him. I have therefore concluded that the facts do not enable the Commissioner of Taxation to rely on T's bankruptcy to assert that the latter was precluded from actively engaging in the business of share trading. Mr Searle cited Shaw v Hyett and Anor (1891) VLR 612 as authority for the proposition that a bankrupt is not prevented qua bankrupt from being engaged in business with the knowledge and consent of the trustee. Whether or not T was engaged in share trading, is, of course, another question which will be dealt with in due course.

45. Next, Mr Searle relied on sec 58(2) of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 (dealing with the vesting of property upon bankruptcy in those cases where Commonwealth or state law requires certain transmission of property to be registered), and submitted that until registered, such property vests in law in the bankrupt until registration. In any event, Mr Searle submitted that as a consequence of the decisions in Wood v W and G Dean Pty Ltd [1929] HCA 44; (1930) 43 CLR 77 and Franklin's Selfserve Pty Ltd v FCT [1970] HCA 33; (1970) 125 CLR 52:

...neither the bankruptcy itself nor the acquisition of the shares on

the bankruptcy - the bankruptcy, of course, is totally irrelevant

because this is at best after-acquired property - but when the bankrupt

acquires that property subsequently, there is no reason of necessity

(to) conclude that he is holding every component of those shares, if at

all, for his trustee in bankruptcy, or for his former creditors (tr p

61).

46. If I understand Mr Searle's argument correctly, it comes to this: The shares T acquired after his bankruptcy in his capacity as a so- called share trader - and being permitted so to "trade" by his trustee in bankruptcy - constituted the bankrupt's trading stock "as opposed to simple property, (and) is not after-acquired property for the purposes of the Act and does not automatically vest in the trustee" (tr p 53 et seq). It is, with respect, an interesting submission, but I am not persuaded that is supported by the authorities on which Mr Searle relied. One must therefore look elsewhere whether or not T was a sharetrader.

47. Mr Searle took me with his customary thoroughness through the usual authorities dealing with share trading, including, of course, Investment and Merchant Finance Corporation Ltd v FCT [1971] HCA 35; (1971) 125 CLR 249, which held that shares were capable of being trading stock if the taxpayer was in the business of share trading. Much reliance was placed on the decision of Walsh J in that case, who noted:

I do not assert, of course, that shares are always trading stock in the

hands of their owner; and even where the owner is a dealer in shares,

the circumstances may show that particular shares are not trading stock.

But when shares are bought by a dealer in shares and it is intended that

they are to be resold and that this will probably occur in the not

distant future, I do not think they are to be denied the description of

trading stock, either because the trader expects or intends that they

will be sold at less than their cost price or because he seeks to obtain

a commercial advantage from the transaction otherwise than from a profit

on the resale, that is, an advantage from an expected dividend and from

an expected taxation benefit. (at pp 270-271).

48. In the end, I have not been persuaded that whether or not T was a sharetrader in the relevant years can be resolved by recourse to authority, however august. Ultimately, the test as to whether a taxpayer is in business, as Webb J pointed out many years ago in Martin v FCT [1953] HCA 100; (1952-1953) 90 CLR 470, is both subjective and objective and:

it is made by regarding the nature and extent of the activities

under review, as well as the purpose of the individual engaging in

them, and, as counsel for the taxpayer put it, the determination is

eventually based on the large or general impression gained.

(at p 474).

A like view was expressed by the majority in John v FCT (supra)

Whether or not a person is a trader seems to us to become a question

of fact, albeit that in some circumstances the determination of that

fact may depend on questions of impression and degree. (at p 430)

49. I am satisfied on the evidence, that whatever may have been his purpose of entering the market in 1983, T's bankruptcy put an end to his buying and selling, and that by the time he re-entered the market, he had changed his "strategy" and purpose. It follows that his earlier activities cannot colour his actions in the years now before me. I have reached that conclusion after a careful examination of his answers to questions put to him by Mr Searle, and by Mr Murphy in cross- examination, in relation to what was referred to as T's "second strategy", as well as the documentary evidence, and I am satisfied that, in 1987 and subsequently during the relevant years, T's activities showed none of the characteristics which, viewed objectively, could be characterised as a business of trading in shares. In answer to the requisitions by the trustee, T responded with the statement that his business of share trading had ceased "since early 1986". For good measure, had he commenced (or re-commenced) in the business of share trading, he would have had to inform his trustee. Had such a business indeed been undertaken, T's failure to so inform his trustee would constitute a breach of the Bankruptcy Act. I am not prepared to assume that he deliberately committed an unlawful act, the more so since he was made aware of his duties under the Bankruptcy Act. Again, T's "strategy" in 1987 had altered substantially from his frenetic activities in the earlier days, when it could be said that his quick "in out" buying and selling was consistent only with being a speculator in the stock market. To the extent that a new "strategy" emerged in 1987, it shows a desire to invest predominantly in gold (or, rather gold shares, which T equated as being much the same thing in that the rise and fall in the price of gold would be reflected in the price of goldmining shares). There were many questions addressed to "support and resistance levels", but, whatever this may mean to the cognoscenti, the evidence is clear that as early as August and September 1987, when T's portfolio showed a "paper" profit of some $55,000, T resisted the temptation to sell them. Even after the October 1987 market crash, when the general share market fell precipitously, the value of gold shares "went down a little bit, but not very much, and the gold price actually went up ...". This notwithstanding, T held on to his shares. To the extent that T claimed, in his evidence, that he intended to trade in the shares he held, I reject his evidence as "benevolent reconstruction". Looking at the "one off" transactions in the 1987 tax year (the bulk of the shares now in issue), T's assertion that he "traded" in that gaggle of mining shares - which he did not sell when they could have yielded him a substantial profit within a short time of acquisition and which were finally only sold when their holding cost (interest on borrowed money) became prohibitive, defies belief.

50. On the whole of the evidence, I am satisfied that (i) after T got his fingers burnt in his previous stock market activities, he was cured of his manic "trading" activity and had abandoned any intention to engage in share dealing regularly, routinely or systematically, and (ii) the acquisition of his share portfolio was intended to be an investment for his retirement. No evidence was led as to T's purchases in Woodside Petroleum Ltd (800 shares), Placer Pacific Ltd (700 shares), BHP Minerals options to buy 13000 shares, or Western Mining Corporation Holdings Ltd options (2000), which were purchased, according to a letter from T to the responent dated 23 October 1992 ("T7") in the relevant period; cf letter from S to T dated 12 December 1990, para 22 above, which refers to a holding in Pioneer Mineral Exploration Ltd not mentioned anywhere else. In these circumstances, there is nothing before me which would justify me in making any separate finding with respect to these shares.

51. In the result, I have concluded on the evidence that all of the impugned share transactions in the years before me were intended as an investment. Further support for this conclusion can be found by the demonstrated fact that T's commitment and interest in the market was markedly less in and after 1987 than it was in 1983. It follows, as pointed out before, and contrary to counsel's submission, that I can derive no guidance with respect to the shares bought between 1987 and 1990 by looking at T's earlier activities in the market. To the extent that it was sought to persuade me that the subjective intent of T was to trade, I find that this purported intent is not borne out by the objective surrounding circumstances. The bulk of his share acquisitions, bought within a few days of each other and held for a number years - and sold for reasons wholly unconnected with trading - have none of the characteristics of a continuing business enterprise. The matter was put succinctly, albeit in a different context, by Stephen J in AC Williams v FCT [1972] HCA 48; (1972) 128 CLR 645:

I should, I think, first decide whether the taxpayer's assessable income

derived from his share transactions can more appropriately be

ascertained by treating him as conducting a business of dealing in

shares, his income being ascertained by the conventional methods

outlined by Menzies J in the Investment and Merchant Finance Case (1971)

[1971] HCA 35; 125 CLR 249, at p 264 or by the application of s.26(a).

The former course would involve treating as trading stock at least those

shares which the taxpayer acquired as a dealer in shares; it would treat

his transactions as having the character of a continuing business

enterprise the result of which could meaningfully be reflected by the

process of annual accounting. (at p 655).

52. For the above reasons the objection decisions under review are affirmed.
