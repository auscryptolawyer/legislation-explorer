Re Australian National Hotels Limited v the Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1988] FCA 292 (18 August 1988)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: AUSTRALIAN NATIONAL HOTELS LIMITED

And: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

Nos. NSW G581, 678 and 679 of 1987

Income Tax

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Bowen C.J.(1), Pincus(2) and Burchett(1) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Deductions under s.51(1) - Whether, where taxpayer borrowed foreign currency for capital purposes, premiums in respect of insurance against loss by adverse exchange movement were deductible on revenue account or were of a capital nature - Whether it was open to the trial Judge to hold that premiums had been "incurred" in the relevant year - Whether s.260 applied.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (C'wlth) ss.51(1), 260

DECISION

The principal question in these appeals is whether certain insurance premiums incurred by the appellant company are deductible under s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 for the purposes of income tax assessments in respect of the company's financial years which ended 31 July 1976, 31 July 1977 and 31 July 1978. The insurance in question indemnified the appellant against exchange losses in relation to a foreign currency loan. The period of the loan was long before the coming into operation of Division 3B of Part III of the Income Tax Assessment Act which applies (putting it generally) to exchange gains and losses of a capital nature in respect of certain loans received on or after 19 February 1986.

2. The context of the problem may be briefly stated. In 1973 the appellant, which carried on the business of a hotel known as the Wrest Point Riviera Hotel, undertook major building work in order to extend the hotel and erect a casino. It borrowed approximately five million dollars, about four million dollars for the building work and about one million dollars for working capital in relation to the casino. In June 1975, in order to take advantage of the lower interest rates available to a borrower of Saudi Arabian riyals than those available to a borrower of Australian dollars, the appellant paid out its existing borrowing by raising in substitution for it, from a consortium of Arab banks, a loan of approximately twenty-seven million Saudi Arabian riyals (then equivalent to $A5,759,754). No point has been taken in respect of the failure of the figures to correspond precisely, and the argument proceeded, both before the learned Judge who decided the matter in the Supreme Court and before us, on the basis that the foreign exchange loan was simply to replace the Australian dollar borrowing the purposes of which have been described. Given that assumption, its character was accepted as dependent upon those purposes.

3. When the loan was being negotiated, a member of the board of the appellant, a Mr. Wolfe, drew attention to the risk that the company's obligations might be enlarged by adverse currency movements. It was agreed, as a result of discussions between Mr. Wolfe and a fellow director who was financial controller of what was known as the IPEC group, of which the appellant was a member, that insurance cover would be obtained in respect of the appellant's exposure to exchange fluctuation. It was also agreed, in principle, that the cover should be obtained for a premium which, together with the relatively low interest to be paid for the foreign currency borrowings, would not exceed, in total, the amount being paid in interest in respect of the Australian dollar borrowings.

4. Efforts were made to obtain appropriate cover. They failed. In early 1975, as the Judge pointed out, the concept of hedging foreign exchange risks was not well understood in Australia, and insurance of the kind contemplated was difficult to obtain. The Judge held that no appropriate cover was available outside the IPEC group. A solution was found when a member of the group, Tjuringa Securities Limited, a finance company, announced itself prepared to provide the insurance required. It did so, the Judge found, as a commercially genuine venture in which it could perceive advantage for itself. From the appellant's point of view, of course, the genesis of the transaction had nothing to do with Tjuringa Securities Limited, it having been concerned, as Mr. Wolfe made clear, about its own exposure to risk by reason of the possibility of an adverse movement of the relevant exchange rate (actually of two exchange rates, since it was necessary to exchange Australian dollars into United States dollars and then to exchange United States dollars into Saudi Arabian riyals). The Judge said: "I was very impressed with Mr. Wolfe as a witness." He also expressly accepted the evidence of other witnesses called on behalf of the appellant in respect of the implementation of the transaction.

5. The foreign currency loan agreement was made between the appellant and Arab Finance Corporation S.A.L. and certain Arab banks on 24 May 1975, and the loan was drawn down in June. The agreement provided for repayment by instalments spread over the period between 2 December 1977, when 10% of the principal of the loan was repayable, and 2 June 1980. There was provision for early repayment. In the event, the whole of the loan was repaid on 2 December 1977, but the Judge has found that at its inception and throughout the period when arrangements were made for insurance between the appellant and Tjuringa Securities Limited, it was contemplated that the loan would run its full term. The making of the repayment crystallized a foreign exchange loss for the appellant in excess of one million dollars. That loss approximated the total of premiums payable to Tjuringa Securities Limited.

6. The agreement for insurance between the appellant and Tjuringa Securities Limited was formalized by a deed expressed to be "made as of the 6th day of August 1975", but executed in September 1976. The deed contained an appropriate definition of the expression "currency fluctuation liability", and then provided that Tjuringa Securities Limited (referred to as the insurer) covenanted "to indemnify the Insured (that is, the appellant) against the currency fluctuation liability in respect of each payment of principal under the Loan Agreement (that is, the agreement by which the Saudi Arabian riyals had been borrowed) and to pay to the Insured forthwith after payment by the Insured of such amount of principal the amount of the currency fluctuation liability in respect of such payment of principal." There was also a covenant by the insured to pay premiums, including provision for the following:

"(a) in respect of the period from 9th June

1975 to the 31st July 1975 the sum of

One hundred and fifty thousand dollars

($150,000) payable forthwith after the

date hereof;

(b) in respect of each period of one (1)

calendar month during which any part of

the principal is outstanding under the

Loan Agreement commencing on 1st August

1975 the sum of thirty thousand dollars

($30,000) payable in advance on the

first days of August and February in

each year provided that if any such

payment of principal in advance covers

a month or months during the whole of

which no principal is outstanding then

an appropriate refund shall be made by

the Insurer to the Insured."

7. As the deed was not executed until September 1976, and as no premium money actually changed hands prior to the winding up of the whole transaction by repayment of the loan (the companies in the group having a well established and regularly used system of inter-company accounting within the group, by appropriate book entries, which was utilized in respect of this transaction), the question arose, in respect of the appellant's financial year ended 31 July 1976, whether during that year it had incurred any outgoing in respect of premiums. This question was answered by the Judge in the appellant's favour. He answered it largely as a matter of inference from records of the company which were in evidence. The inference was attacked by senior counsel for the Commissioner, partly on the basis that there had demonstrably been changes of mind concerning the terms on which indemnity would be granted, and partly on the basis that entries in the company's books were not always made contemporaneously, and, so he contended, the relevant ones may not have been made until after the end of the fiscal year 1976. Other matters bearing on the credit to be given to the appellant's case were also adverted to. But it is clear a cheque for $187,500 was in fact drawn in May 1976 in respect of premiums, and cancelled, not on the basis that premiums were not actually due under a firm agreement, but because the then financial controller of IPEC Insurance Limited, the holding company of the IPEC group, "advised (Mr. Eakins, the accounts controller of the appellant) that the premiums would not be paid as they fell due. They would be credited against an inter-company account and show the liability to Tjuringa Securities." (Emphasis added.) Of course, if payments in fact fell due under a firm arrangement, a subsequent variation of the terms of that arrangement would not require a conclusion that the arrangement had, after all, not really been firm.

8. The Judge referred to two letters as indicative that, by July 1976, a firm arrangement had been arrived at. Each was written on behalf of Tjuringa Securities Limited to its solicitor. In one, dated 12 July 1976, reference is made to "premiums payable by ANH." In the other, dated 21 July 1976, under the heading "ANH - overseas currency fluctuation cushion policy," reference is made to:

"the above insurance policy and whether TSL

could enter into such a transaction without

being a licensed insurance company. Our

understanding is that one transaction is not

'carrying on the business of an insurer'.

If this is the case, would you please provide

us with a suitable letter indicating that the

policy is an insurance policy and that for

this one transaction TSL is not required to

be a licensed insurer."

It seems significant that neither of these letters refers to the policy as a proposed policy. The second letter refers to it three times as if it were an existing insurance policy, without once adding any qualification, while the first refers to "premiums payable by ANH". The Judge regarded these letters as providing "a strong indication that, after discussion and negotiation, a firm agreement had been reached prior to the end of 1976 fiscal year as to the terms of the insurance arrangement and that all that was then required was confirmation that Tjuringa could legally enter into such an arrangement and the formal Deed embodying the agreement." He considered the entries in the accounts also threw light on the matter, and finally reached his conclusion taking into account those entries, and the documentary material, together with the evidence of the appellant's witnesses, all of whom it is clear he accepted.

9. It was submitted the evidence did not establish that in fact there were entries in the accounts capable of supporting the Judge's conclusion that there was a firm arrangement in respect of the insurance, and in particular capable of supporting a finding of an agreement under which a premium was payable of $150,000 for the period of not quite two months to 31 July 1975 and $30,000 per month thereafter. Reference was made to the evidence of Mr. Eakins, and it was suggested that answers he gave in cross-examination left it in doubt whether all the relevant entries may not have been made after the close of the company's fiscal year on 31 July 1976. But a close examination of Mr. Eakins's evidence supports the Judge's view. In chief, he was referred to two entries, as at January 1976, opposite the name Tjuringa in the books of the company, of $125,000 and $62,500, totalling $187,500. He said they represented part of the premium due to Tjuringa Securities Limited as at January 1976, the other part of which was the last entry in January 1976 of $142,500, making a total of $330,000. That of course is the amount which was payable to the end of January 1976 if the agreement embodied in the deed, or an earlier form of that agreement involving the same premium liability, in fact applied. Mr. Eakins was asked:

"Are you able to tell his Honour to the best

of your recollection when those entries are

likely to have been made?"

and answered:

"The entries would have been made some time in

February 1976 to relate to the half financial

year January 1976."

(The appellant's fiscal year being from 1 August to 31 July, its half year ended on 31 January.) There was also introduced into evidence an invoice from Tjuringa Securities Limited to Australian National Hotels Limited dated 30 April 1976 for the sum of $330,000, shown as comprised of $150,000 for the period to 31 July 1975 and $180,000 for the period to 31 January 1976, relating to an item described in the following terms: "TO Premium due in respect of Indemnity on currency fluctuation on Saudi Arabian Riyal Loan". Mr. Eakins said he would have received that invoice "soon after 30 April".

10. It is true that, in the course of some general questions about the lateness of preparation of the annual accounts of the company and the lateness of preparation of invoices, Mr. Eakins was asked:

"It was quite normal they were received - in

the case of invoices inter-company - later

when the accounts were being prepared?"

and he replied:

"I am not saying normally. It could well be

they were received some time after July but

before the time the accounts were prepared."

However his evidence makes it clear that there was writing up of the books for the semi-annual period to 31 January 1976, as well as for the annual period to 31 July 1976, so that this answer, sensibly understood in relation to the logic of the question, suggests that an invoice for the period to 31 January 1976 would probably have been received when the accounts to that date were being prepared, which Mr. Eakins said was shortly after the end of that period. In fact, the invoice referred to above does relate to the semi-annual period and bears the date 30 April 1976. On the face of it, that appears to be a genuine date, since a backdating would more logically have been to 2 February 1976, the first working day after the close of the period, and there is no logic at all to 30 April 1976 unless that was the date of preparation of the invoice.

11. There was then some somewhat confused cross-examination about the date when the invoice was translated into an entry in the books of the appellant, in the course of which, if the transcript is correct, the cross-examiner himself became mistaken about dates, and it seems the witness and the cross-examiner must have been for a while at cross-purposes. However, the witness, whose evidence concerning the amounts shown in the accounts for 1976 as payable by the appellant to Tjuringa Securities Limited was expressly relied on by his Honour, cleared the matter up in the following terms: "I am sorry, the invoice was dated 30 April 1976 so I would have received that invoice some few days later than that. The entries would have been made around that time." If it is suggested that the previous answers, and the use of the expression "would have", cast doubt upon the acceptability of Mr. Eakins's evidence, the answer must be that what the witness was meaning to convey, particularly in the context of a less than precise cross-examination, and whether the witness's credit had been damaged or whether, on the other hand, previous answers had simply been affected by an innocuous mistake or misunderstanding, are matters in respect of which the trial Judge had a very great advantage. That advantage should not be overlooked on appeal.

12. Having regard to the inferences which were open on the face of the documents, and bearing in mind that it is plain his Honour did accept the oral evidence called for the appellant, some of which he found impressive, this is not a case in which this Court can say the trial Judge was wrong in finding the premium obligations were incurred during the fiscal year ended 31 July 1976. See Warren v. Coombes [1979] HCA 9; (1979) 142 CLR 531.

13. The next point to consider is whether the repayment of the principal of the loan was chargeable to capital or to revenue. The trial Judge treated the loan as primarily concerned with the extension of the appellant's hotel and the erection of a casino building. On this footing, the borrowing was clearly an affair of capital: Commercial and General Acceptance Limited v. The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia (1977) 137 CLR 373; Avco Financial Services Limited v. The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1982] HCA 36; (1982) 150 CLR 510; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Hunter Douglas Limited 83 ATC 4562. No argument was advanced attempting to distinguish the position of so much of the loan as related to the provision of working capital for the operations of the casino. It may be that the working capital of a casino should be viewed in a different light: cf. the Avco case itself and also Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Marshall and Brougham Pty Ltd (1987) 73 ALR 261. It is, however, inappropriate to pursue this question, having regard to the basis on which the matter was argued. It was accepted that the exchange losses suffered by the appellant were losses on capital account.

14. The Judge considered that the nature of the losses insured against was in itself sufficient to determine the characterisation of the premiums. He said:

"The simple fact is that the payment of the

premiums was designed to result in the making

good to the Appellant of capital losses,

should they occur. To my mind, this

indelibly stamps the payments with the

character of outgoings of a capital nature."

The appellant's starting point in the appeal was to contest the reasoning so tersely stated by his Honour.

15. The proposition that a payment of a premium to insure a capital item must also be of a capital nature seems contrary to the view expressed in the joint judgment of Gibbs C.J., Stephen, Mason and Wilson JJ. in The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v. Smith [1981] HCA 10; (1981) 147 CLR 578 at 582, where they said:

"It is convenient to consider first the issue

which is raised on the cross appeal, because

if it is correct to include the moneys

received under the policy in the assessable

income of the taxpayer then that

consideration is relevant to, though not

necessarily determinative of, the question

whether the premium is deductible under

s.51."

It is also contrary to the decision in Usher's Wiltshire Brewery, Limited v. Bruce (1915) AC 433, where premiums for the insurance against fire of tied houses, through which a brewery company sold its beer, and to insure against loss of the publican's licence, were held to be chargeable against revenue. At 452 Lord Atkinson said:

"They are designed to cover the loss of the

fabric by fire and the loss sustained if the

licences were not renewed."

These are clearly capital losses.

16. In The Texas Company (Australasia) Limited v. The Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1940] HCA 9; (1940) 63 CLR 382 at 468-9 Dixon J. said:

"Some kinds of recurrent expenditure made to

secure capital or working capital are clearly

deductible. Under the Australian system

interest on money borrowed for the purpose

forms a deduction. So does the rent of

premises and the hire of plant. No doubt the

difficulty of assigning an outgoing to

capital or income is often very great. ...

The delay increased the chances of a loss

expressed in pounds (he was referring to a

deliberate delay in payment for stock

purchased overseas in order to augment

working capital), but the fact that the

reason for the delay related to capital does

not make the outgoing a capital loss. It is

rather a standing contingency representing

the recurrent expenditure which must be

incurred to obtain the use of the money and

is much more like annual outgoings to obtain

the use of capital assets, such as rent, hire

or interest."

This passage has been repeatedly relied on: see the Avco case (supra) at 516, 528; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Total Holdings (Australia) Pty Limited 79 ATC 4279 at 4283; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Ilbery 81 ATC 4661 at 4669.

17. In the Texas case, Latham C.J. at 427, using language which Gibbs C.J. cited in the Avco case at 517, referred to the increased outlay, by reason of adverse exchange movements, required to pay for the stock "not necessarily as payment of the price of the goods, but as a necessary outgoing made in the normal course of the continuance and maintenance of the business as an enterprise conducted for the purpose of profit." At 430, he said:

"The Income Tax Assessment Act does not

include any provision which prevents the

deduction of amounts paid in order to obtain

the use of capital. Sec. 23(1)(a) expressly

permits the deduction of interest if an

outgoing of interest is actually incurred in

gaining or producing assessable income. If a

liability for interest is incurred in order

to introduce capital into an income-gaining

business enterprise the amount of interest

paid is allowable as a deduction ... ."

18. Where capital is committed to a business, it will not in general lose the character of capital, and a loss of the whole or part of it will remain a capital loss. There are exceptions, as for example in the case of the purchase of trading stock or the employment of the capital in the ordinary operations of a bank or a finance company. If the capital is raised by loan, an investment of the borrowed moneys in a business will ordinarily remain an investment of capital, and the same consequences will follow. But there is a special feature of loan capital, which flows from the ephemeral nature of a loan. The cost of securing and retaining the use of the capital sum for the business, that is to say, the interest payable in respect of the loan, will be a revenue item. It creates no enduring advantage, but on the contrary is a periodic outgoing related to the continuance of the use by the business of the borrowed capital during the term of the loan. If capital, whether or not raised by borrowing, is invested in a building which burns, or in securities which become worthless by the failure of the body which issues them, the loss is a capital loss. A careful business manager will protect his business against the loss of the capital invested in it by appropriate insurance, by the employment of security guards to patrol buildings or skilled staff to monitor investments, and by other measures. The advantage sought thereby is not an enduring one. No amount is added to the capital of the business. But during the recurring periods of insurance, the patrolling of premises, or the monitoring of securities, the continued availability of the capital investment by which the business earns its income is protected. The cost of protecting it is as much a revenue cost as the interest by which the capital, to the extent that it was borrowed, was obtained in the first place. Indeed, in the case of recurring expenditure, it is difficult to distinguish between expenditure which obtains and expenditure which maintains the investment. While Dixon J., in the Texas case, used the words "secure" and "obtain" in the passage cited above from his judgment, Latham C.J. expressed the same conclusion, as has been seen, by the phrase "a necessary outgoing made in the normal course of the continuance and maintenance of the business ...". It is to be noted that, although the Texas case was not concerned with a borrowing at interest, it was concerned with an analogous use of short-term capital, the deliberate withholding of payments in order to utilize the moneys withheld as working capital. In that context, Dixon J. treated the consequential costs incurred in relation to exchange as similar to interest paid to obtain the use of capital in a business.

19. Rent, which Dixon J. also used as an example, and interest are both periodic payments for the use, but not the permanent acquisition, of a capital item. Therefore, a consideration of the often-cited three matters identified by Dixon J. in Sun Newspapers Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337 at 363 assigns interest and rent to revenue. See The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v. South Australian Battery Makers Proprietary Limited [1978] HCA 32; (1978) 140 CLR 645 at 654-5, per Gibbs ACJ., and 662, per Stephen and Aickin JJ. See also, concerning the significance, as an indication that an expense is chargeable to revenue, of the fact that the advantage it brings is not enduring, the cases cited in Commissioner of Taxation v. Ampol Exploration Ltd (1986) 13 FCR 545 at 575-7. If recurring insurance premiums are a natural incident of an overseas borrowing of capital for a relatively limited period, these cases would support the view that they, in common with the interest also payable, are part of the regular demands on the income of the business.

20. Senior counsel for the Commissioner sought to distinguish the insurance in the present case from an insurance against fire of the hotel or casino, premiums in respect of which he conceded would be deductible, on the ground that the latter insurance is in respect of a physical thing by the use of which income is earned. He sought to contrast the insurance against adverse exchange movement as an insurance, not of anything by the use of which income is earned, but of the very capital itself. However, the distinction is extremely hard to maintain. The object of insurance of a building is not to maintain in existence the building itself as a spring from which a stream of income flows. Armed guards may do that. But an insurance policy simply provides, apart from the exceptional case of a policy requiring replacement of the building, for the replacement so far as the insurance extends of the capital invested in the building used by the business. The annual premiums are a recurring expense outlaid in order to make the investment safe, and thereby to secure its continuance. A business that did not incur those outlays, as a practical matter, could not be sure of retaining the investment and thereby earning its income.

21. It would not be logical to treat the price of obtaining the capital sum borrowed (the interest) as deductible, and not admit the deductibility of the price of securing that its use may continue to be enjoyed (the premiums for insurance without which it would not be safe to keep invested in Australian dollars for use in the business a sum repayable in Saudi Arabian riyals).

22. In the circumstances of this case, the similarity in function between interest paid to obtain the use of borrowed capital and insurance premiums paid to secure that use against particular hazards which might prevent its continuance is not only to be seen as a matter of principle. The evidence of Mr. Wolfe, which his Honour accepted, showed clearly that in fact the company regarded the premiums as part of the cost which it was necessary to incur to secure the use of the borrowed capital from the chosen source. The amount of the premiums was related to the difference between the local interest rates and the interest rates available in Saudi Arabia. In order to reduce the one outgoing, it was considered necessary to incur the other. To expend moneys, in order to procure an indemnity of the kind obtained, was essential to the conduct of business by the use of this particular investment. Cf. Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Adler 81 ATC 4687 at 4692-3.

23. For these reasons, it is proper to conclude that the premiums incurred by the appellant were deductible under s.51(1), unless the Commissioner can avoid this result by the application of s.260.

24. The trial Judge held that the appellant's claim was not vulnerable to s.260. In part, his conclusion depended upon his acceptance of Mr. Wolfe's evidence. Upon that evidence, the insurance transaction was a commercial solution to a commercial problem. Certainly, the appellant's search for an insurer of this particular risk originated in considerations far removed from tax avoidance. But reliance was placed on behalf of the Commissioner on the fact that Tjuringa Securities Limited was said to have had available to it tax losses against which the premium income could be set off, and also on the fact that the exchange losses suffered and premiums paid were almost equal so that little money passed hands. However, there is an inconsistency in the Commissioner's relying on both of these considerations. If the transaction was planned to produce mutually off-setting figures, Tjuringa Securities Limited's tax losses were of no significance. On the other hand, if those tax losses were a significant feature of the arrangement, that would argue a genuine insurance transaction from which a substantial profit might be expected in the event that ultimately a loss did not eventuate. As the Judge pointed out, the tax advantage which would then be secured would be a tax advantage accruing, not to the appellant, but to Tjuringa Securities Limited.

25. There are some indications in the evidence which, if they stood alone, might lead to an inference favourable to the Commissioner. In particular, there is evidence of a note in a bank file of a conversation with Mr. Gordon Barton, who was then the dominant mind in the affairs of the appellant, on 30 November 1976. The note suggests that Mr. Barton had said his group had anticipated a devaluation and "to offset this for taxation purposes, Australian National Hotels Limited has entered into a contract of insurance with Tjuringa Securities Limited to indemnify itself against exchange losses." The trial Judge described this note of a conversation which was not the subject of any other evidence as a "flimsy indication". It is certainly readily explicable on the basis that a captain of industry, who had on the evidence been particularly eager to obtain the Saudi Arabian loan, would be likely to represent the situation to his banker, when the exchange rate was moving in a manner that was seriously adverse, in the best light he possibly could. In any case, there was ample evidence to justify the view taken by the Judge, and there is no sufficient reason to find that he was wrong in taking that view.

26. As was recognized by counsel for the Commissioner, his submission is in the teeth of the decision in Commissioner of Taxation v. Janmor Nominees Pty Ltd (1987) 15 FCR 348. See also Commissioner of Taxation v. Lau [1984] FCA 401; (1984) 6 FCR 202 at 224-5; Cecil Bros. Pty Ltd v. The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1964] HCA 82; (1964) 111 CLR 430. This Court should follow the Janmor Nominees case, which is indistinguishable.

27. In the result, we are of opinion that the appeals should be allowed with costs.

These are appeals from a judgment given in the Supreme Court of New South Wales in three related income tax matters, concerning the appellant's assessments for the financial years ended 31 July 1976, 31 July 1977, and 31 July 1978. In each case, the question is whether the appellant is entitled to a deduction under s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 for what were claimed to be insurance premiums covering the appellant against losses due to changes in the value of the Australian dollar. Those losses were incurred in repaying in 1977 a loan made to the appellant in Saudi riyals in 1975.

2. The respondent contended before the learned primary judge that no deduction could be allowed in respect of the financial year ended 31 July 1976, because during that year there was neither a payment of premium nor a binding agreement to make such a payment; his Honour held that the making of such an agreement should be inferred.

3. Next, the respondent contended that s.260 of the Act should be applied against the appellant, and that also was rejected by the learned primary judge. Its rejection was only formally challenged by counsel for the respondent, who regarded himself as precluded by decisions of this Court from succeeding on that issue here. It is unnecessary to deal with it.

4. On what was described below as the "critical question", however, the respondent succeeded: it was held that the premiums constituted outgoings of a capital nature and were therefore not deductible. The relevant part of s.51(1) of the Act uses the expression "losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature ..."

Factual History

5. The first question referred to above, which was reagitated before us, involves largely factual considerations. For that reason, and because resolution of the last point mentioned above requires an appreciation of the history of the matter, it is convenient to set that out in some detail.

6. The appellant was described in evidence as a member of the Ipec group, the leading figure in which at material times was Mr Gordon Barton. He and a Mr G.P. Farrell owned about half the shares in the group's holding company, Ipec Insurance Limited, the rest being held by members of the public. The appellant was a subsidiary of The Federal Hotels Limited, which was a subsidiary of Ipec Insurance Limited, and its shares were listed on stock exchanges. The insurer, Tjuringa Securities Limited (Tjuringa), was at material times privately owned by interests associated with Messrs Barton and Farrell; it functioned as an investment company and merchant banker for the group.

7. At relevant times, the appellant carried on business as the proprietor of the hotel and casino at Wrest Point in Tasmania. The latter had been built with borrowed funds in 1973. The appellant decided to borrow further moneys to repay the debt incurred in constructing the casino and also to repay moneys borrowed to provide working capital. The purpose of the new borrowing was to take advantage of low interest rates available in respect of moneys borrowed in the currency in question, Saudi riyals.

8. Mr P.C. Wolfe, a director of the appellant, whose evidence was accepted below, said that when or after the proposal to borrow in Saudi riyals was raised, he told other directors that he was concerned that the company could be exposed to serious foreign exchange fluctuation losses. He expressed the view that if the funds could not be obtained from a source which did not present that risk, then the risk should be assumed by another company in the Ipec group, namely Ipec Insurance Limited.

9. In the event, Saudi riyals were borrowed pursuant to an agreement of 24 May 1975, no effective steps having then been taken to cover any exchange risk. Mr Wolfe said in his evidence, however, that a Mr Konstas, believed by Mr Wolfe to be "a member of Tjuringa", was present at meetings during which Mr Wolfe expressed his concern about exchange fluctuations; Mr Konstas said that he would try to see if an insurance arrangement could be made to cover the risk, within the Ipec group. Mr Wolfe told Mr Konstas he was content for the loan to proceed "on the basis that the cost to the company, including the insurance premium and the interest on the loan, was less than the amount that was paid by the company at the moment to the loans from the finance company and the Australian Bank". Mr Konstas said he agreed with that and would "fix it".

10. The loan agreement of 24 May 1975 provided for loans totalling 27 million Saudi riyals repayable by instalments at stated dates, the last being 2 June 1980; clause 7, however, gave a right to repay the loans ahead of time, subject to certain conditions. After the loan agreement was executed, Mr Wolfe continued to express his concern about the possibility of losses related to exchange fluctuations, as did Mr Konstas. Inquiries were set in train as to the availabity of insurance to cover the risk. Apparently as a result of them, an approach was made by a company named Peter Hodgkinson Pty. Ltd., offering a policy but at a premium which was not acceptable to the appellant. The evidence of Mr R.J. Maclure, which was also accepted, was to the effect that after rejection of the approach from that company, "later during the 1975 year", meetings of the appellant's directors were informed that attempts to obtain cover had failed. Mr Barton said, about the time that information was given, words to the effect:

"Tjuringa will provide the required cover by way of

an insurance policy. The premiums are to be

determined upon the basis of commercial rates

existing in the market."

Unfortunately, the detailed arrangements from that point on were not fully proved, as none of the witnesses called was familiar with them.

11. On 26 February 1976, Tjuringa wrote to its solicitors asking them to draw up documents providing for "forward exchange risk cover by way of an indemnity, to Australian National Hotels Ltd., against the risk of loss caused by exchange fluctions (sic) on the 27m Saudi Riyal loan". The instructions said there was to be a charge of $125,000 per annum for that cover and went on:

"Somehow provision should be made to allow for a

fluctuation in this annual charge, such fluctuation

to move in unison with the actual exchange

fluctuations."

There was no concluded agreement on that basis - i.e. none fulfilling the description in the letter of 26 February 1976.

12. On 21 April 1976, Mr Barton signed a document addressed to the appellant and headed "Cover Note". The body of it read as follows:

"This is to confirm that our Company holds your

Company covered and indemnified against loss by

reason of currency fluctuation in respect of the

principal owing by your Company under loan

Agreement dated 24th May 1975, securing advances

made in Saudi Arabian Riyals, between your Company

and a consortium of Arab Banks, such cover and

indemnity to apply and have effect during the

entire currency of the loan."

Mr Barton signed as chairman of Tjuringa; he gave no evidence. Nor did it appear directly how it came about that the cover note was executed by Mr Barton.

13. An invoice was tendered as having been issued by Tjuringa to the appellant, dated 30 April 1976; it charged a premium "in respect of Indemnity on currency fluctuation on Saudi Arabian Riyal loan" of a total of $330,000, being $150,000 for the period 31 July 1975 and $180,000 for the period to 31 January 1976. At some time in 1976, according to the evidence of a Mr Eakins, who was apparently also accepted, the appellant's accounting records showed entries debiting the appellant and crediting Tjuringa with the appropriate amounts of premium. The learned primary judge relied on these entries, as will appear, in determining in favour of the appellant the first issue referred to above, namely whether there was a binding agreement to pay premiums in the year ended 31 July 1976. They are further discussed, below.

14. On 27 May 1976, the cover was discussed at a meeting of directors of the appellant; the minutes recorded, rather cryptically:

"It was decided that Tjuringa Securities Limited be

requested to vary and expand the wording of the

Cover Note issued in respect to currency

fluctuation affecting repayments of loan monies in

Saudi Riyals."

15. There was no evidence as to the respects in which variation or expansion of the wording was desired. About the same time, Mr Wolfe, and a person engaged to audit the company's books, each commented separately upon deficiencies in the cover note and those comments were passed on to Mr Farrell by a note dated 28 May 1976 from the appellant's secretary; particular mention was made of the fact that the cover note did not state any consideration.

16. There was tendered an invoice dated 1 July 1976 from Tjuringa to the appellant, referring to a further premium due "on overseas currency fluctuation cushion policy re Saudi Arabian Riyal Loan", the period covered by the invoice being six months to 31 July 1976 and the amount $180,000. To that point, invoices were being prepared on the basis that the initial premium was $150,000, with further charges of $30,000 per month six monthly from 31 July 1975.

17. Suggestions were put that the invoice just referred to as well as its predecessor dated 30 April 1976 might have been predated, but there is no ground on which such a finding could be made.

18. On 9 July 1976, accountants engaged by Tjuringa gave a written advice to the effect that in their opinion premiums of the kind in question would be deductible under s.51 of the Act. That opinion was sent on 12 July 1976 by an officer of Tjuringa to its solicitor requesting that "yesterday]" there be provided a solicitor's opinion that Tjuringa could give the insurance and requesting "the insurance agreement itself for execution". On 21 July 1976, Mr Maclure sent a note to the solicitor referring to the necessity of preparing Tjuringa accounts, and asking for a letter indicating that the "ANH - Overseas currency fluctuation cushion policy" was an insurance policy and that Tjuringa did not need to be a licensed insurer.

19. On 7 September 1976, Tjuringa's solicitors wrote to it confirming their advice that there were no legal difficulties in Tjuringa's "underwriting the currency exchange risk of Australian National Hotels in relation to payments of principal under the Loan Agreement between Australian National Hotels Limited and certain Arab banks". On 30 September 1976, a meeting of directors of the appellant was held, at which it was resolved to approve and confirm an agreement by Tjuringa to indemnify the company against "its currency fluctuation liability" in respect of the loan agreement in question. On the same day, there was executed a deed to that effect, but it was dated 6 August 1975 - i.e. nearly fourteen months earlier.

20. The deed dated 6 August 1975 contained a covenant by Tjuringa to indemnify the appellant against "currency fluctuation liability". That expression was defined to mean, to put it simply, the difference between the sum borrowed in Australian dollars and that repaid in Australian dollars.

21. As to premium, the deed was consistent with the invoices referred to above, i.e. in providing for an initial premium of $150,000 for the period to 31 July 1975 and payments of $30,000 per month six-monthly thereafter. It should be mentioned that Mr Wolfe gave evidence that Mr Konstas told him, some weeks before 30 September 1976, that "he was proposing to charge basically $30,000 a month and I agreed with that". That was one of the circumstances relied on by the respondent in support of an argument that there was no concluded agreement during the year ended 31 July 1976.

22. On 30 November 1976, a bank official made a note of a conversation with Mr Gordon Barton, referring to the Saudi riyal loan in question. According to the note, Mr Barton said that:

"... the Group had anticipated a devaluation and, to

offset this for taxation purposes, Australian

National Hotels Limited has entered into a contract

of insurance with Tjuringa Securities Limited to

indemnify itself against exchange losses."

23. The note, part of which has just been quoted, was relied on as indicating the purpose of the whole transaction. However, the learned primary judge was satisfied that "the venture" was "not entered into in any way simply for the purpose of obtaining some taxation advantage". In arriving at that conclusion, he relied upon the evidence of Messrs Wolfe and Maclure and rejected the note just referred to as a "flimsy indication in the opposite direction". It does not appear that there is any sufficient ground on which the conclusion just mentioned could be upset.

24. On 30 June 1977, Tjuringa sent a further invoice to the appellant in respect of premium for the twelve months to 31 July 1977, in the sum of $360,000. That, again, was consistent with the written agreement entered into in September 1976. However, at some time in October 1977, Tjuringa sent a further invoice claiming:

"Additional premium payable for the year

ended 31st July, 1977 under Overseas

Currency Fluctuation Cushion Policy

dated 6th August, 1975 as agreed $150,000.00"

25. There was no evidence as to how it came about that this additional premium was charged. The inference seems inescapable that it was connected with a proposal, which was put to a meeting of directors of the appellant on 27 October 1977, to repay the Saudi riyal loan with moneys borrowed within Australia. That proposal was accepted at the meeting.

26. There followed some communications concerning the amount payable and the total premium. It appears that the Saudi riyal loan was repaid on 3 December 1977 and the parties calculated the amount payable under the policy as being $1,051,204. Insurance premiums receivable were calculated by Tjuringa on 14 December 1977, to be $1,020,000; that figure is arrived at by adding to the initial premium of $150,000 twenty-nine (being the proper number of months) times $30,000. On that basis, its loss was $31,204.

27. However, when settlement was effected on 20 December 1977 (on the basis of an exchange of cheques) Tjuringa charged a total premium of $1,170,000, being $150,000 more than the sum calculated in accordance with the agreement executed on 30 September 1976. The inference is that the $150,000 additional premium derived from the agreement mentioned in the invoice charging that sum, referred to above as having been sent in October 1977.

28. The appellant did not press, before the learned primary judge or in this Court, for allowance of a deduction in respect of that additional $150,000. However, the abandonment of that claim did not make it irrelevant: it throws light upon the character of the transaction between the appellant and Tjuringa. It is unusual, in an arm's length transaction, for an insurer to demand an extra premium for a risk, which it had bound itself to accept, for no apparent reason other than that the risk has become more onerous than it was expected to be. Similarly, it must be thought unusual conduct on the insured's part to agree to pay an extra premium after the loss has crystallised.

Agreement during year ended 31 July 1976

29. As is mentioned above, the learned primary judge held that there was a binding agreement to pay the appropriate premiums in respect of the year ended 31 July 1976 and that conclusion is challenged by the respondent.

30. His Honour expressed the view that it was likely that Mr Barton was the author of the idea of Tjuringa's providing cover. He noted that Tjuringa had "some accumulated tax losses which would have made it an attractive vehicle for the underwriting of the proposed risk and the receipt of premiums", but was satisfied that Tjuringa did not enter into the arrangement simply for the purpose of obtaining some taxation advantage.

31. His Honour went on to say that efforts were being made by persons responsible within the Ipec group to arrive at an agreement in final form - i.e. one to fill out the decision taken that Tjuringa provide the cover. His Honour expressed himself satisfied that "the parameters of the agreement had been set in the discussions between Mr Wolfe and Mr Konstas ..." and that "... what that premium was to be and how it was to be paid was the subject of discussion in the financial year ending 31 June 1976".

32. His Honour relied upon the letter to Tjuringa's solicitors dated 26 February 1976, mentioned above, as providing "an indication of matters under consideration at that stage". He pointed out that the proposal in that letter was not put into effect.

33. His Honour then pointed out that the accounting records of the appellant, insofar as they recorded payments of amounts to Tjuringa, indicated "a state of uncertainty as to the precise nature of the agreement". He referred to the letter of 12 July 1976 and that of 21 July 1976, which have already been mentioned, and proceeded:

"In my view, these letters provide a strong

indication that, after discussion and negotiation,

a firm agreement had been reached prior to the end

of 1976 fiscal year as to the terms of the

insurance arrangement and that all that was then

required was confirmation that Tjuringa could

legally enter into such an arrangement and the

formal Deed embodying the agreement."

34. The letters in question were the subject of some discussion before us. The body of the first read:

"I enclose photocopy of letter from Fell & Starkey

in connection with the deductibility of premiums

payable by ANH.

It is most urgent that we have (yesterday]) -

1. AA & H's opinion that TSL can give the

insurance providing the indemnity to ANH; and

2. the insurance agreement itself for execution."

35. Counsel for the respondent pointed out that the choice of the words "can give" in the paragraph numbered one accords ill with the theory that there was, on 12 July, a binding arrangement; on the other hand, the word "payable" in the introductory sentence perhaps points the other way. The second letter read as follows:

"This year we will endeavour to prepare all TSL

Group accounts early and have them audited and out

of the way within eight weeks of balance date.

In preliminary discussions with Brian Gray on

potential problem areas, one of the matters raised

was the above insurance policy and whether TSL

could enter into such a transaction without being a

licenced insurance company. Our understanding is

that one transaction is not 'carrying on the

business of an insurer'.

If this is the case, would you please provide us

with a suitable letter indicating that the policy

is an insurance policy and that for this one

transaction TSL is not required to be a licenced

insurer."

36. His Honour then turned to certain book entries which he said threw light on the matter. He remarked:

"In January, 1976 three amounts were debited to the

Appellant in favour of Tjuringa Securities Limited,

these being respectively $135,000; $62,500 and

$142,500, a total in all of $330,000. In May, the

cancelled cheque transaction for $187,500 already

referred to is shown. Clearly, this cheque would

have covered the two debit amounts of $125,000 and

$62,500 already referred to. Finally, on 3 July,

an amount of $180,000 was debited to the Appellant

in favour of Tjuringa Securities Limited."

37. In this passage, his Honour referred to the evidence of Mr Andrew Eakins, an accountant who kept the appellant's books of account at relevant times. His Honour apparently relied upon the dates given by Mr Eakins in his evidence in chief in support of the conclusion he reached that "a clear agreement was reached before the end of the year that the appropriate premium was to be $30,000 per month payable by way of appropriate book entries on the current account between the two companies."

38. However, Mr Eakins was in fact quite uncertain as to when the entries were made. The first invoice charging premium against the appellant was dated 30 April 1976. When referred to it, Mr Eakins said that "The physical entry would have been made some time after receipt of that invoice". He added that the entries were made prior to closing off the tax for July 1976.

39. He then said, twice, that the entries were made after July 1976 and finished by saying, in effect, that entries relating to the amounts in the invoice dated 30 April 1976 "would have been made" around the time he received the invoice, which he "would have received" some few days after 30 April 1976.

40. It should be added that Mr Eakins made it clear that in making such entries he relied, not upon any personal knowledge of the state of negotiations, but simply on directions he was given by the financial controller of the Ipec Group. It seems, with respect, impossible to base any finding as to the date on which a firm agreement was reached on the book entries, since one simply does not know when they were made.

41. As to the letters on which his Honour also relied, they are, in my respectful opinion, equivocal, being at least as consistent with a firm agreement's having been made after they were written as with the conclusion his Honour reached. Further, the letter of 7 September 1976, referred to above, is not written on the basis that the solicitors understood their client had made an underwriting agreement.

42. His Honour also relied upon the evidence of Messrs Wolfe and Maclure, but neither of them claimed to know when the final agreement was made. One might comfortably infer from the minute of 27 May 1976, quoted above, that it was made after that date, but precisely when was left unproven.

43. In the confused and incomplete state of the evidence placed before his Honour, one could not be dogmatic as to the date of the insurance agreement, but, in my respectful opinion, the onus being on the appellant, it failed to adduce such evidence as to give proper support to the conclusion that an agreement was made before 31 July 1976, or indeed at any time before the execution of the written agreement on 30 September 1976.

44. The matter was argued before us, correctly in my view, on the basis that the appellant could not succeed as to the year ended 31 July 1976 unless there were a concluded agreement during that year. In my opinion, there was not shown to be such an agreement, and the appellant's claim to deduct payments of premium allegedly made by book entry in that year must fail.

Capital or Revenue

45. The principal question is, in the end, whether the learned primary judge was right in holding the outgoings in question to be on capital account. His Honour remarked:

"The simple fact is that the payment of the premiums

was designed to result in the making good to the

Appellant of capital losses, should they occur. To

my mind, this indelibly stamps the payments with

the character of outgoings of a capital nature."

46. That was criticised by counsel for the appellant on the basis that insurance premiums are often intended to make good capital losses, but are nevertheless deductible, at least if the capital item to which the premium relates is an income producing asset such as a factory. No doubt that is so. Insurances designed to guard against adverse affects of the ordinary operations of the financial markets, however, raise special considerations.

47. Suppose a taxpayer holds a capital fund invested at interest. The fund may be adversely affected by a number of circumstances, the most obvious being that the "real value" of the money invested may fall on account of a diminution in the value of the currency - i.e., on account of inflation. Again, a general rise in interest rates may bring about a decline in the capital value of the securities held, assuming they reflect earlier, lower interest rates. A third mode of decline in capital value, relevant to the present case, is that a fund held in Australian securities may be said to decline in value by comparison with foreign currencies, if the Australian dollar falls.

48. Assuming the postulated taxpayer is not in a business part of which involves buying and selling such securities, none of these three events will be treated as a revenue loss, even if the capital loss is realised (in the second case) by a sale of securities.

49. Similar events may affect the capital structure of a taxpayer which is a long-term investor in shares, the simplest adverse event being, of course, a decline in the market value of the shares.

50. It is no doubt possible to make arrangements which might be described as being, or being equivalent to, insurance contracts, to guard against events of the sort I have been discussing. There is, for example, a species of contract available called portfolio insurance, which investors may engage in to protect the value of shares held. It is characteristic of all such arrangements that they involve accepting a certain known loss rather than the risk and uncertainty of the operation of financial markets.

51. The sort of insurance being considered need not be confined to personalty; no doubt it would be legally possible for an investor in real property to make a contract preserving him against the consequence of a decline in the market value of the property. What contracts of this sort have in common is that they are designed to guard against, not some unexpected catastrophe, but the everyday operation of the financial markets; in that sense, they may be thought of by some as "gambling contracts" and indeed arrangements having a similar purpose are commonly entered into on the futures markets in a speculative way.

52. It was common ground before us that the exchange loss relating to the Saudi riyals loan was on capital account: Commercial and General Acceptance Limited v. The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia (1977) 137 CLR 373, Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Hunter Douglas Limited (1983) 83 ATC 4,562. Counsel were unable to find any helpful authority, however, on the specific point whether the cost of insurance against such a loss would be deductible, or on the more general point whether insurance against the operation, on the value of a capital fund, of changes in the state of the financial markets would be deductible. Counsel for the appellant argued that a payment made to secure a capital fund and preserve it from diminution must be a revenue item, at least if the capital fund is or represents an income-earning entity. At first sight, the analogy between, say, fire insurance as securing the value of a physical entity and this insurance as preserving the value of another sort of capital is attractive; but moving by analogy from one marginal case to another can, in the end, produce an erroneous result.

53. In the ordinary use of the term "capital", a payment made to fix in advance a loss of capital due to the operation of the financial markets, rather than taking a chance on that operation would, in my opinion, be regarded as a capital item. There is, in general, no certainty of any loss because the markets may go either way, but there is a virtual certainty of fluctuation, and I suppose about a 50% chance of an adverse fluctuation. If the loss when simply allowed to occur is concededly a capital loss, I do not think a payment which represents the market's assessment of its chances of occurrence and its amount (plus no doubt a profit) can be regarded as producing a revenue item.

54. One view is that the idea that there is a fundamental distinction between an income loss or gain and a capital loss or gain resulting from business transactions is one having no sensible basis, and that money so made or lost is simply made or lost. The tax laws require one, however, to endeavour to preserve the distinction. It is my opinion that the distinction is best preserved by holding that a payment made to guard against the operation of the ordinary financial markets on the value of, or liabilities associated with, a capital fund takes its character from that of the loss being guarded against.

55. The considerations just mentioned apply with particular force to the facts of the present case. Here, as counsel for the respondent pointed out, when the "insurance" contract was made, the Australian dollar had already moved sharply down against the Saudi riyal and consequently the appellant's dollar liability had, at that time, risen substantially. Although it could not be postulated that there was a certainty of loss, there was a considerable likelihood of loss. If the appellant had then determined to "insure" against the loss and immediately repay the loan, the equivalence of the "premium" and the capital loss would have been more evident.

56. The other analogy on which counsel for the appellant relied was that of interest. As is explained above, there was at one stage discussion about making the premium equivalent to the interest saving, that is, the saving made by taking a loan in the foreign currency. However, there was no evidence that that was in fact done. Counsel's point, then, must be taken to be merely that one may treat the premiums as commercially equivalent to, or representing, the interest. All that can be said in favour of the argument is that strong currencies thought to be likely to hold their value against others are associated with low available interest rates. That circumstance, however, is quite insufficient to make the premiums in issue of the same character as interest payments.

57. In my view the appeals should be dismissed with costs.
