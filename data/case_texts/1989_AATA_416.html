ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Assessable Income - Association of importers and exporters of chemicals - Whether established for purpose of promoting the development of agricultural, pastoral, horticultural, viticultural, manufacturing or industrial resources - Whether income exempt from tax.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Commonwealth) S.23(h)

Boating Industries Association of New South Wales V. F.C.T. 85 A.T.C. 4,224

REASONS FOR DECISION

1 June 1989 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. In the twentieth century, for good and for ill, there has been a vast increase in the use of chemicals in society to the point where, according to ACIC ("The Australian Chemical Industry Council") almost all manufacturing industry is dependent on chemical products and many other industries, such as agriculture, mining and the energy industries, depend on chemical products to remain competitive. But despite the wealth of natural resources in this country, development relative to the use of chemicals has largely come from overseas. We have had to import chemicals and "know-how" suitable for our industries even though, in some instances, we have been influential in exporting chemical products and even "know-how" overseas. So pervasive is the influence of chemicals in our society that members of the industry are constantly concerned with Government in such matters as trade; occupational health; environmental considerations; personal safety; and provision for the transport and storage of chemicals.

2. In 1963 a number of chemical manufacturers came together to form the Australian Chemical Industry Council ("ACIC") as a company limited by guarantee. Its principal objective was:

"To promote and advance in the Commonwealth of Australia and elsewhere, trade commerce and industry in relation to chemicals made in Australia."

It has pursued that objective since and, at the time of hearing, had 37 major manufacturers as full members of ACIC and 38 associate members. It employed a full-time secretariat and had a budget of the order of $800,000 per annum. In recent years it has promoted the formation of the Chemical Confederation of Australia (CCA): an organisation which represents a consolidation of three industry groups which had existed with slightly different interests (the Australian Chemical Specialities Manufacturers Association (ACSMA) and the Agricultural and Veterinary Chemicals Association (AVCA)). It continues to be involved with another industry based group, the Australian Chemical Industry Group (ACIG).

3. However, those organisations were not the only organisations within the industry. Nor did they in all respects represent all within the industry. In about 1968 some members of the industry with a special interest in importing and exporting came together as a special interest group. The common interest which brought them together was their role as importers and exporters and the particular circumstance which prompted their association was an earlier tariff inquiry. Following that inquiry, on 22 April 1968, they caused a company limited by guarantee to be incorporated in the State of New South Wales.

They incorporated under the name "Chemical Importers & Exporters Council of Australia Limited" (CIECA). The objects of CIECA were broadly defined. Among them were:

"(a) To advance, advocate and encourage in the Commonwealth of Australia and its territories trade, commerce and industry relating to the import and/or export of chemicals and allied materials.

(b) To encourage and foster the consideration and free discussion of all matters and questions relating to the import and/or export of chemicals and allied materials including but not limited to studies of tariff effects and consequences and economic considerations affecting the prosperity and stability of the Australian community.

(c) To conduct educational and publicity campaigns ...

(d) To conduct an educational campaign amongst the people of Australia in order to engender and stimulate interest and knowledge in the import and export of chemicals and ...

(e) To conduct competitions and exhibitions in schools, universities and other educational institutions and ...

(f) To arrange deputations to Ministers of the Crown of the Commonwealth of Australia or of the several States of the Commonwealth or to any public body in relation to Bills presented to Parliament or action proposed to be taken or suggested calculated to effect or have any impact on the import and/or export

of chemicals and allied materials.

..."

4. Since incorporation the Association has pursued those objectives. Its current membership is of 38 companies. Its business is conducted by a Board of Directors of 10 appointees of members who meet monthly to consider and direct the affairs of the Association. CIECA has no full-time staff, preferring to employ services available on a part-time basis from the State Chamber of Commerce. Meetings are for the most part held in Sydney but on special occasions are held elsewhere.

5. CIECA operates on a modest budget, not greatly in excess of its operating costs. But the budget does not fully reflect all the costs of its operations. That is because members represented on the Board of Directors bear the costs of the attendance of their representatives, including all travel costs. Unlike ACIC, where subscriptions are calculated at a function of turnover, the subscription to CIECA is a fixed sum per annum.

6. Considering itself to be a "non-profit association" the Applicant did not file any income tax returns with the Commissioner until 1984. When it did so, it lodged returns for the years of income ended 30 June 1978 to 1983. The income and expenditure statements appearing in those returns disclose the following:

7. Having considered the returns, the Commissioner issued notices of assessment in relation to the years of income ended 30 June 1980 to 1983 (inclusive) in which he fixed upon taxable incomes of $562, $716, $925 and $1,631 and levied tax accordingly. No penalties for late returns were lodged. By notice dated 20 December 1984 the Applicant objected and, following disallowance of the objections, the Applicant requested reference to an independent tribunal for a review of the decisions upon its objections.

8. As the total tax involved in the assessments is less than $1,200 it is fortunate that, in those circumstances, its requests for reference was made when they were. Had they been made shortly thereafter the price to be demanded of it for such a single independent adjudication would have been $960. It is perhaps even more fortunate that at such later date the Commissioner did not assess the two earlier years. Had he done so the fees payable to challenge the community's claim to $1,459 in tax would have been $1,440.

9. In assessing the Association the Commissions made no allowance for any expenses which might have been considered to have been incurred in the course of earning the interest income. No objection is taken on that account. Rather the Applicant objects that it is not liable at all to income tax. It claim exemption from income tax by force of Section 23 of the Income Tax Assessment Act ( "the Act") which (so far as is material) provides:

"The following income shall be exempt from income tax:

...

(h) the income of a society or association not carried for the purposes of profit or gain to the individual members thereof, established for the purpose of promoting the development of aviation or of agricultural, pastoral, horticultural, viticultural, manufacturing or industrial resources of Australia;

... "

10. CIECA has contributed to the national development of policies and practices in relation to chemicals in Australia, both directly in its own name and indirectly through committees and associations in which it participates. I accept that what was to some extent detailed in the evidence through extracts from the President's reports was typical of the years in question. Those reports indicate that the type of matters dealt with were tariff inquiries; IAC inquiries into export concessions, manufacture involvement and 2% revenue duty; development of legislation on the notification and assessment of chemicals; regulation of health safety and environmental matters; export controls on potential chemical weapons; uniformity of material safety data sheets; waste minimisation of chemicals; chemicals, drugs, poisons and controlled substances; marking of buildings for hazardous chemicals (HAZCHEM); labelling and packaging standards; safety and warehousing; and educational matters, both through luncheon addresses to members and their guests and through the promotion of community education.

11. In particular I find that CIECA does not trade in any way. It does no more than invest at interest monies over the years subscribed by its members and provided by them for the financial support of the objectives of the Association.

12. The issue has to be determined by reference to the provisions of the Act as interpreted by the courts The correct interpretation of Section 23(h) of the Act was considered by the Supreme Court of New South Wales in Australian Insurance Association v F.C.T. (79 A.T.C. 4,569). The Association there in question was an association of insurance companies. They had come together as a company limited by guarantee. Their first objective was:

"(a) To promote and foster the Australian insurance and/or assurance industry and Australian Insurance and/or Assurance Companies both in the Commonwealth of Australia and overseas in all fields whether national, commercial, industrial, sociological or otherwise."

13. The claim to exemption failed, principally upon the basis that the "resources", promotion of the development of which could lead to exempt status, did not extend to "trade" and "commerce", such as the fields of insurance, notwithstanding the references in the objects of the Association and in common speech to the "insurance industry". As his Honour Sheppard J. said (at page 4574):

"An analysis of the section discloses that the first group of resources dealt with (omitting from consideration that of aviation) are all of primary industry, namely, agricultural, pastoral, horticultural and viticultural. Next are the manufacturing resources. It would seem to have been the draftsman's intention to distinguish between such resources and the final category of industrial resources. There is a question as to whether there are two separate and distinct categories, or whether there may not be some overlapping between the two. Whether that be so or not one must reach conclusion as to what type of resources were intended to be referred to as industrial resources as opposed to those which were embraced by the expression manufacturing resources'. If, as I think is the case plant and equipment, manpower, skill and know-how in manufacturing such articles as steel products, clothing and furniture, and such non-tangible commodities as gas and electricty, are 'manufacturing resources', one has to ask oneself what kinds of other resources there are which are industrial as distinct from manufacturing."

".................."

"There is a degree of specificity in the words used in the section. It refers to aviation, then to fall resources of primary industry, then to manufacturing and finally to industrial resources. The use of these various expressions does not suggest that the draftsman intended to give to the word 'industrial' any wide meaning intending to embrace business or commercial resources. If it had been intended to do so, it would have been quite unnecessary to use the word 'manufacturing' or to be as specific in relation to the primary industry resources which are mentioned. Certainly manufacturing resources would have been within industrial resources and a more general word to describe the resources of primary industry could have been used."

"....................."

"The building, mining and quarrying industries, and the resources which these industries have, are not only aptly described as manufacturing industries but they are industries in the accepted sense of the word. Industries in a similar category are the shipping and transport industries. Thus reference to the resources of those industries as the industrial resources of Australia does not impose upon the language any strain and enables the two expressions 'manufacturing resources' and 'industrial resources', to stand alongside each other and each to be given meaning and effect."

14. In my view, by the criteria expressed by his Honour, it is appropriate to refer to the "chemical industry": the industry which uses chemicals as a resource in mining, manufacturing and primary industry and in every facet of social life.

15. The most recent judicial authority on the section is the decision of the Supreme Court of New South Wales in Boating Industries Association of New South Wales v F.C.T. (85 A.T.C. 4,224) in which his Honour, Lusher J., had to consider claim to exemption by an association of a wide range of persons interested in boating (manufacturers, retailers, boat shed operators, importers and others) who, through the Association, engaged directly or indirectly in the development of the manufacturing, distribution and marketing of recreational boats, equipment related services. His Honour held that the "crux of the matter was his finding that:

"The Association's principal activity was safe boating not the promotion of Australian manufactured boats."

That being so, his Honour concluded that:

"The Association was not established for the purpose promoting the development of manufacturing resources Australia."

As his Honour said:

"Membership was not limited to manufacturers or ever Australian manufacturers, but extended to those with of interests in the boating industry, including imported not only of boats, but of manufactured gear accessories for use with boats. If the purpose was as sub-section requires, it might well be expected that importers of foreign manufactured boats and supplies would not have been eligible."

His Honour agreed with the view of Sheppard J. (ante) that the relevant purpose must be:

"The principal or dominant purpose for which the Association was established."

16. His Honour's comments as to "importers of fore manufactured boats and supplies" are of some relevance in present case, but they do not resolve it. The evidence is to what is exported is ordinarily the product of Austral industry; and that what is imported is not a finished product such as manufactured boats and supplies, but rather materials use in Australian primary and secondary industry. The distinction is of critical significance in my view. I find that the principal or dominant purpose of the Association was:

"The purpose of promoting the development of ... the agricultural, pastoral ..., manufacturing (and) industrial resources of Australia."

by the use of chemicals.

17. Two questions remain. It was argued for the Commissioner that the Association could not satisfy the test that the Association must:

"not (be) carried on for the purposes of profit or gain to individual members thereof".

It was argued that the end result of successful promotion must be to increase the market in chemicals to the financial advantage of members of the Association. So much, I would have thought, was unarguably correct. But if that were the correct test to be applied in the construction of the sub-section it would seem that there would be no scope for its application. Very few associations in the world of commerce and industry are established for altruistic purposes. However, in my view that is not the test.

18. In formal terms the constitution of the Association, as expressed in its Memorandum and Articles of Association, prohibits any distribution of profit to its members. Further, by Clause 7 of its Memorandum of Association, it is provided that any surplus upon winding up or dissolution:

"Shall not be paid to or distributed among the members of the Council but shall be given or transferred to some other institution or institutions having objects similar to the objects of the Council and which shall prohibit the distribution of its or their income and property among its or their members to any extent at least as great as is imposed on this Council under or by virtue of the paragraph hereof ..."

As to the prospect that members of the Association and others would profit from any enlargement of the commercial market, I am of the view that that is not of itself a relevant consideration. The enlargement of the market is one of the objectives "promoting the development" of any relevant industry. In my view the relevant test is satisfied provided that members do benefit "as members". I find that the Association does not have as an object the generation of increased profits to any one or more members to the exclusion of others or have as an object equal or equitable distribution of any increased market between its members to the exclusion of others. The argument the Commissioner fails.

19. The final difficulty arises out of the terms of section and the limited evidence surrounding the circumstances attending the incorporation of the Association. The sub-section refers to Associations "established for the purpose of promoting industries. The point was touched on by Lusher J. in the Boat Industries case when he said:

"The section itself gives a criterion, namely, established for the purpose stated. The requirement is 'established' not 'involved'. This admission tends to concentrate on the result or effect of the activities (some of them) of the Association as having the promotion development results rather than to look at the purpose the establishment of the body. It does not follow that body whose activities or involvement have the effect result of promoting the development of manufacturing resources of Australia, who otherwise are within section as to the disposition of income, is necessarily established for that purpose. They could be established for another purpose altogether but still have the res mentioned."

20. But the difficulty I have is somewhat different Having considered the matter I am satisfied that the term "established" is not to be used in the narrow sense of considering only the motives and objectives which led to the incorporation. For to so narrowly construe the term would seem to suggest that, provided the organisation was incorporated for the relevant dominant purpose, it would not matter in later years that it no longer-pursued them. Conversely, on that view it would also seem that, if an organisation not originally incorporated for the required dominant purpose later came to be wholly committed to the pursuit of such purposes, it could not satisfy the requirements of the sub-section. Any such construction is in my view unreasonable and is to be rejected. In my opinion it is sufficient that the question be considered as to whether during the period under review the organisation was in existence and was operated and maintained in an established or stable condition as an organisation having as its principal or dominant purpose, one of the purposes provided for in the sub-section.

21. The order of the Tribunal will be that the decisions of the Commissioner upon the objections under review shall be varied and the objections wholly allowed.
