Longstaff and Australian Atomic Energy Commission [1985] AATA 394 (13 February 1985)

Last Updated: 15 February 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Compensation - injury to toe - surgery to correct longstanding hammer toe - whether cost and consequential incapacity compensable.

Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees') Act 1971 s37(1), s45

DECISION AND REASONS

Re: Walter Longstaff

And: Australian Atomic Energy Commssion

No 84/298

AAT Decision No 1994

R C Jennings QC, (Deputy President)

Date: 13 February 1985

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No 84/298

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: WALTER LONGSTAFF

(Applicant)

And: AUSTRALIAN ATOMIC ENERGY COMMISSION

(Respondent)

DECISION

Tribunal: R C Jennings QC, (Deputy President)

Date: 13 February, 1985.

  1. The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.
  2. Liberty to apply is reserved.

R C Jennings,

Presiding Member

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

)

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION ) No 84/298

Re: WALTER LONGSTAFF

(Applicant)

And: AUSTRALIAN ATOMIC ENERGY COMMISSION

(Respondent)

REASONS FOR DECISION

1. On 19 July 1983 Mr Walter Longstaff bruised his toe at work when he accidentally kicked an air conditioner. The liability of his employer, the respondent, to pay compensation in respect of that personal injury was admitted but the question to be determined on this application to review a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner for Employees' Compensation is whether there is any liability in the respondent to meet the cost of surgery to correct a hammer toe and to recompense the applicant for the incapacity for work which followed that procedure.

2. The applicant who was employed as an electrical foreman lost three days work following the accident and returned on Monday 25 July to do some sedentary office work. But the toe had become infected and the next day Dr Ow Yang prescribed antibiotics and certified him unfit for work until 29 July. He also referred him to an orthopaedic surgeon, Dr Vote. Neither of these persons gave evidence but three reports by Dr Vote dated 9 August 1983 and 2 September and 28 September 1984 were admitted. The respondent called Dr. Ehrlich who supplemented his report of 29 October 1984 and was cross-examined.

3. When the applicant first saw Dr Vote on 5 August 1983 there were still signs of chronic inflammation of the injured toe and a further course of antibiotics was prescribed.

4. Dr Vote noted that the toe was a longstanding hammer toe. The applicant who is 41 recalled that he had become aware of it as a small child but that he had never been bothered by it at all. From his description of its condition following the trauma of 19 July Dr Vote described the infection as "quite severe" and as "a significant staphlycoccal infection". On 9 August he wrote:

"I certainly think this toe needs correction as he will have further problems with footwear and local trauma if it is not corrected. However, I feel at this stage I feel it would be better to wait a week or two and give him antibiotics to settle down the infection and then admit him for a corrective arthroplasty of the distal interphalangeal joint. In the interim he is quite fit to continue work".

5. When the applicant saw Dr. Vote again on 19 August a decision was made to operate four days later. At that time the applicant described the toe as still painful although the infection had completely cleared leaving a small hole in the nail.

6. On 2 September 1984 Dr. Vote wrote the following report to the applicant's solicitors:

"Mr. Longstaff first consulted me on 5/8/83.

His presenting problems related to his left second toe. He had a long standing hammer deformity of the second toe and this deformed toe was subject to an episode of trauma at work on the 19/7/83. It subsequently became infected and at the time of consultation his toe was still quite swollen and tender over the nail.

It was felt that he should initially be treated by antibiotics to allow the local inflammation to subside and subsequently corrective surgery on his toe was indicated. This was carried out at Kareena Hospital on 23/8/83.

His convalescence was uneventual apart from some difficulty removing the pin which had been inserted in the toe to hold in the corrected position. It was necessary to admit him to hospital to have this procedure carried out.

He was reviewed on 13/10/83 and 10/11/83. At the later date he was signed fit to resume work on 11/11/83.

He should have no further trouble with his toe.

Certainly the indication for surgery was a long standing deformity rather than an acute episode of infection which followed the episode of trauma on 19/7/83".

He wrote again on 28 September in the following terms:

"Thank you for your correspondence 10th September 1984.

On reading my report of 2 September, 1984 I do wish to amend in part the last paragraph.

I considered that because of the long standing deformity and established infection it was quite likely that further bouts of infection would continue if nothing were done to correct the problem. In that light Mr Longstaff's surgery was precipitated by the injury and subsequent infection. Notwithstanding that the original problem is one of a deformed toe and the surgery was designed to correct this problem and subsequently prevent further infection".

7. The solicitors' letter of 10 September was not produced although during the hearing the applicant's counsel claimed it was a privileged communication. However, I was not required to rule on this claim because ultimately the respondent's counsel withdrew his objection to production of the reports without the intervening correspondence.

8. Counsel for the applicant submitted that the operation to the toe was necessary as a result of the trauma and therefore the incapacity associated with it was compensable. He said that the applicant was entitled to rely on his treating surgeon's advice. He also submitted that the effect of Dr Ehrlich's evidence was that if the toe had not been straightened it was possible that rubbing on the shoe may have brought about further infection. This he said supported the view which Dr Vote expressed in his "amending" report of 28 September that:

"because of the long standing deformity and established infection it was quite likely that further bouts of infection would continue if nothing were done to correct the problem".

9. Counsel for the respondent relied on Dr Vote's report of 2 September 1984 in which he firmly stated:

"Certainly the indication for surgery was a long standing deformity rather than an acute episode of infection which followed the episode of trauma on 19 July 1983".

  1. He argued that the subsequent report was no more than an attempt by "playing with words" to put a causal connection between the injury and the operation.
  2. It would certainly seem that the word "established" to describe the infection adds nothing. Dr Ehrlich said in effect that he could not understand what reason Dr Vote might have for asserting that further bouts of infection would continue merely because infection had occurred on one occasion. He said he would expect any toe, hammer or otherwise, to return to its pre-injury condition after any infection from bruising had settled. I accept this evidence.

12. The liability of the respondent to compensate the applicant for the cost of medical treatment is confined to treatment obtained "in relation to the injury". s37(1) Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act 1971 ("The Act"). Liability to compensate him for total incapacity for work is dependent on the injury resulting in that incapacity (s45).

13. The ultimate question for the Tribunal in this case therefore is "was the surgical treatment and incapacity thereby occasioned" in relation to the injury or a result of the injury or was it no more than an operative procedure performed to correct a long standing deformity after the consequences of injury were spent?

14. Dr Ehrlich did not quarrel with Dr Vote's decision to operate. He did not have the advantage of seeing the toe. But I am not persuaded by Dr Vote's latest report or any other evidence or argument that the infection which followed the injury was an indication for the surgery. Indeed surgery could not properly be performed until infection had cleared. It could have been performed many months later. The fact that it was done a little over a month after the injury does nothing to advance the proposition that it was "in relation to the injury" or that the incapacity for work which followed resulted from the injury.

  1. Likewise I cannot accept the submission that infection having occurred the toe became predisposed to further injury and infection. I prefer the oral evidence of Dr Ehrlich to Dr Vote's efforts to improve the applicant's case by "amending" his earlier report.
  2. It is not necessary to consider submissions as to onus of proof or whether the applicant was entitled to rely on his surgeon's advice because the provisions of s37(2) of the Act only apply to medical treatment "obtained in relation to the injury". I have found that the surgical treatment was not of this character.

17. The decision under review is affirmed.
