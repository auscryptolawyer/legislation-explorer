REASONS FOR DECISION

  1. This is a review of objection decisions made by the respondent on 1 August 1994 in relation to the income tax returns lodged by the applicant for each of the years ended 30 June 1985, 1986, 1987 and 1988 (“the relevant years of income”). The objection decisions were made by the respondent pursuant to s14ZY of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 [“TAA”] notwithstanding that the respondent did not have before it a formal taxation objection or objections which, in the opinion of the Tribunal, complied with the strictures of s14ZU of the TAA. In the opinion of the Tribunal the written objection lodged by the applicant pursuant to s14ZW of the TAA failed to state fully and in detail the grounds upon which the applicant relied, as required by that section (c.f. CCH Federal Tax Reporter, para. 972-740 and Williams J in H R Lancey Shipping Co Pty Ltd v FCT (1951) 9 ATD 267).

  2. The Tribunal had before it documents [“T documents”] filed by the respondent pursuant to s37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 [“AAT Act”]. The notice of objection referred to above is at T48 (pages 429a and 429b). That document is dated 20 December 1991 and although it directs the attention of the addressee, the “Australian Taxation Office” to “any Assessments or Amended Assessments issued for any years”, the respondent treated it as an objection against notices of assessment for the above four years of income, all of which issued on 30 October 1991. The Tribunal notes that prior to the issue of the assessments in question the respondent wrote to the applicant on 7 November 1990 (T16, pp 85-108) providing him with details of proposed amendments and offering an opportunity to discuss them. That meeting was eventually held on 20 June 1991, after the applicant had postponed it from an earlier agreed time in April 1991, (T23, pp 121-129). Subsequent to the receipt of the applicant’s so called “Notice of Objection” dated 20 December 1991(supra), the respondent wrote to him requesting an elaboration of grounds in conformity with s185 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”); (T51, pp 432-433). This the applicant failed to do.

  3. In preliminary proceedings of the Tribunal pursuant to s33 of the AAT Act, the Tribunal, on 10 November 1994, directed the applicant to file, within twenty one days, a detailed statement of grounds upon which he relies. Although out of time the applicant wrote to the Tribunal on 25 January 1995 setting out the grounds upon which he relies. There being no objection by the respondent, the Tribunal accepts this document as a circumscription of the grounds upon which the applicant relies. It is by reference to that letter that at the hearing, and by consent of the parties, the Tribunal settled the scope of the grounds which are set out in detail below. If the Tribunal needs authority for this concession favouring the applicant then it relies on the joint judgment of Lochart, Burchett and Hill JJ in Lighthouse Philatelics Pty Ltd v FCT 91 ATC 4942, which, although concerned with s190(a) of the Act, would be equally applicable to s14ZZK of the TAA Act, the current provision, and which, in the present context, is couched in the same terms as the previous provision.

  4. At the hearing the applicant was initially represented by Mr Ross, a barrister and the respondent by Mr Maloney, a departmental advocate.

  5. At the commencement, Mr Ross requested an adjournment of proceedings, submitting that as he had only been instructed some two weeks previously he has not had sufficient time to prepare. Mr Maloney objected in the strongest terms to an adjournment. The Tribunal decided that no adjournment should be given. Its reasons for this are that the applicant had more than reasonable time and opportunity to prepare for the hearing. Also, he has experience and qualifications which ought to equip him to deal properly with the matter. He is a graduate in Commerce and was, before, during and after the income years in question, either practicing as a registered tax agent or working on commission preparing income tax returns for a registered tax agent. The applicant has had specific and extensive experience preparing returns for persons and partnerships practicing professions and in attending to their income taxation affairs generally. This experience, on his own admission (letter of 25 January 1995) included the preparation of over a hundred objections and pursuit of those with the respondent. The T documents reveal that the applicant has been provided with timely and sufficient information to be fully apprised of the basis for the assessments to which he has objected. All requests (whether objectively viewed as reasonable) for information from the respondent have been responded to giving the applicant plenty of time to prepare for the presentation of his case. Finally, the T documents reveal a pattern of behaviour by the applicant consistent with thwarting the proper administration of the respondent’s statutory obligation under s8 of the Act. The Tribunal is sympathetic to the submission made by Mr Maloney that this rather late request for an adjournment was but another “antic” engaged in by the applicant to delay the due process of this matter.

  6. The taxpayer, apart from handing up three exhibits [Ex. A1, A2 and A3], declined to give evidence or call witnesses despite having informed the Tribunal, in response to a direction, in a letter dated 15 March 1995 (over two months prior to the hearing) that he would be giving evidence as would six named persons and the “Manager of the Commonwealth Bank in Wembley”, the “Loans Officer - Commonwealth Bank in Wembley” and “clients of the Partnership of S Financial Consultants”. Therefore, the Tribunal relies on the exhibits and the T documents.

  7. From the contents of the applicant’s letter of 25 January 1995, the Tribunal summarised the grounds upon which the applicant relies, as follows:

(a) Whether there existed, for the relevant years of income, a partnership as defined in subsection 6(1) of the Act, which carried on the business of S Financial Consultants.



(b) Whether the respondent was correct in calculating the assessable income, for the purpose of s25(1) of the Act, of the taxpayer (or the partnership) derived from S Financial Consultants for the relevant years of income.



(c) Whether expenses incurred by the applicant (or the partnership) in the relevant year or years of income in contesting the elections for the City of A are allowable deductions under s51(1) or any other provision of the Act.



(d) Whether telephone and motor vehicle expenses incurred by the applicant (or the partnership) in the relevant year or years of income are allowable deductions under s51(1) or any other provision of the said Act.



(e) Whether the partnership known as C Hill carried on business in the relevant years of income and if it did, whether deductions claimed in the relevant years of income were incurred giving rise to a partnership loss under s92(2) as claimed by the applicant in the relevant years of income.



(f) Whether the penalties imposed by the respondent as additional tax in respect of the relevant years of income, under Part VII of the Act, should be remitted to a greater extent pursuant to s227(3) of the Act.



  1. The applicant was granted an adjournment to consider whether he wished to extend or amend those grounds. Mr Maloney was also offered an opportunity to comment on the grounds. The applicant made no request to alter the grounds in any respect. Mr Maloney submitted, in relation to ground (e), should the Tribunal find, as a matter of fact, that the partnership did carry on business then the respondent takes issue in relation to the quantum of deductions claimed by that partnership, requiring the applicant to produce evidence of the expenses having been incurred. Apart from this and with the exception of ground (b) above, the respondent contends that quantum is not an issue.

  2. After the short adjournment Mr Ross withdrew from representing the applicant. The applicant then sought, pursuant to s34(4) of the AAT Act, to object to the Tribunal being constituted by a member who had previously conducted a conference under s34, in the matter. That subsection reads:

“34(4)If:

(a) a conference held in accordance with this section in respect of a

proceeding is presided over by a member of the Tribunal; and

(b) a party to the proceeding who, or a representative of whom, was

present at the conference notifies the Tribunal before, or at the

commencement of, the hearing that he objects to that member

participating in the hearing;

that member is not entitled to be a member of the Tribunal as constituted for the purposes of the proceeding.”



  1. The Tribunal ruled that this objection, neither having been made “before, or at the commencement of, the hearing” was too late. The hearing at that time had been in progress for over one-hour. The Tribunal observes in this respect that it would be very strange indeed if either party, disenchanted with the way the proceedings were progressing, could use the simple expedient of removing their legal representative and then have the hearing abandoned by dint of s34(4).

  2. The applicant was then invited to present his evidence, which he declined to do.

EXISTENCE OF A PARTNERSHIP KNOWN AS S FINANCIAL SERVICES



  1. Subsection 6(1) of the Act provides:

In this Act, unless the contrary intention appears:

“partnership” means an association of persons carrying on business as partners or in receipt of income jointly, but does not include a company;”



  1. Whether a partnership, for the purposes of the Act, exists is a question of fact; Morden Rigg & Co & R B Eskrigge v Monks (1923) 8 TC 450. The oft quoted passage of Lord President Clyde in I R Commrs v Williamson (1928) 14 TC 335 bears repeating in this context:

“You do not constitute or create or prove a partnership by saying that there is one. The only proof that a partnership exists is proof of the relations of agency and of community of losses and profits and of the sharing in one form or another of the capital of the concern; the only proof of a partnership consists in proof of these things.”



  1. The evidence before the Tribunal in this respect comprises:

- the result of a register of business names search carried out by the respondent (referred to at T59, p.452). Those registered, at various times, as owners of the business name were:



the applicant 13 April 1984 to 22 April 1985

the applicant’s spouse 13 April 1984 to 22 April 1985

the applicant’s mother 13 April 1984 to 10 August 1984

an unrelated person 22 April 1985 to 21 April 1987



- copies of two Commonwealth Bank bank statements dated 30 December 1994 (Ex. A1) and 13 September 1991 (T29, p 402) (both subsequent to the years in question) in the name of the applicant and his spouse as the proprietors of the registered business name, S Financial Consultants. These each show an overdrawn balance and no relevant transactions.



- a copy of a cardboard file cover used to bind reports provided by S Financial Consultants which shows that business name and on the back thereof, the applicant’s name, the business’ street and post office box addresses and an address at Council House, A.



- a copy of the applicant’s Tax Agent’s client list issued by the respondent on 31 January 1988 showing the name of the taxpayer only. It bears no reference to the business name nor a partnership; (Ex. A1).



- copies of S Financial Services statements of account sent to various clients during 1987 and 1988, all of which show the business name and the name of the applicant, his university post nominals and “Councillor - City of A” with addresses as mentioned above; (Ex. A1).



  1. None of the above evidence supports “proof of the relations of agency and of community of losses and profits and of the sharing in one form or another or the capital of the concern” as referred to by Lord President Clyde in Morden Rigg. There is no evidence that the capital of the partnership was contributed by those in whom the business name was registered, there are no balance sheets to reflect the capital interests of those persons and in any event the income tax returns for the years in question show the “partners” as having shared the net income in each year disproportionately as follows:

“Partner” Share of “net income”

1983/34 1984/85 1985/86 1986/87 1987/88

$ $ $ $ $

Applicant’s mother 162 - - - -

Applicant’s spouse 6,222 1,462 5,667 - -

Applicant 1,584 11,786 3,344 20,115 24,680



  1. The T documents indicate that after April 1985 the business was transferred to another registered tax agent who retained the applicant on a commission basis. In that event there would be no community income in that respect as the income from commissions was derived by the applicant alone.

  2. The evidence before the Tribunal supports the respondent’s objection decision that at all material times the business was that of the applicant and was not being conducted in partnership. There was no evidence that either clients or others dealing with S Financial Consultants believed they were dealing with a partnership. The way that the applicant chose to allocate the so called “net income of the partnership” appears to be an attempt to alienate personal exertion income for income tax purposes without any underlying legal support.

  3. In the absence of evidence to the contrary the Tribunal concludes, in relation to ground (a) above, that the applicant has not discharged the burden of proof as required by s14ZZK(b) of the TAA.

CALCULATION OF ASSESSABLE INCOME DERIVED FROM S FINANCIAL SERVICES.



  1. No evidence was provided by the applicant to support his submission that the respondent was in error in making adjustments to the amount of gross fees earned by the tax agency business as disclosed in appendix 1 to T28 at p.179. The respondent, as a result of an extensive audit evidenced in the T documents, adjusted the disclosed tax return assessable income from the tax agency business of the applicant. The adjustments were to reflect a cash basis of earnings (c.f. C of T (SA) v Executor Trustee & Agency Co of South Australia (Carden’s case) [1932] HCA 25; (1932) 48 CLR 26, FCT v Firstenberg 76 ATC 4141 and FCT v Dunn 89 ATC 4141). The applicant adopted an obscure basis which neither complies with the conventional alternative “cash” or “accruals” bases established by the above decisions, among others. When queried by the Tribunal during his submissions (Transcript pp 55-57) it became apparent that fees received in particular months would not necessarily be treated as derived then (or earlier upon the issue of the client fee account).

  2. The Tribunal accepts that the respondent, in making the assessment amendments as shown at page 179 of the T documents has discharged the duty imposed upon him under s8 of the Act and that, in respect of ground (b) above, the applicant has not discharged the burden of proof as required by s14ZZK(b) of the TAA.

EXPENSES INCURRED IN CONTESTING THE CITY OF A COUNCIL ELECTIONS



  1. The respondent’s audit report refers, at pages 349-350 of the T documents, to this item in the following terms:

“Section 74A of the ITAA provides for a deduction of up to $1,000 for expenditure incurred by a taxpayer during the year ended 30 June 1986, or subsequent years of income, in contesting local government elections. The $1,000 limit applies to each election contested, whether or not the expenditure has been incurred over one or more years of income.



In (the applicant’s) income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1986 there was a claim for a deduction of $1,000 in respect of expenses incurred in contesting an election in the City of A. However, as a result of the audit it has been established that S Financial Consultants has also claimed deductions during the years ended 30 June 1986 and 1987 for expenses incurred in relation to the same election, and in the year ended 30 June 1985 for expenses incurred in relation to a previous election. These expenses were claimed by S Financial Consultants as advertising, printing, secretarial and postage.”



  1. The report goes on to say that as a deduction for the year ended 30 June 1986 was allowed to the applicant as claimed in his return, therefore no deductions for the 1985, 1986 or 1987 years are allowable to S Financial Consultants. These amounted to $1,153, $3,359 and $2,221 respectively.

  2. The applicant submitted that the deductions disallowed (presumably to the extent not claimed twice) are properly allowable under s51(1) of the ITAA. That subsection denies outgoings of a private nature. The applicant submitted that he used his position as a councillor of the City of A to promote his business and obtain new clients. Even if there was evidence to support this contention (which there was not), that being so, the Tribunal would find that the expenses, at best, are of a capital nature, incurred to expand the business’ profit-yielding structure; (c.f. Ronpibon Tin NL v FCT [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 and Softwood Pulp and Paper Ltd v FCT 76 ATC 4439). There is no nexus between the direct derivation of assessable income and the role as a councillor of the City of A, that being an honorary office of community service carrying no remuneration. There is no sufficient nexus between the incurring of election expenses and the derivation of assessable income. The deduction under s74A, to the extent permitted, is a concession, overcoming the provisions of s51(1) which operate to deny a deduction for any outgoings of a private nature. The applicant has been allowed a permissible deduction under s74A and no further deductions in this respect are allowable.

TELEPHONE AND MOTOR VEHICLE EXPENSES DISALLOWED



  1. The respondent’s audit report refers, at page 350 of the T documents, to the claims for motor vehicle expenses in the following terms:

“For the years ended 30 June 1984 to 30 June 1986 S Financial Consultants has claimed a deduction for 100% of (the applicant’s) motor vehicle expenses. In order to confirm that (the applicant’s) motor vehicle was used solely for business purposes he was requested to provide the documentary evidence on which the claims were based. The documentary evidence which he provided to support the claims consisted of a one page hand-written explanation ... (in which he stated) that when S Financial Consultants was operated from an office in South A he lived adjacent to an income producing rental property. Consequently, he has claimed that his travel to and from work was in fact travel between two income producing properties.”



  1. There is no evidence to support the usage of the vehicle. The respondent has reduced the claims by 20% on the basis that, pursuant to s51(1), the extent to which the vehicle was used for income producing purposes was estimated to be 80%. Even if, as the applicant asserted in the T documents, that his home was adjacent to a rent producing property, it is not apparent how the passive leasing of a property can constitute the carrying on of a business and that the applicant was travelling between businesses and on business when travelling from that property to his office. The law is well settled that in circumstances such as these, expenses of travel from home to the place where one works are not deductible under s51(1) of the Act (c.f. Haley v FCT; Lunney v FCT (1958) 100 CLR 478; see remarks of Williams, Kitto and Taylor JJ). In any event the evidence as to the location of the applicant’s office during the years of income in question is somewhat equivocal since it is apparent that for some of the time the applicant operated from his home. Notwithstanding, the Tribunal concludes, in relation to this aspect of ground (d) above, that in the absence of evidence to the contrary the applicant has not discharged the burden of proof as required by s14ZZK(b) of the TAA.

  2. Again, at T28, page 351 of the T documents the respondent provides reasons for disallowing 20% of the telephone calls claimed for the years ended 30 June 1984 to 30 June 1986. These claims, it seems, relate to the home telephone and for at least some of the time the applicant conducted his business from home. In his submissions the applicant argued that he was entitled to claim 100% of telephone charges even though he admits that the telephones were used to make private calls as well as business calls. He maintains that it is reasonable to assume that all business telephones are used for private calls but it is not the custom for those businesses to differentiate between calls made by employees for private purposes and those made in the course of business. Therefore, he opines, the same basis should be adopted for him, making no adjustment for private usage of the telephone which was primarily and predominantly a business instrument.

  3. The applicant’s submissions must fail on two counts. Firstly, if employees use their employer’s telephone for private use this is a cost of employing that person and at worst, being a fringe benefit the value of the benefit would not be assessable to the employee. Secondly, s51(1) requires the respondent to allow a deduction to the extent to which the outgoing is incurred in (the course of) deriving assessable income. The provision requires apportionment (c.f. Ronpibon Tin NL v FCT [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47) where the outgoing serves one or more purposes. Apportionment may be by dissection, where the facts allow identity of the component parts of the outgoing as directed to allowable and non-allowable purposes, or by estimation where there is no evidence of the actual amounts incurred in meeting the allowable and non-allowable objectives. There is no evidence in the present case which allows the respondent to identify the cost of business calls as opposed to the cost of private calls. Therefore, in relation to this aspect of ground (d) above, the applicant has not therefore discharged the burden of proof as required by s14ZZK(b) of the TAA, that the adjustment to disallow 20% of the outgoings claimed is unwarranted.

WHETHER THE PARTNERSHIP KNOWN AS C HILLS CARRIED ON BUSINESS AT ALL MATERIAL TIMES



  1. In each of his income tax returns for the years of income ended 30 June 1987 and 1988 the applicant claimed a deduction for a partnership loss incurred by a partnership known as C Hills, of $3,390 (T10, p56) and $8,685 (T13, p71) respectively. The respondent reduced these claims by $2,627 and $5,310 respectively on the basis that there was no business of primary production being carried on on the 400 acre (~180 hectare) property owned by the partnership. The only income returned by the partnership was from agistment and there was no evidence to indicate that that partnership intended to conduct a business of primary production on the property in the future. The applicant has not since produced any evidence to refute the respondent’s conclusions.

  2. Agisting another’s livestock does not ordinarily constitute the carrying on of a business. Agistment fees ordinarily are in the nature of rent. However, where a land owner is charged with the management, maintenance and care of the animals agisted then it is possible that the person is carrying on a business, the reward for which is the agistment fee. This is more likely if the level of the agistment fee depended on the effective management, maintenance and care of the animals. For example, if a land owner agreed with the owner of a herd of cattle to ensure their good health, proper veterinary care and husbandry of the progeny, marketing of their bodily produce and maintenance of the herd, then that land owner may be carrying on a business of primary production as that term is defined in s6(1) of the Act, which, so far as it is relevant provides:

s6(1) “primary production” means production resulting directly from:

the cultivation of land;

the maintenance of animals or poultry for the purpose of selling them or their bodily produce, including natural increase;

...

...; or

...

and includes ....



  1. The Tribunal does not have any evidence before it either supporting the list of expenses claimed in the relevant income tax returns for C Hills nor evidence of the extent to which the activities of the partners of that partnership engaged in activities which would amount to the carrying on of a business of primary production: (c.f. the joint judgment of Bowen CJ and Franki J in Ferguson v FCT 79 ATC 4261 where their Honours found that there was evidence of a considerable system and organisation in relation to the breeding of cattle). For this reason the Tribunal finds, in relation to ground (e) above, that the applicant has not discharged the burden of proof as required by s14ZZK(b) of the TAA to satisfy the Tribunal that the amounts disallowed should be allowed as deductions under the Act.

WHETHER THE ADDITIONAL TAX IMPOSED SHOULD BE REMITTED TO A GREATER EXTENT UNDER S227(3)



  1. The applicant’s income tax returns for the years under review disclosed no taxable income for any of the years ended 30 June 1985, 1986 and 1987 and a taxable income of $4,688 in respect of the year of income ended 30 June 1988. As a result of the respondent’s audit of those, and the two prior year’s income tax returns, the taxpayer was assessed for income tax and Medicare levy. Medicare levy, by s251R(7) is taken to be “income tax” or “tax” for the purpose of Part VII of the Act which provides for additional tax penalties.

  2. The respondent increased the assessed taxes by additional tax pursuant to s223 of the Act (as it was in October 1991 when the assessments issued). The tax and additional tax is set out below:



  1. Section 223 of the Act relevantly provides:

S223(1) Where -

(a) a taxpayer -

(i) makes a statement to a taxation officer, or to a person other than

a taxation officer for a purpose in connection with the operation of

this Act or the regulations, that is false or misleading in a material

particular; or

(ii) omits from a statement made to a taxation officer, or to a person

other than a taxation officer for a purpose in connection with the

operation of this Act or the regulations, any matter or thing

without which the statement is misleading in a material particular;

and

(b) the tax properly payable by the taxpayer exceeds the tax that would

have been payable by the taxpayer if it were assessed on the basis

that the statement were not false or misleading, as the case may be,

the taxpayer is liable to pay, by way of penalty, additional tax equal to double the amount of the excess.

...



(7) Where a person omits from a return furnished under or pursuant to this Act or the regulations, being a return of income derived by the person, a partnership or a trust estate during a period, any assessable income derived by the person, the partnership or the trust estate, as the case may be, during the period, the person shall, for the purposes of this section, be taken to have made a statement in the return to the effect that the person, the partnership or the trust estate, as the case requires, did not derive the assessable income during the period.



(8) A reference in this section to a statement made to a taxation officer is a reference to a statement made to a taxation officer orally, in writing, in a data procession device or in any other form and, without limiting the generality of the foregoing, includes a statement -

(a) made in an application, certificate, declaration, notification,

objection, return or other document made, given or furnished , or

purporting to be made given or furnished, under or pursuant to this

Act or the regulations;

(b) made in answer to a question asked of a person under or pursuant

to this Act or the regulation;

(c) made in any information furnished, or purporting to be furnished,

under or pursuant to this Act or the regulations; or

(d) made in a document furnished to a taxation officer otherwise than

under or pursuant to this Act or the regulations,

but does not include a statement made in a document produced pursuant to paragraph 264(1)(b).



  1. Subsection 227 relevantly provides:

s227(1) The Commissioner shall make an assessment of the additional tax payable by a person under a provision of this Part.

(2) ...

(3) The Commissioner may, in the Commissioner’s discretion, remit the whole or any part of the additional tax payable by a person under a provision of this Part, but, for the purposes of the application of sub-section 33(1) of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 to the power or remission conferred by this subsection, nothing in this Act shall be taken to preclude the exercise of the power at a time before an assessment is made under sub-section (1) of the additional tax.



  1. Because the letter taken to be the taxpayer’s objection made no reference to additional tax or penalties the level of penalties imposed in the assessments was not reviewed when the respondent considered the objection and determined to disallow it pursuant to s14ZY of the TAA.

  2. In a letter from the respondent to the applicant dated 8 August 1991 (T26, pp.141-148), just prior to the issue of the notices of assessment consequent upon completion of the audit, the respondent provided the applicant with a detailed statement of several matters relevant to the audit, and in particular details of the basis for the calculation of the additional taxes.

  3. The T documents, which the applicant has possessed since October 1994, contain, at T25, pp135-140, detailed notes by the respondent’s audit officer of the reasons for the imposition of the additional tax, including a chronology of relevant events from December 1987 when the respondent first began to make enquiries about the income tax affairs of the applicant, to March 1990, a period of time over which audit enquiries were made. Further, at T28, pp 347-380, the detailed reasons and justification for the level of penalties (as reviewed by the respondent’s Team Manager), provide the applicant with a full explanation of the basis of the additional tax assessed.

  4. In the opinion of the Tribunal, those T documents provided the applicant with all the relevant information he needed to enable him to assess whether the additional taxes imposed were unreasonable and to make relevant submissions to this Tribunal accordingly.

  5. In his letter to the Tribunal of 25 January 1995, which has been taken to set out the applicant’s grounds of objection (supra), the applicant had this to say about the penalties levied under s223 and remitted pursuant to s227(3) from the possible 200% to the percentage as shown in the table above:

“Culpability penalties. Taxpayer suffered a traumatic marriage breakdown in August 1988 and the outcome of this is still being of affect to the taxpayer. He recently had a Family Court Custody trial and is now a single parent having sole custody and guardianship of his three young children aged 12, 9 and 7.



Even now it is extremely difficult for the taxpayer to clearly focus on these proceedings, whilst totally involved in caring for three young school age children and the emotionally energy (sic) involved in doing so.



The A Tax Office in 1984 adjusted at least 1/3 of all Tax Returns lodged and this resulted in an ongoing “battle” by the taxpayer with the A Tax Office. Returns had been consistently lodged and up until this time only approx 5% (sic) of all returns were ever adjusted.



Over 100 returns were adjusted and objections and Appeals were lodged.



The end result on Appeal to the Taxation Board of Review (prior to actual appearance) was that 50% of all claims were allowed without any documentation being provided.



This was the operational status of A Tax Office at that time and this matter can only be correctly assessed in the historic light of those practices.



The Tax Practice was severelly (sic) effected and effectively lost as a business to the taxpayer.”



  1. The Tribunal is at a loss to know what to make of those pleadings. They do not enlighten it as to the reasons for the apparent persistent obstructive and delaying tactics employed by the applicant and evidenced in the reports and letters referred to above. In sum, the applicant has simply failed to provide the Tribunal with supportable argument or rebuttal of the assertions made by the respondent, which were provided in sufficient time and detail to enable the applicant to make proper representations in this regard. As to the applicant’s claim that his domestic duties precluded him from attending to his affairs in a proper manner it is noted that the adjusted taxable incomes as assessed relate to years prior to the marriage breakdown and the respondent’s audit report discloses what can only be seen as income tax returns prepared carelessly, negligently, or with due disregard for the law (bearing in mind the profession of the applicant at all relevant times) or, possibly fraudulently. The respondent has not levied the statutory maximum additional tax of 200% of the excess but at rates considerably below that (see table above). Further, at T28, p380, it is noted that the respondent has taken account of the possible adverse effect of the taxpayer’s marriage breakdown and remitted in part, the additional tax. The Tribunal is not minded to alter those assessments in any respect.

  2. Finally, as the applicant left the hearing before the respondent made his submissions, the Tribunal directed that the applicant be provided with a copy of the transcript of the respondent’s submissions and be given seven days thereafter to respond, with a copy to the respondent, who, in turn was given a further 7 days to respond. Both parties complied with the direction.

  3. The Tribunal has considered the applicant’s twenty-one page hand written submission and finds that it does not address any of the grounds of objection as set out above, nor indeed any of the respondent’s submissions. It neither assists the applicant nor the Tribunal in any respect.

DECISION



  1. The Tribunal affirms the decisions under review.
