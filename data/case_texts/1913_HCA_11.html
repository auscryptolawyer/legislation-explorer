Hedderwick v Federal Commissioner of Land Tax [1913] HCA 11; (1913) 16 CLR 27 (3 March 1913)

HIGH COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Hedderwick and Others Appellants; and The Federal Commissioner of Land Tax Respondent.

H C of A

3 March 1913

Griffith C.J., Barton, Isaacs and Gavan Duffy JJ.

Irvine K.C. and Starke (with them Arthur), for the appellants.

Pigott, for the respondent.

Irvine K.C., in reply.

March 3

Griffith C.J.

The appellants in this case are the trustees of the will of John Duerdin, who died in 1868. By his will he directed his trustees to pay the income of his real estate to his wife for her life. She died on 27th August 1911, being at that time liable for land tax in respect of the real estate of which she was equitable owner on 30th June preceding. A return was duly sent in by the trustees in August 1911, and the unimproved value of the land was duly assessed.

The general rule is that land tax is assessed upon the whole unimproved value less £5,000, but there were in the Principal Act certain provisions which had the effect of diminishing the amount of the tax payable in the case of certain tenants for life under old settlements or wills.

Sec. 25 of that Act provided, and still provides, that "(1) The owner of any freehold estate less than the fee-simple shall be deemed to be the owner of the fee-simple, to the exclusion of any person entitled in reversion or remainder." The definition of "owner" in the Act includes any person entitled to an estate of freehold in possession either at law or in equity. Sec. 35 of the Act provides that "Subject to this Act, the owner of any equitable estate or interest in any land shall be assessed and liable in respect of land tax as if he were the legal owner of the estate or interest." Sec. 25 of the original Act also contained a definition of the term "tenant for life" expressly including "a person entitled to the income of land under a trust or direction for payment thereof to him during his own or any other life." The same section also contained a proviso that for the purpose of the assessment of tenants for life of land without power of sale under settlements made before 1st July 1910 or under wills of testators who died before that date, the unimproved value of the land should be calculated upon a specific and less onerous basis.

As the law stood under that Act, there can be no doubt that the trustees, who were only liable in respect of the value of Mrs. Duerdin's interest as on 30th June 1911, would have been entitled to the benefit of the proviso to sec. 25. But, in the year 1911, and while proceedings for assessment were still going on, the Parliament passed an amending Act (No. 12 of 1911), which provided (sec. 3) that sec. 25 should be amended in three respects: (1) by inserting the word "legal" before the words "tenant for life" in the proviso, (2) as to the mode of calculating the unimproved value in cases coming within the proviso, and (3) by omitting from the definition of "tenant for life" the words already quoted. These amendments were made applicable to assessments for the year beginning 1st July 1911.

As I have already pointed out, under the original Act a tenant for life, whether legal or equitable, was entitled to the benefit of the proviso. When, therefore, the legislature said that the word "legal" should be inserted before the words "tenant for life," they said as clearly as possible that in future the privilege was to be conferred only upon legal tenants for life, and not upon equitable tenants for life. The general words of sec. 35 cannot affect this result. Nor can it be suggested that the trustees representing Mrs. Duerdin's estate were tenants for life.

In my opinion, therefore, it is clear beyond doubt that the trustees were no longer entitled to claim the benefit of the proviso to sec. 25 as then amended. When the Act was re-printed the proviso was printed with the word "legal" inserted before the words "tenant for life;" but, if the proviso had been originally enacted in the words as they stood after the amendment, I should have come to the conclusion, as a mere matter of construction, that the insertion of the word "legal" in a clause which otherwise would have included both legal and equitable tenants for life, indicated a clear intention of the legislature that equitable tenants for life should not have the benefit of it. So that, whether the word "legal" is regarded as there originally, or as inserted by amendment, the result was that the Crown became creditors of the trustees as from 30th June 1911 in respect of land tax to be assessed on the unimproved value of the land, subject to the statutory deduction, and the tax was then a debt due to the Crown by the taxpayers. And that debt is still payable unless by some subsequent legislation it has been remitted. It is suggested that it has been remitted.

The taxpayers did not accept the view of the law which I have stated, but appealed against the assessment, claiming that they were entitled to the benefit of the proviso to sec. 25. These proceedings were begun some time in 1912, and, on the appeal coming before my brother Isaacs, he stated a case for the opinion of this Court. That case was stated on 14th October 1912.

Now, I apprehend that the case must be decided according to the rights of the parties when the litigation began, unless by some subsequent Statute the Court is required to decide in some other way. It is suggested, however, that the law has been altered retrospectively since that time. I have already pointed out the position. The Commissioner claimed that the taxpayers were not entitled to the benefit of the proviso to sec. 25, and the taxpayers claimed that they were entitled to it. That was the matter in litigation, and, having regard to the so-called uncertainty of the law, it was possible that the matter might be decided either way.

In 1912 Parliament again amended the Act, and by sec. 7 of the Act of that year provided that sec. 35, which is the general section declaring that equitable owners are to be liable to the same extent as legal owners, should be amended by adding the following proviso:—"Provided that an equitable tenant for life of land under a settlement made before the first day of July 1910 or under the will of a testator who died before that day shall not be entitled to be assessed as if he were the legal tenant for life of the land, without power to sell." This amendment was to apply to assessments made for the year beginning 1st July 1912, and did not therefore affect the assessment now in question. That is to say, that the view which, in my opinion, is the true construction of sec. 25 as amended, was in future to be inserted in the Act itself as a proviso to sec. 35. That section was passed pending litigation to determine whether that was the law or not.

The appellants contend that the provision is to be taken as a retrospective declaratory enactment, and that it operates to deprive the Crown of a vested right and to remit a tax which was already due and payable. To my mind such a construction is inconsistent with all known canons of construction. The fact that a provision in an Act of Parliament declares in clear terms that the law is what it really was before does not alter the law. Sec. 7, therefore, cannot be regarded as an alteration of the law. On that point I will refer to some old authorities as to the effect of what is called "surplusage." Lord Brougham, in Auchterarder v. Kinnoull[1], said:—"A Statute ... is always allowed the privilege of using words not absolutely necessary." In Commissioners of Income Tax v. Pemsel[2] Lord Macnaghten pointed out that "it is not so very uncommon in an Act of Parliament to find special exemptions which are already covered by a general exemption." In the same case[3] Lord Herschell said:—"Such specific exemptions are often introduced ex majori cautelâ to quiet the fears of those whose interests are engaged or sympathies aroused in favour of some particular institution, and who are apprehensive that it may not be held to fall within a general exemption." And, of course, vice versâ. So that the mere introduction of words by way of precaution to clear up doubts—which, having regard to the pending litigation, must be taken to have existed—cannot be said, even primâ facie, to indicate an alteration of the law. Persons who have had practical experience of parliamentary work know that sometimes it is desirable to insert words where they are not really necessary. For myself, I may say that, however clear a provision might seem to me, if any intelligent person thought that its construction was doubtful, I always thought it my duty to amend the language in order to make its meaning absolutely clear, although the effect was in no way altered.

As a matter of construction alone, therefore, it seems to me that sec. 7 made no difference in the law. But, when one considers the circumstances under which it was passed—that is, during pending litigation—it is impossible to say that it had any retrospective effect.

But it is said that since the amendment made by sec. 7 was not formally made retrospective, its effect is retrospective—that when it is said that that should be the form of the law as from the 1st July 1912, there is a necessary implication that before that date the law was different. That seems to me an unwarranted, and, indeed, violent inference under the circumstances.

As to the rule applicable to changes in the law pending litigation I read from Hardcastle on Statutes, 2nd ed., p. 374:—

In Main v. Stark[4] Lord Selborne said:—"Their Lordships, of course, do not say that there might not be something in the context of an Act of Parliament, or to be collected from its language, which might give to words, primâ facie prospective, a larger operation; but they ought not to receive a larger operation unless you find some reason for giving it." And, "Words not requiring a retrospective operation, so as to affect an existing Statute prejudicially, ought not to be so construed."

"It is a well recognized rule that Statutes should be interpreted, if possible, so as to respect vested rights. For it is not to be presumed that interference with existing rights is intended by the legislature, and if a Statute be ambiguous the Court should lean to the interpretation which would support existing rights."

It is hardly necessary to remark that the Crown's vested rights are to be respected as much as are the rights of private persons.





Again, at p. 375, the learned author says:—"In Gardner v. Lucas13 App. Cas., 582, at p. 603. Lord Blackburn stated this rule of law in the following way with regard to the effect of a Statute upon a transaction past and closed, where the effect would be to alter a transaction already entered into. Where, said he, the effect would be to make that valid which was previously invalid, to make an instrument which had no effect at all, and from which the party was at liberty to depart as long as he pleased, binding—I think the primâ facie construction of the Act is that it is not to be retrospective, and it would require strong reasons to show that it is not the case. In Moon v. Durden22 Ex., 22. an action to recover a sum of money alleged to have been won upon a wager, was commenced in June 1845. In August 1845 the 8 & 9 Vict. c. 109 was passed, which enacted, in sec. 18, that no suit shall be brought or maintained for recovering any such sum of money, and the question was whether that enactment was retrospective so as to defeat an action already commenced. It was held that it was not retrospective, and Parke B., in his judgment, said:—It seems a strong thing to hold that the legislature could have meant that a party who under a contract made prior to the Act had as perfect a title to recover a sum of money as he had to any of his personal property, should be totally deprived of it without compensation. So also, in Couch v. Jeffries14 Burr., 2460. a verdict was obtained for a penalty for not paying stamp duty. After the verdict, 9 Geo. III. c. 37, was passed, which enacted, in sec. 4, that if the duty before neglected to be paid shall be paid by September 1, 1769, the person who had incurred the penalty by the omission shall be discharged from the said penalty. In pursuance of this Statute the defendant had paid the duty which he had before neglected to pay, and thereupon moved that judgment should not be entered up against him. But, said Lord Mansfield C.J., here is a right vested, and it is not to be imagined that the legislature could by general words mean to take it away from the person in whom it had so legally vested, and who had been put to expense in prosecuting. ... It is a general rule, said Jessel, M.R., in Re Joseph Suche & Co.21 Ch. D., 48, at p. 50., that when the legislature alters the rights of parties by taking away or conferring any right of action, its enactments, unless in express terms they apply to pending actions, do not affect them. But there is an exception to this rule, namely, where enactments merely affect procedure, and do not extend to rights of action."

Those rules are perfectly well known and established. In this case there are no express words which can be suggested to take away the vested rights of the Crown.

For these reasons I am of opinion that the Act of 1912 has no bearing on this case, which must be decided according to the law applicable when Mrs. Duerdin died, that is, on 27th August 1911.

I think, therefore, that the appellants are not entitled to the benefit of the proviso to sec. 25.

Barton J

. read the following judgment:—

The short question is whether the holder of an equitable estate for life under the will of a testator who died before 1st July 1910—that is to say, in 1868—is entitled, since the passage of the amending Acts, to the benefit of the proviso to sec. 25 (1). This is not precisely the question asked as No. iv. in the case stated by my learned brother Isaacs, but the parties agree that it is the real point at issue, and the argument has proceeded upon that footing. The proviso, as amended in 1911, gives to a legal tenant for life without power to sell, under a settlement made before 1st July 1910, or under the will of a testator who died before that day, the privilege of having the unimproved value calculated, for the purpose of his assessment, on the basis of the rent which he obtains, or which "he ought reasonably to be able to obtain" upon a letting. The appellants, though Mrs. Duerdin was not in fact the legal tenant for life, demand this privilege as the result of their construction of sec. 35. They claim that, she having been the owner of an equitable estate, they are by virtue of that section to be assessed, and to be liable, as if she were "the legal owner of the estate and interest," and that the section thus gives them the benefit of the proviso to sec. 25 (1), notwithstanding the amendments; in other words, that the proviso is still to be read subject to sec. 35, and therefore that she is to be treated as the legal tenant for life.

The contention of the appellants would have been conclusive if the amendments of 1911 had not been made. Being entitled to be assessed and being liable as if she were the legal owner of her interest, though it was an equitable interest only (sec. 35), Mrs. Duerdin was to be deemed the owner of the fee simple to the exclusion of any person entitled in reversion or remainder. See the first three lines of sec. 25 (1); also sec. 3, defining the word "owner." In the Principal Act, before it was amended, the proviso to sec. 25 (1), in its opening words, applied "for the purpose of the assessment of a tenant for life" &c., and by the operation of sec. 35 this was the case whether he were a legal or an equitable tenant for life. See Sendall v. Federal Commissioner of Land Tax[9]. But the legislature, for what purpose we know not, has so altered the proviso by the Act of 1911, secs. 3 (1) (a) and (c), that, unless sec. 35 has had an over-riding effect, the result is to confine the benefit to the legal tenant for life. This it has done not only by the insertion in the proviso to sub-sec. (1) of the word "legal" before the words "tenant for life," but further by omitting paragraph (c) of sub-sec. (2), so that the term "tenant for life" is no longer declared to include "a person entitled to the income of land under a trust or direction for payment thereof to him during his own or any other life." Thus the proviso, if taken by itself, debars the equitable life tenant of its privilege. Does sec. 35 then operate so as to conserve it to him despite the amendments?

On the whole I am of opinion that sec. 35 has not the effect of over-riding what would otherwise be the clear meaning of the amendments. To give it that effect would be to say that the insertion by Parliament of the word "legal" and the omission of sub-sec. (2) (c) are nugatory, or rather that Parliament had no clear idea of what it was doing when it made these alterations. If the language of the sections taken together is capable of two constructions—and I scarcely think it is—we should adopt that construction which will give some effect to the words rather than that which will give none: Cargo ex Argos[10]. Unless the words differentiate the position of a life tenant holding the legal estate from that of a life tenant holding only the equitable estate, notwithstanding sec. 35, one is at a loss to assign to them any meaning at all. On the other hand, this construction does not deprive sec. 35 of operation, but merely excepts certain cases of legal life tenancy which arose before the first year of assessment. I think, therefore, that the amendments must have the actual effect of making the differentiation. The words "subject to this Act," with which sec. 35 begins, seem to weaken the appellants' contention, but it is not necessary to place any reliance on them.

It should be mentioned that sec. 13 of the amending Act of 1911 prescribes that the amendments which it makes in the Principal Act of 1910 shall apply to assessments for the financial year beginning on 1st July 1911, and to those for subsequent years. They, therefore, apply to the assessment in the present case, which was made for the financial year 1911-1912.

The appellants relied rather strongly on the effect of secs. 7 and 12 of the amending Act of 1912, which showed, they said, that Parliament attached to the enactments in question, as amended in 1911, the meaning for which they contended. Sec. 7 adds the following proviso to sec. 35 of the Principal Act:—"Provided that an equitable tenant for life of land under a settlement made before the first day of July 1910, or under the will of a testator who died before that day, shall not be entitled to be assessed as if he were the legal tenant for life of the land, without power to sell." The 12th section provides that, with an exception not material to this case, the amendments which it makes in the Principal Act "shall apply to assessments for the financial year beginning on the first day of July 1912 and all subsequent years." The appellants contend that, on reading secs. 7 and 12 together, it is apparent that Parliament considered that, unless this amendment were made, sec. 25 (1), as amended by sec. 3 of the 1911 Act, would not, in view of sec. 35, operate to deprive the equitable tenant for life of the benefit of the proviso, and as sec. 7, by virtue of sec. 12, applies only to assessments for the financial year 1912-1913 and subsequent years, Parliament, it is urged, meant that the privilege was to subsist in relation to the assessments of previous years.

Having regard to what I conceive to be the meaning of sec. 25, as amended in 1911, and of sec. 35 at the time when this litigation began, I cannot think that the amendments of 1912 alter the law applicable to the present case.

The amending Act of 1912 came into force on 24th December 1912, and the case with which we are now dealing was stated by my learned brother Isaacs two months earlier. While sec. 7 is an amendment as to the future, the only way in which it can be held to apply to assessments made in 1911 is that it should be construed as declaratory by implication as to the past. That is a novelty which I cannot quite assimilate. Jessel M.R. said in In re Joseph Suche & Co.[11]:—"It is a general rule that when the legislature alters the rights of parties by taking away or conferring any right of action, its enactments, unless in express terms they apply to pending actions, do not affect them. It is said that there is one exception to that rule, namely, ... where enactments merely affect procedure and do not extend to rights of action."

I think that this case is within the principle of the rule; that the two sections were not intended to apply to any litigation commenced before they came into operation, that the legal rights of the parties must be determined upon the law as it stood when they invoked its aid, and that the sum claimed as tax, which was then due to the Crown, is still so due. In other words, the Crown has not been deprived of this chose in action.

I am therefore of opinion that the Commissioner was right in assessing the appellants under secs. 35 and 25 (1) as owners in fee, and in declining to apply the proviso to this assessment.

Isaacs J

. read the following judgment:—

The question is whether on 30th June 1911 the law entitled an equitable tenant for life without power of sale to be assessed for the financial year beginning 1st July on the same terms as if he were a legal tenant for life. And the answer depends on the true construction of three Statutes forming a code, relating to land tax assessment—the first passed in 1910, the second in 1911, and the third in 1912.

As I have the misfortune to differ from my learned brethren, both as to the decision itself and the principles of approaching it, I feel under the obligation of stating my views explicitly.

In the first place, I lay aside all question of retrospective law, in the sense in which that term is properly used. A retrospective law in that sense is one which alters the law, and professes to alter it as from some antecedent date. Now, in this case nothing of the kind is asserted or relied upon by the appellants. They rely, not upon any change of law, but upon sec. 35 of the Act as originally passed, and as the code declares it shall remain for the period covered by this case; and they contend that upon the true interpretation of that unaltered section, the interpretation it always had from the beginning to the end of the financial year 1911, they have a right to the concession they claim. The next point to notice is this: that the date of the actual assessment or of the institution of the appeal is, in my opinion, an immaterial fact. The question always is: What was the law on 30th June 1911?

It is difficult to see how two persons can be differently treated in respect of the financial year 1911 merely because one sued for a declaration of right in September 1912, and the other in January 1913. It must be the same right and the same liability in both cases. And the right and the liability must be determined according to what the Court now finds, at the time it gives its judgment, to have been the law at 30th June 1911, and irrespective of when the litigation arose in which that question was raised. It is not as if it were claimed that post litem motam the law was changed so as to alter the Crown's rights, in which case I would admit the force of the principle adverted to by the learned Chief Justice. Attorney-General v. Theobald[12], where the latest Act was passed after the institution of proceedings and even the close of the pleadings, is an instance in point of the class of cases to which the present one belongs; Moon v. Durden[13] is a classical instance of the other type; and the two are quite distinct.

Then we have to consider how the meaning of the law as it stood on 30th June 1911 is to be gathered. In ascertaining that, we are, I apprehend, both entitled and bound to be guided by everything that Parliament has stated on the subject up to the present time; and if preference is to be given to any one of its legislative statements over another, that preference belongs to the most recent of its declarations: See per Lord Macnaghten in Lord Advocate v. Stewart[14]. It is, as I conceive, a radical error to discard the latest Act, and inquire first in its absence what was the true meaning of sec. 35; to find on those limited materials alone, that a certain debt existed; and then to ask how the latest Act is to revoke or lessen that debt. With the greatest deference, it appears to me that such a method of inquiry must lead to mistake, because it is a petitio principii. Before we can tell whether the debt as claimed existed or not on 30th June 1911, we must take into account the assistance and guidance which Parliament has given us by its latest utterance as to the meaning of sec. 35. I repeat, it is not upon any alteration of that section that the appellants rely, but upon its original force, as that may be discovered from all the material now at our disposal. In Maxwell on Statutes, 5th ed., p. 499, it is stated:—"Where it is gathered, from a later Act, that the legislature attached a certain meaning to certain words in an earlier cognate one, this would be taken as a legislative declaration of its meaning there."

Among the illustrations that may be found in decided cases, none better can be given than the recent one of Attorney-General v. Clarkson[15]. It is an instance both of the disregard which the Court has in such a case to the date when the debt accrued, and of the refusal of the Court to proceed by first inquiring as to the meaning of the law apart from the most recent Act. There a testator had died in 1895, and whatever estate duty was payable was, of course, then and there a debt under the existing law. But before the dispute between his representatives and the Crown was settled, an amending Act was passed, that is, in 1898, which did not even purport to be retrospective, but made by sec. 14 an enactment as to what should happen in certain cases occurring after its commencement. There had been a prior decision on the earlier Act, by a Divisional Court, and Clarkson's Case[16], which followed the earlier one, came up to the Court of Appeal. It was argued as here, that the later Act had no application to the case, and was a mere amendment for the future. Sir Robert Finlay S.-G. contended that it was a legislative adoption of the judicial interpretation, and urged[17]:—"If this testator had died after the Act of 1898 came into operation, it would have been impossible to argue that settlement estate duty was not payable. How can the construction of sec. 5 be different according as the death takes place before or after the Act of 1898 came into operation?" At this point he was stopped by the Court. Lindley M.R. did not think it necessary to determine how he would have construed the earlier section in the absence of the later Act. He abstained from considering whether the earlier decision was right or wrong, because the later section, as he said, proceeded on the assumption that it was right. He added[18]:—"I do not say that we should not have arrived at the same conclusion without the Act of 1898, but, with that Act before us, it is not possible to adopt their" (appellants') "argument."

Sir F. H. Jeune said[19]:—"Our duty is to interpret the meaning of the legislature, and if the legislature in one Act have used language which is admittedly ambiguous, and in a subsequent Act have used language which proceeds upon the hypothesis that a particular interpretation is to be placed upon the earlier Act, I think the Judges have no choice but to read the two Acts together, and to say that the legislature have acted as their own interpreters of the earlier Act."

I may here say that whether ambiguous or not, apart from the latest addition to the code, these Land Tax Assessment Acts have to be read as one, because sec. 15 of the Acts Interpretation Act so directs in the absence of contrary intention. There is no contrary intention here; the opposite is indicated by each amending Act. That really follows the established common law rule of construction: Per Lord Truro in M'William v. Adams[20].

Not only was Clarkson's Case[21] the decision of a powerful Court of Appeal, but the Privy Council in Attorney-General for Victoria v. Melbourne Corporation[22] approved of it because the enactment in question contemplated, as their Lordships said, the payment of the duty in question, and was therefore an adoption of the construction put upon an earlier Act by the Court. The former decision did not, of course, affect the matter except as indicating the construction which the legislature in its later Act impliedly placed on the earlier Statute. That construction, though only "contemplated," as the Privy Council said, was sufficient to govern the matter. Their Lordships also distinguished the case from the one then in hand on grounds which, at all events, and especially in view of sec. 12, are immaterial to the present matter, except that, by contrast, they strengthen the application of that case to the one in hand. That clears the ground of all the difficulties as to retro-active legislation, and its ancillary considerations of vested rights and accrued debt, and, as I regard the matter, leaves us in the position that our very first inquiry must be that which the Lords Justices entered upon in Clarkson's Case[23], namely, to find the "assumption" or "hypothesis" or "contemplation" upon which the Parliament passed secs. 7 and 12 of the Act of 1912. For if any assumption be involved in the enactment, it is binding and conclusive on this Court. Whatever may be the case as to recitals, whether of law or fact, I take it as law, settled by the highest authority for us, that whatever is involved, expressly or impliedly, in the enacting part of a Statute, is unchallengeable by a Court of law. In Labrador Co. v. The Queen[24] Lord Hannen for the Judicial Committee, speaking of a statement of fact in sec. 10 of a Canadian Act, said:—"This is an absolute statement by the legislature that there was a seigneurie of Mingan. Even if it could be proved that the legislature was deceived, it would not be competent for a Court of law to disregard its enactments. If a mistake has been made, the legislature alone can correct it. ... The Courts of law cannot sit in judgment on the legislature, but must obey and give effect to its determination." And it needs little reflection to perceive that an "assumption,""hypothesis" or "contemplation," or tacit declaration by Parliament as to its own will, can stand on no weaker ground than a declaration as to some external fact.

The last Act was passed on 24th December 1912. It made amendments, and called them all amendments, in nine sections of the Principal Act, and it added one section. It amended sec. 35 by adding a proviso that an equitable tenant for life should not be entitled to be assessed as if he were the legal tenant for life without power to sell. If that were all, the natural import of the word "amendment" would afford a strong presumption that Parliament recognized it was altering the law. But it is said, that is explained by the fact that, some two or three months before, this question had arisen between the present parties, and a case had been stated for the Full Court, and therefore it was a mere precautionary and express statement of what was already intended and understood by the legislature to be comprised in the then existing law. To this the answer is clear and decisive. If the legislature was prompted to make its express declaration by reason of this case, it took the very course which should destroy the respondent's argument. It did not declare that the law already was so, it did not say simply that that construction should henceforth be given to sec. 35, and should be applied to assessments for the year 1911; but it said the direct contrary, namely, that sec. 35 should be "amended" as prescribed, and it further enacted, specifically, that the exclusion of equitable tenants for life from the benefits enjoyed by legal tenants for life should be postponed for another year, that is, should not take effect for the year covered by the dispute, which, it is said, actuated Parliament to make the amendment, and, lastly, it did not provide, as is so often done, that pending cases should be excepted. But for the contrary opinion held by my learned brethren, I should think it beyond controversy that Parliament had legislated on the manifest assumption that the amendment of sec. 35 was new matter, that, apart from that new matter, sec. 35 meant from the beginning and down to the last amending Act, that equitable tenants for life had the same rights as legal tenants for life, that the new matter added by the last Act should have no operation in any case until the financial year succeeding the one now in controversy; and if the present dispute is to be regarded as then present to the mind of Parliament, it is evident to me that its determination was that the rule of exclusion should not apply to the present appellants for the year 1911.

How can we, then, attribute to Parliament so contradictory an intention that the same rule, the identical legislative direction as to assessment, was both to be deferred until 1st July 1912, and yet was to be in force, and to be put in force continuously, for the year before? It seems to me that it is the Crown that virtually asks for a retrospective application, first, of the Act of December 1911 altering the rights of the parties as they stood on 30th June 1911, and then of the rule of exclusion in the year 1912, which Parliament has subsequently enacted, and even then explicitly deferred. What, I may ask, would be the duty of the assessing officer for 1911 on reading sec. 12 directing the postponement of the excluding provision until 1912? Without attributing inconsistency and absurdity to the solemn enactments of Parliament, I am unable to reconcile sec. 13 with the respondent's case. That is the problem which confronts the Crown. Unless such an exclusion was already intended by Parliament in sec. 35 by reason of the words "Subject to this Act"—the respondent has no case. In the original Act it is common ground that those words had no reference whatever to sec. 25, because equitable owners were then within the proviso. Did Parliament by limiting the proviso of sec. 25 to legal tenants for life, and without negative words, intend to say anything necessarily inconsistent with the retention of the original application of sec. 35, by excluding equitable tenants for life from that section also? That Parliament did not so intend, is, as I say, evident to me from the assumption in sec. 7 of the later Act, with the definite direction of postponement in sec. 12. I think it therefore clear that the appellants should succeed.

I have said nothing so far about the construction of sec. 35 apart from the latest Act, for the reasons I have given. Mr. Irvine, however, relied on that construction; and it is perhaps proper I should add a word or two from that aspect.

Originally by sec. 25, both legal and equitable tenants for life were equally entitled to a concession by reason of the proviso. The amendment of sec. 25 altered that by limiting the concession given by that section to the legal tenant for life only. Henceforth the equitable tenant for life was cut out of the concession so far as sec. 25 was concerned. And, unless he could find it elsewhere in the Act, he was no longer to enjoy it. But it must be clearly observed that there are no negative words, declaring that equitable tenants for life shall not receive the concession. And the legislature did not cut down the effect of sec. 35. It might have intended to say so—that is pure conjecture; but, if so, its enactment, which is all we can have regard to, stopped short of its intention.

And Jervis C.J., in York and North Midland Railway Co. v. The Queen[25], said:—"Courts of justice ought not to depart from the plain meaning of words used in Acts of Parliament: when they do so, they make, but do not construe, the laws." Lord Blackburn, in Coltness Iron Co. v. Black[26], speaking of taxing Acts, observed:—"The only safe rule is to look at the words of the enactments and see what is the intention expressed by those words." Now, when sec. 35 is reached, it provides that "subject to the Act" equitable owners are to be assessed as if they were legal owners; and, further, that the legal owner is to be the primary taxpayer, and the equitable owner the secondary taxpayer. There being no negative words, no statement inconsistent with the enactment in sec. 35, it seems to me the latter section places the equitable owner for life in as good a position as the legal owner for life.

If the legislation at that stage is to be considered apart from the latest Act, it is fair to point out that where the legal tenant for life was trustee for an equitable tenant for life, neither having the power of sale, the construction which the Crown gives to sec. 35 would leave the primary taxpayer, the legal tenant, entitled to pay on a different, that is a lower, basis than the secondary taxpayer, the equitable tenant. The trustee would pay on a rental value, and the cestui que trust on a fee simple value—a result quite opposed to the doctrines laid down in Sendall's Case[27].

This is a result which may now be forced upon us by the express enactment of exclusion from sec. 35, but it seems to me a very cogent reason for not implying, without that express enactment, a construction of sec. 35 which leads to it.

If the matter be not so clear as I think it, and if it be merely doubtful, still the rule is that words which after full consideration remain ambiguous are not sufficient to lay an impost on the subject. The Act must be shown "quite clearly and strictly" to make the applicants liable: Per Privy Council in Harding v. Commissioners of Stamps for Queensland[28]. But, again, at this point the latest legislative declaration becomes, as I consider, decisive, with the effect I have already stated. And so, quâcunque viâ, I think the appellants should succeed.

Gavan Duffy J.

In this case the substantial questions to be determined are these:—Did the legislature in the amendment effected by sec. 3 of Act No. 12 of 1911 deprive an equitable tenant for life of the benefit of the method of assessment in the case of certain tenants for life without power of sale provided by sec. 25 of Act No. 22 of 1910; and, if so, has the law, so far as it is applicable to the present case, been altered by the provisions of Act No. 37 of 1912? The reasons for answering yea or nay to each of those questions have been fully stated in the judgments already delivered. I prefer the view expressed in the judgment of the Chief Justice to that adopted by my brother Isaacs. I am therefore of opinion that the first of these questions should be answered in the affirmative, and the second in the negative.

Questions answered accordingly.
