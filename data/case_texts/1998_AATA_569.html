CATCHWORDS

Fringe benefits tax - Trainers of computer training courses - Cost of attending luncheons attended by course participants - Whether provision of luncheons constitutes a property fringe benefit - Applicability of “otherwise deductible” rule - Meaning of “entertainment” and “recreation” - Income tax - Allowable deductions - Whether cost of attending luncheons is deductible- Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act, ss 40, 41, 44, 136 - Income Tax Assessment Act, ss 51, 51AE.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss 51, 51AE

Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act, ss 40, 41, 44, 136



FC of T v Cooper 91 ATC 4396

Roads and Traffic Authority of NSW v FC of T 93 ATC 4508

Ronpibon Tin NL v FC of T [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 8 ATD 431

Lunney v FC of T (1958) 11 ATD 404



REASONS FOR DECISION

29 July 1998 J Block (Senior Member)



  1. The Applicant sought the review of a decision by the Respondent to disallow an objection dated 12 July 1996 against a notice of private ruling issued on 21 December 1995 in respect of the year ended 31 March 1995.
  2. Mr S. Rothman, S.C. appeared for the Applicant and Mr N. James, an officer of the Respondent appeared for the Respondent. The Tribunal had before it the T Documents lodged under section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. There was no oral evidence, the matter being argued on the papers.
  3. There is one matter of a preliminary nature which can be dealt with summarily. Mention was made of the possibility that the benefit in question might be exempt as a minor fringe benefit under section 58P of Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act (“FBTAA”). This possibility was not raised in the T Documents, and in any event, was not persevered with (correctly so in the opinion of the Tribunal) having regard to the frequency with which (so the Tribunal was told) the luncheons in question occurred.
  4. (a) The relevant facts are set out in clause 9 of the attachment to the application for private ruling (T3) at pages 12 and 13 of the T Documents as follows:
“9. Full Description of the Facts:

Pollak Partners Pty Limited (“the taxpayer”) conducts business as a presenter of computer training courses, using its own staff as trainers, for its clients. Courses are conducted upon the taxpayer’s business premises, within classrooms specifically designed for this purpose.

Each course is conducted by a trainer for up to eight participants at a time. The taxpayer conducts its business at three locations, namely Sydney, Parramatta and Melbourne. The circumstances described below relate to the Sydney location, although the comments apply equally to the other locations.

Courses run by the taxpayer typically last a full day, with a short break of approximately one hour being taken during which a half-hour luncheon is provided. As the taxpayer’s Sydney business premises are located in a building adjacent to the Menzies Holiday Inn Hotel, participants are provided with a light buffet lunch including sandwiches and/or salads in the Hotel. No alcohol is served at the luncheon and staff are instructed that no alcohol may be purchased by the trainer or participants.

Attendance by the trainer at the luncheon is an integral part of the training course as it provides the opportunity for:

(a) participants to discuss material presented at the course;

(b) participants and the trainer to expand on the concepts covered during the intensive learning sessions in a more conducive manner for exchange of information.

The luncheon sessions are planned and trainers are instructed to cover various topics during the break. Accordingly, the taxpayer views the short luncheon break as very much an adjunct to, and an important part of, the instructional material presented throughout the day. Participants use the time to evaluate and/or question the course material; time pressures in presenting the courses often do not allow for such necessary “reflection” on the course material during the formal instruction periods.

During the 1994/95 FBT year, the average cost of a meal consumed by trainers in Sydney was $13.50. For the year, the amount incurred by the taxpayer in respect of meals consumed by its trainers is detailed below:

(b) The facts referred to in subclause (a) were amplified to some extent in the objection (T5) and in particular paragraphs (iii), (iv) and (v) (pages 25 and 26 of the T Documents) as follows:

“(iii) whilst the programmed lunch break is usually 75 minutes, the duration of the light refreshment meals was short and rarely exceeded 30 minutes; indeed, it was usually the practice that the trainers left the clients well before the formal training sessions were reconvened; after the discussions over the light refreshment concluded, clients were free to utilise the remaining time as they so wished;

(iv) the opportunity to discuss aspects of the courses in an information non-classroom setting is seen as a vital segment of the training provided to clients due to the formalised structure of course presentation, the volume of material presented, and the time constraints in ensuring that the course is completed in the time allotted;

further details relevant to this comment are as follows:

(a) each topic covered by the taxpayer for its clients can only be presented in the training room and requires a practical “hands-on” approach;

(b) during the luncheon break, the trainers were required by the taxpayer to adhere to certain guidelines which covered their behaviour, the types of discussions they should have and the structure of their conversations; matters which the trainers were required to discuss included:
  • how clients were finding the course;
  • whether the course was meeting its objectives outlined at the commencement of the training session;
  • whether there are any features of the course which require further elaboration;
  • how clients expected to apply what they were learning to their own work environment;
  • whether clients were having any difficulties understanding the examples presented during the formal part of the course;
(c) to ensure that clients got the most from their training sessions, the trainers were expected to use the information gleaned from the luncheon break to plan the afternoon session; this approach allowed the trainer to more accurately match the course to the needs of the clients and was considered by the taxpayer to be an important factor in maintaining the quality advantage of its courses; the time constraints of the structured course material and the confined spaces within the training rooms meant that this informal information gathering process was an invaluable part of the course;

(v) all training courses conducted by the taxpayer were run in the abovementioned structured format; since the receipt of the Private Ruling, however, the taxpayer discontinued the practice of allowing the trainers to attend the luncheon break and those trainers were forced to obtain the abovementioned information during the shorter, and less congenial, coffee breaks; the taxpayer has received the following unsolicited comments from the clients who attended courses held since the discontinuation of the abovementioned practice which, in the taxpayer’s view, clearly emphasises the importance of the informal segment of the course:

“During the lunch break I would have liked to be able to talk with the trainer Kim about certain things we learned in the morning lesson.”

“A pity that staff do not attend lunches anymore as this is very important time to learn all the things that aren’t in the course.”

“I think it was a pity that our trainer did not join us for lunch on the 2nd and 3rd days. It would have been a good opportunity to discuss any problems or ask “silly” questions.””


  1. The relevant questions are as to whether:

(a) the provision of a light luncheon by the Applicant (sometimes in these Reasons referred to as the “employer”) to its employee (sometimes referred to as the “trainer”) at the Menzies Hotel (in the case of Sydney courses) during the course of a seminar or training course constitutes a fringe benefit and if so the type of fringe benefit in question;

(b) if the fringe benefit is a property fringe benefit, whether the “otherwise deductible” rule set out in section 44 of FBTAA applies.

  1. (a) There can be no doubt that the luncheon constitutes a benefit given that property is provided by a person to another person: section 40 of FBTAA. The wide definition of “property”, in section 136(1) of FBTAA, which provides that it means both “tangible” and “intangible” property, ensures that this is so.

(b) The exemption in section 41 of FBTAA does not apply because the property is not provided to, and consumed by, the employee on business premises of the employer, or a company which is related to the employer. The property is, as set out in the T Documents, provided from hotel premises which in the view of the Tribunal cannot be so described, and being, in the case of Sydney courses, the Menzies Hotel.

(c) The benefit was a fringe benefit because it was provided in respect of the employee’s employment, and the provider was the Menzies Hotel under an arrangement with the employer; see section 136(1) of FBTAA as to the definition of “fringe benefit” and particularly paragraph (3) of that definition; since it was a property fringe benefit it is not necessary to consider the provisions of FBTAA as to any other type of fringe benefit. It is to be noted that the stated facts provide that they related to the provision of luncheons by the Menzies Hotel in respect of Sydney training courses, but the facts in clause 9 (page 12 of the T Documents) state that similar arrangements applied in the other locations.

  1. Having concluded that the benefit was a property fringe benefit, it is next necessary to consider whether the value was reduced by the “otherwise deductible” rule set out in section 44 of FBTAA. Before doing so, it is necessary to consider whether a deduction is precluded under section 51AE of Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“ITAA”); in this context:

(a) It was accepted that each training course was an eligible seminar as defined in 51AE(1) of ITAA; the facts indicate that each course lasted for a full day, and certainly in excess of the period of 4 hours prescribed in the definition contained in section 51AE(1) of ITAA.

(b) In general terms, deductions are not allowable under section 51(1) of ITAA for entertainment; see section 51AE(4) of ITAA.

(c) However, section 51AE(4) does not apply where one of the exceptions contained in section 51AE(5) of ITAA applies; for this purpose, the provisions of section 51AE(5)(f)(iv) (“the relevant exception”) will be apposite; it reads as follows:

“(iv) the provision of entertainment to a person (including the taxpayer) that:

(A) is reasonably incidental to the person’s attendance at an eligible seminar; and

(B) is not by way of, or in connection with, the recreation of the person;”


(d) Before considering the relevant exception in detail, it is then necessary to consider the meanings to be attributed to the terms “entertainment” and “recreation”:

(1) Section 51AE(3) in relation to the concept of entertainment provides, in somewhat circular fashion, that:

“(3) A reference in this section to the provision of entertainment is a reference to the provision (whether to the taxpayer or to another person and whether gratuitously, pursuant to an agreement or otherwise) of:

(a) entertainment by way of food, drink or recreation; or

(b) accommodation or travel in connection with, or for the purpose of facilitating, entertainment to which paragraph (a) applies (whether or not the accommodation or travel is also in connection with something else or for another purpose.”


(2) Since the reference to “entertainment” in section 51AE is, in some respects, circular, Mr Rothman drew the attention of the Tribunal to the fact that the term “entertainment” is defined in The Macquarie Dictionary (Third Edition) as follows:

“entertainment - 1. the act of entertaining; agreeable occupation for the mind; diversion, or amusement. 2. something affording diversion or amusement, especially an exhibition or performance of some kind. 3. hospitable provision for the wants of guests.”


(3) “recreation” is defined (inclusively) in section 56AE(1) of ITAA as follows:

“recreation includes:

(a) amusement;

(b) sport or similar leisure-time pursuits; and

(c) recreation or amusement provided on, or by means of, a vehicle, vessel or aircraft”


(4) Having regard to the fact that the statutory definition is inclusive, Mr Rothman pointed to the definition in the Macquarie Dictionary (Third Edition) as follows:

“recreation - 1. refreshment by means of some pastime, agreeable exercise, or the like. 2. a pastime, diversion, exercise, or other resource affording relaxation and enjoyment. 3. the act of recreating. 4. the state of being recreated. 5. of or relating to an area, room, etc., set aside for recreation.”


(5) It is clear that the relevant exception would not apply if the luncheon were provided to the trainer by way of recreation; it is quite plain also that the luncheon does not fall within either the statutory or the dictionary meaning of that term.

(6) However, the relevant exception can apply where the luncheon constitutes entertainment; having regard, inter alia, to section 51AE(3)(a) of ITAA, the provision of the luncheon does indeed constitute entertainment.

  1. As to the relevant exception:

(a) It is clear from the facts that the provision of the luncheon (which is entertainment) was reasonably incidental to “the person’s attendance at any eligible seminar”; it is also clear that it was not excluded under subsection (iv)(B) as recreation.

(b) The relevant exception applies in its terms to “the provision of entertainment to a person (including the taxpayer); the bracketed words indicate that the relevant exception can apply where the entertainment is provided not only to the trainees, but also the trainer; the taxpayer for this purpose must, in the view of the Tribunal, be the person in respect of which a deduction under section 51(1) of ITAA is or might be applicable, and being in this instance the employee or trainer; however, and in the unlikely event that it could be construed so as to refer to the Applicant itself, the trainer is clearly the agent of the Applicant for the purpose of conducting the eligible seminar.

(c) Accordingly, the relevant exception operates to exclude the disqualifying provisions of section 51AE(4) of ITAA.

  1. The facts show that the employee was required to be present at the luncheon for the purposes, and to carry out the functions and obligations referred to in the facts. This is also so in relation to the expanded facts, and in particular paragraph (iii) (at page 25 of the T Documents) which indicate that while the programmed lunch break was usually 75 minutes, the light luncheon took 30 minutes and the trainees were then free to use the remaining time as they wished. The important aspect is that, as set out in both the facts and the expanded facts, the trainer was obliged to be present during the luncheon.
  2. As to section 51(1) of ITAA:

(a) Section 51(1) provides that:

“All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income.”


(b) The applicable tests, in general terms, are admirably summed up in the judgment of Lockhart J in FC of T v Cooper 91 ATC 4396 at page 4400 where he said:

“For expenditure to be an allowable deduction as an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, it must be incidental and relevant to that end; Ronpibon at ATD 435; CLR 56. This test of deductibility has been explained in subsequent judgments of the High Court, so that to be deductible the expenditure must be incidental and relevant in the sense of having the essential character of expenditure incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable income. This formulation of the test has its origins in the joint judgment of Dixon C.J., Williams, Webb, Fullagar and Kitto JJ. in Charles Moore & Co. (W.A.) Pty. Limited v FC of T [1956] HCA 77; (1956) 11 ATD 147 at 149; (1956) 94 CLR 344 at 351. It has been applied subsequently in cases which include Lunney v FC of T (1958) 11 ATD 404 at 412; (1957-1958) 100 CLR 478 at 497; Handley v FC of T 81 ATC 4165 at 4171; [1981] HCA 16; (1980-1981) 148 CLR 182 at 194; FC of T v Forsyth 81 ATC 4157 at 4161; [1981] HCA 15; (1980-1981) 148 CLR 203 at 210: John v FC of T 89 ATC 4101; (1988-1989) 166 CLR 416. The essential character test is also applied to determine if the expenditure is of a capital, private or domestic nature as these cases illustrate.

Outgoings incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and outgoings of a capital or domestic nature are not mutually exclusive; Ronpibon Tin at ATC 435; CLR 56; Forsyth, John at ATC 4105-4106; CLR 427. It was decided in John that there is no “necessary antipathy between a loss or outgoing incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and a loss or outgoing of a private nature”: John at ATC 4108; CLR 431.

It is relevant to have regard to the terms and conditions of a taxpayer’s employment in determining whether expenditure incurred in determining whether expenditure incurred by an employee satisfies the first limb of s. 51(1): FC of T v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 12 ATD 348; (1961) 106 CLR 60 per Kitto J. at ATD 352: CLR 69; FC of T v Hatchett 71 ATC 4184; [1971] HCA 47; (1971) 125 CLR 494.


(c) In Cooper’s case Hill J said at page 4415:

“Food and drink are ordinarily private matters, and the essential character of expenditure of food and drink will ordinarily be private rather than having the character of a working or business expense. However, the occasion of the outgoing may operate to give to expenditure on food and drink the essential character of a working expense in cases such as those illustrated of work-related entertainment or expenditure incurred while away from home. No such circumstance, however, intervenes here. In particular, the mere fact that Mr Masters suggested or even directed Mr Cooper to eat particular food, does not convert the essential character of the food as private into a working expense.”


(d) Cooper’s case concerned a footballer who was given instructions to consume additional food with a view to the preservation of his position as a first grade player. The court held that the additional food lacked the necessary connection with his income producing activities and so disallowed his claimed deduction.

(e) That the cost of a meal can be work-related expenditure is clearly set out in the judgment of Hill J in Roads and Traffic Authority of NSW v FC of T 93 ATC 4508 at page 4521; after quoting the passage set out above from his judgment in Cooper’s case His Honour said:

“With respect, the same is true in the present case. Where a taxpayer is required by his employer, and for the purposes of his employer, to reside, for periods at a time, away from home and at the work site, and that employee incurs expenditure for the cost of sustenance, or indeed other necessary expenditure which, if the taxpayer had been living at home, would clearly be private expenditure is incurred, that is to say, the occasion of the outgoing operates to stamp that outgoing as having a business or employment related character.”


And later at page 4522 Hill J said:

“The facts of the present case are quite different. First, each of the persons deemed hypothetically to have incurred the expenditure are employees. They are not carrying on their own business. Second, they are required, as an incident of their employment, by their employer and for the purposes of the employer to live close by their work site for relatively short periods of time. No question arises of their choosing to live in these places. Each of the persons in question has a permanent house in which he lives when not in camp. None of the employees spend inordinate periods of time in the camps so that the camp becomes their home. Their house is retained and the employees in question travel home at weekends. They do not remain in the camps. The costs in question here are an incident of the employment. The costs in Toms were not.”


(f) What emerges from the decisions cited is, that while food and drink will generally be domestic in character, this will not always or necessarily be so and that the cost will be deductible where there is a sufficient nexus with the relevant work activity, or when in other words the “essential character” test is satisfied. The passages quoted from both of Cooper’s case and the RTA case contain references to “away from home”; moreover the first passage from RTA quoted above considered that the character of the expenditure would be private “... if the taxpayer had been living at home”. The trainer consumed the luncheon while not at home, but probably not while away from home. The references to “away for home” or “living away from home” in Cooper’s case were, in the view of the Tribunal, illustrative in the sense that they were inserted to set out circumstances in which an expenditure which is normally private might be work-related. The RTA case was, of course, concerned inter alia with living-away-from-home allowances. It was not suggested in argument that expenditure of this nature is deductible only when incurred while living away from home, and indeed any such suggestion would not, in the view of the Tribunal, be correct. Each case must turn on its own facts; the Tribunal considers that in this matter the facts point to a clear and close connection with work, and so that the essential character test is satisfied, whether or not the trainer was living away from home.

(g) In this instance and on the facts, the trainer was provided with a light and inexpensive luncheon which he was obliged to consume together with the trainees and during which time he was required to perform the duties previously set out in these Reasons. This then, on the facts, is in the opinion of the Tribunal clearly a work-related cost, which had the necessary connection with the trainer’s income derivation activities, and so that the “otherwise deductible” rule set out in section 44 of FBTAA would operate to reduce the taxable value of the property fringe benefit to nil.

  1. Mr Rothman furnished the Tribunal with an outline of the Applicant’s submissions. The thrust of his argument during the hearing was that the benefits in question were property fringe benefits and that section 44 of FBTAA operated to reduce their value to nil. As appears from these Reasons, I consider that his argument was correct. Mr Rothman did not in the hearing argue that the hotel was in effect the premises of the Applicant (albeit by licence) and as to which clause 9 of his outline is relevant. I note that I would not, had such an argument been put to me, have accepted that it is tenable. Nor did Mr Rothman, at the hearing, argue clause 11 of his written outline, as to the possible applicability of section 51AE(5)(h) of ITAA, and it is not necessary for me to deal with this provision.
  2. Accordingly, and in the circumstances, the objection decision under review is set aside, and the matter is remitted to the Respondent to issue a private ruling which accords with these Reasons.
