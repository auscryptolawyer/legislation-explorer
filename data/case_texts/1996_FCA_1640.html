Ici Australia Limited v the Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1996] FCA 1640 (24 July 1996)

ORDER

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

1. The appeals in both matters (VG 95 and 96 of 1995) be allowed.

2. The cross-appeals in both matters be dismissed.

3. Orders 2, 3 and 5 made at first instance on 9 February 1995 in both matters be set aside.

4. The decisions of the Commissioner concerning objections in respect of the assessments for income tax for the years of income ended 30 September 1986 and 1987 be set aside and in lieu thereof the objections be allowed.

5. Both assessments be remitted to the Commissioner for reassessment in accordance with law.

6. The Commissioner pay the appellant's costs of the appeals and cross-appeals and of the proceedings at first instance.

NOTE: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

Introduction and Facts

LOCKHART J These are two appeals, heard together by consent, from the judgment of a judge of the Court (Ryan J) who held that the difference between the amount paid by the appellant, ICI Australia Limited, under an agreement described in the evidence and the judgment as the 'Principal Assumption Agreement', and the amount which the appellant would have been required, but for that agreement, to pay to redeem certain debentures, was income according to ordinary concepts derived by the appellant and assessable under s. 25 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ('the Act'). His Honour also held that such a difference was assessable income within s. 25A of the Act as profit or gain derived from carrying out a profit-making undertaking or scheme; that Division 16E of the Act applied to determine the times at which parts of the 'gain' made by the appellant notionally accrued to it for the purpose of being included in its assessable income; and that, if the rights of the appellant under the Principal Assumption Agreement were an 'asset' to which the provisions of Part IIIA of the Act were capable of applying (his Honour held that they were not), then the performance by the corporation which was a party to that agreement to meet the obligations of the appellant concerning repayment of principal to debenture holders constituted a disposal of those rights for the purposes of Part IIIA of the Act (Capital Gains Tax).

2. There is also a cross-appeal before the Court in which the Commissioner challenges principally the finding of the learned primary Judge that the whole of the relevant profit or gain derived by the appellant should not be assessed in the 1986 year of income, but in that year and subsequent years.

3. The relevant facts are set out in the reasons for judgment of the primary Judge and they are not in dispute. It suffices to summarize them. The appellant has been at all material times a listed Australian public company. Pursuant to two debenture trust deeds, one dated 17 October 1966 and the other dated 10 August 1970, and amending deeds made on 28 April 1978, the appellant raised moneys from time to time by the issue of debenture stock. The trust deeds under which the debenture stock was issued contained restrictions on the borrowing capacity of the appellant.

4. In 1979 the business operations of the appellant were reorganized. As a result of the reorganization, the moneys raised from the debenture stock issue were lent to a subsidiary of the appellant, ICI Operations Pty Limited, at a sufficient interest to meet the cost of external interest payments incurred by the appellant. By the end of the 1980s the constraints imposed by the trust deeds upon the appellant inhibited it considerably from achieving its desire to expand its activities with new capital works or the acquisition of new businesses, without injecting equity capital or disposing of assets. Without resort to funds from these sources the appellant perceived that the cost of its future needs could not be met without infringing the asset-to-liability ratios in the debenture trust deeds. The constraints applied particularly to the appellant's wish to construct an 'Olefines' plant for ethelene production on its site at Botany in New South Wales.

5. The primary Judge observed that the difficulties which the asset-to-liability ratio posed for the appellant were compounded by the introduction in March 1984 of a new Accounting Standard: AAS17, by the National Council of the Institute of Chartered Accountants and the Australian Society of Accountants. The standard required that obligations under certain equipment leases be identified in the appellant's balance sheet as liabilities, with a consequent adverse effect on the assets-to-liabilities ratio.

6. A paper was prepared by Mr Dickson, the former corporate Treasurer of the appellant, and presented to a meeting of the Board of Directors of the appellant in December 1985. It outlined the reasons which the writer of the paper perceived to exist for removing or liberalizing the borrowing limitations to which the appellant was subject; and it identified four available options in these terms:

'i Seek modifications to the Trust Deed

Provisions are available to have the Deed modified to possibly

expand borrowing ratios (eg by increasing percentage of qualifying

assets from 60% to 66%) but the process is legally cumbersome

requiring an extraordinary resolution to be passed by debenture

holders and the possibility of having to increase existing coupon

rates.

ii Seek early redemption of all outstanding debentures

This would involve a similar approach to that adopted in the early

redemption of $10M of debentures during 1984/85. However as the

Trust Deed does not provide an option for the company to

compulsorily redeem its debentures and some debenture holders may

not accept the offer no matter how attractive, the problem with

the Trust Deed would remain.

iii Undertake a defeasance

A defeasance involves the purchase of a portfolio of risk free

securities such as government or semi-government stock with the

cash flows from the portfolio matching as closely as possible

those of the company's own debentures. This defeasance portfolio

of investments is held by the Trustee and as the interest and

principal commitments of the company's debentures are matched by

the defeasance portfolio, negotiation with the Trustee results in

the release by the Trustee of the debenture Trust Deed floating

charge and restrictive borrowing ratios.

iv Arrange a liability assumption

As an alternative to a defeasance, a liability assumption can be

arranged. Under this alternative, in consideration for a payment

by the company, the obligations to pay interest and principal on

the debentures are assigned to a risk-free party such as the

Commonwealth Bank, State Bank or a semi-government guaranteed

body, the Trustee can be satisfied that obligations to debenture

holders will be honoured and agree to release the floating charge

over assets and the restrictive borrowing ratios. The success of

a liability assumption is dependent upon identifying a suitable

counter-party to assume the liability for debentures.'

7. At a meeting of the board of directors of the appellant held in December 1995, the board authorized an investigation and assessment of the legal, tax, funding and accounting implications of each of those options, in conjunction with discussions with the trustees for debenture holders and auditors. The board also requested that a preferred option be brought to a board meeting in March or April 1986. Executives of the appellant then received several proposals from merchant banks and similar financial institutions, after which a further memorandum was submitted to the Board at its meeting in March 1986. That memorandum recommended a 'separated liability assumption' to remove the constraints imposed by the trust deed, and in an attachment it indicated why the other options outlined in the paper considered at the earlier board meeting had been discarded. The recommendation concerning the separated liability assumption was expressed in the following terms:

'It is recommended that the company enter into two separate

assumption agreements, one for the outstanding debenture principal

and the other for the ongoing interest payment obligations until

the respective debenture maturities. These obligations would be

assumed by two separate parties whose obligations are guaranteed

either by the Commonwealth or a State Government.

Under the principal assumption agreement ICI Australia would pay

to a Principal Assumption Party an amount calculated as the net

present value of the debenture principal repayments only,

discounted at a rate approximating the Commonwealth Bond yield for

each of the principal repayment dates. The amount of principal

assumed would be $99 million and the payment required would be

approximately $59 million which could be provided from available

cash sources of the company.

In the interest assumption agreement ICI Australia would undertake

to make a series of payments to an Interest Assumption Party to

coincide precisely with existing debenture interest obligations.

A fee would be payable to the Interest Assumption Party as

consideration for assuming the primary legal responsibility for

these payments.

It is expected that an extraordinary profit of approximately

$17 million (after tax) would arise from the two transactions in

the 1986 year comprising a capital profit of $40 million on the

principal assumption less $23 million (after tax) for the interest

assumption payments.

While the $40 million capital profit would not be subject to

income tax the regular fee payments would be claimed as tax

deductions.

It is proposed that debenture holders be given the option to early

redeem their holding based on current market yield to redemption

rates prior to the liability and interest assumption agreements

being entered into. This step is recommended to avoid any adverse

reaction from debenture holders. The number of debenture holders

accepting the early redemption offer will affect the final

extraordinary profit on the transaction.

Imperial Chemical Industries PLC have been advised of the need to

restructure the Trust Deed and the options considered.

Accounting and Audit Implications

The accounting and auditing implications of separate liability and

interest assumption agreements have been discussed with Price

Waterhouse.

It is expected that in the year the principal liability assumption

transaction takes place the accounts would disclose a general

description of the transaction and the amount of debt that has

been extinguished. The gain arising from the liability assumption

less the aggregate amount payable to the Interest Assumption Party

(net of tax) would be treated as an extraordinary profit.'

8. The board of the appellant approved the recommendation; this was recorded in the minutes of its meeting held on 18 March 1986 as follows:

'Restructuring Debenture Trust Deed

A memorandum (Annex 10113/2) was discussed and the Board gave

approval to proceed to negotiate "separated liability assumption"

agreements substantially along the lines recommended in the Annex

subject to the requirements that debenture holders be informed of

the proposal and given the opportunity to seek early redemption

of their securities and that the Board be kept fully informed of

progress on the transaction at subsequent meetings. The Finance

Director and Treasurer were authorised to appoint the financial

advisor to manage the transaction.'

9. The proviso expressed in the minutes - that debenture holders be given the opportunity to seek early redemption of their debentures - was not proceeded with for the reasons expressed to the appellant's board by Mr Eddey, a director:

'The volatility of interest rate movements on Commonwealth Bonds

in recent weeks (a 1.3% drop to 12.2% since March) has prompted

us to reconsider the desirability of proceeding to offer early

redemption as originally planned. My conclusion, shared by Chris

Hampson and the Treasury team is that we should not.

Our original proposal was to so structure the redemption offer to

debenture holders that there would be only a minimal acceptance

and the funds required ($58 million) and the capital gain

($17 million) were calculated on this basis.

It has become increasingly evident that in the current interest

rate climate it will be very difficult to strike the appropriate

"yield to redemption" rates to be offered for early redemptions,

particularly as the offer must remain open for 14 days which

increases the uncertainty of the outcome. In this regard, a high

acceptance of the early redemption offer by debenture holders

would significantly reduce the extraordinary capital profit on

the transaction and increase the cash required. For example, a 50%

acceptance of the early redemption offer would increase the funds

required to $79 million and reduce the capital gain to $7 million.

Our financial advisors agree that with the present interest rate

uncertainties the debenture restructuring should proceed without

an offer for early redemption. The Trustee for debenture holders

has also indicated that he would have no objection if the early

redemption alternative was not offered.

Whilst there may be some adverse reaction from debenture holders

if they are not offered the early redemption option we could

equally attract criticism if in offering early redemption the

discount factor is perceived by debenture holders as being too

high.

In terms of the original loan arrangements no debenture holder

will be disadvantaged by the decision not to offer early

redemption. From our point of view it removes uncertainty, and

quantifies the cash requirements and the net capital gain.'

10. On 6 June 1986 the appellant entered into the Principal Assumption Agreement between itself, the Melbourne and Metropolitan Board of Works ('MMBW'), ANZ Executors and Trustee Company Limited as trustee for the debenture holders, and a number of corporate guarantors.

11. Under the Principal Assumption Agreement, in return for the immediate payment by the appellant of an amount equal to 'the aggregate of the respective Present Values of the respective principal amounts of all Stock', MMBW agreed to assume the appellant's obligations under the trust deeds to repay the principal amounts due on the debentures on their respective maturity dates. The amount of the payment by the appellant to MMBW was to be calculated by reference to Commonwealth Bond Rates (see clauses 1, 2 and 3 of the Principal Assumption Agreement). So calculated, the amount was $62,309,546. As at 6 June 1986 the total amount of principal due on the debentures on their respective maturity dates was $98,662,800, repayable on various dates between 30 November 1986 and 31 January 2000.

12. Also on 6 June 1986, the appellant entered into the Interest Assumption Agreement with the State Bank, the trustee, and a number of corporate guarantors. Under clause 2 of the Interest Assumption Agreement the appellant agreed to pay to the State Bank, on each date when interest fell due in respect of the debentures, an amount equal to the aggregate of the amount of the interest payable in respect of the debentures on that date and, subject to subclauses 2(b), (c) and (d), an amount equal to 0.125% of the amount of interest payable. In consideration of the agreement by the appellant to make the payments under clause 2, the State Bank agreed to assume the obligations of the appellant to make payments of all amounts of interest in respect of the debentures in accordance with the trust deeds (clause 3).

13. No novation agreement was entered into between the appellant, the trustee and MMBW concerning principal, nor between the appellant, the trustee and the State Bank concerning interest, because this would have required the agreement of the debenture holders. Hence, the appellant itself remained liable at all material times, as it always had been, for repayment of both principal and interest to the debenture holders, notwithstanding that under the agreement between itself and MMBW with respect to principal and the State Bank with respect to interest, in commercial terms the liabilities of the appellant, for repayments of principal on the one hand and interest on the other, were assumed by MMBW and the State Bank respectively.

14. The appellant paid to MMBW the sum of $62,309,546 during the year ended 30 September 1986 pursuant to the Principal Assumption Agreement. The appellant paid to the State Bank the sum of $3,410,626 during the same financial year pursuant to the Interest Assumption Agreement.

15. The effect of the Principal Assumption Agreement and the Interest Assumption Agreement on the appellant's profit and loss account for the year ended 30 September 1986 was set out in the following terms in the accounts appended to its tax returns for that year:

'Extraordinary Profit

Capital profit arising from refinancing

Company's debenture borrowings under

separate principal and interest liability

assumption agreements.

Principal liability transferred to

assumption party 98,662,800

Less payment to assumption party (62,309,546)

Capital profit 36,353,254

Fees payable from 1/7/86 until maturity

of debentures in respect of interest

liability assumption (50,629,595)

Income tax - Future Income Tax Benefit 23,289,615'

16. What Mr Dickson described as a resultant 'extraordinary gain after income tax' was calculated by him in a memorandum of 11 February 1992 as follows:

'Debenture liability assumed 98,662,800

Less Liability Assumption Payment 62,309,546

Accrued future fee obligations 50,629,595 (112,939,141)

Loss before income tax (14,276,341)

Plus Future tax benefit of future

obligations (at 46% rate) 23,389,614

Extraordinary Gain after income tax $9,013,273'

17. In its statutory profit and loss account for the year ended 30 September 1986, the appellant treated the difference between the total amount of the principal liability in respect of the debentures ($98,662,800) and the amount of its payment to MMBW ($62,309,546), being the sum of $36,353,254, as an extraordinary profit.

18. In its statutory profit and loss account for the same year the appellant brought to account the whole of the amount payable by it to the State Bank under the Interest Assumption Agreement from 1 July 1986 until the maturity of the various debentures, being $50,629,595. In its tax returns the appellant claimed as a deduction the amount actually paid by it during two years of income, namely: $3,410,626 for the year of income ended 30 September 1986 (there is some disparity in the figures claimed by the appellant for the 1986 year), and $11,801,357 for the year of income ended 30 September 1987.

The assessments

19. By notice of amended assessment the Commissioner added the sum of $36,353,254 to the appellant's taxable income for the year ended 30 September 1986. The adjustment was made on the basis that the difference between the total principal amount that the appellant would have been obliged to pay as principal in respect of the debentures, but for the Principal Assumption Agreement, and the amount that it paid to MMBW, was assessable income under s. 25(1) or s. 25A of the Act or both. The claim by the appellant for a deduction in respect of payments made by it to the State Bank of $3,410,626 under the Interest Assumption Agreement in the year ended 30 September 1986 was disallowed by the Commissioner. At the appellant's request, that decision was referred to this Court and resulted in the proceeding at first instance VG 208 of 1991. The appellant objected to the amended assessment, which was allowed in part in respect of the $3,410,626 paid to the State Bank as interest assumption party.

20. Following a request for particulars made by the appellant with respect to the alleged gain of $36,353,254, the Commissioner issued a further notice of amended assessment in 1991, adding to the appellant's assessment for the year ended 30 September 1987 an amount of $8,033,418 in respect of 'profit made from debt defeasance transactions included as income'. This adjustment was made on the alternative basis that Division 16C of Part III of the Act applied to apportion gain to each of the years of income ending 30 September 1986 and 1987 - from the time of entering into the Principal Assumption Agreement until the time of the last payment by MMBW. By further and better particulars dated 18 October 1991, the Commissioner confirmed that the amount should be $7,898,095 instead of $8,033,418. The appellant made an objection to that amended assessment which was disallowed by the Commissioner. That disallowance was referred to this Court and resulted in the proceeding below VG 209 of 1991.

21. The two proceedings were heard together before Ryan J by consent.

22. Was the difference between the amount paid by the appellant under the Principal Assumption Agreement and the amount it would have been required but for that agreement to pay to redeem the debentures a profit derived by the appellant which was assessable income under s. 25(1) of the Act?

(i) Submissions

23. The argument on appeal proceeded on the basis that there were two questions: first, whether the appellant derived a profit or gain; second, if it did, whether that profit or gain constituted income according to ordinary concepts under s. 25(1). In my view it is more convenient to consider these two questions as one, in the form stated above, but it must be borne in mind that the two issues are discrete.

24. The primary Judge answered the question in the affirmative.

25. The appellant contended that, underlying the conclusion of the primary Judge that the difference between the amount paid by the appellant under the Principal Assumption Agreement and the amount which it would have been required, but for that agreement, to pay to redeem the debentures was income, was the premise that the accounting difference was a gain or profit for the purposes of the Act. The appellant challenged the validity of that premise. Counsel for the appellant submitted that the $36,353,254 was the accounting difference that bore no proximity to the gain on the transaction treated by the appellant as net profit in its financial accounts. It was argued that the amount which the appellant paid, namely, $62,309,546, was equal to the value of the promise it received from MMBW; and, applying ordinary business concepts, there was no profit. It was said that the promise which the appellant received from MMBW was not income, was not capable of being turned to account, and did not become income just because it brought about a saving of expenditure. It was submitted on behalf of the appellant that there was no reduction in the appellant's liability at any time; that the right which the appellant received to compel performance by MMBW of its corresponding obligation was not realized by the appellant and that there was no occasion for measuring a profit. Rather, the right was consumed in the process of performance at a time when the value of the right was equal to the amount expended in the performance process.

26. It was further argued on behalf of the appellant that, even if it could be said that there was a reduction in the appellant's liability (which the appellant denied), the reduction would only be characterized as a gain for the purposes of the Act where the underlying liability was on revenue account and carried forward from a previous period, or where the taxpayer was a finance company which had borrowed in the ordinary course of the finance company's business for on-lending at profit, not for some other special purpose. Here it was said that the appellant was never a finance company and that the liability was held on capital account. The proceeds of the debentures issues were, until the re-organization of the appellant's group in 1979, used by the appellant in its business operations. After that reorganization the debenture liabilities were passed down to the appellant's subsidiary, as a result of the appellant becoming the holding company of the group, for use in the subsidiary's business operations at a rate of interest sufficient to offset the costs of the appellant's external interest payments. The passing down of those funds did not take place as part of a business of borrowing money to be turned over at a profit.

27. Counsel for the Commissioner submitted that the substance and form of the transaction embodied in the Principal Assumption Agreement was that, by the immediate outlay of a smaller sum ($62,309,546), the appellant acquired the right to the future payment of a greater sum ($98,662,800) by MMBW, to be applied in discharge of the appellant's liability. It was said that there was a profit and that the character of the profit was income.

28. It was further submitted by counsel for the Commissioner that, if the appellant had outlaid the sum of $62,039,546 in order to obtain payment to itself over a period of years of the sum of $98,662,800, there could be no doubt that the difference would be income according to ordinary concepts and usages; and that it would make no difference that, upon receipt, the appellant used the moneys to pay out the debentures as and when they matured. In substance, this was said to be what occurred here. It was argued that, properly characterized, the profit accruing to the appellant was analogous to interest or recompense for the loss of the use of the moneys outlaid by it in entering into the Principal Assumption Agreement, rather than compensation for capital risk. Counsel pointed to the fact (a submission which found favour with the primary Judge) that the assumption party was a 'low risk' statutory authority, the profit was calculated by reference to Commonwealth bond rates, and the period before the debentures had to be redeemed was 'relatively short and ascertainable'. Accordingly, it was said that the profit was income according to ordinary concepts.

29. Counsel for the Commissioner also argued that just because the entry into the Principal Assumption Agreement was an isolated transaction, the profit could not be deprived of its character of income, and reliance was placed upon Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Myer Emporium Limited [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 163 CLR 199 at 220. It was further argued that the fact that the moneys to be paid by MMBW upon the maturity of the debentures would not be received by the appellant, but would be applied in discharge of liabilities that may be of a capital nature, could not deprive the profit of its character as income. Reference was made to s. 19 of the Act and to G P International Pipecoaters Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1990] HCA 25; (1990) 170 CLR 124 at 136.

30. Reliance was placed by both parties upon the decision of this Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Unilever Australia Securities Limited (Lockhart, Beaumont and Hill JJ) (1995) 56 FCR 152 which, like this case, was concerned with the effects of a debt-defeasance transaction for the purposes of income tax. The structure of the debt-defeasance transaction there closely resembled the one here. However, the natures of the principal parties, and their inspirations for undertaking the transactions in each case, differ. Unlike here, the protagonist in Unilever - Unilever Australia Securities Limited - was a finance company. Also unlike here, methodical withdrawal from financing was the protagonist's reason for the transaction in Unilever. It was held by the Court in that case that the transaction gave rise to a profit; that the profit was derived on revenue account; and that the profit was realized when the debentures matured.

(ii) Findings

31. The sum of $62,309,546 paid by the appellant to MMBW was a smaller sum than the face value of the debentures, namely, $98,662,800. The appellant paid the smaller sum to MMBW for the assumption by MMBW of the appellant's obligations under the Principal Assumption Agreement. When the time for repayment of the debentures arrived, the appellant was relieved of those obligations. In my view, even if the difference between the two sums may have constituted a gain to the appellant, the critical question is whether that gain was of an income or capital nature, and it is to that question I now turn.

32. Essential to the reasons for judgment of the majority in Unilever (Lockhart and Hill JJ, Beaumont J dissenting) was the fact that the taxpayer in that case carried on business as a finance company and the profit derived by it from a debt-defeasance transaction was held by the Full Court to be of a revenue character. The business of Unilever included the receipt of interest on funds lent by it and the payment of interest on moneys borrowed by it. This continued at least until the moneys borrowed by it had been repaid or while moneys were owing to it by its related companies.

33. It is true that after 1979 the business of the appellant in the present case consisted of owning land and raising long term finance for its groups' operations, principally by the issue of debentures, and on-lending the borrowings at a commercial rate of interest to its operating subsidiary. Hence, in 1986 the business of the appellant 'included the elements of borrowing, lending and repaying moneys borrowed, servicing the loans including receipt of interest': cf Unilever per Lockhart J at 156.

34. However, in the present case the appellant could not be described as a finance company at any relevant time. The proceeds of the debentures issued by it were, until the reorganization of the appellant's group in 1979, used by it in its business operations which were otherwise than as a finance company. Following the reorganization in 1979 the debenture liabilities were 'passed down' by the appellant to its subsidiary, as a result of the appellant becoming the holding company of the group, for use in the subsidiary's business operations at a rate of interest sufficient to offset the costs of the appellant's external interest payments. In my view the passing down of those funds did not take place as part of a business of borrowing money which it turned over at a profit: cf Avco Financial Services Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1982] HCA 36; (1982) 150 CLR 510 per Mason, Aickin and Wilson JJ at 527; and Commissioner of Taxation v Hyteco Hiring Pty Limited [1992] FCA 515; (1992) 39 FCR 502. It is unreal to describe the activities of the appellant after 1979 as being those of a finance company or even akin to the activities of such a company, notwithstanding that the evidence about its activities from that time onwards is somewhat lean. It was not in the ordinary course of the appellant's business or a necessary incident of its business after 1979 to make the necessary arrangements to repay the moneys borrowed by it. The Principal Assumption Agreement was an unusual or isolated transaction, and any gain to the appellant did not answer the description of income according to ordinary concepts. The case is distinguishable from Unilever.

35. By the outlay in 1986 of the sum of $62,309,546, the appellant acquired from MMBW the right to have its future liability of repaying debentures to its debenture holders assumed by MMBW. Once the appellant paid that sum to MMBW (or perhaps at the earlier point in time when it agreed to do so, but nothing turns on this) it lost forever the right to use that sum or any part of it. Subsequently, it derived no interest or other form of income from it. The right to the use of that sum passed to MMBW for whatever use it chose for the money. What the appellant received in return was the legally binding promise from MMBW that, as the debentures matured from time to time, MMBW would discharge the obligations of the appellant to the trustee for the debenture holders, and therefore discharge the obligations to the debenture holders themselves.

36. The appellant entered into the Principal Assumption Agreement for the purpose of alleviating the adverse consequences of the asset-to-liability ratio restrictions imposed by the debenture trust deed, which placed significant inhibitions upon the appellant's capacity to expand by undertaking new capital works or acquiring new businesses without injection of equity capital or disposal of assets. The amount which the appellant paid to MMBW of $62,309,546 was equal to the value of the promise which it received from MMBW.

37. It is true, as the primary Judge observed, that, if the appellant had retained the $62,309,546, and if interest rates had become higher in 1986, then it would have obtained the benefit of that increase. But if it adopted the course (which it did) of paying the money to MMBW, then the latter would get the benefit of the increase. In the result, the appellant was, as his Honour observed, prepared to take the risk of unfavourably high interest rates in order to remove the debenture liability from its balance sheet and thus free itself from the restrictions imposed by the borrowing ratios: ICI Australia Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1994) 125 ALR 63 at 80. I must confess, though, to having some doubt about the statement made by his Honour which immediately followed that last-mentioned observation:

'On the other hand, it was an attraction of the arrangement for

ICI that it was assured of some return from the use of the money

which it advanced to the assumption party over the period which

was to elapse before discharge of the debentures.'

38. With respect to his Honour, I have difficulty identifying that attraction.

39. According to ordinary concepts, by entering into the Principal Assumption Agreement, the appellant did not acquire a gain which might constitute income for the purposes of the Act. Following payment of the $62,309,546 to MMBW, the appellant was, in commercial terms, freed of its obligations to pay out the debentures when the debentures matured. The difference between the $62,309,546 and the amount of liabilities to debenture holders of $98,662,800 was essentially a saving in expenditure that otherwise it would have had to meet, measured by the difference between what was paid by the appellant to MMBW in 1986 and the sum of the amounts that otherwise the appellant would have been obliged to pay on the respective maturity dates of the debentures, but for the payment.

40. In my opinion the benefit which the appellant received in the form of the promise of MMBW to meet the appellant's obligations of repayment of principal to debenture holders could not have been turned to revenue account by the appellant: see Tennant v Smith [1892] UKHL 1; (1892) AC 150. See also Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Cooke [1980] FCA 37; (1980) 42 FLR 403 at 416:

'If the receipt of an item saves a taxpayer from incurring

expenditure, the saving is not income: income is what comes in, it

is not what is saved from going out. A non-pecuniary receipt can

be income if it can be converted into money; but if it be

inconvertible, it does not become income merely because it saves

expenditure.'

41. I accept that a reduction in the amount of a liability of a taxpayer can give rise to a profit or gain for accounting purposes and, where the liability reduced is on revenue account, a profit or gain for the purposes of the Act: see International Nickel Australia Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1977] HCA 49; (1977) 137 CLR 347. But in the present case the liability of the appellant to repay debenture holders was on capital account; and there was no reduction in its liability to debenture holders at any time. The appellant's liability to debenture holders remained unchanged (see clause 3(a) of the 1966 trust deed and clause 3(1) of the 1970 trust deed), notwithstanding the entry by the appellant into the Principal Assumption Agreement and the amendments made to the 1966 and 1970 trust deeds by the second amending trust deed. See also Unilever per Lockhart J at 156-157 and Hill J at 182-183. Although the amount paid by the appellant for the assumption by MMBW of its obligations under the Principal Assumption Agreement was less than the face value of the debentures, any gain derived by the appellant was on capital account.

Was any profit or gain derived by the appellant properly described as profit or gain arising from the carrying out by the appellant of a profit-making scheme within the meaning of s. 25A(1) of the Act?

42. I reject the submission of counsel for the Commissioner that, once it is found that the appellant did derive a profit or gain from the entry into the two Assumption Agreements, it must necessarily follow that it was part of a profit-making undertaking or scheme. Profit or gain made in the ordinary course of carrying on a business will constitute income. Also, the making of a gain or derivation of a profit otherwise than in the ordinary conduct of a business may, but will not necessarily, constitute income. Whether it does so depends on the circumstances of the case, as was observed by the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Myer Emporium Limited [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 163 CLR 199. For the reasons given earlier, any profit in question here was not derived by the appellant in the course of its ordinary business activities and as a result of those activities; it was not income arising from a profit-making undertaking or scheme.

43. Accordingly, in my opinion the appellant derived no gain or profit for the purposes of the Act at the time it entered into the Principal Assumption Agreement. Nor did it do so when debentures were redeemed. Nor will it do so when they are redeemed in the future, as some of them will be.

When was any profit or gain derived by the appellant?

44. It is not necessary for this question to be considered because I have concluded that any profit or gain derived by the appellant was of a capital nature. But the point was argued and can be answered briefly. On this particular question the reasoning of the majority in Unilever (Lockhart J at 156-157; Hill J at 182-185) should be applied. In the result, it appears that any profit or gain was derived by the appellant when the relevant debentures matured - when the times of repayment of capital arose and the repayment obligations were discharged. Profit or gain did not arise when the debt defeasance arrangements were made.

What is the impact of Division 16E of Part III of the Act?

45. The Commissioner assessed the appellant to tax on an alternative basis, namely, that the Principal Assumption Agreement was a 'qualifying security' within the meaning of Division 16E of Part III of the Act, that the appellant was the 'holder' of that security as defined by the Act, and that the appellant, as 'holder', was required by s. 159GQ(1) of the Act to include a portion of the gain from the Principal Assumption Agreement in its assessable income for the year of income ended 30 September 1986.

46. The expressions 'security' and 'qualifying security' are defined in s. 159GP(1) of the Act in these terms:

'"security" means:

(a) stock, a bond, debenture, certificate of entitlement, bill of

exchange, promissory note or other security;

(b) a deposit with a bank, building society or other financial

institution;

(c) a secured or unsecured loan; or

person is liable to pay an amount or amounts, whether or not the

liability is secured;'

'"qualifying security" means any security:

(a) that is issued after 16 December 1984;

(b) that is not a prescribed security within the meaning of

section 26C;

(ba) that is not part of an exempt series (see subsection (9A));

(c) the term of which, ascertained as at the time of issue of the

security will, or is reasonably likely to, exceed 1 year;

(d) that has an eligible return; and

(e) where the precise amount of the eligible return is able to be

ascertained at the time of issue of the security - in relation to

which the amount of the eligible return is greater than 1 1/2% of

the amount ascertained by multiplying the amount of the payment or

the sum of the payments (excluding any periodic interest) liable

to be made under the security by the number (including any

fraction) of years in the term of the security;

but does not, except as provided by subsection (10), include an

annuity'.

47. The word 'holder' is defined in s. 159GP(1) as follows:

'"Holder", in relation to a security at a particular time, means

the person who, if the amount or amounts payable under the

security were due and payable at that time, would be entitled to

receive payment of the amount or amounts'.

48. The primary Judge held that Division 16E applied to fix the time at which the appellant was to be assessable to tax in respect of parts of the premium involved in the Principal Assumption Agreement. He held that the Principal Assumption Agreement was a 'security' as defined in paragraph (d) of the definition of that expression in s. 159GP(1), being a contract under which MMBW was liable to pay an amount or amounts in redemption of the debentures. His Honour held that it was also a 'qualifying security', having a term exceeding one year from the date on which it was executed, even if some debentures matured for redemption before the expiration of the first year of the term of the agreement. His Honour further regarded the appellant as being at all times before redemption the 'holder' of the Principal Assumption Agreement in the sense of being the person in relation to that 'security' who was entitled to have the amounts payable in redemption of the debentures paid on its behalf to the trustee for the debenture holders. He said that, although the trustee was nominated as the recipient of the payment, it was the appellant's obligations which were pro tanto to be discharged by the payment; and it was the appellant who had provided consideration for the promise by the assumption party to procure that discharge. His Honour ordered that the assessments for the 1986 and 1987 years be amended by reducing the amount of assessable income in respect of the principal assumption transaction in each of those years to an amount calculated in accordance with Division 16E.

49. Division 16E of the Act is discussed in Australia and New Zealand Savings Bank Limited v Commissioner of Taxation [1993] FCA 282; (1993) 42 FCR 535 per Hill J at 561 and in Unilever per Hill J at 187 and 188. I need not recite what his Honour said.

50. The Principal Assumption Agreement does, in my view, answer the description of a 'security' as defined in paragraph (d) of the definition of that expression in s. 159GP(1) of the Act. It is also a 'qualifying security' as defined in s. 159GP(1). The question is whether the appellant fits the description of the 'holder' of a security.

51. It was argued on behalf of the Commissioner that on the proper construction of the definition of 'holder', the words 'entitled to receive payment' are not confined to actual or personal receipt, and that the expression 'the person who ... would be entitled to receive payment' encompasses a person on whose behalf or at whose direction another received the money. It was submitted that, by entering into and providing consideration for the Principal Assumption Agreement, the appellant procured MMBW's promise to make future payments in discharge of the appellant's obligations in respect of the debentures. The primary Judge upheld this submission.

52. The following passage from the judgment of Hill J in Unilever at 188 (a passage with which Lockhart and Beaumont JJ agreed) is apt:

'The language of the definition of "holder" in s 159GP(1) is not

ambiguous. It means what it says. For a person to be a holder for

the purposes of the Division in relation to a security, that

person must, at a relevant time, be entitled to receive payment of

amounts payable under the security. UAS was never, at any relevant

time, so entitled. Nor does s 19 of the Act, as the Commissioner

submitted, assist. That section does not operate to deem a person

to be entitled to receive payment of an amount. It deems money to

be derived: cf Howell v Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) (1994)

94 ATC 4,186 at 4,189-4,190 for a discussion of that section.'

53. Under the Principal Assumption Agreement, MMBW assumed the obligations of the appellant to make due and punctual payment of the principal amount of the debenture stock in accordance with clause 3(c) of each of the Trust Deeds - to pay or cause to be paid into a bank account, held in the name of the trustee and nominated by it, the said amounts; such payment was to be in pro tanto satisfaction of the obligations of MMBW under clause 3(a), and likewise in pro tanto satisfaction of the obligations of the appellant to repayment of principal under clause 3 of each of the trust deeds (clause 3(b) of the Principal Assumption Agreement).

54. Although the provisions of the Principal Assumption Agreement in this case and the relevant provisions of the Liability Assumption Agreement in Unilever are not in precisely the same terms, there is no material difference between them so far as this question of the application of Division 16E is concerned.

55. In my opinion the appellant does not answer the description of a 'holder' within the meaning of s. 159GP(1). It is not entitled to receive payment of amounts payable under the Principal Assumption Agreement and never at any relevant time has been so entitled. Division 16E can have no application to this case.

Capital Gains Tax

56. There are two questions which arise here. The first question is whether the appellant's rights under the Principal Assumption Agreement answer the description of an 'asset' within the meaning of Part IIIA of the Act.

57. Section 160A provides, so far as relevant, that an 'asset' means any form of property, and includes a debt or a chose in action or any other right, whether legal or equitable, and whether or not a form of property.

58. Counsel for the Commissioner argued that the rights acquired by the appellant under the Principal Assumption Agreement constituted an asset within the meaning of Part III; in this regard, reliance was placed on Coulls v Bagot's Executor and Trustee Co Limited [1967] HCA 3; (1967) 119 CLR 460 and Beswick v Beswick [1967] UKHL 2; (1968) AC 58. It is true that, if X promises Y to pay money to Z, the promise may be enforceable by a decree of specific performance in favour of Y; and those two cases do support that proposition.

59. Counsel for the Commissioner then contended that the obligation assumed by MMBW to the appellant was so like a debt that it fell within the definition of an 'asset' in s. 160A.

60. In my opinion the right acquired by the appellant under the Principal Assumption Agreement was a right to compel MMBW to specifically perform its obligations under clause 3 of that agreement; but this right was not an asset for the purposes of Part IIIA of the Act. It was not a proprietary right, because by its very nature it was not capable of being assumed by third parties. It was merely a personal right acquired by the appellant, which was incapable of being assumed by a third party: see Hepples v Commissioner of Taxation (1990) 22 FCR 1 per Lockhart J at 10, Gummow J at 27; Commissioner of Taxation v Cooling (1990) 22 FCR 42 per Hill J at 59; R v Toohey; Ex parte Meneling Station Pty Limited [1982] HCA 69; (1982) 158 CLR 327 per Mason J at 342-343.

61. I respectfully agree with the primary Judge (1994) 125 ALR 63 at 92, where his Honour said that:

'no conceivable assignee would have any interest in enforcing

MMBW's obligation which was to discharge ICI's obligation to the

debenture holders. The right to the enforcement of the assumption

agreement was thus personal to ICI and enforceable, unless ICI had

itself redeemed the debentures, only by a decree of specific

performance'.

62. In Unilever none of the members of the Court considered the question whether the right acquired by the taxpayer under the Liability Assumption Agreement (the equivalent of the Principal Assumption Agreement in this case) was an 'asset' for the purposes of Part IIIA. The learned Judge at first instance in Unilever made a passing reference to this point when he said [1994] FCA 1172; (1994) 122 ALR 402 at 414:

'It seems to me that UAS, pursuant to the Agreement, acquired a

proprietary right and therefore an asset for the purposes of Part

IIIA of the Act.'

63. With respect, I do not agree with his Honour on that point.

64. The second question that arises under this heading is whether, assuming the appellant acquired a proprietary right under the Principal Assumption Agreement which was an asset for the purposes of Part IIIA of the Act, that right was disposed of by the appellant by MMBW's performance of its obligations under that agreement. The relevant section is s. 160M, subsection (1) of which provides that, where a change has occurred in the ownership of an asset, the change shall be deemed for the purposes of Part IIIA to have effected a disposal of the asset by the person who owned it immediately before the change, and an acquisition of the asset by the person who owned it immediately after the change. Subsection (2) relevantly provides that a reference in subsection (1) to a change in the ownership of an asset is a reference to a change that has occurred in virtually any way.

65. In my opinion if the appellant acquired an asset for the purposes of Part IIIA of the Act (which it did not), that right was not disposed of by MMBW's performance of its obligations under the Principal Assumption Agreement.

66. The performance by a party to an agreement of its obligations to another party thereto is not in my view, on the ordinary understanding of the notion of disposal of an asset, a disposal by the party to whom the obligations are owed of the rights constituted by those obligations. The performance of the contractual obligations by a party to an agreement does not involve any dealing whatever by the party to whom the obligations are owed with the rights constituting those obligations.

67. The Commissioner argued that MMBW's performance of its obligations under the Principal Assumption Agreement constituted a disposal of the appellant's rights under that agreement as the rights were 'satisfied or discharged', to use the language of s. 160M(3)(b) of the Act. That provision provides that a change shall be taken to have occurred in the ownership of an asset by the cancellation, release, discharge, satisfaction, surrender, forfeiture, expiry or abandonment at law or in equity of the asset (this applying in the case of an asset being a debt, a chose in action or any other right or an interest or right in or over property).

68. The words 'discharge' and 'satisfaction', which are the relevant words in s. 160M(3)(b) for present purposes, cannot in my view be construed as extending to the performance of obligations under an agreement giving rise to the rights in accordance with the terms of the agreement. Those words must be confined to cases where the rights are satisfied or discharged otherwise than by performance of the obligations which give rise to the rights by the other party to the contract.

69. This view appears to me to be similar to the view adopted in Unilever by the trial Judge at 414-5 and on appeal by Beaumont J at 177 where his Honour said:

'... there must be a change in ownership of the respondent's

rights in some way or other. But, in my view, there was no such

change. At all times, the respondent's rights remained vested in

the respondent. The rights were never divested and there was no

change in their ownership.'

That passage is apt in the present case.

70. In my view there is no room for the application of Part IIIA to this case.

The debenture deeds

SUNDBERG J Pursuant to two trust deeds, one dated 17 October 1966 and the other dated 10 August 1970, the appellant raised money by the issue of debenture stock. The deeds imposed borrowing limitations on the appellant by reference to various financial criteria. Clause 6 of each deed provided that the total liabilities of the appellant and related companies that had guaranteed its obligations would not be permitted to exceed 60 per cent of the tangible assets of all those companies; that their secured liabilities would not be permitted to exceed 40 per cent of those assets, and that their total liabilities which ranked in point of security prior to the money owing on the security of the deed would not be permitted to exceed 20 per cent of their tangible assets. Under clause 10 of each deed, breach of the restrictions enabled the trustee to enforce the security. Prior to 1979 the money raised by the issues was retained and used by the appellant in its business operations. As a result of a reorganisation in 1979, the appellant lent the money to ICI Operations Pty Ltd at interest, to enable that company to meet the cost of external interest payments.

2. The borrowing constraints imposed by the deeds significantly inhibited the appellant's expansion of its operations. It could not construct new capital works or acquire new businesses without a further injection of equity capital or disposing of assets. The difficulties created by the asset to liability ratios were compounded by the introduction in March 1984 of accounting standard AAS17, which required the appellant to identify as balance sheet liabilities any payment obligations under certain types of equipment lease. This had an adverse effect on the asset to liability ratios.

The assumption proposal

3. In 1985 the appellant's Corporate Treasurer, Mr. Dickson, sought external advice about removing or liberalising the asset to liability ratios. In December of that year he presented a paper on the topic to the appellant's board. It identified four options. The first was to seek the agreement of debenture holders to the modification of the trust deeds. The second was to seek their agreement to early redemption. The third was to undertake a defeasance. As the interest and principal commitments under the debentures were matched by the portfolio, the trustee would agree to release the floating charge and borrowing ratios. The final option was to arrange for a risk free third party to assume the appellant's obligations under the debentures. Trial judge's holdings

16. The matters were heard together by Ryan J. His Honour held that the difference between the amount paid by the appellant and the amount it would have been required, but for the principal assumption agreement, to pay to redeem the debentures was income according to ordinary concepts (s.25(1)), alternatively income under s.25A. His Honour then held that the amounts in question should be assessed as accruing in the manner stipulated in Div.16E of Pt III.

Any gain or profit?

17. The appellant contended that it made no gain or profit as a result of entering into the principal assumption agreement. The amount it paid was equal to the value of the promise it received from MMBW. The difference between the amount paid and the face value of the debenture was a saving in expenditure. It was not an accretion to the appellant's economic power. MMBW's promise could not have been turned to pecuniary account. The appellant relied on the observations of Brennan, Deane and Fisher JJ in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Cooke [1980] FCA 37; (1980) 42 FLR 403, at p 416:

If the receipt of an item saves a taxpayer from incurring

expenditure, the saving is not income: income is what comes in, it

is not what is saved from going out. A non-pecuniary receipt can be

income if it can be converted into money; but if it be

inconvertible, it does not become income merely because it saves

expenditure.

18. The appellant accepted that a reduction in the amount of a liability can give rise to a profit or gain for accounting purposes and, when the liability reduced is on revenue account, a profit or gain for the purposes of the Act: International Nickel Australia Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1977] HCA 49; (1977) 137 CLR 347, at p 366. But in the present case there was no reduction in the appellant's liability to debenture holders. That liability remained unchanged: clause 3(a) and (b) of the principal assumption agreement, and Commissioner of Taxation v. Unilever Australia Securities Ltd. (1995) 56 FCR 152, at pp 156-157, 182-183. Further, the appellant's liability to debenture holders was on capital and not revenue account.

19. Nor, submitted the appellant, was there any profit or gain to the appellant when MMBW from time to time performed its obligations under the agreement. There was then no realisation of any right or property of the appellant. The performance by MMBW of its contractual obligation to the appellant did not involve the realisation of a right. Performance of the contract brought the appellant's chose in action to an end.

20. In Unilever Australia Securities Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1994] FCA 1172; (1993) 122 ALR 402 the primary judge held that the sum paid by Unilever to the assumption party represented the value at the date of the payment of the obligations assumed by the assumption party: the value to Unilever of the assumption by the other party of the primary liability to the debenture holders equalled the amount paid by Unilever. No profit or gain came home to Unilever as a result of the defeasance agreements. On appeal to the Full Court the taxpayer conceded that the primary judge was wrong in this respect: (1995) 56 FCR 152. At p.155 Lockhart J said:

the sum of $6,301,706 paid by UAS to LGFA for the assumption by

LGFA of UAS's obligations to pay the face value of the debentures

was a smaller sum than the $7,385,000 being the face value of the

debentures; the difference must constitute a profit to UAS.

21. At p.182 Hill J said:

Before this Court counsel for UAS conceded that his Honour had

erred in holding that no profit arose as a result of the defeasance

arrangements. In my view that concession was properly made. At the

very least, when the time came for the debentures to be repaid, UAS

had been relieved of an obligation to pay the face value of the

debentures. The amount it paid for the obligation of LGFA under the

assumption agreement was less than the face value of the debentures

and the difference clearly represented a profit.

22. The other member of the Court, Beaumont J, did not deal with this issue.

23. Lockhart and Hill JJ did not explain why the "difference" was a profit. But in my view there was in the present case a profit, and not a saving, for this reason. The appellant had an asset, namely $62 million. It employed that asset to produce a yield in the future of a larger amount in order to discharge its liabilities under the debentures. It could have lent its money to MMBW on terms that the capital and interest would be paid to it at some time in the future. Instead of doing that, it paid the money to MMBW on terms that MMBW would in the future discharge the appellant's liability to pay $98 million to a third party. The only difference between the two cases is that the obligation undertaken by MMBW was not to pay the money to the appellant, but to pay it directly to the appellant's creditor. This in my opinion is not a significant difference. Therefore, effectively, money came in and was not simply "saved from going out".

24. The appellant's argument that there was no profit because the $62 million paid to MMBW was the then present value of the appellant's long term liability to debenture holders confuses liabilities and assets. The appellant's liability of $98 million had no present value. What the appellant had was a cash asset. It exploited that asset by converting it into a right to have its obligation to pay $98 million in the future discharged by MMBW.

25. For these reasons I respectfully agree with the conclusion reached by Lockhart and Hill JJ in Unilever. Accordingly, the difference between the amount paid to MMBW and the face value of the debentures was a profit to the appellant.

When was the profit or gain derived?

26. A profit or gain was derived when the relevant debentures matured and the terms of repayment of capital arose and the repayment obligations were discharged. What Lockhart J said of the debt defeasance arrangements in Unilever is applicable to the principal assumption agreement: (1995) 56 FCR, at pp.156-157. See also per Hill J at p.185. There has been no profit or gain in relation to the two debentures that have not yet matured.

Income according to ordinary concepts?

27. Although not in fact interest on the money paid over, the appellant's profit is analogous to interest. It is, in my view, recompense for loss of the use of the money the appellant outlaid when it entered into the agreement, rather than compensation for the risk that MMBW might not perform its part of the agreement. MMBW was a "low risk" statutory authority. The period before the debentures had to be redeemed was relatively short: eleven of the fourteen maturity dates were within six years of the date of the agreement. And the profit was calculated by reference to Commonwealth bond rates: clause 2(a) and the definitions of "Present Value", "Discount Rate" and "Bond Rate". See generally Lomax v. Peter Dixon and Son (1943) 1 KB 671, at pp 673-677 and Commissioner of Taxation v. Hurley Holdings (NSW) Pty Ltd [1989] FCA 392; (1989) 23 FCR 435, at p 440.

28. It is accordingly not necessary to decide whether the appellant made the profit in the course of its business. A profit made in the ordinary course of carrying on a business is income. But a gain made otherwise than in the ordinary course of carrying on the business may be income if it arises out of a transaction entered into by the taxpayer with the intention or purpose of making a profit. And this may be so even though the profit is made as the result of an isolated venture. But where a transaction occurs outside the scope of the taxpayer's ordinary business activities, in order for the profit to be income, the transaction must not only be commercial in nature, but must have been entered into with the intention or purpose of making the profit. See Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Myer Emporium Ltd [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 163 CLR 199, at pp 209, 211-213 and Westfield Ltd v. Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 28 FCR 333, at p 342.

29. After 1979 the appellant's business consisted of owning land and raising long-term finance for its group's operations, principally by the issue of debentures and on-lending the borrowings at interest. Had I not held that the profit was income according to ordinary concepts on the ground that it was recompense for the loss of use of the appellant's money, I would have held that in 1986 it was in the ordinary course of the appellant's business of borrowing, lending and repaying money borrowed, to make the assumption arrangements for the repayment of money borrowed by the issue of debentures. Cf. Unilever, at p.156 per Lockhart J. But even if the transaction was extraordinary (i.e. not in the ordinary course), it was a commercial one and, at the time it was entered into, the appellant intended to make a profit from it. The memorandum which recommended the liability assumption transactions contemplated a $40 million "capital profit" on the principal assumption agreement. The option of early redemption was abandoned because the possibility of a high acceptance rate would significantly reduce the extraordinary capital profit on the transaction. So it is clear that at the time the transaction was entered into, the appellant had the intention or purpose of making the relevant profit.

30. That the arrangement involved MMBW discharging capital liabilities of the taxpayer does not deprive the profit of its character as income: G.P International Pipecoaters Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1990] HCA 25; (1990) 170 CLR 124, at pp 136-137.

Profit-making scheme?

31. A scheme may be a profit-making scheme notwithstanding that neither the sole nor the dominant purpose of entering into it was the making of the profit. It is enough that making a profit was a not insignificant purpose of the scheme. See Commissioner of Taxation v. Cooling (1990) 22 FCR 42, at pp 56-57. The primary judge concluded that although the receipt of a return on its money was not the appellant's sole or dominant purpose in entering into the principal assumption agreement, "it underlay, and was an integral part of, the whole transaction". There was ample evidence for his Honour's finding that the transaction was a profit-making scheme. As I have said, the recommendation that the liability assumption transactions be entered into contemplated a $40 million capital profit on the principal assumption agreement, and the option of early redemption was abandoned because of the risk that a high acceptance rate would significantly reduce the extraordinary capital profit. The appellant's gain was in my view a profit which arose from the carrying out of a profit-making scheme within s.25A(1).

Time of derivation of income

32. The respondent did not contend below that the appellant's gain should be brought to account on an emerging basis over the interval between the date of the agreement and the dates when the various principal amounts became payable by MMBW. It sought leave to do so on appeal. However, neither in its written submissions nor in argument did it say why it should have leave, or why, if leave were granted, the gain should be brought to account on an emerging basis. For those reasons I would not grant leave.

Division 16E

33. The provisions of this Division (ss.159GP to 159GZ) have the effect of taxing as it accrues part of the overall general yield on discounted and other deferred interest securities. A security that falls within the Division is called a "qualifying security". To be a qualifying security, the security must be issued after 16 December 1984, its expected term must exceed or be likely to exceed twelve months, and the sum of all payments under the security (other than payments of periodic interest) must exceed its issue price: s.159GP(3). This excess is called the "eligible return" of the security, and must be such as would otherwise constitute assessable income of the taxpayer when actually paid: s.159GX. In addition to any periodical interest payments received, the holder of a qualifying security is assessable in each year of income during the life of the security on part of the eligible return: s.159GQ.

34. It was common ground that the principal assumption agreement is a "security" within par.(d) of the definition in s.159GP(1) and a "qualifying security" within the definition of that expression. The primary judge held that the appellant was the "holder" of the security, with the result that Div.16E applied to accelerate the derivation of the appellant's gain. The "holder" of a security at a particular time is "the person who, if the amount or amounts payable under the security were due and payable at that time, would be entitled to receive payment of the amount or amounts". Ryan J acknowledged that under the agreement MMBW was to make payment to the trustee, but considered that because MMBW's payment was made on behalf of, or at the direction of, the appellant, the payments were payments which the appellant was entitled to receive. It seems to me, with respect, that that gives a very strained and unnatural meaning to the words "entitled to receive payment". Under clause 3(b) of the agreement MMBW has a primary liability to pay the trustee, not a secondary liability to pay the appellant after the appellant has paid the trustee. In any event the matter is concluded by Unilever, where at pp.187-188 Hill J, with whom Lockhart and Beaumont JJ agreed, declined to follow Ryan J's reasoning in the present case.

Capital gains

35. In relation to the 1987 year, in the alternative to contending that the appellant's profit was assessable as income, the respondent argued that the principal assumption agreement was an asset acquired by the appellant which was partly disposed of on each occasion on which MMBW redeemed debentures in discharge of its obligations under the agreement. Three debenture issues matured in that year. None matured in the appellant's 1986 tax year. The consideration on each such partial disposal was said to exceed the indexed cost base of the asset, thereby realising a capital gain taxable under Pt IIIA.

36. Because I have concluded that the gain is assessable as income, I need not decide whether it is a capital gain. However, as the matter was fully argued, I will express my views on two issues that were canvassed - whether the agreement is an "asset", and if so whether the performance of the agreement was a "disposal" of it. The word "asset" is defined in s.160A as "any form of property" and includes a debt, a chose in action, and any other right "whether or not a form of property". Under s.160M(1) a change in the beneficial ownership of an asset is deemed to have effected a disposal of the asset by the person who owned it immediately before the change. By sub-s.(3)(b) a change is to be taken to have occurred in the ownership of a debt, a chose in action or any other right, or an interest or right in or over property, by its "cancellation, release, discharge, satisfaction, surrender, forfeiture, expiry or abandonment".

37. Ryan J did not decide whether the appellant made a capital gain. Nevertheless he expressed the view that the principal assumption agreement was not an "asset" because it was not, before breach by MMBW, a chose in action that was assignable. That was because "no conceivable assignee would have any interest in enforcing MMBW's obligation which was to discharge ICI's obligation to the debenture holders". To be an "asset" for capital gains purposes a right must be proprietary in character: Hepples v. Commissioner of Taxation (1990) 22 FCR 1, at pp 15,27; Commissioner of Taxation v. Cooling (1990) 22 FCR, at pp 59, 65. A characteristic of "property" is assignability: Reg. v. Toohey; Ex parte Meneling Station Pty. Ltd. [1982] HCA 69; (1982) 158 CLR 327, at pp 342-343. That characteristic, however, looks not to whether, in a particular case, an assignee could be found (i.e. whether anyone would be interested in taking an assignment), but to the inherent nature of the right. Is it capable in law of assignment? The agreement was clearly assignable in that sense, and is an "asset".

38. The second question is whether the agreement was disposed of when MMBW performed its obligations under it by paying the debenture holders. For ease of exposition I will put aside the fact that, on the respondent's argument, there was a series of partial disposals rather than one single disposal. The narrow issue here is whether the performance of a contract is a disposal of the asset it constitutes. In ordinary usage "disposition" in relation to an asset contemplates the continued existence of the asset, with one owner before the disposition and another after it. This is reflected in sub-s.(1) - "the person who owned it immediately before the change ... the person who owned it immediately after the change". And sub-s.(2) does not in this respect modify sub-s.(1). It merely describes a number of ways in which a change within sub-s.(1) may occur. But sub-s.(3) gives instances of changes in ownership where after the "change" there is no new owner because the asset has ceased to exist. Most of the concepts listed in sub-s.(3) - cancellation, release, discharge, satisfaction, surrender, forfeiture and expiry - involve the asset going out of existence, and thus expand the notion of disposition beyond its ordinary meaning. See Cooling (1990) 22 FCR, at p.60. A lease that has been surrendered does not survive in the former lessor's hands. The lessor has the land free from the lease. So with a forfeiture. From the time of re-entry, the lessor has the land as if the lease had not been made: Hartshorne v. Watson [1838] EngR 349; (1838) 4 BingN.C. 178, at p 182. In the case of a debt, its payment discharges it. In the case of a contract, its performance discharges it. "Discharge" is the technical legal word appropriate to describe the effect of the payment of a debt and the performance of a contract. See Cheshire and Fifoot, Law of Contract, 4th Aust. ed. (1981) par.2301.

39. The appellant sought to limit the words "discharge" and "satisfaction" in s.160M(3)(b) in relation to debts and choses in action to cases where rights are satisfied or discharged otherwise than by performance. In my view there is no warrant for reading down the provision in this way. As a result of the deeming provision in s.160M(1), a change in the beneficial ownership of an asset will result in a disposal of the asset by the person who owned it immediately before the change, even if that person does not engage in any dealing with the asset. Consequently, as long as there is a change in the beneficial ownership, the fact that the 'discharge' or 'satisfaction' of a debt does not involve any activity on behalf of the obligee, will not prevent the discharge or satisfaction from constituting a disposal by the obligee of the contract under which the debt is owed. Thus the performance of a contract is a disposal of the asset constituted by it. Whether the disposal gives rise to any capital gain is another matter.

40. The view I have just expressed was not shared by the two Unilever judges who considered the matter. At first instance, Spender J said that on performance of the liability assumption agreement, the taxpayer's asset, namely the right to compel performance, was no longer owned by anyone because it had ceased to exist. But that approach assumes, contrary to s.160M(3)(b), that there can be no disposal unless the asset survives the relevant event. Beaumont J, the only member of the Full Court to consider Pt IIIA, appears to have shared Spender J's view. He quoted, with apparent approval, the passage from Spender J's judgment where his Honour said that there was no disposal because the asset had ceased to exist, and added that none of the expressions in s.160M(3)(b) describes the case where another party promises to meet the primary liability of the taxpayer and subsequently meets it: "The taxpayer's contractual right to have this done was not cancelled, released etc.". I would agree with his Honour that the agreement was not cancelled or released by its performance. But I would not agree with his Honours "etc.", for that must cover "discharge". The expansive effect of sub-s.(3)(b) prevents me from accepting the view that the performance of a contract cannot be a disposal of the asset constituted by the right to compel that performance for the reason that after performance that right is no longer owned by anyone.

Conclusion

41. On the issues that have fallen for decision I have differed from the primary judge only on the time at which the appellant's gain was made. Accordingly, to that extent only I would allow the appeals. I would uphold the contention in paragraph 2 of the respondent's notice of cross-appeal relating to the 1987 year, and reject the contention in paragraph 2 of its notice of cross-appeal relating to the 1986 year. I would dismiss the cross-appeal relating to the 1986 year, and make the order sought in paragraph B of the notice of cross-appeal relating to the 1987 year. I would remit the matters to the respondent to amend the appellant's amended assessments in accordance with these reasons.
