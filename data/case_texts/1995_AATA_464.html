DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



Income tax - assessable income - partnership income - meaning of partnership dissolution agreement.



Ford v Beech [1848] EngR 10; (1848) 11 QB 852



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

No. TT94/2

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

Place : Hobart



Decision : The decision under review is set aside. The matter is remitted to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with a direction that the taxpayer’s assessable income from the partnership for the year ended June 1992 amounted to only $15,000.

REASONS FOR DECISION



10 March 1995 Mr A M Blow (Deputy President)



In this application for the review of a decision made by the Commissioner of Taxation, the taxpayer was objecting to a decision made in regard to the tax year ended June 1992. The Commissioner decided he should be taxed on 50% of the net profit of a partnership in which he was partner. He contends that he should be taxed on a lesser sum, namely $15,000, which he contends was his agreed share of of the profits of the partnership for that year.



The partnership was established in the early 1980’s. There were only ever two partners. They entered into a written partnership agreement which was not varied prior to 30 June 1982. Clause 9 of that agreement included a provision for the sharing of profits in the following terms:



“...on the 30th day of June in each ... year during the continuance of the partnership an account shall be taken of all the capital and assets and liabilities for the time being of the partnership and a balance sheet and profit and loss account making due allowance for depreciation and recouping any lost capital shall be prepared and a copy thereof furnished to each of the partners who shall be bound thereby unless some manifest error shall be discovered within three months in which case such error shall be rectified. Immediately after the preparation of the said profit and loss account and a balance sheet the net profits (if any) shall be divided between the partners equally.”



An accountant who had dealt with the affairs of the partnership for the last several years of its existence was called as a witness for the Commissioner. He produced a summary of undrawn profits and capital contributions which was tendered as exhibit R2. Its contents were not challenged. They reveal that, for the period from the commencement of the partnership until 30 June 1991, the partners’ drawings exceeded the partnership profits by $24,089.98. That is to say, there were no undrawn profits at 30 June 1991.



On 30 June 1992 the taxpayer and his partner agreed to dissolve their partnership as from the following day. Their dissolution agreement was constituted by a draft deed of dissolution with handwritten amendments. Clause 11 of that document provided as follows:



“11.1 Clause 9 of the Partnership Agreement is amended by adding at the end thereof the words “or in such other shares as the parties shall agree upon”.



11.2 The Retiring Partner shall be entitled to $15,000 as his share of the undrawn

profits of the partnership in the financial year ending 30th June 1992.



11.3 The Retiring Partner shall make no claim in respect of undrawn profits in any prior period.”



The taxpayer contends that what this clause meant, or what both parties meant it to say, was that the partnership profits for the year ended June 1992 would not be divided equally in accordance with the original partnership agreement, but that he would receive only $15,000 of them, and his former partner would receive the rest.

In order to determine the meaning, or intended meaning, of clause 11 of the dissolution agreement, it is necessary to have regard to the circumstances leading up to that agreement being made. The parties had been discussing the terms of the dissolution of their partnership for some months. They had had a series of meetings. They had reached agreement in principle that the taxpayer was to sell his share in the partnership business to his partner. A suggested lump sum price had been discussed. There had been no discussion as to who was to receive what profits from the then current financial year until June 1992. During June, at a meeting between the partners and their advisers, the taxpayer’s partner estimated the 1991/92 profits at about $30,000. Subsequently clause 11 was proposed by one of his advisers, and agreed to by his partner.



The dissolution agreement also provided for the taxpayer to be paid a substantial sum by his former partner in consideration of the assignment of the taxpayer’s “share and interest of and in the said business and the property assets credits effects and goodwill thereof including the stock-in-trade and other personal chattels and effects”. There is nothing else in the dissolution agreement that could possibly be construed as affecting the rights of the partners in respect of the profits for the year ended June 1992.



In interpreting the relevant provisions of the dissolution agreement, its meaning must “be collected from the whole of the agreement, and ... greater regard is to be had to the clear intention of the parties than to any particular words which they may have used in the expression of their intent”: Ford v Beech [1848] EngR 10; (1848) 11 QB 852 at 866. Sub-clauses 11.2 and 11.3 spoke of “undrawn profits of the partnership in the financial 30th June 1992”, and “undrawn profits in any prior period”. (My emphasis.) Technically speaking, no partnership or business has undrawn profits in any particular period. It may have undrawn profits as at a particular date. It may have undrawn profits that can be broken down into sums that accumulated during particular periods. I think it follows that clause 11.3, which related to “undrawn profits in any prior period”, must have been intended by the parties to refer to “undrawn profits in respect of the period prior to the year ending June 1992”. Otherwise, that clause would be meaningless. The parties must have intended it to mean something. Otherwise it would not have been included in their agreement. I think it also follows that clauses 11.2 and 11.3 were intended to supersede the original partnership agreement pursuant to clause 11.1, however cumbersome a process that might have been.



It must also follow that clause 11.2, which related to “the undrawn profits of the partnership in the financial year ending 30th June 1992”, must have been intended to relate to the profits, from which there had been no drawings, of the partnership in respect of the financial year ending 30 June 1992.



For these reasons I believe that, if the taxpayer were to sue his former partner for the balance of one half of the partnership profits for the year ended June 1992, the court would find as a fact that the meanings of the relevant clauses of the dissolution agreement were such that the taxpayer was entitled only to $15,000 from the profits for that year.



I therefore have no need to consider whether a court exercising equitable jurisdiction would have made an order for the rectification of the dissolution agreement so as to make it clearly provide to that effect.



It follows that the assessable partnership income of the taxpayer for the year ended June 1992 for the purposes of s.92(1)(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 is only $15,000.



The decision under review will therefore be set aside. The matter will be remitted to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with a direction that the taxpayer’s assessable income from the partnership for the year ended June 1992 amounted to only $15,000.
