Joseph Gutwenger v the Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1994] FCA 1187; (1994) 28 Atr 329 (1994) 94 Atc 4437 (30 June 1994)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

JOSEPH GUTWENGER v. THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF

AUSTRALIA

Nos. VG397 of 1991 and VG398 of 1991

FED No. 419/94

Number of pages - 8

Taxation

[1994] FCA 1187; (1994) 28 ATR 329

(1994) 94 ATC 4437

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

VICTORIA DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

OLNEY J

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - taxpayer liable to income tax on profit from sale of land acquired for resale at a profit - land transferred to taxpayer by his wife for a consideration less than its market value - method of calculating profit - whether value of land at date of acquisition or its cost to the taxpayer should be deducted from the sale proceeds to establish amount of profit.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s 25(1)

Transfer of Land Act 1958 (Vic), s 46(2)

Comptroller of Stamps v Rylaw Pty Ltd 81 ATC 4411

Official Receiver v FCT [1956] HCA 63; (1956) 96 CLR 370

Bernard Elsey Pty Ltd v FCT [1969] HCA 46; (1969) 121 CLR 119

FCT v Williams [1972] HCA 31; (1972) 127 CLR 226

Income Tax in Australia - R.W. Parsons

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 10, 11, 14, and 15 February 1994

30:6:1994

Mr J. de Wijn (instructed by Gledhill Burridge and Cathro) appeared for the applicant.

Mr G. Davies (instructed by the Australian Government Solicitor) appeared for the respondent.

DECISION

OLNEY J On 12 May 1994 I published reasons for my conclusion that the applicant is liable to income tax in respect of the sale of certain land at Frankston in each of the years ending 30 June 1987 and 30 June 1988 on the basis that the profit derived from such sales is income according to ordinary concepts and usages. The parties are in dispute as to the manner in which the profit should be calculated, and have made extensive written submissions in relation thereto.

2. The facts of the case are set out in my earlier reasons. For present purposes it is necessary only to recall the following details:

(i) The land was acquired by Mrs Gutwenger in 1982 and was

transferred to the applicant in 1987.

(ii) Prior to the execution of the transfer, the land had been

subdivided into 27 lots, and 10 of those lots were scheduled

to be offered for sale by auction on 11 April 1987.

(iii) Mrs Gutwenger and the applicant executed a transfer of the

land on 24 March 1987. The consideration in the transfer is

expressed as "a gift between husband and wife".

(iv) The transfer was registered on 9 April 1987, subject to a

registered mortgage in favour of Westpac Banking Corporation

which secured advances made by the bank to Mrs Gutwenger.

3. The following findings made in the course of my reasons are also relevant:

In order to register the transfer it had been necessary for the bank

to give its consent. The consent was duly given on the basis that

the debt to the bank did not increase and that no further funds

could be drawn without a new mortgage being executed.

Notwithstanding the transfer of the land into the applicant's name

the liability to the bank remained in the name of Mrs Gutwenger and

same was subsequently satisfied by proceeds from the sale of the

subdivided lots by the applicant. There is no evidence of any

specific arrangements having been made as between the applicant and

his wife concerning Mrs Gutwenger's liability to the bank. From

the facts established I draw the inference that the applicant

accepted the transfer of the land subject to an understanding that

he would discharge Mrs Gutwenger's liability to the bank from the

sale proceeds.

(Reasons p 22)

Although the transfer of the land from Mrs Gutwenger to the

applicant has been called a gift it was not in fact made without

consideration. Certainly, the transfer document identifies the

consideration for the transfer as "a gift between husband and

wife" and there is no evidence to suggest that any consideration

passed between the parties to the transfer other than the

acceptance by the applicant of the responsibility to discharge Mrs

Gutwenger's liability to Westpac out of the sale proceeds. I have

earlier referred to this matter and have found by inference from

the facts that the transfer was made conditional upon the

applicant accepting that liability. That being the case the

transfer should not properly be regarded as a gift but rather that

the applicant should be treated as having given valuable

consideration for the land to the extent that he undertook to

discharge the debt to the bank.

The applicant was under no obligation to accept the transfer from

his wife, but having done so, and having, as I have found,

undertaken to discharge Mrs Gutwenger's liability to the bank, he

continued with the process of putting the land on the market for

sale. The arrangements for the initial auction were already in

place and the sale was but a couple of weeks off. Indeed, the

particulars accompanying the applicant's 1987 income tax return

show that lot 22 was sold by the applicant on 23 March 1987 "in

anticipation of the gift on 24 March 1987".

(Reasons p 35-6)

Unlike the relatively casual and unbusinesslike process whereby

Mrs Gutwenger originally acquired the land, the applicant's

response upon the acquisition of it in his name was both immediate

and decisive. He acquired the land in a state ready for sale;

arrangements had already been made to auction lots 1 to 10; he

had presold lot 22; the auction was duly held on 11 April 1987

and a number of lots were sold; thereafter he caused the land to

be marketed effectively so that by 30 June 1988 all but one of the

27 lots had been disposed of. All of these facts are indicative

of something more than a mere realisation of an asset.

There can be no other explanation than that the applicant acquired

the land with the intention of selling it at a profit. Although

it was a one-off transaction, it falls comfortably within the

concept of carrying on a business of dealing in land. There is no

question of the applicant having merely realised a capital asset.

In my opinion any profit which accrued to the applicant from the

sale of the land should properly be regarded as income according

to ordinary concepts and usages. The applicant's profit from the

sale of the land is assessable under s 25(1) of the Act.

(Reasons p 37)

4. The first point made in the applicant's written submission is that by virtue of s 46(2) of the Transfer of Land Act 1958 (Vic) upon registration of the transfer subject to the registered mortgage, the applicant became bound as between himself and Mrs Gutwenger, to pay the interest secured by the mortgage and to indemnify her against all liability in respect of the principal sum so secured. It is said that in these circumstances it was unnecessary to draw an inference to the effect referred to in the above extracts concerning the acceptance by the applicant of the obligation to satisfy Mrs Gutwenger's liability to the bank.

5. With respect, I have difficulty in understanding the relevance of this submission in the present context. Section 46(2) of the Transfer of Land Act provides that in every transfer subject to a mortgage there shall be an implied covenant as between the transferor and transferee to the effect stated above. It does not appear to advance the applicant's case to demonstrate that apart from the understanding I have found to have been reached between the applicant and Mrs Gutwenger, the transfer contained an implied covenant to the same effect by operation of s 46(2).

6. The applicant's second point is that the assumption of the liability under the mortgage is not consideration for the transfer so as to make the transfer a sale. The Victorian Supreme Court decision in Comptroller of Stamps v Rylaw Pty Ltd 81 ATC 4411 is cited as authority for this proposition.

7. The facts of Rylaw (so far as presently relevant) can be summarised thus: At the relevant time, Rylaw Pty Ltd was in liquidation. All its debts had been paid or were provided for out of money set aside for the purpose, except for a mortgage debt on a piece of land and a large debt owed to the shareholders of the company. All the shareholders entered into an agreement between themselves that, inter alia, all the loans by them to the company were cancelled and that each would acknowledge to the company and liquidator that the loan debts had been so cancelled and that none of the shareholders was entitled to payment thereof upon the liquidation of the company. A second agreement was entered into between the shareholders and the company, in which the shareholders made the acknowledgment to the company in accordance with the first agreement, and requested the liquidator to transfer to them in specie the mortgaged land. Subsequently, the liquidator executed a transfer by which the company transferred the land, subject to the mortgage, to the shareholders as a distribution in the winding up. The Comptroller assessed stamp duty on the transfer of the land from the company to the shareholders on the basis that it was a conveyance on sale in which the consideration was the amount of the mortgage debt subject to which the land was conveyed.

8. On an appeal against the assessment, the Victorian Taxation Board of Review upheld the Comptroller's assessment. The shareholders appealed to the Supreme Court of Victoria.

9. In the Supreme Court Fullagar J held that the transfer of the land by the company to the shareholders was a distribution to the shareholders on the winding up of the company and was not a sale. The implied covenant under s 46(2) of the Transfer of Land Act 1958 (Vic) that each transferee would indemnify the transferor against all liability under the mortgage over the land, was not in fact the consideration for the transfer, or any part of the consideration, and did not transform a distribution in specie by a liquidator into a sale. At p 4416 of the report his Honour said:

In the present case, it was certainly no part of any bargaining

between the transferor and the transferees that the latter would

indemnify the former against the mortgage debt. In my opinion,

the giving of the statutory indemnity "was not in fact the

consideration for the transfer, or any part of the consideration",

and in my opinion it did not transform a distribution in specie by a

liquidator into a sale, any more than the covenant to indemnify

could transform a gift into a sale; for reasons indicated by the

cases cited, the presence of a mortgage, with the resultant implied

covenant to indemnify, is simply not the kind of quid pro quo which

can make a transfer of the kind under discussion into a transfer on

sale.

10. And after referring to provisions of the Stamps Act 1958 (Vic) his Honour continued:

The provisions apply to measure stamp duty, by measuring the price,

on a conveyance on sale, because on a sale the cost of discharging

the mortgage can be looked at as part of the price paid or to be

paid by the purchaser for the land sold - it is, as it were, part of

what the purchaser gives in order to obtain the transfer, whereas I

think that a true donee, e.g., gives no such covenant in order to

obtain the transfer, and the recipient of the distribution in specie

by the liquidator gives no such covenant in order to obtain the land

or in any sense as the price for the land - the shareholder has an

equitable or statutory right to a transfer immediately before the

distribution in specie by the liquidator.

11. Fullagar J then examined the state of the company's affairs and said:

It seems to be to be quite clear therefore that, on both 4 May 1979

and 5 May 1979, all creditors (other than the mortgagee of the land

and the shareholders of the company) had either been paid out or

else had adequate provision made for them by the cash sums referred

to. It is also clear that, as early as 4 May 1979, the

shareholders had requested the liquidator to transfer to them in

specie the land in question: see para 1 of the Agreement being

document C.2 which is, of course, not the critical agreement in

these proceedings and which no one contends attracts duty of itself.

Therefore, immediately before the execution of the critical transfer

dated 5 May 1979, the liquidator was bound to transfer the land to

the shareholders, and not by virtue of anything that could be called

a sale or a contract for sale but by virtue of the fact that the

liquidation of the company was so far as relevant complete

except for the distribution of the surplus property (the land) to

the shareholders, in specie as requested. I think the better view

is that the liquidator held the land on trust to convey it to the

shareholders, but whether it was a trust or a mere statutory duty

appears to me not to matter for the purposes of the present case.

What matters in my opinion is that the liquidator was strictly bound

and legally compellable to transfer the land in specie to the

shareholders as part of his duty to distribute the assets of the

company in the winding-up. In those circumstances it seems to me

impossible to say that the implication of covenants implied by the

Transfer of Land Act could have the effect of turning a distribution

in the winding-up into a "conveyance on sale".

12. Taken in its full context there is nothing in the judgment in Rylaw to support the general proposition asserted by the applicant to the effect that the assumption of liability under an existing mortgage of land by a transference does not constitute consideration for the transfer so as to make the transfer a sale. Indeed, the passages quoted above from Fullagar J's reasons suggest that in the situation which arises in this case, the contrary is so.

13. In my opinion the undertaking of the applicant to satisfy Mrs Gutwenger's liability under the mortgage should be "looked at as ... the price paid or to be paid by the purchaser for the land sold", it being "what the purchaser gives in order to obtain the transfer".

14. The balance of the applicant's submission is devoted to a consideration of authorities relevant to cases in which a capital asset previously acquired either by way of purchase or gift or by operation of law has been ventured into a profit making undertaking. In my opinion none of the three authorities cited are relevant in the present context.

15. In Official Receiver in Bankruptcy v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1956] HCA 63; (1956) 96 CLR 370 (Fox's case) the trustee of a bankrupt deceased estate, with the authority of the creditors, completed the development and sub-division of land which had been acquired by the deceased and partially developed by him during his lifetime. The Commissioner assessed the trustee to tax on the basis that the gross proceeds from the sale of the allotments were assessable income and deductions were made therefrom of an amount calculated by an apportionment of the deceased's expenditure over the whole area and for expenses incurred by the trustee to determine the taxable income. The Full Court of the High Court held that the trustee was carrying out a profit making scheme and that the profit, if any, consisted of the excess of the price recovered over the value of the assets which came into his hands.

16. Bernard Elsey Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1969] HCA 46; (1969) 121 CLR 119 was decided by a single judge of the High Court. A company, intending to establish a motel adjacent to a service station and having purchased land sufficient for that purpose, also acquired adjacent land and built a motel on portion of the latter. The land first acquired was subsequently sold to an oil company. Fourteen months after acquiring the land on which the motel was built, the company conceived a scheme to build four shops on the remaining portion of that land, which it did, and later sold the shops separately, after two of them had been let for some time. Windeyer J held that the building of the shops and the ultimate sale thereof after letting amounted to more than the realisation of a capital asset, and that the profit gained arose from a profit-making undertaking or scheme within the meaning of (the then) s 26(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, and thus formed part of the taxpayer's assessable income. His Honour further held that the profit from the sale of the shops should be ascertained by deducting from the amount realised on sale all outgoings incurred in the carrying out of the profit-making undertaking which outgoings included the value of the land which the taxpayer had embarked in the carrying out of same.

17. The third authority cited by the applicant is Commissioner of Taxation v Williams [1972] HCA 31; (1972) 127 CLR 226 in which the facts were these: In 1959 the husband of the taxpayer and two others purchased land near Perth as tenants in common in equal shares. In 1962 the husband gave his interest to the taxpayer. In 1969 the land was cleared, developed and subdivided. The greater part was sold. The Commissioner assessed the taxpayer on the basis that the profits from the sale of the land should be included in her assessable income. The Full Court of the High Court held (by a majority) that neither s 25(1) nor the second limb of (the then) s 26(a) applied as the taxpayer had done no more than realise a capital asset in an enterprising way.

18. The applicant's case is argued on the basis that he acquired by way of gift from Mrs Gutwenger on 24 March 1987 an asset, namely her net equity in the land, and that, consistent with the authorities, the profit from the sale of the land should be ascertained by deducting from the net sale proceeds, the value of the land at the date when it was ventured into the project, namely 24 March 1987. If the premise on which the applicant's case is based, namely that he acquired an asset by way of gift on 24 March 1987 is valid, then there may be some merit in the argument advanced on the applicant's behalf. As to that I express no opinion. But the applicant's premise misstates the finding of fact made in these proceedings. My finding as stated in the reasons published on 12 May 1994 is that the applicant acquired the subject land for valuable consideration with the intent that he thereupon resell the land at a profit. None of the cases referred to above, nor indeed any other authority cited in argument addresses the particular issue raised in the facts of this case. And it must necessarily follow that if the fundamental premise of the applicant's argument cannot be sustained the whole argument is without foundation and the conclusion advanced cannot be valid.

19. The applicant's submission quotes at some length from Income Tax in Australia by Professor Parsons. The portion of the text quoted from is that dealing with the question of "The identification of cost where property is held before a process of income derivation is entered on, or property is acquired wholly or partly by way of gift, or where an excessive payment is made for property". The applicant's submission quotes the following passages from pp 718 - 719:

"(12.84) Property may be acquired wholly or partly by way of gift,

and, in being acquired, become subject to a process that may yield

a profit that is income. Where the element of gift involves a

derivation of income by the receiver, for example as a reward for

services, the principle considered in (12.74) above, will require

that the value of the element of gift be treated as a cost of the

property. A person may sell land to a taxpayer at less than its

market value in order to reward him for some service, and the

taxpayer may acquire with a purpose that attracts the first limb of

s.25A(1). The element of gift will be an income item derived at

the time of acquisition. The element of gift must be treated as a

cost of the land in computing a profit on sale of the land by the

taxpayer in order that there should not be two taxes on the same

gain.

(12.85) There is another reason why the element of gift must be

treated as a cost. The gift may be a pure gift which is not

income. That gift may, in the very transaction in which it is

acquired, be committed by the donee to a process of income

derivation. Treating the value of the gift as a cost is necessary

to prevent it being taxed in the taxing of the proceeds later

arising in the income derivation ....

(12.86) The second reason why the element of gift must be treated as

a cost is an expression of the principle in Whitfords Beach Pty Ltd

[1982] HCA 8; (1982) 150 CLR 355. The element of gift is treated as a cost in

treating the value of the property at the time of receipt as the

cost of the property acquired ..."

20. In the submission, the applicant's comments upon the foregoing are as follows:

It is significant to note that Professor Parsons speaks of the

element of gift and clearly has in mind the type of case where

property may be sold for less than its market value. He does not

limit the principle to cases where there is no consideration at

all. No doubt as Professor Parsons points out the position may be

different where as a result of an "honest bargain" between a vendor

and a purchaser the purchaser is able to buy an asset for an amount

less than the prevailing market value. That however, is not the

situation presently before the Court. The "transaction" between

the applicant and Mrs Gutwenger cannot be compared with an arms

length sale where the purchaser simply gets a good deal]

21. With respect, it appears to me that the applicant's argument is based on a false premise, namely, that the applicant acquired the land by way of gift. Such a premise is contrary to the specific finding to which reference has been made. Whatever the motives of the applicant and Mrs Gutwenger it cannot be said that they did not enter into an "honest bargain". Further, the applicant's argument seems to suggest that if the transaction was something less than an "honest bargain" the tax consequences would be more favourable to the applicant. In my opinion there is nothing in the extract from Professor Parsons' work or indeed in any of the authorities to which reference has been made to support the proposition, which is implicit in the applicant's argument, that when a purchaser acquires property at a price less than the market value with the intention of dealing with the property in a manner which will render the purchaser liable to tax on any profit derived, the market value of the property, rather than the actual price paid, is to be treated as the cost of acquisition of the property for the purpose of ascertaining the profit.

CONCLUSION

22. The applicant acquired the land previously owned by Mrs Gutwenger at a cost equivalent to Mrs Gutwenger's liability to Westpac under the mortgage registered against the title to the land.

23. The land was acquired by the applicant for valuable consideration with the intention that it be sold at a profit.

24. The land was sold at a profit. For the purpose of calculating the profit derived by the applicant the cost of acquisition rather than the value of the land at the date of acquisition should be allowed as a deduction against the net sale proceeds.

25. The applicant has not demonstrated that the Commissioner's income tax assessments for the years 1987 and 1988 are erroneous. The applications will be dismissed.
