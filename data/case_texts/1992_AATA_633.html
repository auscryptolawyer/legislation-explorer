ST91/3, ST91/4 and Commissioner of Taxation [1992] AATA 633; (1992) 23 ATR 1195; 92 ATC 235 (12 May 1992)

Last Updated: 21 May 2009



TAXATION - level of taxable income - proceeds from sale of drugs - funds statement - alleged loan from friend - extent of personal and living expenses - additional tax - culpability factor - failure to call key witness



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss.190(b), 223, 237

Income Tax Ruling No. 2517



Jones v Dunkel [1959] HCA 8; (1959) 101 CLR 298

Trautwein v F.C. of T. [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63



AAT Decision No 7951

Re:



Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



Respondent



No: ST91/3, ST91/4



Tribunal: Mr D.J. Trowse (Member)



Place: Adelaide



Date: 12 May 1992



Decision: The Tribunal affirms the objection decisions under review.



(sgd)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

Taxation Appeals Division )

Applicant

REASONS FOR DECISION



12 May 1992 Mr D.J. Trowse (Member)



1. These references relate to the 1988 and 1989 years of income and by consent were heard together. It seems that the Commissioner had reason to believe that the applicant had understated his taxable income and on that basis had prepared Funds Statements for the years in question. Initially, those calculations revealed discrepancies of $47,755 and $10,932 respectively for which amended assessments, including additional tax levied pursuant to section 223 of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 ('the Act'), were raised. Objections against those assessments were considered by the Commissioner and partial allowances, to the extent of $21,329 and $6,428 respectively, resulted. The remaining issues requiring this Tribunal's determination are:



. whether the applicant had received a cash advance of $6,000 from a Mr S



. the quantum of monies applied towards the applicant's cost of living and personal expenditure during both years; and



. the appropriateness of the penalties imposed for the omission, if any, of assessable income.



The only concession made by the applicant was that he had omitted from his 1988 return savings bank interest of $1433.



2. At the hearing the applicant was represented by his current accountant, Mr N. Storer, and the respondent by Mr S. Cole, of counsel. Oral evidence was given by the applicant, Detective Senior Constable Kranz of the South Australian Police Force, and Mr Lee, a taxation auditor with the Australian Taxation Office.



3. The applicant, aged 37 years, is a process worker with a glass manufacturer. The only income disclosed in his 1988 and 1989 returns was his salary from that source, the gross amounts being $18,646 and $20,818 respectively. He is divorced from his former spouse and for portion of the two years under review lived in a de facto relationship with a Ms F. For the period July 1987 to August 1988 he resided in rental accommodation and for the remainder, i.e. to the 30 June 1989, in a home purchased by him for a consideration of $59,950. Documentary evidence tendered at the hearing indicated that the applicant had been found guilty of having unlawful possession of personal property, namely two video cassette recorders, one radio cassette recorder and cash to the amount of $10,920, and that not only was he sentenced to eleven months imprisonment, which was suspended upon him entering into a bond of $200 to be of good behaviour for a period of two years, but in addition the cash and recorders were confiscated by the Crown. Furthermore, it is clear that, at about the same time, the applicant was found guilty of having drugs in his possession and was released upon entering into a bond of $500 and upon the condition that he be of good behaviour for a period of two years. It was also established to the satisfaction of the Tribunal that the applicant has a lengthy criminal record including convictions for assault, larceny, receiving and the giving of a false name and address.



4. The Commissioner takes the view that the applicant was engaged in the activity of trading in illegal drugs and that the profits so made are represented by the understatements of income as revealed in his calculations. The applicant counters that proposition by maintaining that the surpluses arrived at by the Commissioner are incorrect inasmuch that the levels of living expenses so applied are excessive and that a loan of $6,000 from Mr S had not been brought to account. The Tribunal agrees with the statement made by Mr Storer that the lack of financial records makes the satisfaction of the onus stipulated in sub-section 190(b) of the Act more difficult and yet that deficiency is entirely of the applicant's making. In giving his evidence, the applicant continually referred to his right of choice as to the methods used in entering into financial transactions. That is not disputed and yet the adoption of a system lacking in logic and commonsense will invariably create problems in the discharge of the onus, particularly where no records have been maintained. Even more detrimental to the applicant's case was his demeanor in the witness box. Not only was he unco-operative and abrasive but his total lack of mental recall on some of the more salient matters and his attempts to change his evidence in mid stream were destructive of his credibility. The Tribunal views the applicant as an unsatisfactory witness and accordingly is not prepared to accept his evidence unless corroborated by some other independent source.



5. The Tribunal now turns to consider the alleged loan of $6,000 from Mr S and commences with a recital of the applicant's version of the relevant events. In April 1988 he was contemplating the purchase of a residence. Notwithstanding that no property had then been selected the applicant approached Mr S, a 'mate' of two year's standing and a beneficiary of a vehicular accident compensation claim, with a request that he lend him a sum of $6,000 on the understanding that that amount was to be used for that purpose and was to be repaid by the applicant when he sold his Harley Davidson motor cycle. Mr S assented to the request and on 12 April 1988 he withdrew $6,000 in cash from his account with a building society and on that same day handed cash, to the value of $6,000 and consisting of banknotes in the denominations of $20s, $50s and $100s, to the applicant. There is no dispute that the withdrawal of $6,000 from the building society was in notes of $100 denomination and the Tribunal is left to ponder on how or why the transmogrification of currency was affected. A similar position arises as to an amount of $6,000 paid into Mr S's account on the same day as the withdrawal. The applicant appeared to possess some knowledge as to the financial affairs of Mr S and stated that the deposit represented a transfer from a 'trust account' maintained by Mr S with the same building society. In the initial stages of the investigation it seems that the Commissioner inferred that this deposit was in fact funded by the applicant and thus concluded that no genuine loan existed. Such a possibility was not pursued at the hearing and thus the source of the $6,000 credit remains a mystery. The Tribunal acknowledges the existence of a letter from Mr S which is supportive of the loan and yet, without more, limited weight is ascribed to that correspondence. The Tribunal on several occasions suggested the calling of Mr S but to no avail. On the authority of Jones v Dunkel [1959] HCA 8; (1959) 101 CLR 298 the Tribunal concludes that the failure to call Mr S as a witness leads to an inference that his evidence would not have helped the applicant's case.



6. The narrative as told by the applicant continues. The cash reserves of the applicant were further increased by proceeds received from the sale of a motor vehicle ($4,000) and withdrawals from bank ($920). That cash, now totalling $10,920, was kept in a plastic nappy bag which was hidden for safekeeping in the spare room of the house then being rented by the applicant. When asked why he decided to retain the cash as opposed to paying it to the credit of his existing bank account, he replied -' that's just normal family practice and always has been'. The disadvantage of such a practice was highlighted to the applicant when on 20 April 1988 members of the South Australian Police Force visited the home being occupied by the applicant and Ms F and took possession of the $10,920, two video cassette recorders, a radio cassette recorder and a small quantity of a drug called speed. The applicant contended that the drugs belonged to a visiting friend and yet accepted that as the occupier of the premises he was the person to be charged. After some confusion and claims of not understanding questions asked of him during cross-examination, the applicant's final evidence was that he endeavoured to explain to the Police the sources of the $10,920 but that they were not interested.



7. Subsequent to the above intervention, the applicant was charged and found guilty of the unlawful possession of the $10,920 together with the above-mentioned electrical items. The applicant did not give evidence before that Court nor was any mention made of the alleged source of the cash. It seems that the decision not to give evidence was upon legal advice and that such instructions were based upon the opinion that the applicant would not be in a position to discharge the onus of proving that he had come by the property honestly. If the explanations provided to this Tribunal as to the source of the cash are to have any standing then the earlier decision not to present evidence as to those circumstances can only be described as extraordinary. When questioned about the electrical items which formed part of the charge, the applicant's reply was - ' Nothing to say '. From that it may be concluded that the satisfaction of the honesty test for those articles could well have presented problems for the applicant.



8. The Tribunal found Detective Senior Constable Kranz to be an impressive witness. He gave his evidence with clarity and precision and the Tribunal unhesitatingly accepts his account of the events as they occurred on 20 April 1988. The visit to the applicant's residence resulted from a tip off which suggested that drugs were being used and sold from those premises. The informant further indicated that electrical items were being traded in exchange for drugs. The cash totalling $10,920 was located in the nappy bag together with a notebook which contained details of various names, telephone numbers and sums of money. The notes were in denominations of mostly $20s and $50s and perhaps some $100s. The electrical items confiscated by the Police were selected on the basis of the number of such items in the house and the way in which they were stored. By way of example, some of the equipment in question was found in a garbage bag located in the laundry. Detective Senior Constable Kranz was firm in his view that at no stage did the applicant offer any explanation as to how either the cash or those goods came into his possession.



9. Once the Detective Senior's evidence is accepted, the conclusion that the applicant was dealing in drugs is compelling. The only question then remaining is whether in those circumstances the Tribunal should accept the applicant's submission that $6,000 of the sum of $10,920 represented a loan from Mr S. On the balance of probabilities, that submission is rejected, and in reaching that conclusion the Tribunal has given due recognition to the following factors -



. as at 12 April 1988 there existed no more than an expectation that the applicant would purchase a home at some time in the future and thus the need to borrow at that stage had not arisen;



. on the same question of need it is noted that the credit balance of the applicant's savings account with the State Bank in April 1988 was $20,963;



. the absence of any information as to how or why the alleged loan figure of $6,000 had been arrived at;



. the lack of any explanation as to the currency differences between monies withdrawn from Mr S's account with the building society and those handed to the applicant;



. the retention of those monies in the manner described;



. the non-disclosure of the alleged loan to either the Police or the Court.



For those reasons the Tribunal is not satisfied that the applicant borrowed an amount of $6,000 from Mr S and accepts as correct the Commissioner's decision not to include such a liability in the preparation of the Funds Statement compiled for the 1988 year of income.



10. The question of the amounts expended on personal living expenses has caused the Tribunal some frustration. Constant changes in method and calculations became common-place and demonstrated in the Tribunal's view a lack of preparedness. As new matters evolved in the course of the hearing so the calculations changed. At the end of the day, the calculations provided on behalf of the applicant are of little use.



11. The extent of those variations is revealed in the following summary -

amounts nominated in objections lodged on behalf of the applicant 5,200 5,200



revised amounts accepted by Commissioner

in partial allowance of objections 15,000 15,000



first submission at hearing 10,504 11,180



final submission at hearing - figures accumulated for both years [21,815]



In fairness to the applicant's representative, it is mentioned that the purpose of the aggregated figure resulting from the final calculation was to verify the accuracy of amounts contained in the first submission. The Tribunal now turns to consider these submissions in some detail and in so doing is mindful of the requirement that the applicant bears the onus of proof and must not only establish that the Commissioner's assessments are wrong, but must go further and positively show what the correct assessments should be - see the case of Trautwein v F.C. of T. [1936] HCA 77; (1926) 56 CLR 63.



12. The figures relied upon in the first submission are those contained in two loan applications lodged with the State Bank, one on 30 May 1988 and the other in August 1988. The purpose of the exercise was to demonstrate a capacity to service loans to be made by the bank and it seems that the forms were completed by an officer of the bank in the presence of the applicant. 13. The Commissioner's representative contended that the statements were inaccurate and were the subject of much estimation. In particular he referred to the amounts allowed for Bankcard and also to the fact that loan repayments to a work related credit union had not been disclosed. Information tendered demonstrates that the payments to Bankcard totalled $3,060 for the 1988 year and $1880 for the 1989 year, i.e. amounts significantly in excess of those declared in the Statement of Position. Furthermore it does appear as if loan repayments to the credit union have been omitted and to that extent the results recorded are inaccurate. It seems that the applicant had borrowed funds from the credit union prior to the period under review and in that regard had organised fortnightly deductions of $150 from his salary, $82 of which was offset against his debt with the balance of $68 being credited to his savings account with that same organisation. While it appears that monies were transferred ex the savings account to the loan account, a closer examination of the statement covering the savings account reveals cash withdrawals totalling $2,470. How those monies were expended was not canvassed before the Tribunal.



14. Mr Storer expressed the view that the net weekly salary figures of $274 and $275 as outlined in the Statements of Position were net after allowing for the reduction of $75 and on that basis no such omissions had occurred. It is clear that a reduction of $75 from the applicant's net salary, i.e. gross less tax instalments, produces results appreciably lower than $274 and $275 and for that reason the submission is rejected. The inclusion of $12 for rates and taxes in 1988 is also an error. The applicant did not at that time own real estate nor was he required to pay rates on the property then being leased. Not only are the statements wrong in content but they are based on much estimation. On that basis the Tribunal rejects any suggestion that the information contained therein accurately reflects the levels of personal living expenses for the 1988 and 1989 years.



15. The final method considered by Mr Storer involved a dissection of all transactions occurring through the applicant's cheque account with the State Bank. All cash withdrawals, bankcard payments and cheques relating to living expenses were added together and from that total was deducted a sum of $7,588 which was said to represent the re-banking of excess drawings. The net result was a figure of $21,814 and it seems that Mr Storer took some comfort from the fact that that figure approximated the sum total of the two figures arrived at in terms of the previous method. The applicant gave evidence that he regularly made withdrawals in excess of his requirements and that from time to time he would re-deposit those surpluses into the cheque account. It was argued that as the original withdrawal had been included in the relevant calculations then the re-banking of portion of those funds should be treated as an offset. The Tribunal has no difficulty with the concept, the problem is whether such evidence is to be believed. The applicant's evidence on this unusual practice is hardly convincing:



' Q. Why did you keep drawing money to build up the amount of cash you had on hand when you weren't, on your version, doing anything with it?



A. Because I just wanted it on hand.



Q. For what purpose?



A. Just to have on hand. '



Without more, the Tribunal has no alternative but to reject such an implausible explanation. In any event, the cash withdrawals from the credit union account totalling $2,470 have been ignored in these calculations and likewise withdrawals from the applicant's savings bank account with the State Bank totalling $22,000 have also been overlooked. The evidence before the Tribunal does not support a conclusion that the Commissioner has erred in the bringing to account of living expenses at $15,000 in each of the years under review.



16. The Tribunal takes the opportunity to express a view as to the final method adopted on behalf of the applicant and in particular to all taxpayers who form part of the cash economy. Cash received and then expended in that form will not be reflected in bank accounts. It is therefore an exercise in futility to concentrate all one's effort in analysing and dissecting transactions appearing on bank statements.



17. Finally, the Tribunal turns to consider whether the additional tax levied pursuant to section 223 of the Act should be reduced beyond the levels previously decided upon by the Commissioner. Copies of the relevant calculations were made available and it is noted that the 'per annum' component was based on a percentage of 14.026% whereas a figure of 45% was used in determining the 'culpability' component. While the Commissioner relied upon information contained in Taxation Ruling No.IT2517, no submissions were forthcoming from the applicant.



18. It is well established that the omission of assessable income comes within the scope of sub-section 223(1) and thus the only matter requiring the Tribunal's attention is the extent of the remissions afforded by the Commissioner in terms of sub-section 227(3).



19. The Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant omitted assessable income from his 1988 and 1989 returns and that those omissions represented deliberate evasions. Furthermore, the Tribunal is unable to recognise any mitigating circumstances for those omissions and is therefore of the opinion that no additional remissions are warranted.



20. For the reasons enunciated above the Tribunal affirms the objection decisions.



This is to certify that this and the preceding ten (10) pages are a true copy of the decision and reasons for decision herein of Mr D.J. Trowse (Member)



Dated 11/5/92 (sgd) J. Deheleanu (Secretary)
