TAXATION - transportation business - entitlement to deduction for depreciation in respect of costs incurred on capital modification and additions to vehicles under lease and subsequently acquired - residual value of leased item - units of property - meaning of “cost” - respondent’s exercise of discretion.

REASONS FOR DECISION



9 August 1995 Associate Professor R D Fayle (Senior Member)



  1. The applicant in these proceedings is a proprietary limited company carrying on a transportation business. The two applications relate to the years of income ended 30 June 1988 and 1989 respectively and, in essence, concern the same matter - whether the applicant is entitled to a deduction for depreciation in respect of costs incurred previous to the years of income on capital modifications and additions to vehicles then under lease but which, subsequently, were acquired at the end of the lease term, during the year of income ended 30 June 1988. There is no dispute that the applicant is entitled to depreciation on the amounts paid to acquire the units of property in question.

  2. The applications, pursuant to s29 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, relate to objection decisions made by the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [“the respondent”] on 9 December 1994 which have been referred to this Tribunal for review pursuant to s14ZZ(a)(i) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953, [“the TAA”].

  3. At the hearing the applicant was represented by two senior employees of the parent company of the applicant and the respondent was represented by Mr P Martino of counsel, instructed by Mr K Morgan of the Australian Government Solicitor’s Office.

  4. As the applicant had changed its stance in relation to the original objection decision and subsequently confined this application to the issue mentioned above, leave was sought to amend the grounds of objection so far as that was considered necessary to place that issue formally before the Tribunal. The respondent did not oppose this motion. On the authority of the Full Federal Court decision in Lighthouse Philatelics Pty Ltd v FCT 91 ATC 4142, leave was granted to extend the grounds, as necessary, to include a claim that the applicant is entitled to a deduction for each year of income, under s54 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, [“the Act”] in respect of depreciation of the costs incurred by the applicant to modify and improve the leased vehicles, to commence from when those vehicles were acquired by the applicant at the expiration of the respective lease term. In the light of these reasons the Tribunal takes those grounds to extend to an assertion that the respondent ought to have exercised his discretion under subsection 60(2) of the Act accordingly.

  5. The case proceeded on the basis of agreed facts (Ex.A2), some additional exhibits and expert evidence for the respondent by Mr J Duivenvoorde, a Chartered Accountant.

Facts



  1. For some years prior to and during the years in question, the applicant carried on a business of road haulage contractor.

  2. On 30 September 1982 the applicant entered into sale and lease-back agreements relating to heavy haulage vehicles which it then operated or was about to acquire. The purchaser/lessor was Bain Leasing (ACT) Pty Limited (“the lessor”). The relevant agreements were tendered in evidence (Ex.A1). That lease covered five trucks and trailers delivered between 16 September 1982 and 1 February 1983, the leases to commence at those dates and end on 5 October 1987.

  3. The lease provided that each leased item would have a residual value of 15% of its cost, which in effect, meant that should the lessor not realise that amount at the expiration of the lease then the applicant (“the lessee”) would be required to pay over the shortfall. On the other hand, should the lessor realise more than the residual value then the excess after meeting all costs associated with the realisation, would be paid over to the lessee. As it happened the lessee acquired the five vehicles in question, all of which had been previously modified at the lessee’s expense, at the expiration of the lease (presumably on 5 October 1987, not 1 October 1987 as stated in the agreed facts - c.f. Perpetual Trustee Co (Canberra) Ltd v Commr for ACT Revenue [1994] FCA 1150; (1994) 28 ATR 307).

  4. Modifications to the leased vehicles [“units of property”] to the rear axle assembly of the vehicles to increase the load capacity and the installation of air conditioning units were carried out at the lessee’s expense. Clause 4.3(a) of the lease (Ex.A1) states:

“... Any replacement, alteration or addition of a major or minor nature shall be made at the cost of the Lessee and shall be deemed to be parts of the whole and be the property of the Lessor and be subject to all the terms and conditions of this Lease.”



  1. The units of property in question, their respective capital costs of modifications and their acquisition cost comprised:

  1. All the units of property were owned by the applicant from 5 October 1987 and used by the applicant in its business during the whole of the years of income in question.

  2. The respondent has exercised his discretion pursuant to s60(2) of the Act and has not applied s60 to the units of property. In so doing the applicant has been allowed a greater deduction for depreciation than that which would have been allowed to the lessor had the units of property been retained instead. However, as will be explained, this is a limited concession on the part of the respondent.

  3. A letter from the lessor (Ex.A3) supports the applicant’s contention, which was not disputed, that depreciation had not been allowed on the cost of the modifications, during the lease term, either to the applicant who bore those costs or the lessor to whose equipment those capital improvements were made and to whom they belonged in terms of clause 4.3(a) of the lease agreement (supra).

Legislation



  1. The relevant provisions of the Act are:

S54(1) Depreciation during the year of income of any property, being plant or articles owned by a taxpayer and used by him during that year for the purposes of producing assessable income, and of any property being plant or articles owned by the taxpayer which has been installed ready for use for that purpose and is, during that year held in reserve by him shall, subject to this Act, be an allowable deduction.



S56(1) Subject to this section, depreciation allowable under this Act in respect of a unit of property in relation to a year of income is -

(a) one and one-half times the percentage fixed by or under section 55, or under the previous Act of the depreciated value of that unit at the beginning of the year of income; or

(b) at the option of the taxpayer, to be exercised in accordance with section 56A, the percentage fixed by or under section 55, or under a previous Act, of the cost of that unit.



S60(1) Where, either before or after the commencement of this Act, a person has acquired any property in respect of which depreciation has been allowed or is allowable under this or the previous Act, he shall not be entitled to any greater deduction for depreciation than that which would have been allowed to the person from whom the property was acquired if that person has retained it:



Provided that, where under section 59 an amount is included in the assessable income of the person selling the property, the person acquiring the property shall be allowed depreciation calculated on the sum of that amount and the depreciated value of the property under this Act immediately prior to the time of the sale.

...



S60(2) This section does not apply where the Commissioner is of the opinion that the circumstances are such that depreciation in respect of property should be calculated without regard to this section.



S62(1) In this Division, “depreciated value” of any unit of property at any time means the cost of the unit to the person who owns or owned the property at that time less the total amount of depreciation (if any) allowed or allowable in respect of that unit in assessments of the income of that person, for any period prior to that time, under this Act or any previous law of the Commonwealth.



S62(2) For the purposes of subsection (1), in any case in which section 60, or the corresponding provision of a previous Act, applied or applies in relation to any unit of property, the person who acquired or acquires the unit shall be deemed to have acquired or to acquire it at a cost equal to the depreciated value of the unit immediately prior to the time of the acquisition, or, if the case is one in which the proviso to subsection (1) of that section applied or applies, the sum of that depreciated value and the amount required to be added to that depreciated value for the purposes of that proviso.



Application of the law



  1. It was conceded by the applicant that as it did not own the leased units of property at the relevant time it incurred the cost of modifications, which it admitted were of a capital nature, then no deduction for depreciation during the lease term was permitted under s54(1) of the Act. Therefore, there has been no deduction, for depreciation or otherwise to the applicant or the lessor, for those capital costs during the lease term.

  2. The Act does not define what is “cost” for the purpose of the depreciation provisions, and in particular, for s56(1)(b) which was relied upon by the applicant (T 8 & 9) in claiming depreciation deductions.

  3. Mr Duivenvoorde’s evidence was that for accounting purposes and in keeping with the then exposed Australian Accounting Standard AAS 17, the leases ought to have been treated as “finance leases” which results in allowing the lessee to depreciate or amortise a capitalised value of the property under lease rather than expense the lease rental payments. This accounting treatment required the lessee to create an asset and liability equivalent to the present value of the anticipated lease payments and to depreciate that asset over the life of the lease or asset, whilst reducing the liability by the capital component of the lease rents. The interest component of the lease rents is expensed each year on an actuarial basis. Therefore, under the “finance lease” accounting treatment, the total expense charged to the operating profit account for each year of the lease’s duration would be the depreciation or amortisation of the asset, based on its expected useful life, and the expensed interest component, rather than the lease rents.

  4. The witness’s evidence was confined to what, in his view, would have been the appropriate accounting treatment for the applicant to have adopted in relation to its obligations under the lease. AAS 17 would require the applicant to have capitalised the cost of modifications as and when they were incurred and amortised that amount as well as the present value of the anticipated lease rents over the estimated life of the units of property. When asked, under cross-examination, to offer an opinion as to what would have been the cost of the units of property to the applicant, the witness said that it was a combination of the residual value paid at the end of the lease term and the cost of the modifications previously incurred. His written submission (Ex.R1) makes it clear that, in his opinion, the net cost of the unit of property at that time would be a combination of the amortised value of the modifications at that time and the residual value paid to the lessor.

  5. There is precedent which indicates that the cost of a unit of depreciable property for the purposes of the depreciation provisions of Division III of Part III of the Act includes capital costs incurred after acquisition which directly improve the particular item of plant or article and any costs incurred in the installation of the plant or article; refer Kitto J in B P Refinery (Kwinana) Limited v FCT (1960) 12 ATD 204; and Case 21, 8 CTBR (NS) 100 at paragraph 7, page 102. However, there is no direct precedent, or at least none was brought to the attention of the Tribunal, where costs of improving someone else’s depreciable property which is used by the taxpayer who bore the cost, can be included as part of the cost for the purposes of s56 of the Act.

  6. Subsection 60(1) of the Act ensures, in effect, that no greater allowance for depreciation will be made than the original cost and subsequent costs of improvements to the owner of the item of plant or article, regardless of how often it changes ownership; Ferling v FCT (1966) 14 ATD 372. This subsection is subject to the proviso that where the vendor is assessed under s59 on any balancing charge arising upon sale[1] then the purchaser is allowed depreciation on the balancing charge amount as well as the vendor’s depreciated value.

  7. However, subsection 60(2) gives the Commissioner of Taxation (i.e. the respondent) a discretion to allow the purchaser depreciation on some basis other than that stipulated in subsection (1). A bare reading of that discretion suggests that it must be intended to allow the purchaser a greater amount of depreciation than would otherwise be the taxpayer’s right under subsection (1) since that would be the minimum statutory amount on which depreciation is calculated. The respondent has exercised his discretion in a limited way under this provision to allow the applicant depreciation on both the amount paid to the lessor for the units of property (i.e. the residual value) and the notionally depreciated value of the cost of modifications from the time those costs were incurred by the applicant to the date the applicant acquired the units of property.

  8. The T documents offer reasons as to why the respondent exercised his discretion only in this limited way in favour of the applicant. The T documents, at T2, page 6, in this respect state: “However, in accordance with sub-section 60(2) of the Act, the Commissioner is of the view that the circumstances are such that depreciation in respect of the motor vehicles should be calculated without regard to sub-section 60(1).” That view seems to be based on conclusions reached by the respondent’s Appeals and Review Officer at T29, p135, where, in this respect he states:

25. It is considered that the depreciated value of the modifications at the time of acquisition should be its notional depreciated value as if depreciation on the modifications had been allowed during the term of the lease. Support for this view can be found in the decision of McTiernan J in F C of T v Anderson (1956) 11 ATD 115 at p 118:



‘The depreciated value is arrived at by writing down cost price by the total amount of depreciation ‘allowed or allowable’ etc (s62). These words are rigid: nothing less can be written off cost price to fix depreciated value.’



26. This method is consistent with Taxation Laws Amendment (Foreign Tax Credits) Act 1986, s.34. That provision deems the cost of depreciated property used in a (sic) overseas business prior to 1 July 1987 to be the amount that would have been the depreciated value under section 62 if the property had been used solely for the purpose of producing assessable income since its acquisition.”



  1. Two observations can be made about those reasons. Firstly, in Anderson’s case his Honour, in referring to “depreciation allowed or allowable” must have been referring to depreciation properly allowed or potentially allowable under the Act; see s62(1) and (1967) 17 TBRD Case S63. In the present case there never was, during the lease term, any proper entitlement to depreciate the modifications; (see respondent’s reasons, with which the Tribunal agrees, that the modifications were not themselves plant or articles capable of depreciation in their own right and that they were not owned by the lessee applicant, hence, during the lease term, they did not qualify to be depreciated under s54 by either the applicant or the lessor: T29, p132-133).

  2. Secondly, the reference to the s34, Taxation Laws Amendment (Foreign Tax Credits) Act 1986 amendment simply ensured that no depreciation could be claimed by a resident taxpayer on plant or articles used in the past to generate exempt income from foreign sources, presumably then exempt under paragraph 23(q) which provision was omitted contemporaneously by Act No. 51 of 1986. Those amendments made foreign income derived by residents after 1 July 1987, assessable income, subject to foreign tax credits. Not to have made the amendment contained in s34 may have resulted in the resident taxpayer qualifying for depreciation on the basis of the original cost of the plant used to derive exempt income in the past. This amendment was clearly intended to ensure no “double dipping” in that respect, that is, to prevent a deduction for depreciation for a period of ownership of plant whilst it was used to produce exempt income. Insofar as the reference is relevant to justify the respondent’s decision not to exercise favourably the discretion under s60(2), with respect, it is flawed for the following reason. In the present case, there can be no “double dipping”, but indeed and on the contrary, to deny the applicant depreciation, from the date of acquisition of the plant, based on the full cost of the modification made previously (and the cost of acquisition of the plant), is to deny a deduction, to that extent, permanently when clearly, that capital modification has been used at all material times to derive assessable income.

  3. In terms of s60(2) it was open to the respondent to allow the applicant, once it became the owner, to depreciate the cost of modifications to each of the units of property, incurred whilst those units were leased, bearing in mind that the units of property were at all times used exclusively in the applicant’s business to derive assessable income. Also, but less significantly, had the cost of modifications been borne by the lessor it would have been entitled to a deduction for depreciation under s54(1) in respect of those costs.

  4. As mentioned, the respondent did exercise his discretion under subsection 60(2) in favour of the applicant but only to the extent that the depreciated value (or cost) for the purpose of s56 be calculated on the basis of the amounts paid for ownership of the leased vehicles at the expiration of the lease, i.e. the residual value; and a value for the cost of modifications calculated by depreciating hypothetically those costs from the time they were incurred until the end of the lease term. In the result, the difference between the actual cost of modifications and their hypothetically depreciated value at the end of the lease, represents amounts incurred by the applicant which have not been, and in the absence of a favorable exercise of discretion under s60(2), never will be the subject of a tax deduction to either the applicant or the lessor.

  5. The Tribunal observes that instead, the respondent may have been minded, and indeed, justified in terms of equity, to exercise his discretion, available under s60(2), to allow the applicant to depreciate the full capital costs incurred in modifying depreciable plant which amounts have not been the subject of a depreciation allowance or any other deduction to either the applicant or the lessor. In those circumstances the respondent might have justified such an exercise of favourable discretion on equity grounds alone. It seems to the Tribunal, that from an operational basis alone, Parliament’s purpose of granting the respondent the discretion in s60(2) could well have been to provide an equitable solution to a set of circumstances which are outside the ordinary. The Tribunal is of the view that the circumstances of the present case, whilst explicable in ordinary commercial dealings between lessors and lessees of equipment which needs modification to be fully functional for the lessee, are of the kind that are not accommodated by the mandatory provisions of Division III of Part III of the Act. For these reasons the Tribunal, standing in the shoes of the respondent, would exercise its discretion under s60(2) of the Act to entitle the applicant to depreciate the full cost of the modifications, as mentioned above, from their date of acquisition, 5 October 1988.

  6. Counsel for the respondent submitted that in the light of the legislative framework of Division III of Part III, and in particular, s62, the proper basis for depreciation is the amounts paid by the applicant to gain ownership of the units of property at the expiration of the lease term. He further submitted that the respondent had allowed a concession to the applicant by exercising his discretion, albeit in a limited way, under subsection 60(2) also to allow depreciation on the notionally depreciated value of the cost of the modifications at that date.

  7. Subsection 62(1), states that “... ‘depreciated value’ [for the purpose of s56(1)(a) etc] of any unit of property at any time means the cost of the unit to the person who owns or owned the property at that time less ...”. (emphasis added) Taking s62(1) in isolation, it can be inferred that the owner of depreciable plant or articles is not entitled to depreciate that property beyond the cost to him/her or it. If the item is improved at someone else’s cost then the owner has no right to depreciate those costs, and the corollary of that is that neither can the taxpayer who incurred the cost, even if the plant or article is used by that taxpayer to derive assessable income, because it was not owned by that person.

  8. Therefore, it was submitted, as no right exists for the owner (lessor) to depreciate the user’s (lessee’s) cost of improving the unit of property, then, in terms of s62 the “cost” of the unit to the applicant for the purpose of s56(1)(b) [or s56(1)(a) for that matter] excludes the cost of improving the unit before ownership. No deduction is possible in relation to the costs of modification of the units of property[2] unless the respondent exercises his discretion under s60(2) as discussed above. In this light the Tribunal was urged to affirm the decision under review.

  9. The evidence indicates that the parties to the lease, the applicant and the lessor, were at arm’s length and the lease agreement was based on ordinary commercial bargaining at the time. There has been no evidence to suggest that the transactions on 5 October 1987, whereby the lessee acquired the five units of property in question, was not commercial. There is no evidence to suggest that the price paid by the applicant to the lessor was not reasonable. The evidence suggests that the modifications carried out by the applicant were essential for its business and were done with the tacit consent of the lessor. In the circumstances, the cost to the applicant of the modified units of property can be viewed in two ways - in strict legal terms it is what it paid the lessor and nothing else for that was all that was required to obtain full legal title, but in accounting (commercial) terms, as the witness opined, it is a total of that cost and the cost of the modifications.

  10. But, it was argued for the applicant, whether the legal basis cost is the appropriate “cost” to the applicant of acquiring the units of property must be determined, having regard to commercial tests and common sense. Kitto J said, in B P Refinery (Kwinana) -

“It seems to me impossible to suppose that the depreciation provisions of the Act are intended to apply only to those simple cases in which the ascertainment of cost is a purely arithmetical process. I interpret it as embracing the whole sum which, according to accepted accountancy practice as applied to the circumstances of the case, ought to be considered as having been laid out by the taxpayer in order to acquire the subject-matter as plant, that is to say installed and ready for his use as plant for the purpose of producing assessable income.” (pp.207 - 208)



  1. His Honour was there addressing the question of apportioning costs to arrive at a depreciable cost, some of which related to the plant. In this case there is no question of apportionment. However, his Honour went on to say, in the same context -

“But it cannot be in accordance with the intention of s.56 [of the Act] that one should chase the rainbow of absolute accuracy beyond the point at which the practical accountant would stop. In my opinion it is at that point that the requirement of the word “cost” is satisfied, so that the question in each case is, what is the figure reached by a proper application of the recognized principles of costing to the particular circumstances.” (p.208)



  1. In tax cases one must be cautious in applying accounting treatment of particular outgoings or income amounts to determine the appropriate tax law treatment. In this respect the Tribunal’s attention was directed to Kitto J’s judgment in FCT v Broken Hill Proprietary Company Limited [1968] HCA 16; (1968) 120 CLR 240 and in particular at p.263 where his Honour said:

“I think that no assistance at all is to be gained on the question now to be decided by considering what is a proper way of dealing with an outgoing as a matter of practical accountancy. The reason is that the Act does not provide for any relevant allowable deduction for depreciation of plant, except in the form of a percentage of the cost of the plant or that cost less previously allowed depreciation: see ss56, 62.”



  1. It is noted that s60(2) of the Act as it then was, was substantially different to the present provision - the former, in the context to which his Honour was referring read:

“s60(2) This section shall not apply where the Commissioner is of the opinion that the circumstances are such that depreciation based on the



actual consideration given should be allowed.”[3]



  1. So what his Honour had to say in the Broken Hill Proprietary case above has no application to the present circumstances.

  2. However, accounting practice can be taken as a guide where the statute is imprecise. In C of T (SA) v Executor Trustee & Agency Company of South Australia Limited [Carden’s case] [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108; 5 ATD 98, Dixon J said, in the context of discussing the framework of the Act to measure assessable income, that:

“Speaking generally in the assessment of income the object is to discover what gains have during the period of account come home to the taxpayer in a realised or immediately realisable form. .... The computation of profits from manufacture and trading has always proceeded upon the principle that the profit may be contained in stock in trade and ‘outstandings’. Whether this is to be explained on some view that the purpose is to ascertain what is the detachable increase in circulating capital, or more simply on the ground of common sense and the teachings of experience, the result for the purposes of taxation is the same. The result is that a tax upon the profits or income of such a business must be understood as a tax upon the profit or income computed according to the system, because according to common understanding and commercial principles, that is the method of determining profits.” (ATD p.132)



  1. The mere existence of the discretionary provision, s60(2) in the context of this case, in the opinion of the Tribunal, requires the decision maker (the respondent) to consider, having regard to commercial principles, whether the discretion ought to be favourably exercised. This the respondent did but, as mentioned, in a limited way. After having considered the submissions by the respondent the Tribunal is of the opinion that the proper decision in this case, for the reasons expressed, would be its conclusion previously reached.

Decision



  1. The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in substitution therefor decides that the applicant be entitled to depreciate the five units of property listed in Table 1 at paragraph 10 of the Reasons for Decision, for the years of income ended 30 June 1988 and 1989 and beginning on 5 October 1987, pursuant to s56(1)(b) of the Act, on the basis that the cost consists of both the cost of the modifications and the price paid as set out in Table 1.
