REASONS FOR DECISION



24 November 1999 J. Block, Senior Member



1. The objection decision in this matter is the disallowance by the Respondent of an objection dated 13 November 1998 against an assessment issued on 21 March 1997 in respect of the year ended 30 June 1993 ("the Relevant Year").

2. (a) Mr Gregory Ganz of Parry Carroll Kanjian, solicitors, appeared for the Applicant while Ms Virginia Rands, an officer of the Respondent, appeared on his behalf.

(b) The Tribunal had before it the T Documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and accepted the tender of the following exhibits:



  • Exhibit A1 is a statement by the Applicant dated 1 November 1999;

  • Exhibit A2 is a statement by Mr Artin Etmekdjian, who was the Applicant's tax agent (and who is referred to as the "tax agent") dated 8 November 1999;

  • Exhibit R1 is a bundle of documents entitled "Respondent's Case List and Additional Documents";

  • Exhibit R2 is an affidavit of service by Robert Cashman as to his service of a summons on 29 June 1997;

  • Exhibit R3 is a document entitled "Applicant's Lodgment History".

3. The findings by the Respondent as contained in his Statement of Findings on Material Questions of Fact, Evidence and Reasons for Decision are set out in full as follows:

"FINDINGS

1. The Applicant's income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1993 was due to be lodged by 31 October 1993.

2. A demand notice was issued in relation to the lodgment of the 1993 tax return, as well as for the 1991, 1992, 1994 and 1995 returns.

3. On 24 May 1996, the Applicant's representative requested an extension of time to lodge the returns, until the end of July 1996.

4. On 13 June 1996, the ATO advised the Applicant that no formal extension would be granted, but that legal action would be withheld until 31 July 1996. The fact that the returns would still be liable to additional tax was specifically drawn to the attention of the Applicant.

5. The Applicant lodged his 1993 return during the week ended 6 December 1996. The Applicant's 1991 and 1992 returns were lodged at the same time.

6. Notices of Assessment for the 1991, 1992 and 1993 income years issued on 21 March 1997.

7. The Applicant objected to the 1993 assessment in a letter dated 13 November 1998.

8. The objection was disallowed in full in a notice of decision dated 24 February 1999.

9. The Applicant was prosecuted in relation to the non-lodgment of his 1994, 1995 and 1996 income tax returns. On 19 August 1997, the applicant was convinced in the St James Local Court and ordered to pay a fine of $600 plus $76 costs, and was given 2 months to lodge the returns.

10. A demand notice was also issued in relation to the 1997 return. This return was also lodged late and additional tax imposed in this regard."



4. In respect of the evidence of the Applicant:

(a) Exhibit A1 reads as follows:



"1. I am the Applicant in these proceedings.

2. In 1989, I appointed Mr Artin Etmedjkian [sic] as my tax agent. In that capacity, he had responsibility for preparation and filing of my income tax returns for the years ended 30 June 1989 to 30 June 1996 inclusive.

3. In 1992 and 1993, I supplied to Mr Etmedjkian [sic] all the information I believed was required by him to prepare my income tax returns for the years ended 30 June 1991, 30 June 1992 and 30 June 1993. During the period 1992 to 1996, every few months I would telephone Mr Etmedjkian [sic] and ask him when my returns would be ready for review, signing and lodgment. In all conversations with him, I indicated that I was very concerned as to the late lodgment of the returns.

4. At all times, Mr Etmedjkian [sic] would tell me that I had nothing to worry about as there was no tax owing for the years ended 30 June 1991, 30 June 1992 and 30 June 1993 because the ATO owed me money. Despite this assurance, I continued to telephone Mr Etmekdjian and ask him when my returns would be ready for review, signing and lodgment.

5. In November/December 1996, I signed the income tax returns for the years ended 30 June 1991, 30 June 1992 and 30 June 1993. I understand that those income returns were subsequently lodged.

6. In or about June 1997, I became aware that assessments had issued for the years ended 30 June 1991, 30 June 1992 and 30 June 1993 and that some $70,000 was owing as a result of those assessments. This was the first time I became aware that the ATO considered that an amount of tax was due and payable. Mr Etmedjkian [sic] stated that he would lodge an objection and, as a result of that objection, the ATO would acknowledge that no amount of tax was due and payable.

7. It is of considerable concern to me that the continual late lodgment of my income tax returns has tarnished my reputation with the ATO. As a consequence, I appointed Mr Kevin Taylor in November 1998 as my new tax agent who attended to the lodgment of my income tax return for the years ended 30 June 1997 and 30 June 1998. I have instructed Mr Kevin Taylor to lodge all my income tax returns on time regardless of whether a refund is due or tax will be assessed in relation to the relevant year of income."



(b) The Applicant is a freelance copywriter who works on a commission from advertising agencies. He lived at 2 Clovelly Street, Watsons Bay, Sydney until he moved to 159 Hopetoun Avenue, Watsons Bay, Sydney towards the end of 1997.



(c) The tax agent was originally appointed to act as such in 1987 when he was a member of a firm; the Applicant followed the tax agent (and utilised his services) when he set up his own practice in 1989, and until he appointed a new agent (Mr K Taylor) in 1997.



(d) He said that he first became aware of the fact that action was being taken against him in respect of his tax affairs when a notice was served on him at his home in 1997. He also testified (in cross-examination) that he did not recall the receipt of a notice by the Respondent (which is page 28 of Exhibit R1) dated 5 January 1996 addressed to him at his home in Clovelly Street, Watsons Bay and in which the Respondent required returns for the years ending June 1991 to June 1997 (both years inclusive); that notice contains a warning as to the fact that refusal could result in additional tax amounting to double the tax assessable being imposed.



(e) The Applicant also said that (despite the affidavit of service which is Exhibit R2) he had no recollection of the criminal prosecution in 1997 which resulted in the imposition of a fine and costs but he believed that he had paid the amounts involved. He did not appear and was not represented at that hearing.



(f) The Applicant said that he had approached a tax agent who was also a chartered accountant to ensure that his tax affairs were properly attended to. Nevertheless, he telephoned the tax agent every few months to enquire as to progress. He did not recollect any mention by the tax agent of illness in respect of the tax agent's partner.



(g) During cross-examination, the attention of the Applicant was drawn to Exhibit R3 which indicates that the Applicant has been late with his returns every single year from 1984 through to 1998 (both inclusive). (That exhibit contains asterisks in respect of the 1988, 1989 and 1990 tax years in that lateness was assumed for those years having regard to the due dates and actual dates of lodgment; the Respondent no longer has records for those years.) But in respect of all of the other years, returns were furnished only after some form of action by the Respondent. In respect of the Relevant Year the return was more than three years late.



5. In respect of the evidence of the tax agent:

(a) Exhibit A2 (as amended to reflect a correction made by the tax agent during the course of the hearing) reads as follows:



"1. I was the Applicant's tax agent in relation to the lodgment of his income tax returns for the years ended 30 June 1988 to 30 June [1996] inclusive.

2. The Applicant provided to me the information required to prepare the relevant income tax returns. On a number of occasions, the Applicant contacted me in an attempt to have his income tax returns prepared and lodged. My failure to lodge the Applicant's income tax returns in the time period required was due to a number of extenuating circumstances including:

(a) in 1994, my business partner was diagnosed with a terminal illness and died in August 1995. This placed an enormous amount of stress on me and dramatically increased my work load. The result was that all of my work was delayed, including the preparation of the Applicant's income tax returns for the relevant years of income; and

(b) the death of my father in May 1995 which caused considerable distress.

3. In any case, I took the view that no tax would be payable for late lodgment of the Applicant's income tax returns for the years ended 30 June 1991, 1992 and 1993. This view, as expressed in the notice of objection prepared by me dated 13 November 1993, was on the basis:

(a) credit assessments would issue for the years ended 30 June 1991 and 30 June 1992 which resulted in a net refund of $36,443.42;

(b) the tax liability for the year ended 30 June 1993 was the sum of $31,732.09 so when the refund for the earlier years of income was taken into account, no tax was due and payable for the year ended 30 June 1993;

(c) the receipt of a lump sum in the year ended 30 June 1993 was a "once off" receipt by the Applicant for consulting which was never received again and could have been varied to nil and, in any case, was reversed upon the lodgment of the Applicant's income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1994."



(b) The tax agent said that he commenced his own practice in 1990 (not 1989 as specified by the Applicant), the year in which his mother died.



(c) In 1994 his business partner became ill; that partner was running the Parramatta branch of the office; subsequently and in 1995 his partner (and also his father) died. He found a replacement for his partner only after his death.



(d) He said that because of these personal tragedies his work fell into arrears. He was obliged to prioritise his work and he gave priority to clients whose assessments would result in tax being paid, and so as to avoid penalties. Clients entitled to credits were not so treated, even though their late receipts of refunds might have been prejudicial to them.



(e) The tax agent said that the Applicant gave him the information he needed for the Relevant Year return; when asked why he did not seek an extension he said that with the benefit of hindsight he should have done so. After it was pointed out to the tax agent that the Applicant's Relevant Year return was late, even before his partner became ill, he said that he might not have had all of the information required. He agreed also that a variation notice should have been filed in respect of provisional tax in view of the fact that the Applicant received the lump sum in 1993.



(f) The tax agent said that he was originally registered to practise as such in 1985 or 1987 (he could not remember which), was aware of the lodgment rules, and also said that he did inform the Applicant of what he referred to as his "extenuating" circumstances.



6. (a) Each of the parties furnished me with written submissions which were of assistance.

(b) The Applicant placed some considerable reliance on the fact that:



(1) The Applicant was entitled to refunds for the years ending June 1991 and 1992 in the amount of $36,443.42, and that his tax liability for the Relevant Year (excluding provisional tax) was $31,732; if those returns had been filed together (and not years later) the Applicant would have had no tax liability, always assuming that provisional tax had been varied to nil. Put succinctly, so the Applicant argued, there was no great prejudice to the Respondent.



(2) In respect of each of the years 1991 to 1997 (both inclusive) it is only the Relevant Year and the 1997 year in which the Applicant had a tax liability.



(3) Clauses 3.6, 3.7 and 3.8 of the Applicant's submissions are set out as follows:



"3.6 Of course, the Applicant's tax agent, on behalf of the Applicant, should have filed and lodged the returns on time. The failure to lodge the Applicant's income tax returns on time must be understood in the context that the Applicant's tax agent was under enormous pressure due to circumstances beyond his control. Those circumstances are outlined in paragraph 2 of Mr Artin Etmekdjian's witness statement. In the circumstances where a tax agent is suddenly thrust with the responsibility of handling the work of two persons, it is understandable that that person will give lesser priority to the preparation and lodgment of income tax returns which he believes no tax is due and payable. As stated above, this belief was based on the fact that a refund due and payable for the years ended 30 June 1992 and 30 June 1993 and the fact that provisional tax would not be taken into account.

3.7 As is evident from the Applicant's witness statement, from the Applicant's point of view he relied on the advice of his tax agent. The Applicant supplied all the information he believed was required by him to prepare the income tax returns for the years ended 30 June 1991, 30 June 1992 and 30 June 1993 and during the period 1992 to 1996, every few months would telephone his then tax agent and ask when the returns would be ready for review and signing. The Applicant's tax agent would assure him at all times that there was nothing to worry about with the Applicant continuing to press his tax agent for lodgment of the relevant income tax returns. In essence, the Applicant relied on the advice of his then tax agent and did what he practically could to ensure the lodgment of his income tax returns.

3.8 The additional tax for late lodgment arose, in essence, due to the Applicant's tax agent misunderstanding as to the effect of the refunds due for the years ended 30 June 1991 and 30 June 1992 and his failure to lodge a variation of provisional tax for the year ended 30 June 1993. In itself, this is not a ground for submitting that the additional tax for late lodgment should be remitted. However, where:

  • it was clearly intended by the Respondent to impose a late lodgment penalty at the rate of 20%;
    • the Respondent incorrectly included provisional tax in the calculation (which, in any case, could easily have been excluded by variation of the same);
    • a refund was due and payable for the years ended 30 June 1991 and 30 June 1992 so, overall, no tax was due and payable for the years ended 30 June 1991 to 1993 inclusive;
    • the Applicant's tax agent was under considerable pressure due to circumstances beyond his control and, understandably, gave low priority to income tax returns which he thought had no tax due and payable;
    • tax was only due and payable in respect of the years ended 30 June 1993 and 30 June 1997 though the income tax returns for the years ended 30 June 1991 to 30 June 1997 inclusive were lodged late; and
    • the clear policy of sub-section 222(1) of the Tax Act is to only impose late lodgment penalty on "tax payable" for the relevant year of income,

it is submitted that the AAT should exercise the Respondent's power, under sub-section 227(3) of the Tax Act, and remit the additional late lodgment penalty to a rate of less than 42% imposed."



(c) The Tribunal notes that it cannot accept that the alleged absence of prejudice argument is valid in a matter of this nature. The fact is that the Applicant did not file returns, and cannot now be heard to say that if he had done so, and if he had filed a variation notice, there would have been no amount owing.



(d) As to the tax agent's "extenuating circumstances", the Tribunal cannot accept that they excuse his failure to carry out his duties. His lodgment failure in respect of the Relevant Year arose in late 1993 and in respect of the 1991 and 1992 years, long before that. His partner became ill only in 1994 and died in 1995; however he was replaced only after his death and the relevant returns were filed only in 1996. Even allowing for the disruptive effect of his partner's death, there were other (substantial) time gaps for which there was no adequate explanation.



(e) The Tribunal considers that the Applicant's consistent history of late lodgment is indeed indicative of the fact that he did not take his tax obligations as seriously as he should have done, and although the conduct of the tax agent is also deserving of censure, the Applicant is not entitled to treat the latter conduct as anything other than a failure which is to some extent mitigatory.



7. (a) Prior to the hearing, and at a directions hearing held on 11 October 1999, Mr Ganz indicated that he intended to contend that the words "tax payable by the taxpayer in respect of the year of income" in section 222(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("ITAA") should not be construed so as to include provisional tax. This is so, he contended, because these words only operate in respect of the tax assessable for a given year of income but not the provisional tax which, although assessed by reference to income for the given year (in addition to any uplift factor), is in fact an anticipatory tax for the next succeeding year. His contention, as I understand it, is that the words to which I have referred do not extend to include provisional tax in the calculation of additional tax, and which is not tax in respect of the year of income.

(b) (1) The Tribunal drew the attention of the parties to section 221YA(2) of ITAA which provides as follows:



"In sections 206, 207, 207A, 208, 209, 214, 254, 255, 258 and 259, but not in any other section of this Act, "income tax" or "tax" includes provisional tax and additional tax under section 221YDB."



(2) The Tribunal also noted that Division 3 of Part VI of ITAA deals with provisional tax. Section 221YDB is entitled "Additional Tax where income under-estimated or where PAYE deductions over-estimated". It is to be noted in particular that section 221YDB allows the imposition of additional tax (referable to provisional tax) only where the relevant return indicates under-estimates of income or over-estimates of PAYE deductions.



(c) To complete the statutory framework:



(1) Section 222 of ITAA provides as follows:



"222. Penalty for failure to keep or furnish documents or information

222(1) [Additional Tax: return or information] Where a taxpayer refuses or fails to furnish, when and as required under or pursuant to this Act or the regulations to do so, a return, or any information, relating to a year of income, being a return relating to or information relating to, or to the affairs of, the taxpayer, the taxpayer is liable to pay, by way of penalty, additional tax equal to double the amount of tax payable by the taxpayer in respect of the year of income."



(2) Section 227(3) allows the Respondent to remit the penalty provided by section 222(1) of ITAA and, in fact, in this matter the penalty was remitted in such manner that it was calculated at the rate of 20% per annum.



(d) Ms Rands indicated at an early stage of the hearing that she did not intend to press the Relevant Year assessment, insofar as it levied additional tax for late lodgment on the provisional tax component of the assessment. Whether that concession was made by reference to the wording of section 222(1) of ITAA or in consequence of section 221YA(2) of ITAA was not made clear. Suffice it to say that the Tribunal considers that the concession was correctly made. Provisional tax is in fact anticipatory tax, and it is designed to equate PAYE taxpayers who have tax deducted from their salaries with other taxpayers who do not. It is, in my view, of crucial importance that section 221YA(2) provides that "income tax" or "tax" includes provisional tax and additional tax under section 221YDB but only in respect of the sections specified in that sub-section. Section 222 of ITAA is not one of the sections expressly included in section 221YA(2). It is relevant in particular that section 221YA(2) provides that tax includes provisional tax for the purposes of the named sections "but not in any other section of this Act."



(e) It would seem then that additional tax can be imposed by reference to provisional tax pursuant to section 221YDB of ITAA (for underestimates of income or overestimates of PAYE), but not pursuant to section 222(1) of ITAA. This would appear to be because of the specific provisions of section 221YA(2) of ITAA (and without more), and so that it is not necessary for me to decide whether the closing words of section 222(1) of ITAA should be interpreted in the manner for which the Applicant contends.



(f) My attention was drawn to Case 44/97 97 ATC 467 and to Case 19/98 98 ATC 252. In neither case does this aspect appear to have been considered.



(g) (1) In Case 44/97 97 ATC 467 Deputy President McMahon dealt with a case in which penalties were imposed probably by reference to provisional tax. Clauses 16 to 22 inclusive of that decision are reproduced in these Reasons as follows (at 469):



"16. The fact is, however, that the very fact of late lodgment of the return caused the late assessment of his provisional tax for 1996. The Commissioner's administrative practice of using the "net amount payable" as the basis for calculating LLP proceeds on the basis that the 1996 provisional tax debit must be taken into account at the same time as the 1995 provisional tax credit and other credits are taken into account. What the applicant is saying is that the 1996 provisional tax debit should be disregarded because if he had lodged his return at an earlier date, it would not have been payable prior to 1 April 1996. However, what might have been does not offer a logical basis upon which to determine a remission application.

17. The applicant claims some credit for the provisional tax that he paid on 18 July 1996. He says it should have been taken into account at arriving at the "net amount payable".

18. The Lodgment Rules and IT 2475 set out the concessions available to taxpayers who forward a payment of estimated tax and provisional tax when their return is lodged. If a current year return is lodged late, but prior to 15 April, and a taxpayer pays the estimated balance of tax on assessment in full prior to, or with lodgement, no additional tax will be calculated. Where he or she lodges a return after 15 April but before 30 June and payment in full has preceded or accompanies lodgment, additional tax will be calculated on the lesser of the tax payable or net amount payable for the period from the end of December to the date of lodgement. The applicant did not do this. His return was not lodged until 2 December 1996, well into the subsequent financial year.

19. The guidelines followed by the respondent in relation to payments in advance, do not apply to taxpayers for whom prior year returns are outstanding as at 30 June, or to taxpayers who are clients of tax agents who have received final demands to lodge returns by dates earlier than those permitted by their agent's lodgment program.

20. By 2 December 1996, the applicant's 1995 income tax return was no longer a current year return. The respondent's practice was, therefore, to treat it as a prior year return and not to apply the advance payment concession. This does not represent harsh treatment for a delay of eighteen days as the applicant appears to think. It reflects the true situation (what the applicant called "commercially realistic") by recognising that the return in question was lodged some five months after the so-called payment in advance.

21. There is no legislative provision which would allow the Commissioner to credit interest in relation to an amount paid in these circumstances, where no assessment notice was issued. This is a basic requirement of the Taxation (Interest on Overpayments and Early Payments) Act 1983.

22. The guidelines and policies that have been applied in the present case are not new. They have been in effect and have been followed for many years. The applicant must be regarded as having a special knowledge of the application of these guidelines and rules and of the way in which the LLP is calculated. I see nothing in the submissions of both parties which would lead me to diverge from practices well recognised in this field."



(2) Desirable as it may be that there be consistency in decision making in this Tribunal, I do not propose to follow the decision in Case 44/97 which in any event appears to me to be distinguishable on its facts.



8. (a) The Applicant too made a concession and which was also, in my view, appropriate. He accepted that an additional tax composition calculated at the rate of 20% per annum was appropriate in the circumstances, but only if it was calculated by reference to tax exclusive of the provisional tax. He argued in other words that while the percentage was appropriately imposed, the calculation was not because it included additional tax calculated and imposed under section 222(1) of ITAA and by reference to provisional tax. Mr Ganz indicated that his client would naturally not object to a further reduction, but conceded that the 20% percentage was reasonable in the circumstances.

(b) Ms Rands argued that it was the amount of the additional tax which was reasonable, having regard to all the circumstances, even if and in the result it gave rise to a percentage calculation which is higher than 20%.



(c) However and in this case, the Respondent made it clear that it was a percentage which was being applied. See in this context the Respondent's calculations as set out in the T Documents (under Reasons for Decision) as follows:



"Imposition of Late Lodgment Penalty (LLP)

Additional tax, by way of penalty, is imposed under section 222 of the Act where a taxpayer fails to furnish a return when and as required. The rate prescribed by the Act for this penalty is double the amount (200%) of tax payable in respect of the year of income.

However, generally speaking the full 200% is not usually imposed, and the Commissioner normally exercises his discretion under subsection 227(3) of the Act to reduce the level of penalty substantially to a base level of 20%.

In the Applicant's case such a remission was granted, reducing the imposition of the additional tax rate to a rate of 20%.

Calculation of LLP

When calculating late lodgment penalties, the Commissioner will apply a percentage rate of penalty to an amount, the amount being the lesser of the tax payable or the net amount payable.

'Tax payable' is calculated as A + O – G

where A = Gross tax on taxable income

O = Medicare levy

G = Rebates and other credits (not TIDs)

'Net tax Payable' is calculated as: (A + O - G) + B - E - F - Z - P

where B = Provisional tax

E = Tax Instalment Deductions (TIDs)

F = Credit for provisional tax paid

Z = Credit for prescribed payments or reportable payments

P = TFN amounts deducted on interest/dividends

In the applicant's case, for the 1993 year of assessment, the tax payable would be an amount of $96,933.49. The net amount payable would be $66,495.09.

The lesser of these figures, $66,495.09 was therefore used in the calculation of the late lodgment penalty. A rate of 20% per annum was applied to this figure over a period of 160 weeks to reach the penalty amount as notified in the assessment.

$66,495.09 x 20% x 160/52 = $40,920.05

The calculation of late lodgment penalty has reference only to the financial year in question. Whether or not other assessments for other years result in a net debit or credit is irrelevant.

Calculation of period late

Ordinary 1993 individual returns were due for lodgment by 31 October 1993. This in effect was extended to 1 November 1993, as 31 October fell on a Sunday. The applicant actually lodged his return during the working week ending 6 December 1996.

The period for which the return was late was therefore calculated from 2 November 1993 to 29 November 1996, which is a period of 160 whole weeks.

The starting point for the late period is not the extended date allowed under the tax agent lodgment program rules. The lodgment rules are an arrangement which grant a concession to tax agents, and are made in relation to general categories of returns, not individual taxpayers. The rules do not give a specific extension of time to lodge to any taxpayer.

The decisions in Case 44/97 97 ATC 467 and Case 19/98 98 ATC 252 acknowledge that the Commissioner's practice and guidelines in regard to late lodgment are longstanding and well understood. In those cases the start date for the late period was held to be as described in IT 2475.

General remission

Additional tax for late lodgment is imposed under section 222 of the Act at the rate of 200% of the tax payable in respect of that income year. It is the Commissioner's practice to remit this amount in accordance with specified guidelines, such that the full statutory penalty is rarely imposed. In all but exceptional circumstances the Commissioner exercises the discretion in subsection 227(3) and remits part of the additional tax at the time of making the assessment.

Generally, the penalty is imposed at an effective rate of 20% per annum, with further remission only given where there are clearly extenuating circumstances. The guidelines used then prescribe increases to this rate for recurring offences and detected non-lodgers. A recurring offence component may have been warranted in this case as the applicant had lodged his 1991 and 1992 returns late. However, this component was not applied."



9. (a) My attention was drawn to certain non-binding rulings (IT 2241, IT 2371 and IT 2475, the last of which superseded IT 2371 as at 1 November 1988) and which deal with the imposition of penalties in respect of provisional tax when returns are late. IT 2475 relates in its terms to the guidelines which generally should be applied in considering requests for remission of additional tax under section 227(3) of ITAA; clauses 10 and following contain the Respondent's view as to when and in what manner the power to remit should be exercised. IT 2241 provides merely for a reversal by the Respondent of his previously-adopted policy of not making adjustments in additional tax payable by provisional taxpayers who lodge late returns but who later benefit from a provisional tax variation. The rulings may perhaps assume that an imposition of additional tax in respect of a late return can be calculated by reference to the provisional tax component. However, the rulings do not deal with the question raised by section 221YA(2) and, more particularly, to the fact that section 222 is not one of the sections named in it. Nor, for that matter, do the rulings address the submission by the Applicant as to the meaning to be given to the term "in respect of the year of income" in section 222 of ITAA.

(b) As Ms Rands pointed out, section 251M of ITAA provides that a tax agent may in the circumstances therein set out be liable to his client where the client becomes liable to pay additional tax in consequence of the tax agent's negligence. The CCH Reporter (p 642 201) indicates that it is not clear whether the section applies only where the negligence of the tax agent is the sole cause of the liability. See also in this context clause 14(j) of Taxation Ruling 94/4 which provides that a taxpayer who engages a registered tax agent is vicariously liable for the tax agent's careless errors, although clause 14(j) in its terms refers to a somewhat different situation, and that is where the taxpayer prepares his or her own return and seeks advice from a tax agent as to aspects of it. Clause 14(j) of the Ruling is set out in full as follows:



"(j) a taxpayer who prepares his or her own tax return and seeks advice in respect of a specific matter in the tax return from a qualified accountant or lawyer or similar kind of adviser, and follows the advice provided, would ordinarily be accepted as having exercised reasonable care in respect of the matter on which the advice was sought. However, if the adviser is a registered tax agent, whether or not the adviser is also a qualified accountant or lawyer, the new penalties continue to apply on the basis that the taxpayer is vicariously liable for the tax agent's careless errors if the taxpayer has consulted the accountant or lawyer in his or her capacity as a registered tax agent (ie in respect of the preparation of a tax return). The taxpayer's remedy against his or her tax agent is under section 251M of the ITAA, which provides that a taxpayer may recover from a registered tax agent any additional tax or interest which the taxpayer has become liable to pay through the negligence of the tax agent."



(c) Clause 3.1 of the Applicant's Submissions reads as follows:



"3.1 Paragraph 3 of Taxation Ruling IT 2371 notes that in most cases additional tax for late lodgment is actually imposed at the rate of 20% per annum on the lesser of tax payable and net amount payable. This was clearly the intention in the present case which is evident from the way in which the additional tax for late lodgment of $40,920.05 was calculated (see paragraph 9 of the Respondent's Statement), namely:

$66,495.09 x 20% x 160/52 = $40,920.05

The Respondent simply failed to properly take into account the fact that provisional tax should not have been included in the calculation so that the amount should have been calculated as follows:

$31,732.09 x 20% x 160/52 = $19,527.43"



(d) It is my view that the conduct of the Applicant in respect of his tax affairs was culpable. The Applicant is not uneducated, and Exhibit R3 is indicative of his lack of attention to his tax obligations. His failure to file returns cannot be excused on the basis that he left matters to his tax agent. I consider also that it is likely that he was aware of his default prior to 1997 having regard to the 1996 notice previously referred to and also that the criminal summons does appear to have been served on him. On the other hand it does seem clear that at least some of his difficulties arose from the neglect of the tax agent, and this is, in my view, a mitigating factor.



The Respondent was fully aware of all of the surrounding circumstances; he nevertheless saw fit to remit additional tax in such manner that the percentage was 20%. I have no reason to believe that he exercised his discretion incorrectly and, therefore, conclude that it is an appropriate percentage. I have found, however, that the Respondent's calculations were incorrect in that he was not entitled to impose additional tax by reference to the provisional component of the tax assessed. That factor does not warrant an increase in the percentage; at the same time the conduct of the Applicant is such that a decrease is also not warranted.



10. In all the circumstances, the objection decision under review is affirmed to the extent that it relates to additional tax referable to tax other than provisional tax, and set aside insofar as it relates to additional tax referable to provisional tax; the Respondent is directed to amend the assessment for the Relevant Year accordingly.
