W Wehbe Pty Limited and Michael Wehbe Pty Limited v Commissioner of Taxation [1993] FCA 590; (1993) 93 Atc 5015 (1993) 27 Atr 172 (7 December 1993)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

W. WEHBE PTY LIMITED and MICHAEL WEHBE PTY LIMITED v. COMMISSIONER OF

TAXATION

Nos. NG609, NG610 and NG613 of 1992

FED No. 896

Number of pages - 10

Income Tax

(1993) 93 ATC 5015

(1993) 27 ATR 172

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

GUMMOW J

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - service station operators - legal proceedings to preserve petroleum supply contract and lease of premises (including provision of signage) - deductions claimed for legal expenses - whether outgoings of a capital nature.

Sunraysia Broadcasting Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 91 ATC 4530

Smithkline Beecham Laboratories (Australia) Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1993] FCA 389; (1993) 116 ALR 503

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s. 51

HEARING

SYDNEY, 11 November 1993

7:12:1993

Counsel and Solicitors Mr R. Gordon

for the taxpayers

Instructed by Rosenblum and Partners.

Counsel and Solicitors Mr W. Muddle

for the Commissioner of Taxation:

Instructed by the Australian Government

Solicitor.

ORDER

Nos. NG 609, NG610 and NG613

The Court orders that:

(1) The application be dismissed.

(2) The applicant pay the costs of the respondent.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with by Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

GUMMOW J These matters were heard together. The taxpayers are related corporations and in the relevant years of income carried on business as service station operators in the suburbs of Sydney. Mr Wally Wehbe was a director and shareholder of W. Wehbe Pty Limited ("Wehbe") and a shareholder of Michael Wehbe Pty Limited ("Michael Wehbe"). He describes himself as having been, in the relevant years of income, the principal of these companies (and other companies and partnerships which operated service stations); he was not engaged in day to day management of any of the service stations but negotiated leases and supply contracts and was overseer of the financial management of the businesses.

2. In matter No. 609 of 1992 the taxpayer, Wehbe, appeals to the Court against the decision of the respondent to disallow its objection against an amended assessment to income tax and additional tax for the year of income ended 30 June 1987.

3. In matter No. 613 of 1992 the taxpayer, Michael Wehbe, appeals against the decision of the respondent to disallow its objection against an amended assessment to income tax and additional tax for the year of income ended 30 June 1986. In matter No. 610 of 1992, the appeal relates to the disallowance of an objection by Michael Wehbe against an amended assessment for the year ended 30 June 1987.

4. At stake in all three matters are deductions for legal expenses claimed under s. 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. Michael Wehbe claims the sum of $3,000 for the year ended 30 June 1986, and $6,000 for that ended 30 June 1987. Wehbe claims $126,577 in the year ending 30 June 1987. All claims for these legal fees were disallowed as capital expenditures. The taxpayers contend that they should be seen as expenditure on revenue account because they were outgoings incurred in gaining or producing their assessable income or in carrying on their businesses.

5. The legal fees were paid in respect of proceedings taken by the taxpayers in this Court to obtain relief against Caltex Oil (Australia) Pty Limited ("Caltex"). Caltex had sought to terminate a lease and supply contract relating to a service station operated by Wehbe at Chullora and, in the case of Michael Wehbe, a supply contract relating to a service station operated by it at Bankstown on a freehold site which it had purchased from Caltex in 1980. Caltex, by cross-claim, sought possession of the Chullora premises.

6. The proceedings were brought by the taxpayers in reliance upon the Petroleum Retail Marketing Franchise Act 1980 ("the Franchise Act"). The Franchise Act came into force on 19 September 1980, and was amended from time to time. The supply contract of each taxpayer was a franchise agreement (within the meaning of s. 3 of the Franchise Act) with Caltex. So also was the lease of the Chullora premises. The broad intention of the principal provisions of the Franchise Act is to give greater security of tenure to companies and individuals marketing motor fuel by retail; to that end the legislation regulates certain "franchise agreements" in relation to their duration, renewal and termination, the assignment of rights and interests thereunder, and other aspects of the relationship between franchisor and franchisee: Caltex Oil (Australia) Pty Limited v Best [1990] HCA 53; (1990) 170 CLR 516 at 523 and 526-527. What is important is that at all material times the taxpayers carried on their businesses with the protection of the regulatory regime established by the statute.

7. On 3 June 1986, Mr Wally Wehbe was charged with receiving stolen distillate at the Chullora and Bankstown service stations. On 18 November 1986, a magistrate sitting in the Local Court found that there was no case to answer. The costs involved in those proceedings are not in issue in the present proceeding.

8. Michael Wehbe was incorporated on 17 October 1980 and purchased the freehold site at Bankstown from Caltex. Michael Wehbe entered into an agreement for the supply by Caltex of Caltex petroleum products for resale at the Bankstown premises, and traded there under the name "Caltex Service Station" and with Caltex signage. In 1986, upon receipt of a notice of termination of the supply contract, the company instituted a proceeding in the New South Wales District Registry of this Court (No. G258 of 1986) against Caltex. The relief sought by Michael Wehbe included an order declaring the notice of termination had no effect. The proceedings were settled on 12 August 1986. On that date, the Court dismissed the application and noted an agreement of the parties that the supply contract would remain in force until 31 August, whereupon Caltex would remove from the premises all advertising signs and other property of Caltex.

9. Whilst the proceeding had been on foot, Mr Wally Wehbe had negotiated on behalf of Michael Wehbe an agreement with BP Australia Limited for the supply of petroleum products for resale at the Bankstown service station. This contract commenced in September 1986 after the termination, as described above, of the relationship under which Michael Wehbe had traded as a "Caltex Service Station".

10. The evidence disclosed no business activities by either taxpayer other than in relation to the respective service stations at Chullora and Bankstown.

11. The litigation between Wehbe and Caltex, concerning the Chullora premises, took a more complicated course, thereby generating greater liability for costs. Between August 1977 and October 1982, Mr Wally Wehbe had been lessee and sole trader operating a Caltex service station at the Chullora premises. Wehbe was incorporated in 1981. It commenced trading in October 1982 as a service station operator at the Chullora premises. These were held under a lease from Caltex. There was also a supply agreement for Caltex petroleum products for resale at the Chullora service station. This was dated 15 October 1982. On 4 December 1985, Wehbe entered into a new lease with Caltex. This provided for a term of 3 years to terminate on 30 September 1988. Also on 4 December 1985, Wehbe entered into a fresh supply contract with Caltex for the supply and sale of petroleum products at the Chullora service station. The term of the supply contract was for a term of 3 years commencing on 1 October 1985. Wehbe carried on business at Chullora as "Caltex Service Station" and under Caltex signage.

12. On 10 June 1986, a week after Mr Wally Wehbe had been charged, Caltex issued, in respect of the Chullora service station, a notice of termination and notice to quit, addressed to Wehbe. A notice of termination also was issued to Michael Wehbe in respect of the Bankstown service station. After representations by the solicitors for Wehbe, Caltex "revoked" the notices of 10 June 1986. But fresh notices were issued on 23 July.

13. The Franchise Act provides that a franchisor may terminate a franchise agreement in accordance with the provisions of s. 16 but not otherwise. One of the permitted grounds of termination (para. 16 (2) (c)) is that the franchisee has performed an act which constitutes an offence punishable, in the case of a franchisee which is a body corporate, by a fine of $500 or more. Another (para. 16 (2) (d)) is that in connection with the premises the franchisee has performed an act or omitted to perform an act or made a statement (other than an act, omission or statement referred to in para. (c)) where the act or omission or the making of the statement constitutes "a serious contravention of a provision of any law". In the notice to Wehbe, Caltex relied upon 9 grounds, the first 8 of which were supported by para. (c) and the last by para. (d).

14. The Federal Court proceeding (No. NG357 of 1986) by Wehbe, which was heard with two other proceedings (Nos. NG358 of 1986 and NG422 of 1986), went to trial over 7 days in October 1986. A written judgment of 68 pages was delivered on 24 December 1986. The Court held that grounds 1, 2 3 and 5 in the notice relied upon by Caltex had been made out and that it was just and equitable, having regard to all the circumstances, that the relevant franchise agreements be terminated. These grounds were concerned, broadly, with the receipt by Wehbe, the franchisee, of stolen distillate at the Chullora service station. An appeal to the Full Court of this Court was dismissed on 12 May 1987. The primary Judge had described Mr Wally Wehbe as being "in effect . . . the alter ego" of Wehbe. The business of Wehbe at the Chullora site thus was brought to an end.

15. Counsel for the taxpayers put on two bases their case in support of the allowance of deductions for the legal expenses. First, he submitted that the dominant purpose of incurring the expenditure was "to defend the reputation" of the taxpayers. He pointed to the close involvement of Mr Wally Wehbe of the affairs of both companies and invited the Court to hold that it was the charging of Mr Wehbe on 3 June 1986 with the receipt of stolen distillate which precipitated the taking of steps by Caltex to terminate the supply contracts and the lease of the Chullora service station. Counsel submitted that the exigencies of the businesses of each of the taxpayers imperatively demanded that the costs of the Federal Court proceedings should be incurred. He referred, in particular to what was said by Dixon CJ in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Snowden and Willson Proprietary Limited [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431 at 437, and by Brennan J and by Deane and Fisher JJ in Magna Alloys and Research Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1980] FCA 150; (1980) 49 FLR 183 at 197, 211.

16. On the other hand, counsel for the Commissioner submits that whatever might have been the position as to legal costs incurred in relation to the criminal proceedings against Mr Wally Wehbe which were dismissed on 18 November 1986, what was involved in the present dispute was the costs incurred in relation to the Federal Court proceedings. Whilst the alleged involvement of Mr Wally Wehbe, and the two taxpayers, in dealings in stolen distillate presented issues in the Federal Court litigation, what was at stake was much more than criticism of the manner in which the taxpayers were conducting their businesses. Counsel contended that what was at stake in the litigation under the Franchise Act was the right and ability of the taxpayers to continue their businesses with use of Caltex signage and product supply and, also, in the case of Chullora, possession of the site itself. Looked at in that way, the issue of allowance of the deductions turned upon the question whether the outgoings could be described as periodic or arising in the ordinary course of business, and to maintain the existing supply of trading stock or, on the other hand, as incurred to protect the right and capacity of the taxpayers to continue the businesses by use of the existing commercial structure or organisation.

17. I agree that the issue in these matters is to be characterised in this way and, accordingly, I reject the first limb of the submissions for the taxpayer.

18. On the footing that the issues in the litigation were to be characterised as I have done, counsel for the taxpayers submitted that in the case of Michael Wehbe it was significant that after termination of the supply contract with Caltex, the business continued at the same site under arrangements with an alternative supplier, namely BP Australia Limited. Counsel submitted that this indicated that the legal expenditure had not been met in defending Michael Wehbe from action which would necessarily have had the effect of terminating its business as service station operator at the Bankstown site.

19. Whilst, in the events that happened, the business of Wehbe at the Chullora site was brought to an end with the termination both of the supply contract and the lease with Caltex, counsel for the taxpayers submitted that at the time the expenditure was incurred, it was not necessarily the position that the steps being taken by Caltex and resisted by Wehbe necessarily would result in the termination of the business. This was said to be because it was open to Wehbe to continue in business, at least on another site, with an alternative supplier. This was not a case where the expenditure went to "the heart and nature of the business enterprise itself", and therefore was of a capital nature; the petroleum products sold by the applicants were not such that the taxpayers could have any monopoly even as to a geographic area and, counsel said (but without pointing to any evidence), the products of one supplier was "by and large" a direct substitute for those of another. Implicit in these submissions was a broad characterization of the businesses of the taxpayers. It will be necessary to return to the question of characterization after referring to certain authorities.

20. Counsel for the taxpayers sought to distinguish the situation which was presented in the present litigation from that dealt with by Davies J in Sunraysia Broadcasting Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 91 ATC 4530 and by Hill J in Smithkline Beecham Laboratories (Australia) Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1993] FCA 389; (1993) 116 ALR 503.

21. In the first of those cases, the taxpayer operated a commercial AM radio station which enjoyed a monopoly on radio broadcasting in the Mildura area. The taxpayer, fearing the establishment of an independent commercial FM station in the area would interfere with its business, applied to the Australian Broadcasting Tribunal for a supplementary FM radio licence. Davies J upheld the decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal which found that expenditure on the application to the Australian Broadcasting Tribunal was on capital account. His Honour said, at 4532:

"This was a clear case where the expenditure

went to the heart and nature of the business

enterprise. I would accept that, in Sunraysia's

view, the expenditure was designed to protect

the business enterprise rather than to gain an

additional benefit. But Sunraysia considered

that the business enterprise as it had been

carried on since 1933 was at risk. Its

expenditure was therefore capital expenditure .

. . The expenditure was not such as might be

expected from year to year in the carrying on

and maintenance of the business enterprise. In

its crucial finding, the Tribunal referred to

the words of Dixon J in Sun Newspapers (1938)

[1938] HCA 72; 61 CLR 337 at 364:

'. . . in principle the transaction must

be regarded as strengthening and

preserving the business organisation or

entity, the profit yielding subject, and

affecting the capital structure.'"

See also Broken Hill Theatres Proprietary Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 75; (1952) 85 CLR 423 at 431, 434.

22. In Smithkline, the taxpayer unsuccessfully claimed to deduct expenses of two legal proceedings in which it sought to achieve a result whereby in deciding whether to approve the importation by a competitor of the taxpayer of products containing cimetidine, the relevant Australian regulatory authority would be precluded from using in its evaluation process information which had been disclosed to it by the taxpayer. Hill J found that the purpose of the taxpayer in commencing and prosecuting the proceedings was a desire to exclude, so far as possible, competitors from gaining approval of competing cimetidine products. Expressed as a percentage of the total Australian sales of the taxpayer, cimetidine represented 33.26% in 1987, 22.95% in 1988, 16.35% in 1989 and 18.18% in 1990. His Honour held that cimetidine was a significant product in the applicant's business and that loss of its monopoly would have a quite significant impact upon it.

23. Hill J pointed out that (i) where the expenditure sought to be deducted is incurred in the course of litigation, what is required is consideration of the purpose of the taxpayer in undertaking the litigation, (ii) expenditure incurred to preserve or to protect a business "as such" ordinarily will be an expenditure of capital, (iii) where the purpose of the litigation is to stave off a loss which would be fatal, causing the business to cease, the costs expended will be on capital account; this may also be the case if the object of the expenditure is to preserve the business from impairment and dislocation; (iv) legal costs will be on revenue account if incurred in defending the taxpayer and its officers from criticism of the taxpayer's methods of trading, (v) for an expenditure to be on capital account it is not necessary for a capital asset to be acquired, and (vi) had the litigation by Smithkline been successful, it was highly likely that the advantage sought of freeing the taxpayer from competition in respect of cimetidine would have been virtually permanent because without access to the information in question the possibility of the regulatory authority being able satisfactorily to evaluate generic products would have been quite remote.

24. His Honour concluded (at 513):

"By what was once and for all expenditure the

applicant hoped to preserve its goodwill in the

product, if not forever, certainly for a

substantial length of time. The expenditure to

enable this to happen was for that reason, in my

opinion, not expenditure on maintenance, as

submitted by (the taxpayer), but expenditure in

relation to the very business structure of (the

taxpayer) itself and thus expenditure of capital."

25. In Fletcher v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1991] HCA 42; (1991) 173 CLR 1 at 17, in a joint judgment of all members of the High Court, it was said:

"The question whether an outgoing was, for the

purposes of s. 51 (1), wholly or partly

'incurred in gaining or producing the assessable

income' is a question of characterization. The

relationship between the outgoing and the

assessable income must be such as to impart to

the outgoing the character of an outgoing of the

relevant kind. It has been pointed out on many

occasions in the cases that an outgoing will not

properly be characterized as having been

incurred in gaining or producing assessable

income unless it was 'incidental and relevant to

that end'."

Their Honours also (at 18-19) cited the following well known passage from the dissenting judgment of Dixon J in Hallstroms Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634 at 648:

"What is an outgoing of capital and what is an

outgoing on account of revenue depends on what

the expenditure is calculated to effect from a

practical and business point of view, rather

than upon the juristic classification of the

legal rights, if any, secured, employed or

exhausted in the process."

26. In applying to the present proceedings the principles discussed in the authorities, it is important to return to the question of characterization. In that regard, one should begin with an appreciation of the nature of the business structure or enterprise of the taxpayers, and the role therein of the arrangements with Caltex, the continuation of which was at stake in the Federal Court litigation.

27. I referred to the lease dated 4 December 1985 of the Chullora premises. The subject matter of the lease included the land and tanks, pumps, lines and electrical accessories installed in or about the land. An elaborate provision was made as to signage. Clause 9 provided:

"9. The Lessor (Caltex) may (but shall not be obligated so

to do) from time to time and at all reasonable times

enter upon the leased premises and at its cost paint

the exterior or interior of the leased buildings or

any part thereof in the colours and designs it has

adopted from time to time as its standard colours and

designs for identification of premises as a 'Caltex

Service Station' and at its cost may install fix paint

or erect on or about the leased premises (or any part

thereof) any sign (electrical or otherwise),

advertisement, notice or placard which identifies or

promotes the petroleum products marketed by the Lessor

as it thinks fit and may repair, replace, remove or

alter any such sign, advertisement, notice or placard

and the Lessee will not take down remove cover

relocate alter deface detract from or obliterate any

such designs colours or other forms of advertisement

without the prior consent of the Lessor which consent

shall not be unreasonably or unlawfully withheld and

may be given subject to any conditions which may be

lawfully imposed by the Lessor. The Lessee may

display on or about the leased premises signs and

other matter which identifies or advertises any goods

or services which the Lessee desires to sell in the

course of the said business (and any other business

approved by the Lessor)."

The phrase "the said business" is defined in cl. 7 as being the conduct and operation of an automobile service station business. Subject to cl. 9, Caltex was obliged (by cl. 20) to provide such advertising facilities as the parties might from time to time mutually consider appropriate or useful to assist Wehbe sell the petroleum products supplied to it by Caltex; Wehbe was licensed by cl. 20 "to use all copyrighted advertising matter" which Caltex might from time to time furnish to Wehbe or install or affix on or about the licensed premises. It was a condition of the lease (cl. 22) that Wehbe perform and observe the terms of the supply contract as if the same were set out at length in the lease.

28. The supply contract between Wehbe and Caltex obliged Caltex to sell and deliver to Wehbe at the Chullora premises and obliged Wehbe to purchase from Caltex, such quantities of Caltex petroleum products as were necessary to satisfy customer demand for Caltex petroleum products at the Chullora service station during the term of the agreement (cl. 1). This was subject to a proviso that Wehbe would buy a minimum of 50% of its total monthly requirements of petrol and distillate for resale from Caltex, and to the further proviso that Caltex would not be bound to supply more than such quantity of petrol and distillate as were reasonably required from time to time by Wehbe for resale at the service station.

29. Wehbe was obliged (cl. 5) to promote the sale of Caltex petroleum products and place the dispensing equipment, displays and advertisements for Caltex petroleum products and brands in prominent and convenient positions on the service station. Further provision for signage was made in cl. 6, as follows:

"6. All signs or material advertising Caltex petroleum

products and all signs in the colours used by Caltex

to identify its products or the places at which its

products are sold which are installed or displayed at

the service station and all of Caltex Trade Mark

rights therein are, and they shall continue to be, the

property of Caltex. Such signs and material shall be

used by (Wehbe) only in connection with the

advertising or promotion of Caltex petroleum products.

Caltex shall have the right, at all times during the

term of this Agreement to remove replace or obliterate

any such signs or material. (Wehbe) agrees during

this agreement and thereafter, not to simulate in any

way any trade marks or brands, signs or paint schemes

identifying Caltex petroleum products or the places or

outlets where they are sold or marketed."

30. Clearly then, the documents constituting the franchise agreements with Wehbe were vitally concerned not only with supply of Caltex product but also with Caltex signage; cf EM-ES Petroleum Pty Limited v BP Australia Limited (1988) 22 FCR 149. The same was true of possession of the Chullora site by Wehbe.

31. The evidence does not disclose the terms of the supply agreement between Michael Wehbe and Caltex, relating to the Bankstown freehold site. However, it was not suggested that the supply agreement was, in its significant respects, relevantly different from that dealing with the Chullora site. The result is that here also matters of signage and supply were of paramount importance.

32. The effect, in the case of both taxpayers, was that pursuant to the arrangements constituting the franchise agreements which were protected by the Franchise Act, the taxpayers were supplied with the necessary means to carry on a business as Caltex service stations at the Chullora and Bankstown sites. Neither taxpayer, at the time of the dispute with Caltex, had carried on business as other than a Caltex service station at the relevant site. The Bankstown site had been purchased by Michael Wehbe from Caltex. The lease and supply agreement between Caltex and Wehbe were, as I have indicated, each for a term of 3 years.

33. The substantive provisions of the Petroleum Retail Marketing Franchise Amendment Act 1984 ("the 1984 Act") came into force on 1 January 1985. The 1984 Act introduced into the Franchise Act the complex provisions of ss. 17, 17A and 17B. These dealt with renewal of franchise agreements in a way which, subject to important qualifications, entrenched the tenure of franchisees for a maximum period of 9 years; see the discussion of these provisions by the Full Court in Ranoa Pty Limited v BP Oil Distribution Limited (1989) 91 ALR 251, Esso Australia Limited v R.T. and M.I. Abela Pty Limited [1989] FCA 523; (1989) 91 ALR 476. That for which protection was sought by the litigation in this Court included the rights given the taxpayers by the 1984 Act.

34. Counsel for the Commissioner submitted that the connections between the taxpayers and Caltex were a vital part of their respective business structures as they existed in 1986, and that when Caltex sought to bring that situation to an end, the taxpayers could do nothing else but surrender or incur expenditure in defending their businesses by litigation, relying upon their rights under the Franchise Act. Only by doing so might they sustain and continue their businesses at the Chullora and Bankstown sites (the taxpayers having no other businesses) as Caltex service stations; cf Snowden and Willson supra at 437. This expenditure was not of a recurrent nature.

35. In a sense, one might broadly have described the businesses of the taxpayers as that of service station operator. But the arrangements with Caltex in each case gave the taxpayer the vital means for the conduct of such a business. This was not merely the supply of product but the means of visible association with the goodwill and reputation of a major player in the petroleum industry. The business which existed under the aegis of the relevant agreements and the protection of the Franchise Act were two Caltex Service Stations and it was the severe threat to the continuation thereof which precipitated the litigation in this Court.

36. Indeed, in the case of Chullora, what was at stake was the occupation of the site itself. Here Caltex had set about bringing to an end any business association between the taxpayer and that site on which its only operations were conducted. It is true that the Bankstown site itself was not in jeopardy and that arrangements later were made with BP Australia Limited. But the business of the taxpayer as a Caltex Service Station was in jeopardy and the object of the expenditure here was, at the very least, to preserve that business and thereby avoid the need to replace it with another service station business.

37. Accordingly, the expenditures were of a capital nature, being in relation to the very business structures of the taxpayers. The application in each of the three matters should be dismissed with costs.
