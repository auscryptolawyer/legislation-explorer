TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re JAMES GLEN SERVICE

Applicant

And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

Tribunal J Block, Senior Member

Date 19 February 1999

Decision The objection decisions under review are set aside.

CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - Derivation of directors' and other fees - where fees derived by employee and whether on behalf of company - whether constructive trust arises - employee acting as agent for undisclosed principal - deductions under first limb of section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975

REASONS FOR DECISION

19 February 1999 J Block, Senior Member

1. The objection decisions in this matter relate to the disallowance by the Respondent of objections against amended assessments for the years ending 30 June 1993 to 30 June 1996, both years inclusive, (collectively the "relevant years" and each "a relevant year").

2. The Applicant was represented by Mr S. W. McMillan of counsel, instructed by Speed & Stracey; Mr Roger Gormly, an officer of the Respondent, appeared for the Respondent.

3. The Tribunal had before it the T Documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act; it also had the following exhibits:

* A box file containing Exhibits A1, A2, A3 and A4 and being statements by, respectively, the Applicant, his wife Dorothy Service ("Dorothy" or "his wife"), James Glen Service Jnr ("James") and Ms K. E. Mitchell ("Mitchell"). The Respondent did not require Mitchell for cross-examination and accordingly her statement was accepted. It is to be noted that Mitchell's statement as produced to the Tribunal was not accompanied by the annexures referred to in clauses 8 and 9, and the Tribunal accepts that these annexures were not necessary for the purposes of the hearing. The box file also contains another statement (in Tab 5) by Mr G B Parker. The Tribunal was advised that the Applicant did not intend to tender that evidence which thus became irrelevant.

* Exhibit A5 which is a box file entitled "Applicant's Bundle"; Exhibit A5 consists of a large number of documents; Tab 1 contains Rulings; Tab 2 contains income tax returns for the Applicant for the years 1987 to 1996; Tab 3 contains income tax returns for the Company (referred to in clause 4(a)) for the years 1989 to 1996 and Tab 4 contains financial reports for the Company for the years 1992 to 1996.

4. (a) J.G. Service Pty Limited (the "Company") is a company which commenced business in Canberra in 1981 as property consultants and property managers. The Company was originally the trustee of a trust established for the benefit of the Applicant and his family; his family, in this context, includes his four children (being three sons and a daughter, Catherine) and also his parents, both of whom are now deceased.

(b) The original employees of the Company were the Applicant and his wife and daughter, Catherine.

(c) In 1986 the business of the Company was incorporated; this was achieved (according to the evidence of the Applicant) through a transfer of the business from the Company in its capacity as trustee, to the Company in its personal capacity, so that the Company, having previously owned the business legally only, thereafter owned it legally and beneficially. The Applicant said that the purchase price was calculated so as to take into account the fact that at that time the income of the business included certain directors' fees. Another company was then appointed as trustee of the family trust.

5. The managing director and chief executive of the Company was originally the Applicant. He acted as such from inception and in particular during the relevant years. In 1996 he was succeeded in these offices by James, but continues as a director and as the Company's executive chairman.

6. Specifically in respect of the Company:

) Its business has grown considerably over the years; it has expanded geographically and now has offices in Sydney, Melbourne, Perth and Brisbane. It has, for some time, been involved in extensive operations in the Northern Territory. It has a staff of approximately 30, most of whom are professional staff. The gross turnover of the Company is now in the region of $5 million annually.

(b) There are 10 issued shares in the capital of the Company. The Applicant owns six shares, while each of his three sons and his wife own one share. The board of directors of the Company consists of the Applicant, his wife and the three sons.

(c) The Company's activities extend, in broad terms, to two inter-related fields, being project development management and property consultancy.

(d) The Company describes its skills (T98 and T99) as concentrated in the following areas:

* "Property market behaviour and trends;

* Property investment analysis;

* Risk management and portfolio design;

* Identification of opportunities and value added analysis;

* Strategies for property realisation;

* Oversighting of and advising in sale or acquisition techniques;

* Financing strategies and techniques;

* Assessment of client needs/objectives and facility planning;

* Preparation of occupancy strategies, for tenants or owner/occupiers, in both commercial and operating terms;

* Commercial aspects of property, construction and consultancy contracts;

* Creation of project opportunities best meeting client objectives;

* Construction techniques, buildability, cost/value relationships, quality control, budget management;

* Management of the design process for projects, and of the consultants involved;

* Management of the process of project completion - from the calling of tenders to the obtaining of final authority certificates;

* Rescue and regeneration of projects in difficulties."

7. The decision to incorporate the business in 1986 was taken primarily to ensure that those members of the family actively engaged in it would benefit from its growth. Catherine did not become a shareholder in the Company, because she was no longer involved in the business; however, the Applicant explained that he has drawn his will (and that Catherine is aware of its provisions) so as to take these factors into account.

8. Oral evidence was given by each of the Applicant, his wife and James. I note, by way of preface, that the evidence of each of the Applicant and his wife was impressive and altogether worthy of credit; the evidence of James was perhaps less impressive and indeed there were a number of inconsistencies between the evidence of the Applicant and his wife on the one hand and James on the other. Some of those inconsistencies are explicable in the light of the lapse of many years since the events in question occurred. Insofar as there are any such inconsistencies, the Tribunal accepts the evidence of the Applicant and his wife. The statement of Mitchell confirms the evidence of the Applicant and his wife in certain respects.

9. There is no written contract of service between the Applicant and the Company; this is not surprising when one considers that we are here concerned with a business which is, and always has been, a family business. It is clear though that from the inception of the business, the Applicant was employed on a full-time basis, to devote his full time and attention to the affairs of the Company and that he received remuneration in respect of his services which, during the relevant years, was $80,000 per annum.

10. ) By 1984 all three sons were employed in the business. In that year, the Applicant was invited to accept office as a member of the Canberra Development Board, a governmental body in the Australian Capital Territory. The evidence of the Applicant is that there was then a family discussion as to whether that appointment (and if relevant similar appointments offered in the future) should be accepted. The relevant factors requiring consideration included the fact that acceptance of the appointment would require the devotion of time by the Applicant (thus detracting from his time available to the Company) and the benefits which might be derived from acceptance.

(b) There is some degree of discrepancy in respect of the oral evidence on this point as to which members of the family were present at that discussion. Dorothy said that her late father-in-law, she herself, her husband and all of the children, with a possible exception of her middle son, Adrian, were present. The Applicant thought that all three sons (and other family members) were present. James said that of the three sons only he was present. James' evidence also was that the first board offer in 1984 was made by Civic Permanent Building Society. The Tribunal accepts the first offer was indeed made by Canberra Development Board; it accepts moreover that there was a family discussion as to whether it should be accepted notwithstanding that there was some uncertainty as to who precisely was present at that meeting in 1984.

11. () The family discussion resulted in a decision to accept the offer notwithstanding its requirements as to the time of the Applicant. It was felt that membership of the Canberra Development Board would bring the Applicant into close contact with senior government officers and bureaucrats, and that these contacts could not be other than beneficial to the Company. The evidence was that it was agreed furthermore, that any and all fees derived should accrue to the Company; it was also agreed that any offers received thereafter would be dealt with in the same way but subject, in the case of each such offer, to a consideration of it and a discussion as to whether acceptance of the appointment would be beneficial to the Company. Each subsequent board appointment was in fact accepted after a discussion within the family.

(b) Having accepted the offer of membership of the Canberra Development Board, other important board and similar appointments were offered and accepted in ensuing years. Some of those appointments were to boards of companies (including Advance Bank); others related to government bodies such as the ACT Board of Health and the Canberra Theatre Trust. Some of those appointments were to boards of directors; in some instances the boards were not boards of directors but rather boards of management. I use the term "directors' fees" to embrace all of the fees paid by all of the various companies and bodies concerned.

(c) The Applicant gave evidence that in respect of each appointment he was concerned to ensure that there were adequate insurance arrangements and said, moreover, that it was anticipated, or at least hoped, that any inadequacy in any such insurance arrangements might be covered through the Company's own professional indemnity cover.

(d) During the relevant years the director's fees in question were (as set out in the Respondent's Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions) as follows:

"1993 1994 1995 1996

Managerial & Financial Services $79,881 $87,500 $109,938 $136,198

Territory Insurance Office $15,424 $25,000 $25,000 $9,615

Canberra Centre Holdings $10,000 $10,000 $14,000 $5,000

ACT Government $3,082 $18,596 $21,184 $17,328

ACTEW $14,923

ACT Board of Health $7,520

Advance Asset Management $3,257

Canberra Theatre Trust $557 $934 $487

Building Owners & Managers $90

TOTAL $119,254 $141,653 $171,056 $183,551"

(e) Manager and Financial Services is, in fact, referable to Advance Bank. The Territory Insurance Office relates to the Northern Territory and in which Territory the Company was involved in extensive contracts, primarily referable to telecommunications.

(f) In respect of some of the appointments previously referred to, the Applicant was appointed chairman. By way of example, page 40 of the T Documents, which is an extract of minutes of a meeting of the Board of Directors of Advance Bank Australia Limited, notes in paragraph (b) that "the fees payable to Messrs J.G. Service and R.M. Whyte being adjusted to reflect actual service in respect of their occupation of the positions of Chairman and Director respectively"; again, page 46 of the T Documents, also an extract from minutes of a meeting of the Board of Directors of Advance Bank Australia Limited, contains the following resolution under the heading "Directors' Fees - BankSA":

"Resolved that the increase in the remuneration payable by the Company and its subsidiaries to the Company's Directors by a total of $50,000 per annum, as approved at the Annual General Meeting of Shareholders held on 31st August 1995, be allocated as to $25,000 per annum each to Messrs J.G. Service and K.A. Meyer as compensation for their additional obligations as directors of BankSA until that company is formally merged into the Company."

12. () It is not necessary for me to go into detail as to the evidence referable to the various appointments; the applicant gave evidence as to the relevance of the appointments to the Company's business. It is likely that the Applicant, who is a man of very considerable ability (and who was awarded an AM), had the expertise and experience (and probably also the diplomatic and personal relations skills) which led to his appointments to boards of important bodies and companies. It is also very likely that those appointments were of benefit, albeit indirectly, to the Company which has expanded and prospered over the years.

(b) The Applicant is undoubtedly a leader. But there is no evidence to suggest that his was the only voice in relation to the Company. It may well have been the most important voice; it may also be that his desires were not likely to be overruled; however, his wife in particular impressed me as a person of decided character, and by no means to be characterised as a cypher. James' evidence differed in some respects from that of his parents; those discrepancies do not in my view discredit the evidence of his parents; as I have previously indicated, discrepancies are only to be expected when the period which has elapsed is so long.

(c) The Canberra Theatre Trust appointment falls outside (to some extent) the nature of the other appointments. The Applicant's evidence was that, although he is not a keen theatre goer, he (and his family) considered it desirable that he give the Canberra Theatre Trust (when requested to do so) the benefit of his financial and business expertise, more particularly because some of the other members were eminent persons in senior positions with whom contact would be highly desirable. The Canberra Theatre Trust was, as is the case with so many bodies of a similar nature, in financial difficulties. As set out previously, the Tribunal accepts that the appointments were likely to benefit, and most probably did in fact benefit, the Company; the Tribunal does not by this mean to suggest that any of such contacts were used in any way which was not proper.

13. Another discrepancy arose as to the manner in which director's fees were paid. The Applicant and his wife said that cheques were made payable to the Applicant and endorsed by him in favour of the Company. Moreover and insofar as PAYE was deducted from any directors' fees, the Applicant accounted for the sums deducted through debits to his loan account against the Company; that loan account was adequate for this purpose. James' evidence was that cheques were made payable to the Company in that the payers were aware of the fact that the Company was entitled to the director's fees. The statement by Mitchell sets out in clause 8 that "The bank statements of the Company recording payments to its bank accounts of the cheques payable to Mr Service ----". Similarly clause 9 of that statement sets out that "On most occasions tax was deducted by the government authorities and companies prior to forwarding a cheque to Mr Service." Her statement confirms also that all fees were treated and recorded in the books of the Company as its income. The Tribunal accepts that the cheques were drawn in the manner stated by the Applicant and his wife; that PAYE was deducted indicates that this was so. As to whether the various boards were aware of the Applicant's obligation to account to the Company for the director's fees is unclear; the probability is that they were not aware or were not concerned; the modern practice pursuant to which members of various organisations render services in circumstances where they are obliged to account for the fees derived is now commonplace. Nor is it by any means confined to directorships or similar appointments. Academics may be obliged to account to their universities for fees derived for lectures or articles. Indeed, that the Respondent is aware that this is so appears from certain Rulings; in this regard:

) Paragraph 3 of IT2319 reads:

"The director's fees paid by a subsidiary company form part of the assessable income of the director. Where, however, the director is required to pay over all or part of those fees to the parent company as a condition of his employment or appointment, the amount so paid represent losses or outgoings incurred in gaining or producing the director's assessable income."

(b) Paragraph 3 of IT2321 which deals with "fees paid to part-time members of Governmental Tribunals, committees etc - requirement on member to pay fees into partnership account" reads as follows:

"It has been decided that, to the extent that a partner is properly required, in accordance with the terms of a partnership agreement, to pay the sitting fees into a partnership account, it is not appropriate to require deduction of tax instalments from the sitting fees".

(c) TD93/225 provides in paragraph 4 as follows:

"This Taxation Determination does not alter the policy which is outlined in paragraph 3 of Taxation Ruling IT 2319. Director's fees are considered to be derived by the individual director and form part of his/her assessable income. Where the director is required to pay over all or part of those fees to the parent company as a condition of his/her employment or appointment, the amount so paid represent losses or outgoings incurred in gaining or producing the director's assessable income."

TD93/225 applies in a related company situation, that is, where the director is also an employee of a related company and it is a condition of employment or appointment that the director's fees be paid by the director to the related company; that determination, does not apply in the circumstances of this matter;

(d) Taxation Determination TD97/2 applies where director's fees are included in the assessable income of a director who is a partner in a professional partnership and who must pass on those fees to the partnership as required by the partnership agreement. The determination commences with paragraph 1 which reads:

"Generally, no. However, this Taxation Determination, which applies only to partners in professional partnerships such as accounting or legal firms, should be read in conjunction with Taxation Ruling IT 2639.

Paragraph 2 then goes on to say:

"It is common practice for a partner in a professional partnership to be appointed as director of a client company or a public company. The appointment is usually considered to be another service provided by a professional partnership or an extension of the usual business activities of the partnership, and is made because of the partner's connection with the partnership."

TD97/2 similarly, because it relates only to partnerships, does not apply.

14. ) Paragraph 9 of the Applicant's statement reads as follows:

"It was a condition of my employment with the Company that part of my working time as an employee be spent on its behalf in providing services in various capacities to a number of government authorities and companies."

(b) Paragraph 5 of Dorothy's statement reads as follows:

"It was a condition of his employment that part of his working time as an employee of the Company be spent on its behalf in providing services in various capacities to a number of government authorities and companies. It was also a condition of his employment that all fees paid by such government authorities and companies belonged to the Company and not to him."

(c) Clause 6 of James' statement corresponds with clause 5 of Dorothy's statement.

It is clear that all three statements were drawn by the same draftsman. In fact, of course, the statements are not accurate; when the Applicant first became the chief executive officer of the Company, appointments of the nature referred to were not in issue. The evidence before the Tribunal is clear; the business was started in 1981, at a time when there were no such appointments. Appointments of this nature became relevant only after the first offer was received in 1984. Mr Gormly in his cross-examination of Dorothy put it to her that the words in her statement were not her own words; she accepted, perfectly honestly, that they were not. The Tribunal does not think that anything turns on the fact that the statements were prepared by professional advisers rather than the persons actually making them; that is, after all, part of the function of professional advisers engaged in the preparation of a case such as this and is, in fact, the usual method by which statements are prepared. Nor does it consider that the inaccuracy referred to, detracts from the truthful nature of evidence given by the Applicant and his wife.

15. The arrangements made in 1984 in respect of the contract between the Applicant and the Company could be categorised as alterations or amendments to the original contract, or simply as an amplification of the original contract to cater for a set of circumstances not previously contemplated. Nothing turns on this; it is logical in the view of the Tribunal that where the Company is entitled to the full-time services of an employee, it is entitled to any fees referable to any services provided by its own employee utilising the time which properly belongs to the Company.

16. As a director of Advance Bank, the Applicant received 50,000 options in Advance Bank. Those options in turn resulted, in accordance with the Applicant's evidence, in a substantial profit. James referred to the options, in the course of his evidence as remuneration options. The Applicant said that it was decided that the options should go into the family trust; his wife confirmed that this occurred. It would seem that within the family, a distinction was drawn between director's fees and the options, notwithstanding that they were received by the Applicant as a director and thus constituted a benefit to him in that capacity. See in this context page 41 of the T Documents from which it appears that the Applicant and eight other directors of Advance Bank all received "up to 50,000 options".

17. The manner in which the Advance Bank options were dealt with may have been influenced by considerations of tax but there was no evidence before me as to what was realised and what the exact tax implications were. It was suggested by Mr Gormly that tax was a consideration as to the manner in which the directors' fees were dealt with; it is possible that tax was considered; however, Part IVA of the Tax Act was not in issue before me. Nor for that matter was there any issue before me as to whether the Advance Bank options were, so far as the Company was concerned, correctly dealt with.

18. During the relevant years, the Applicant derived a salary of $80,000 per annum; in addition the Company, according to the Applicant's evidence, paid substantial amounts in respect of his superannuation and moreover provided him with what he described as "an expensive motor car".

19. Having made it clear that I accept the evidence of the Applicant and his wife as truthful and not in any way adversely affected by any of the discrepancies to which I have referred previously, I turn now to consider the law.

20. ) During the course of the hearing I expressed the view that this matter is in all probability one in respect of which deduction is a major issue, and this then is a convenient starting point. Assuming then that it is correct to say that the Applicant derived the director's fees beneficially (and that section 19 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("Tax Act") applies) it is then necessary to determine whether the Applicant is entitled to a corresponding deduction under the first limb of section 51(1) of the Tax Act, which allows a deduction in respect of losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income". As I indicated previously, I have accepted the existence of a contract between the Applicant and the Company pursuant to which the Applicant was contractually bound to account to the Company for the director's fees. The first limb refers in its terms to "the assessable income"; (emphasis added). There is case authority which would tend, in the words of Professor R.W. Parsons in "Income Taxation in Australia" (clause 5.30) "to read the word 'the' so far as possible out of the first limb of section 51" (and Professor Parsons refers in this context to a number of decided cases). But there is no issue in this case as to whether the word "the" should be read down. What then is the "the assessable income" which is relevant for this purpose? As I understood his argument, Mr Gormly submitted that the income in question is the Applicant's salary from the Company, and thus excluding the superannuation payments and the provision of the expensive motor car, neither of which would constitute assessable income. Mr Gormly argued, in effect, that the arrangement made in 1984 did not result in any increase in the Applicant's salary, which was payable under the antecedent arrangement made when the business of the Company started. Put succinctly, and as I understood his argument, the payment over of the directors' fees was not incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, which was derived in any event. There was no evidence before me that the Applicant in 1984, or thereafter, stipulated for an increase in his remuneration from the Company in consideration of the directorial duties undertaken by him and productive of the directors' fees. It is my view that Mr Gormly's argument is mistaken, in that it ignores the directors' fees themselves, which on this basis must constitute a part of the Applicant's assessable income. In effect, the correct analysis is that the Applicant sought the Company's acquiescence to enable him to take up of the appointments; the Company in turn granted that acquiescence conditionally upon the payment over of the directors' fees. It follows then that the directors' fees could not have been derived without that acquiescence and so that relevance and nexus is clearly established. (TD93/225, to which I have referred, contemplates just such a procedure.)

(b) The returns of the Applicant should correctly, on this basis, have been prepared so as to reflect the directors' fees derived and the corresponding deduction of the same aggregate amount. They were not; however, Exhibit A5 indicates clearly that the Commissioner was aware, having regard to the returns of the Applicant included in Exhibit A5 for years preceding the relevant years, that he derived directors' fees for which he accounted to the Company. Tab 2 of Exhibit A5 which contains copies of the Applicant's tax returns, indicates in clear terms that the director's fees were included in the income of the family trust or the Company. For example, an "Assessing Information Report" in respect of 1985 reads:

"fees received from Canberra Development Board and Civic Co-Op are included in the net income of the J G Service Family Trust. Said income is not to be included in the TXP RTN. Credit for TID however should be allowed to the TXP."

And again by way of example, and in respect of 1992, there is a note which reads:

"The circumstances in respect of amounts shown in group certificates by Building Owners & Managers Assoc, Canberra Advance Bank, Managerial & Financial Services, Advance Asset Management, ACT Board of Health and Canberra Centre Holdings are the same as in previous years. The gross income is received and included in the tax return of J G Service Pty Ltd."

The result so far as to the quantum of tax assessable to the Applicant, in any event, is precisely the same.

21. For the reasons set out in clause 20, the Applicant is entitled to succeed, and the amended assessments must be set aside. This results inevitably from my acceptance of the existence of a contract pursuant to which the Applicant was obliged to account to the Company for the directors' fees. It is not then strictly necessary for me to deal with certain other issues which arose. However, it is in my view desirable, in case this matter is taken further, and in the interests of completeness, to set out my views as to certain other arguments and contentions which were raised at the hearing; the succeeding clauses of these Reasons are included for this purpose.

22. ) During the course of the hearing Mr McMillan argued that the director's fees were derived, not by the Applicant, but by the Company. He cited in support of this, proposition, and in particular the decision of Dixon J, as he then was, in the Countess of Bective and the Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1932] HCA 22; (1932) 47 CLR 417. I refer in this context to a passage from that decision at pages 425 which reads as follows;

"By the terms of the first trust of the instrument, the trustees are required until the beneficiary attains the age of fifteen years to pay to the taxpayer the net annual income of the trust at such time and in such manner as the trustees shall determine for the maintenance, education and support of the beneficiary, and, in the event of the death of the taxpayer before the beneficiary attains that age, to pay the net annual income to the guardian or guardians of the beneficiary in manner aforesaid for the maintenance, support and education of the beneficiary, and the trustees are to be under no obligation to see to the application of the net annual income by the taxpayer, or by the guardian or guardians. It is to be noticed that, excluding this clause from consideration, the only provision contained in the settlement in favour of the taxpayer is a limitation over upon the entire failure of the trusts declared in favour of the daughter of the settlor and her possible children. The recital and the description of the daughter as "the beneficiary" establish that she was the object of the settlement. The provision, which, upon her daughter's reaching the age of fifteen, terminates the taxpayer's dispensation and requires the trustee to undertake the payment or application of the income to the beneficiary for her maintenance, education and benefit, suggests that no other reason than the tenderness of the girl's age actuated the settlor in directing payment to her mother or her guardian until that time. In the clause containing this discretion there is no difference in the forms of expression used in requiring the trustees to pay the income to the taxpayer and in requiring them to pay it to the guardian if the taxpayer should die. Upon these grounds, I conclude that the taxpayer is not a beneficial object of the provision for maintenance, but is chosen, as is the guardian after her death, as an appropriate person to dispense the income belonging to the child for her benefit. In establishing this conclusion I think the taxpayer has discharged the burden of showing that the assessment was wrong in including the entire sum received by the taxpayer under the settlement as her assessable income."

(b) Earlier in his decision, and at p 424, Dixon J noted;

"Possibly, it might be supported also if the provision were construed as a gift of income to the taxpayer subject to a charge for maintenance. But if either of these two constructions were adopted, a corresponding deduction should be allowed for expenditure upon maintenance ..."

(c) Of course, the Countess of Bective decision related to a trust instrument with a named beneficiary and is thus perhaps distinguishable from the facts in this matter, where and in respect of the directors' fees, the Company does not fall into the same category.

23. ) Mr McMillan argued also that the Company was entitled to the directors' fees through a constructive trust which arose in its favour.

(b) In Zobory v FCT (1995) 95 ATC 4251 an employee stole a substantial sum of money from his employer and Burchett J held that the interest income derived in respect of those moneys belonged, not to the employee but to the employer. (Burchett J in his judgement expressly disavowed the applicability in Australia of the "claim of right" doctrine.) Jacobs' Law of Trusts in Australia sets out at page 306:

"As Cardozo CJ put it, 'When property has been acquired in such circumstances that the holder of the legal title may not in good conscience retain the beneficial interest, equity converts him into a trustee.'"

(c) It is my view, on reflection, that Mr McMillan's argument as to the existence of a constructive trust is tenable. While Zobory (which related to stolen moneys) may perhaps be distinguishable on its facts because it related to stolen moneys, and because the stolen moneys remained the property of the employer, Canon, it is nevertheless my view that the Applicant could not in good conscience have retained the directors' fees.

I refer also in this context to the reference in the judgment of Burchett J in Zobory's case to the judgment of Deane J in Muschinski v Dodds [1985] HCA 78; (1985) 160 CLR 583 at 614 where he said:

"Equity acts consistently and in accordance with principle. The old maxim that equity regards as done that which ought to be done is as applicable to enforce equitable obligations as it is to create them and, notwithstanding that the constructive trust is remedial in both origin and nature, there does not need to have been a curial declaration or order before equity will recognize the prior existence of a constructive trust."

And again in Muschinski v Dodds, Deane J referred to a passage in Scott, Law of Trusts, reading:

"Where the title to property is acquired by one person under such circumstances that he is under a duty to surrender it, a constructive trust immediately arises ---"

24. Is it then apt to characterise the Applicant's position as that of an agent for the Company, on the basis that the Company was an undisclosed principal? It seems to me that this question must be answered in the negative. Where the Company, in this case the principal, is precluded from being a director through a specific provision of the Corporations Law (or the relevant predecessor statute) it cannot lawfully empower someone else (in this case, the Applicant, as agent) to do that which it is legally prohibited from doing. I think that in this context there is a distinction to be drawn between that which a principal is permitted to do, and that which it is legally prohibited from doing. In the former case, but not in the latter case, the principal may appoint an agent to perform an act if it is more convenient for it to do so though an agent. I note that this is not an aspect which was canvassed in any depth at the hearing, perhaps because the answer is clear; (Bowstead & Reynolds on Agency; pages 44 and 45.).

25. There was discussion during the hearing of the fact that the Applicant controlled (and still controls) the Company, because of his majority shareholding in its capital. Assuming then that he received directors' fees for which he did not account, the Company might wish to sue for recovery. The Applicant is one only of a number of directors and thus in a minority, and so that the board of directors of the Company could pass a resolution for this purpose. On the other hand, the Applicant could then use his voting powers in respect of the Company to remove those majority directors from their office as such. But to do so would in all probability, in my view, constitute an oppression of the minority shareholders, and in respect of which they would have a legal remedy under the Corporations Law.

26. In the interests of completeness there are other aspects which can usefully be mentioned:

) the possible relevance of Chapter 13 of "Income Taxation in Australia" by Professor Parsons as to assignments of expectancies contrasted where relevant with assignments of present rights was considered and raised by me at the hearing; it was accepted by both parties that these concepts are not relevant in this matter;

(b) in preparing these Reasons, I considered whether the payments over of the directors' fees could perhaps be categorised as an appropriation of profits (and see Boulder Perseverance Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation (W.A.) [1937] HCA 61; (1937) 4 ATD 389). An argument to this effect would run on the basis that the employment contract was entered into prior to 1984 and was thus antecedent to the directors' fee arrangement and so that in paying over the directors' fees, the Applicant was doing no more than appropriating that profit. This point was not raised at the hearing and the decision in Boulder Perseverance was not cited. In any event, I do not think that such an argument would be tenable. As set out previously in these Reasons, it was the acquiescence of the Company which allowed the Applicant to earn the director's fees, and without which acquiescence (conditional upon payment over of those directors' fees) the directors' fees could not have been earned.

27. When Exhibit A5 was tendered by Mr McMillan, Mr Gormly objected to my acceptance of that portion of it which contains Rulings, if acceptance would be indicative of my acceptance of the correctness of those determinations. I have referred to them for illustrative purposes only and not because I accept that they are necessarily correct (in the case of the non-binding Rulings) or that they are binding in the case of the two binding Rulings. But it is at least worthy of note that the Rulings previously referred to would tend to indicate that the payment over of the directors' fees is deductible. The Tribunal notes (having regard inter alia to page 5 of the T Documents) that it can perceive no valid distinction between public and private companies in relation to arrangements of the nature under review. And, moreover, the Tribunal agrees that the Company is in no sense a "contrived" or "artificial" entity.

28. In summary:

) if the Applicant derived the directors' fees beneficially he was entitled to a matching deduction under the first limb of section 51(1) of the Tax Act; and

(b) in the alternative, and on reflection Mr McMillan's contention as to a constructive trust is tenable, and the Applicant received the directors' fees on constructive trust for the Company.

The Applicant is entitled on either basis to succeed; in respect of each basis I have accepted that the Applicant has established the existence of a contract which obliged him to pay over the directors' fees to the Company, and arising from the Company's grant of its consent to the Applicant to act as director conditionally upon payment over of the directors' fees. (I note in conclusion that after the matter was concluded, I received a letter from the Applicant's counsel referring me to Jacobs, Zobory's case and a number of other authorities. These Reasons had been prepared in rough draft prior to receipt of that letter, and at a time when I had already referred to Zobory's case and Jacobs. That letter then elicited a reply from the Respondent referring me to other authorities and including additional submissions. While I accept that both letters were sent in good faith and in an endeavour to be of assistance, I do not think it desirable to encourage what amounts to further submissions made by either party in the absence of the other after the matter has concluded; accordingly I do not think it necessary or desirable to deal with the content of either letter.)

29. In the circumstances, the objection decisions under review are set aside.
