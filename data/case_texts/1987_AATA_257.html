Re "Vo" and Commissioner of Superannuation [1987] AATA 257 (7 July 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: "VO"

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V86/685

AAT No. 3623

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mrs Rosemary Balmford (Senior Member)

Professor R.W. Webster (Member)

R.A. Sinclair (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - retirement of applicant on grounds of invalidity - issue by the respondent of a benefit classification certificate (BCC) - whether applicant had failed to furnish information or had furnished false information when completing a Medical Report Form - whether the respondent would have issued a BCC had the information in question been provided - what the conditions specified on the BCC would have been - whether the grounds of retirement were connected with the condition which would have been specified on the BCC.

Words and Phrases: 'False or misleading' 'Connected with'

Superannuation Act 1976 - (as amended by the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986 and the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act (No 2) 1986) ss 16, 66

Re Sosnowski and Commissioner for Superannuation (No 3515 decided on 29 May, 1987)

Fry v. Commissioner for Superannuation [1984] FCA 211; (1984) 6 ALD 229

McMullen v. Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 61 ALR 189

Commissioner for Superannuation v. Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 63 ALR 237

Re Whiteford and Commissioner for Superannuation (No. 3201 decided on 6 February 1987)

HEARING

MELBOURNE

7:7:1987

ORDER

The Tribunal varies the first decision under review, being the decision to issue, pursuant to sub-section 16(11) of the Superannuation Act 1976, a benefit classification certificate specifying the condition "personality disorder", by deleting the condition "personality disorder" and substituting therefor the condition "history of an adverse reaction to stressful situations".

In all other respects the Tribunal affirms the decisions under review.

DECISION

Introduction

This is an application under Section 154 of the

Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") for review of two decisions of a delegate of the respondent made on 14 November, 1986:

(a) varying a decision taken by another

delegate on 18 July 1985 to issue,

pursuant to sub-section 16(11) of the

Act, a benefit classification

certificate in respect of the applicant

specifying the conditions "Insomnia",

"Anxiety", "Depression" and "Medication

and Drug Abuse" by deleting those

conditions and substituting the

condition "Personality Disorder"; and

(b) confirming the decision taken by another

delegate on 22 July, 1985, to be of the

opinion under sub-section 66(2)(c) of

the Act that the incapacity which was

the ground for the applicant's

invalidity retirement was caused, or was

substantially contributed to, by a

condition or conditions specified in the

benefit classification certificate or by

a condition or conditions connected with

such a condition or such conditions.

2. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged by the respondent pursuant to Section 37 Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and numbered T1 to T39, together with three additional medical reports, a copy of sick leave records in respect of the applicant's service with the State of Victoria and a report from the applicant's present employer. Evidence was given at the hearing by the applicant and her husband, and by Drs. Hardiker, Rose and Quine, all of whom are psychiatrists, Dr. Hackett, of the Commonwealth Department of Health, Dr. Low, who had been the applicant's treating general practitioner, and Dr. Amato, who had admitted the applicant to Pleasant View Centre.

3. The applicant, who was born on 18 November, 1956, holds the degrees of Bachelor of Arts with Honours and Bachelor of Laws, and was admitted to practise as a barrister and solicitor of the Supreme Court of Victoria after satisfactorily completing, in 1981, a six months postgraduate course of practical training. She was appointed to a position as a clerk in the Commonwealth Public Service on 17 March, 1982, thereby becoming an "eligible employee" for the purposes of the Act. On 4 March, 1982, she was examined by a Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO") for the purposes of Section 16 of the Act. In completing a "Medical Report Form" in connection with that examination, the applicant answered "No" in response to (inter alia) each of the following questions in Part B:

"Are you now suffering or have you ever

suffered from any of the following

disabilities:

1.

(a) Nervous fatigue or neurasthenia

(b) Mental or nervous conditions

(c) Anxiety state

(d) Depression or difficulty in sleeping

(e) Epilepsy or fits

12.

(b) Have you had any complaints, illness or

injury not mentioned above?

(c) Have you been absent from school or work

through sickness over the past two

years? (If so please state the

duration.)

13.

(b) Are you aware of any circumstances

regarding your health which will

interfere with the satisfactory

discharge of the duties of the position

for which you are now applying?"

In response to the question:

"13.(a) Date you last received medical or

surgical treatment and for what condition?"

on the same form, the applicant replied:

"Aug. 1981. Respiratory ailment."

Part B of the form began: "NOTE: Every question in Part B must be answered "Yes" or "No", and full details of all "Yes" answers must be furnished in Part C."

4. The CMO considered that the applicant met the general medical standard, and on 4 February, 1983, a delegate of the respondent decided not to issue a benefit classification certification ("BCC") in respect of the applicant.

5. The applicant was not happy in her position in the Public Service and performance of her duties became increasingly unsatisfactory, as was apparent from a number of reports in the material before the Tribunal. She said in evidence that in 1983 her use of Serepax "really built up ... because I had a lot of problems in that year and I got really addicted to them and I sort of had a real breakdown in my health". Later in the hearing she said "this problem started in 1983 when I was having these terrible problems with this boyfriend of mine and the drug abuse started then".

6. On 15 November, 1984, a CMO recommended that she be retired on the ground of invalidity due to the effects of "personality inadequacy and drug abuse 100%". She was retired from the Public Service on the ground of invalidity on 15 February, 1985, with effect from 15 November, 1984, thereby ceasing to be an "eligible employee". On 18 July, 1985, a delegate of the respondent issued a BCC in respect of the applicant, pursuant to sub-section 16(11) of the Act, specifying the conditions "insomnia", "anxiety", "depression" and "medication and drug abuse". The delegate was satisfied, in terms of sub-section 16(11), that in connection with the medical examination of 4 March, 1982, the applicant had "failed to furnish any information required to be furnished by (her) or furnished false information" and that had that not been the case a BCC specifying those conditions would have been in force in respect of her immediately before her retirement.

7. On 22 July, 1985, another delegate formed the opinion, in terms of paragraph 66(2)(a) of the Act, that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by the conditions specified in the BCC. Following a request by the applicant for reconsider- ation, pursuant to sub-section 154(2) of the Act, another delegate made the decisions under review, which are set out in paragraph 1 of these Reasons.

9. Sub-section 16(11) was repealed and replaced by Section 10 of the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act (No. 2) 1986, which came into operation on 18 December, 1986, after the making of the decisions under review, but before the hearing of this application for review. The principal distinctions of substance, for present purposes, between the old and the new sub-section 16(11) are:

- the substitution, throughout the provision,

of "give" and its congeners for "furnish"

and its congeners

- the addition, throughout the provision, of

"or misleading" after "false"

- the substitution of "a particular physical

or mental condition (or conditions) not

specified in the certificate" for "the

physical or mental condition ... which

caused ... the retirement ... or ...

connected with such a condition ..." in

sub-paragraph (d)(ii).

10. In the circumstances of the present case, the only one of those distinctions which is likely to be significant is the replacement of "false" by "false or misleading". The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary provides two principal relevant meanings of "false", namely "erroneous" and "purposely untrue". It is clear from the decision in Fry v. Commissioner for Superannuation [1984] FCA 211; (1984) 6 ALD 229 (as to which see paragraph 14 infra) that the meaning to be given to "false" in sub-section 16(11) is "purposely (or knowingly) untrue". The verb "to mislead" from which the adjective "misleading" derives, is defined in the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary as "To lead astray in conduct; to lead into error", with no requirement of purpose, knowledge or intent.

11. For the reason set out in paragraph 29 infra, we find, as did the Tribunal in Re Sosnowski and Commissioner for Superannuation (No. 3515, decided on 29 May, 1987) that the amendment is not of significance in relation to the facts in the present case. Accordingly, like that Tribunal, we refrain from embarking on the elaborate investigation which would be necessary to resolve the question of whether the previous provisions or the amended provisions are applicable to this matter.

12. It is not in issue that paragraphs 16(11)(a),(b) and (c) of the Act applied to the applicant, in that she ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity, at a time when her period of contributory service was less than 20 years, she had not attained her maximum retiring age, and a BCC was not in force in respect of her.

Whether the applicant failed to furnish information or furnished false information

13. The respondent submitted that in her answers to questions 1(b), (c), (d) and (e) and question 12(c) in the Medical Report Form the applicant had "at or in connection with a medical examination ... failed to furnish any information required to be furnished by (her) or furnished false information" in terms of sub-paragraph 16(11))(d)(i) of the Act.

14. In Fry's Case (supra) the Full Court of the Federal Court said at p 237 of sub-paragraph 16(11)(d)(i):

"The language of s 16(11)(d) makes it clear

that the Act does not impose an obligation

upon an employee to furnish information

generally but limits his obligation to

information required to be furnished by him

'at or in connection with' a medical

examination. Under s 16(2) the Commissioner

may require the employee to undergo such

medical examination or examinations as he

determines. A medical examiner is at liberty

to ask such questions as he wishes.

There must be a requirement made of the

employee to furnish information before any

obligation arises. The extent of the

obligation depends upon the questions asked

of him and the actual state of his knowledge

in relation to them.

The obligation upon him is to 'furnish

information'. 'Furnish' means 'to provide or

supply with (something necessary, useful, or

desirable)' and 'information' is something

'of which one is apprised or told.' (See the

Shorter Oxford English Dictionary.)

To fall within the scope of the section an

employee must have failed to provide or

supply something required of him of which he

has been apprised or told.

...

An employee complies with the section if he

answers the questions truthfully according to

his actual knowledge and state of mind."

The Court did not accept the submission of the Commissioner that "false" should be interpreted as meaning "false in fact", not "false to the knowledge of the employee".

15. Before considering whether sub-paragraph 16(11)(d)(i) applies to the applicant, it is convenient to set out the only medical reports before the Tribunal which contain entries made in medical records before 4 March, 1982, the date of the applicant's examination by a CMO for the purposes of the Act.

16. Dr. Low's report of consultations over the period from October, 1978, and including the two years prior to the medical examination, taken from his notes, read:

"18/10/78 - Feeling edgy, worried about exams.

Also has some personal family

problem. Treatment: Valium 2 mg.

t.d.s.

30/5/79 - Feels nervous. Having exams. and

not sleeping well. Request

tranquillisers. Treatment:

Ducene 5 mg. nocte., & 2.5mg. b.d.

18/3/80 - sore throat, nausea and physical

tiredness. U.R.T.I. Treatment:

Septrin Forte i b.d., Mogadon

nocte.

16/4/80 - difficulty with sleep. Needs

mogadon to help her sleep. Also

has U.R.T.I. with cough.

Treatment: Mogadon nocte, Lintus

pholcodine t.d.s.

11/9/80 - Has personal problem with family

and friends, mainly with her

boyfriend who is becoming

dependent on her. Can't get along

with her parents. Feeling

confused and depressed.

Treatment: Sinequan 25 mg. ii

nocte.

3/11/80 - Had an epileptic fit 2 days

earlier. Seen by casualty

17. A report from the Dandenong and District Hospital as to the epileptic fit referred to in Dr. Low's notes under the date "3/11/80", was before the Tribunal and read, omitting formal parts:

"The patient attended the Dandenong and

District Hospital on 1 November 1980 at 1500

hours. She had been previously well but had

felt a little nauseated that particular day.

She had taken 2 tablets of Sinequan the

previous night and was also on Carotene

tablets to achieve a sun tan. On 1 November

1980 while driving with her father she had a

typical grand mal epileptic fit.

She was observed at the Casualty Department

for four hours, during which time she

remained stable.

An x-ray of the skull showed no abnormality.

She was referred to her local doctor for

epilepsy."

18. Also before the Tribunal was a report from the University of Melbourne Student Health Service in respect of the applicant. It described two attendances for repeat of contraceptive medication, in May and November, 1981, and one attendance in September, 1981, for a superficial skin lesion and an upper respiratory tract infection. (This last, no doubt, was the attendance described in her reply to question 13(a) on the Medical Report Form as having taken place in August, 1981.) The only passage in the report which was relevant to the matter before the Tribunal read as follows:

"In May, 1981, she also sought some assistance

for a problem with insomnia for the previous

two months. There were no particular

associated anxieties and she was given a

small dose of a mild hypnotic."

19. The applicant did not attempt to deny to the Tribunal that she had taken increasing quantities of drugs after the commencement of her employment in the Commonwealth Public Service, and that the diagnosis of "drug abuse" in November, 1984, had been correct. A number of medical and other reports before the Tribunal, based on consultations after she commenced that employment, referred to statements made by her as to the effect that she had a long-standing history of drug abuse beginning in her school days. Her evidence at the hearing was that "when one is on (benzodiazepam drugs) ... one's whole life is impaired and lying is just a part of your life when you are using a lot of them". She said that the statements as to long-standing drug abuse which she made to doctors and to her supervisors at work, and which are recorded in those reports, were attempts to obtain sympathy, "to get supered out", and in one case to have herself admitted to Pleasant View Centre for treatment. They were thus, in the context in which they were made, self-serving statements.

20. There was no evidence before the Tribunal, other than those statements, of any history of drug abuse before the date of the initial medical examination in March, 1982. Dr. Hardiker, who has been the applicant's treating psychiatrist since June, 1984, said in his report of 15 January, 1987:

"From the evidence of (VO) and from the

enquiries I have made, I cannot determine

that (VO) had any problem with drug

dependence PRIOR TO 1983, except from her own

admissions, made at a time when her behaviour

was seriously disturbed, during her period as

an inpatient, withdrawing from drugs of

dependence."

21. We do have evidence as to the applicant's performance in the years between school and the entry medical examination in March, 1982. In 1979 she completed a combined Arts/Law degree, obtaining first class honours in Arts and completing her Law degree in the minimum time. In 1980 she was employed at the State Law Department and took twenty days of sick leave, as to which Dr. Hackett said:

"We would think that is a little bit more than

the average that most young people take for

sick leave. We would have wanted to know the

reasons for it but we would not - on its own,

we would not have been unduly alarmed by that

situation.

Would you have - would the Commonwealth

medical officer have approached her GPs for

those, particularly for those entries headed

'Medical certificates'?---I do not think so,

no, not if she had an explanation that

sounded a fair and reasonable one."

In 1981 she completed, in minimum time, the course of practical training at the Leo Cussen Institute for Continuing Legal Education.

22. Despite the applicant's description of herself to doctors and supervisors whose reports are before us as abusing drugs since her school days we are not convinced, on the evidence before us, that this was the case. We have no evidence as to her actual work performance in the State Law Department. However, her academic performance, and her completion in the minimum time of the course of practical training at the Leo Cussen Institute, persuade us of the truth of her contention that her abuse of drugs commenced after she began her employment with the Commonwealth Public Service. That drug abuse led to a deterioration in her work performance, as is made clear in a number of documents which are before us. Had she been abusing drugs in earlier years, during her time at University for example, we consider that her performance would have been affected in a manner which would have prevented her from achieving the results she did achieve.

23. Looking at the material before us as a whole, therefore, we accept the evidence of the applicant that she had not been abusing drugs, nor had she had any psychiatric consultations, before the entry medical examination in March, 1982; and that her statements to doctors as to a history of drug abuse were invented for the reasons, and in the circumstances, which she described. Much time was spent at the hearing on considering whether she suffered from a "personality disorder", an expression the meaning of which was the subject of some debate among the medical witnesses. If she was suffering from a "personality disorder" in March, 1982 (as to which we make no finding) we accept that she was not aware of it.

24. Thus, on the material before us, we must conclude that the only knowledge which the applicant had, at the time of the entry medical examination, as to the matters dealt with in questions 1(b) to (e), related to the situations described in paragraphs 13-15 supra.

25. To deal first with question 12(c), which is set out in paragraph 4 supra, the applicant had been employed as an administrative assistant in the Victorian Law Department from 6 February, 1980, to February, 1981. Thus the "past two years" encompassed by question 12(c) included almost the whole of that period of employment. A sick leave report supplied by the Law Department, the accuracy of which was accepted by the applicant, showed that between 17 March, 1980, and 7 January, 1981, she had been absent on sick leave on thirteen occasions, for periods totalling twenty days. The record indicated that medical certificates had been supplied in respect of six of those occasions. She was asked about the sick leave in cross- examination:

"How do you justify not having recollected any

of these sick days?---Well, as I said to you,

I remember going to Dr. Low. It is not a

matter of that it is - no, I think I did

remember some of the sick days actually.

Then why did you not disclose them?---Well, I

think a lot of the sick days, as I say, I was

not actually sick and the other ones are very

minor things and I could not have put an

actual date on them.

You were not being required to specify the

date though, were you?---No. No, well, I

think I probably should have put that there.

So you admit that you failed to disclose that

relevant sick leave record?---Well, not on

any specific occasions but I - I think I

probably should have put a yes there now I

..."

No information was available as to any absences from the course of practical training at the Leo Cussen Institute.

26. As to questions 1(b) to (e), the applicant pointed out that those questions began "... have you ever suffered from any of the following disabilities?". She said that she had therefore interpreted the questions as meaning "... have you ever suffered from any of the following conditions to such an extent as to constitute a disability?". As to 1(d), relating to "difficulty in sleeping", she had suffered from difficulty in sleeping, as is apparent from Dr. Low's report, but submitted that the difficulty was not such as to constitute a disability in a person who was, as she was at the time, working for examinations and having problems in a significant personal relationship. Dr. Low, in his evidence, said that her reaction to the difficulties that she was going through was a normal reaction, by which he meant the type of reaction that anyone, subject to those same stresses, might experience.

27. The applicant agreed that in answering question 1(e), as to "epilepsy or fits", she "probably should have disclosed" an occasion on which she had had a fit, which is described in Dr. Low's report under the date "3/11/80".

28. As to the remaining questions 1(b) "mental or nervous condition", 1(c) "anxiety state", 1(d) "depression", she considered that the descriptions of her as "nervous" and "depressed" appearing in Dr. Low's report were not such, taken in context, as to suggest that she should have answered "Yes" to those questions.

29. It is clear from the applicant's evidence, and she did not attempt to deny, that she knew when she answered "no" to questions 12(c) and 1(e) that these answers were false. On that evidence, the Tribunal is satisfied that, in terms of sub-para- graph 16(11)(d)(i) of the Act, "at or in connexion with a medical examination which the (applicant) was required to undergo under this section", the applicant "failed to furnish any information required to be furnished by (her) or furnished false information". As to the other questions, the position is by no means as clear. However, on balance, the Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant should have answered "Yes" to 1(d) as to "difficulty in sleeping". The other questions (including 1(d) as to "depression") are, from the point of view of a lay person completing a form, vaguely expressed, in terms which have lay meanings as well as technical meanings. However, in the event, on the evidence as to the effect of "Yes" answers, we do not consider that answering "Yes" to 1(b) or (c) would have led the CMO to make any enquiries additional to those which would have followed from answering "Yes" to 12(c), 1(d) and 1(e). Further, referring to paragraphs 9 to 11 supra, we are of the opinion that our decision on this point would be the same if the words "or misleading" appeared in sub-paragraph 16(11)(d)(i) following the word "false".

30. The next questions for us to determine are first, whether if the applicant had not failed to furnish that information or had not furnished that false information, a BCC would have been in force in respect of her, immediately before her retirement; secondly, if so, what would have been the condition specified in that BCC, and thirdly, as to the connection between that condition and the applicant's retirement on the ground of invalidity.

31. Whether a BCC would have been in force and what would have been the condition specified

32. Sub-section 16(4) requires the Commissioner to issue a BCC "if he is of the opinion that the person is not likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the report or reports (of the medical examination) to continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age".

33. The only evidence before the Tribunal from which we can reach any conclusion as to what would have happened had the applicant answered "Yes" to questions 12(c), 1(d) and 1(e), is that of Dr. Hackett, who is a senior medical officer with the Commonwealth Department of Health. In his capacity as delegate of the Public Service Board and adviser to the Commissioner for Superannuation, he had, on the medical report form of 4 March, 1982, ticked a box marked "Superannuation: I consider that the applicant meets general medical standard". He had not conducted the medical examination, and the CMO who conducted it was not called by the respondent. On 26 May, 1986, he had, after perusing a number of documents, agreed that the issue of the BCC on 18 July, 1985, specifying "insomnia", "anxiety depression", and "medication and drug abuse" was justified. In so doing he expressly relied on the statements in medical reports as to the applicant's history of drug abuse which the Tribunal has accepted to be self-serving inventions by her.

34. Dr. Hackett said that the role of the CMO in the medical examination is to protect the system against people with pre- existing conditions. A "yes" answer would lead the CMO to make further enquiries from the person in question, and in some circumstances to seek a report from the treating doctor. Had the applicant answered "Yes" to the questions with which we are concerned, the CMO would have referred to Dr. Low, her treating general practitioner. Dr. Low's report would have indicated to a CMO that it was appropriate to make further enquiries from the applicant.

35. Dr. Low gave evidence at the hearing of this matter, but his evidence added little to his written report. The Tribunal is not able to speculate as to what further assistance he would have been able to give to an enquiry from a CMO in March, 1982.

36. Had the applicant answered "Yes" to 1(e), the CMO'S enquiries would have produced the report from the Dandenong and District Hospital, to which Dr. Low's report also referred and to which that report would thus also have led the CMO. It might be thought that, in response to question 1(d) on the medical report form in March, 1982 (see paragraph 4 supra) the applicant would have remembered the attendance at the Student Health Service relating to insomnia over a period of two months. Alternatively, it might be expected that she would have mentioned it in response to enquiries of the kind that would have followed a "Yes" answer. Save in these respects, the evidence before the Tribunal does not enable it to find that any other information would have been obtained if, in March, 1982, further enquiries, of a general nature, as to matters arising under Dr. Low's report, had been made of the applicant, resulting from correct answers having been given by her to questions 1(b) to (e) and 12(c).

37. On the evidence before it, the Tribunal must thus assume that the only information available to the CMO if the applicant had given correct answers to those questions would have been the information contained in the reports of Dr. Low, the Dandenong and District Hospital and the Student Health Service.

38. Mr. Anforth, for the respondent, submitted that we could have recourse to the later medical evidence in finding what would have been an appropriate diagnosis of the applicant's condition in March, 1982. We do not accept that submission. Sub-paragraph 16(11)(d)(ii) of the Act requires the Commissioner to be satisfied "that, if the person had not failed to furnish that information or had not furnished that false information, there would have been in force in respect of the person, immediately before his death or retirement, a benefit classification certificate ...". Paragraph 16(11)(f) then requires the Commissioner, if so satisfied, to issue a BCC specifying the condition which "would have been the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person specified ... in the benefit classification certificate that would have been in force in respect of the person, if the person had not failed to furnish that information or had furnished that false information" (emphasis added). The Tribunal, standing as it does in the shoes of the Commissioner, is thus required to consider what would have happened if the applicant had supplied correct answers to the questions on the medical report form. We would refer to the comment by the Full Court of the Federal Court as to the similar provisions of sub-section 184(5) of the Act in McMullen v. Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 61 ALR 189 at 205.

39. The only oral evidence we have as to what would have happened is, as noted above, the evidence of Dr Hackett, who said that, if the CMO had had only the applicant's correct answers to the questions, Dr Low's report and the report of the Student Health Service, he would have recommended a BCC specifying "a history of insomnia, perhaps, a history of anxiety; or perhaps in more general terms, a history of an adverse reaction to stressful situations". Later in the hearing, he agreed to the suggestion that the BCC would have specified "an anxiety-related condition". On that basis, we find on a balance of probabilities, that the BCC would have specified, in the general terms to which Dr Hackett referred, "a history of an adverse reaction to stressful situations".

The connection with the ground of retirement

40. The final question before us is whether, in terms of paragraph 66(2)(c) of the Act, the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement "was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or such conditions".

41. The recommendation by the CMO on 15 November, 1984, following which the applicant was retired, was made on a standard printed form and included the following statements and ticked answers (printed material underlined):

"I consider that the examinee is suffering

from the following condition(s) (in order of

severity) and with estimated percentage each

contribute to the incapacity.

Personality inadequacy and medication (and

drug) abuse. 100%. Brief history of the

condition(s) including origins and the time

first noted by contributor: See specialist

psychiatry report from Dr. Rose dated

8/11/84. Mentally unstable all her life and

before employment with the Department. Any

form of work would probably have resulted in

breakdown. Her present stability is tenuous

and a further breakdown is highly probably

(sic). Prognosis poor. I consider that the

examinee is unfit for continued employment

and should be retired on the grounds of

invalidity but may, in due course, partially

or completely recover and become fit for some

form of Commonwealth employment."

...

"How does the employee's medical condition (or

conditions) affect his or her ability to

undertake the duties outlined in the

departmental statement? Work output is

likely to be poor and absences for one reason

or another are likely to increase. How would

continuing to perform these duties affect the

employee's medical condition(s)? May result

in further instability. If redeployment is

considered possible on medical grounds,

please indicate the types of work or working

situations which the employee's medical

condition(s) preclude him or her from

undertaking: Not worth pursuing."

42. We note that "Personality inadequacy and medication (and drug) abuse" were apparently regarded by the CMO as one condition only. We note also that in the report which is referred to and which was before the Tribunal, Dr Rose relied to a considerable extent on the applicant's stated history of drug abuse, which could not be substantiated. However the validity of the diagnosis, made at that time, insofar as it related to medication and drug abuse, was not challenged by the applicant.

43. In Commissioner for Superannuation v. Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 63 ALR 237 the Full Court of the Federal Court considered the meaning of the words "connected with" in paragraph 66(2)(c) Davies J said at pp 238-259:

"The words "connected with" are not technical

words and in ordinary parlance permit of an

association which is not causative. In my

opinion, however, Parliament did not intend

the words to be given a wide interpretation

unrelated to the context in which they

appear.

...

In the context, it appears to me that the

words "connected with" were introduced to

deal with the circumstance where the early

retirement arises not from the medical

condition which existed at the time the

employee became an eligible employee but as a

result of another medical condition that

arose out of or developed from the condition.

...

In the context, I would read the words

"connected with" in s. ... 66(2)(c) as

dealing with the circumstance where the

immediate or proximate cause of the

incapacity leading to the retirement was a

medical condition other than the condition

existing at the time the employee became an

eligible employee, but where, nevertheless,

there is a chain of causation such that the

medical condition existing at the time when

the employee became an eligible employee can

be said, through a chain of events, to have

caused or substantially contributed to the

incapacity causing the retirement.

Pincus J also considered that the required connection was a causal one, which could possibly exist on the basis that both conditions had a common cause; Beaumont J found that in the circumstances of the case it was not necessary to decide whether that was so.

44. The applicant conceded that she had become addicted to Serepax in 1983, during her period of employment with the Commonwealth Public Service. The reason which she gave for her addiction was that she had "had a lot of problems in that year" (see paragraph 5 supra). Dr Hardiker, who first saw her in June 1984, said in his report to the Commissioner for Superannuation on 6 November 1985 "My diagnosis was substance abuse disorder, benzodiazepine habituation due to situational stress (resolved); at present residual symptoms only". The recommendation set out in paragraph 40 supra indicates that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement was substantially contributed to by the condition "medication and drug abuse". In this context we would refer to the discussion of the phrase "substantially contributed to" in Re Whiteford and Commissioner for Superannuation (No. 3201 decided on 6 February 1987). Looking at paragraph 66(2)(c) in the light of the views of the Federal Court in Miller, we consider that the condition of "medication and drug abuse" constituted by the applicant's addiction to Serepax was a condition "connected with" the condition "history of an adverse reaction to stressful situations" which we have found would have been specified in the BCC if the applicant had given correct answers to the questions on the form.

45. Conclusion

46. For the reasons given the first decision under review, being the decision to vary the condition specified in the BCC, will be varied by the substitution for the condition "personality disorder" of the condition "history of an adverse reaction to stressful situations", and the decisions under review will be otherwise confirmed.

47. In view of what we have said about the applicant's admissions as to her medical condition at the time of her retirement from the Commonwealth Public Service, it is appropriate that we should make clear that our perception at the hearing of this matter, more than two years after her retirement, was that she was well able to represent herself, which she did, assisted by her husband, with confidence and competence. To say this is not to suggest that there is any doubt as to the diagnosis which was made at the time of her retirement. The applicant, her husband and Dr. Hardiker all gave evidence of the effects on her of her condition at that time and also gave evidence of the considerable (and to Dr. Hardiker, her treating psychiatrist, "extraordinary" and "extremely impressive") extent of her recovery. That evidence as to her recovery was borne out by her performance over the three days of the hearing. We note the written report of an employer for whom she is presently performing clerical relief duties on a casual basis, and giving satisfaction : and we note the stability and support which she is receiving from her marriage. She has, for reasons which we have set out, been unsuccessful in this application for review. That lack of success derives from the facts before us and the law applicable to those facts. It does not derive from any deficiency or inadequacy in the presentation of her case.
