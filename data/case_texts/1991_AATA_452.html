ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - proceeds of clear felling of mature pine trees whether capital or income

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s.25

Scott v Commissioner of Taxation (NSW) (1935) 35 SR (NSW) 215 White v FCT [1968] HCA 41; (1968) 120 CLR 191

REASONS FOR DECISION

16 April 1991 Mrs R.A. Balmford, Senior Member

Mr D.J. Trowse, Member

Dr D.B. Williams, Member

  1. This is an application for review of a decision of the respondent disallowing an objection by the applicant to the inclusion in the respondent's assessment of his taxable income for the year ended 30 June 1988 of an amount of $14,485, being the applicant's share of the net proceeds of the sale of timber by a partnership of which he is a member.
  2. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged by the respondent pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and numbered Tl to T9. Evidence was given by the applicant who was unrepresented. The respondent was represented by an officer of his department.

3. The facts are conveniently set out in a statement of agreed facts lodged by the parties prior to the hearing, which reads as follows (with evidence of identification removed):

"1. In 1946, the applicant, in partnership with another person, purchased as tenants in common, 506 acres of land in south-east South Australia. The applicant has a fifty percent interest in the partnership.

  1. In 1947 the partners sowed their first pinus radiata seeds. In 1948 the year old seedlings were used to plant the first section of forest. In total, approximately 55 - 60 acres of trees were planted. The partners carried out most of the planting but engaged labour to assist on two occasions.
  2. The applicant had no previous experience in forestry and limited financial resources. The partners obtained advice from an old local farmer who had established an 80 acre pine forest about fifty years earlier. They also obtained information of a general nature about forests from the Woods and Forests Department.
  3. The partners took what steps they could to minimize the damage caused by pests, particularly rabbits, before each section was planted. When burrows were located, phostoxin tablets were placed in them. In clearer areas trails of poisoned oats were laid. The presence of dense bracken hampered these operations. As rabbits were never totally eradicated many seedlings were nipped off.
  4. Seven hundred trees to the acre were planted with the objective that, after thinning, 80 to 100 of the best trees in each acre would be left to grow to full maturity. The partners had no equipment to enable the burning of the bracken and no funds to have the land ploughed. As a result, the seedlings had to be planted into dense bracken about three feet high. Two years would pass before the trees appeared above the bracken and the partners could assess the success of the planting. Seedlings planted to replace trees that died were unable to catch-up with the growth of the original trees. To limit the loss caused by rabbits nipping off the seedlings the partners worked through the forest pruning off unwanted shoots. Nevertheless, many trees developed more than one leader to the detriment of the quality and value of the timber.
  5. In 1962 the applicant moved to Adelaide. Since then he has made two or three trips per year to inspect the plantation. The other member of the partnership continued to live near the land until his death in January 1991.
  6. After an approach by Softwood Holdings Ltd in 1967, the partners entered into an arrangement with the company to advise the partners about thinning and clear-felling, cut the trees when they considered the time to be right and buy the timber.
  7. The partnership received its first return from sales of thinning operations in the year ended 30 June 1967. The first returns from clear felling were received in the year ended 30 June 1982, when seven acres of trees had to be clear-felled prematurely because they had been damaged by fire. The first return from clear-felling of mature trees was in June 1988, when proceeds amounted to $29,535. During the year ended 30 June 1989 the partnership received the amount of $32,394 from the sale of clear felling. The available partnership returns show proceeds from timber sales as follows :

Year Ending Sales

30 June 1978 $ 1,617

30 June 1982 $29,179

30 June 1985 $11,920

30 June 1986 $15,551

30 June 1987 $19,211

30 June 1988 $29,535

  1. The applicant has, since the year of income ended 30 June 1976, returned his share of the partnership income or loss and been accepted as a primary producer by the Commissioner of Taxation. The Averaging Provisions of Division 16 of the Income Tax Assessment Act have been applied to the applicant's taxable income since that year.

10. In the return of income for the year ended 30 June 1988 the applicant disclosed his share in the net proceeds from the sale of timber as $14,485. He claimed that as the proceeds were from the sale of clear-felled mature timber that they were a realisation of capital. The amount was included in the applicant's taxable income when an assessment issued on 22 May 1989. The applicant lodged an objection against the assessment."

  1. The applicant explained in documents before the Tribunal and at the hearing that his intention in establishing the pine forest was as a form of "superannuation insurance", which was within his limited means at the time. He had then envisaged that the mature trees, in 30 or 40 years time, would produce capital returns which he could invest to provide income in his old age. He had also hoped that the forest would be passed on in the family, but he said that that was not now likely to happen. He said that the process of thinning was directed merely to producing the best possible final cut, and was not an end in itself. The Tribunal appreciates that the applicant's intentions were as he has described them.
  2. In his submissions, the applicant argued largely by analogy. However, the Tribunal must consider the matter in accordance with the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") and the authorities which have interpreted the provisions of the Act.
  3. Sub-section 25(1) of the Act reads, so far as relevant:

"The assessable income of a taxpayer shall include -

(a) where the taxpayer is a resident -

the gross income derived directly or indirectly from all sources whether in or out of Australia;

which is not exempt income ... ."

7. The word "income" is not defined in the Act, and the cases establish that it is to be interpreted according to ordinary concepts. In Scott v Commissioner of Taxation (NSW) (1935) 35 SR (NSW) 215, at 218, Jordan CJ said:

"The question which we have to determine is whether, and if so to what extent, the appellant is liable to pay income tax, in respect of this sum, under the Income Tax (Management) Act, 1928 No 35, as amended by the Acts Nos 47 of 1929 and 54 of 1934.

This Act is an Act to provide for the assessment and collection of a tax on incomes; and, by s 8, it provides that, subject to the provisions of the Act, income tax at such rates as may be fixed by any Act shall be paid in respect of the taxable income derived by any person during every income year to which the Act applies. 'Income Tax', said Lord MacNaghten 'if I may be pardoned for saying so, is a tax on income'. London County Council v Attorney-General ([1901] AC 26 at 35); Seymour v Reed ([1927] AC 554 at 560). The word 'income' is not a term of art, and what forms of receipts are comprehended within it, and what principles are to be applied to ascertain how much of those receipts ought to be treated as income, must be determined in accordance with the ordinary concepts and usages of mankind, except in so far as the statute states or indicates an intention that receipts which are not income in ordinary parlance are to be treated as income, or that special rules are to be applied for arriving at the taxable amount of such receipts: A-G of British Columbia v Ostrum ([1904] AC 144 at 147); Lambe v Inland Revenue Commissioners ([1934] 1 KB 178 at 182-3)."

  1. Thus the question before the Tribunal is whether the proceeds of the applicant's clear felling of his trees constitute income according to ordinary concepts. If they are the proceeds of carrying on a business, then that is the case.
  2. The respondent submitted that the activities of the applicant and his partner were such as to constitute the carrying on of a business. The Tribunal accepts that submission. The partners originally entered into the project with a view to making a profit. They sought advice, they dealt with pests, they thinned their trees, they entered into a commercial agreement with Softwood Holdings, they obtained taxation deductions and the benefit of the averaging provisions of the legislation, and they conducted the project throughout in a business-like way so far as their resources permitted.
  3. In White v FCT [1968] HCA 41; (1968) 120 CLR 191, the Full Court of the High Court affirmed a decision of Windeyer J that the proceeds of sale of timber grown on land owned by the taxpayer were income within the ordinary meaning of the word. In that case the taxpayer was a timber merchant, who purchased timbered land, initially without intending to sell the timber. Taylor and Owen JJ said, at pages 222-223:

"On the findings of the learned judge of first instance the land in question here was purchased initially for development as a pastoral property. That in the ordinary course its development would necessitate the removal of a considerable amount of timber is obvious and the effect of his Honour's findings is that it is probable that the timber on the land would be of value to the owner and that this was known to the taxpayer. But its disposal in the course of development would have been only a subsidiary activity in the development of the property and the amount received for it could not be regarded as the proceeds of a business carried on by the taxpayer. But the removal and the sale of the timber did not remain a subsidiary matter in the development of the property; all other purposes became subordinated to the preservation of the timber resources on the land for the purposes of sale and this state of affairs obtained for many years before 1966. In other words, the land became devoted exclusively to the sale of standing timber as it stood from time to time and it was so devoted indefinitely. We cannot, therefore, accept that what took place should be regarded merely as the realization by the taxpayer from time to time of some part of, or some interest in, his asset. Rather the evidence indicates that after 1956 he embarked upon a particular form of trade, that is to say, the sale of standing timber and the land was being maintained exclusively in the interests of such trade. This being so we are of the opinion that the sum of $5,000 was income of the taxpayer according to the ordinary concept of that term and we have no occasion to consider whether, if our view had been otherwise, the second limb of s. 26(a) would apply."

11. In the present case, the timber was created by the activities of the applicant, and those activities were, from the beginning, directed to the ultimate sale of the timber.

12. For the reasons given, the objection decision under review will be affirmed.
