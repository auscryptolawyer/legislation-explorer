REASONS FOR DECISION

3 August 1998 J Block (Senior Member)



This matter arises by way of an appeal against the disallowance by the Respondent on 25 June 1997 of an objection lodged by the Applicant against an amended assessment in respect of the year ending 30 June 1994.

By way of a quick overview, the substantive issue is as follows. The Applicant company is involved in the business of distributing films and television programs; (one of the crucial issues of this matter is as to the precise nature of the Applicant’s business and accordingly I do not say more, at this preliminary stage). On 29 September 1981 the Applicant acquired perpetual rights to a library of films (“the J film library”) for the territories of Australia, New Zealand, Hong Kong and the Philippines. During the course of the following years the Applicant exploited its rights in the J film library in Australia and New Zealand, by way of grant of limited licences of the film rights; however, the Applicant was not able to exploit its rights in Hong Kong or the Philippines. On 1 July 1993 the Applicant disposed of its rights in the J film library in respect of Hong Kong and the Philippines for $514,403. The Respondent claims that the proceeds from the sale are income and hence assessable; the Applicant claims that the proceeds of the sale are (in respect of pre-capital gains tax assets) on capital account and, therefore, not assessable.



  1. APPEARANCES, EVIDENCE TENDERED AND PRELIMINARY MATTERS

The Applicant was represented by Mr B.R. Pape of Counsel; the Respondent was represented by Mr R. Hamilton of Counsel, instructed by the Australian Government Solicitor.

The Tribunal received into evidence the documents lodged pursuant to s37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, and in addition the following exhibits:

  • a witness statement by the founder and managing director of the Applicant company - this individual, for whom the Applicant appears to be an alter ego, was the only witness; he will be referred to as Mr A. His witness statement included annexures containing the financial statements of the Applicant from 1982 to 1995, the agreement of 29 September 1981 in terms of which the Applicant acquired its perpetual rights in (the bulk of) the J film library, and a number of limited licence agreements with Australian and New Zealand television stations in respect of some of the films in the J film library;
  • the agreement of 1 July 1993, entitled “Television Program Licence Agreement”, pursuant to which the Applicant disposed of its interest, in respect of Hong Kong and the Philippines, in the J film library.

I note that square brackets in these Reasons indicate edits to preserve anonymity. Before I set out the facts in more detail, a number of preliminary matters can usefully be mentioned.

Firstly, the J film library consists of 44 films. Forty-two of those films were acquired by the Applicant in 1981, whilst two of those films were acquired in 1988. It was common cause between the parties that this matter concerns only the 42 films acquired in 1981, and in respect of which the capital gains provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”) do not apply. The parties are furthermore (if relevant) agreed as to an appropriate apportionment of the consideration attributable to the pre- and post-CGT films respectively.

Secondly, and in relation to the relevant year of income, the consideration for the J film library arising out of the 1993 disposal was expressed to be payable in two instalments. The first instalment was due on or before 31 July 1993 and the second instalment was due on or before 31 December 1994. Despite the fact that the second instalment was in fact received by the Applicant during the 1995 financial year, it is not disputed that the Applicant is to be regarded as on an accruals accounting basis for taxation purposes, and accordingly that the relevant year of income for the receipt of both instalments is the year ended 30 June 1994 (“the relevant year”).

Thirdly, it should be mentioned that the current matter arises in somewhat unusual circumstances. The amended assessment out of which this matter arises was issued, not as a result of an audit by the Respondent, but by way of a request for amendment by the Applicant of its assessment for the relevant year. When the Applicant lodged its income tax return for the relevant year, it treated the proceeds from the first instalment as being on income account. But, when preparing its accounts for the 1995 financial year, and while considering the appropriate taxation treatment for the second instalment (the aggregate amount was not initially dealt with by the Applicant on an accruals basis) the Applicant’s tax agent considered that an error had been made in relation to the treatment of the first instalment. A request for amendment was made by the Applicant in December 1995 dealing with this and one other issue not relevant to these Reasons. The Respondent conceded the other issue, but refused to amend the initial assessment in respect of the assessability of the first instalment. As will be seen, the Respondent attached some weight to the initial characterisation of the receipt by the Applicant.

  1. THE FACTUAL BACKGROUND

The Applicant was incorporated on 12 January 1970; it was formed to carry on business as a distributor of films and television programs. There are three main areas of its business activity.

The first and central aspect of its business is that it acts as a commission agent for film and television production companies - or other distribution companies - for the distribution of films and television programs. Although it has acted as agent for a number of production and distribution companies, its principal agency was granted by a large English film production company (“the English company”); it has been since 1972, and remains, the representative of the English company for Australia and New Zealand; it was also the representative of the English company for South East Asia in between 1980 and 1996. Its activities as an agent involve the negotiation and execution of limited licence agreements between the principal and a television station. The licences are “limited” in two senses, both by time and by number of ‘runs’; that is, firstly, they confer upon the licensee the right to exhibit or broadcast otherwise copyright material within a certain number of years, and secondly they limit the number of permissible broadcasts of each program within that period. The Applicant is not usually a party to these contracts, except in its capacity as agent, and derives income by way of commission on the licence agreement. Commission is calculated as a percentage of the total consideration for each program licensed, and is paid to the Applicant by the principal and not the television station.

The second aspect of the Applicant’s business arises where the Applicant acquires exclusive rights in its own name for the exhibition of films or television programs or a series of programs, and proceeds to sub-licence those rights. There are two sides to this aspect of the business. The first side (and which is most typical of its activities) involves the acquisition by the Applicant of exclusive rights for a limited period of time - for example, five, seven or ten years. It then either on-licences the rights to a television station for the duration of the period it had acquired, or sub-licences the rights (to television stations) for limited periods (and for a limited number of ‘runs’) within the overall time available to it. Income is derived from licence fees calculated by reference to the number of years and the number of runs for each film, program or episode. The second side to this aspect of the business entails providing licences to exhibit films in respect of which the Applicant has acquired ‘perpetual’ rights. Over the course of its history, the Applicant has acquired the perpetual rights to three collections (or ‘libraries’) of films. These are:

  • the ‘R film library’, a library of over 700 films, for which the rights were acquired by the Applicant in various stages between 1977 and 1982;
  • the J film library (the subject of the present dispute), containing 44 films, for which the rights were acquired by the Applicant in 1981 (42 films) and 1988 (2 films); and
  • the ‘M film library’, containing 8 films, for which the rights were acquired in the early 1980’s.

The Applicant’s usual course of conduct in relation to its perpetual rights film libraries is to sub-licence individual films to television stations for limited terms and for a limited number of runs; the terms do not normally exceed seven years. Income is derived by way of licence fee. Again by way of overview, the essential dispute between the Applicant and the Respondent concerns the taxation treatment to be accorded to a disposal by the Applicant of the entirety of its interest (‘the perpetual rights’) in relation to certain countries in one of these libraries.

The third aspect of the Applicant’s business relates to investment activities. The Applicant has been a passive investor in (amongst other things) a number of Australian films under Division 10BA of the Act, and also has a number of investments in television stations in Australia and overseas. In relation to the latter investments, however, it does not own a controlling interest, and is not engaged in the management of any television station either in Australia or overseas. The Applicant has also made loans from time to time as part of its business. Income under this aspect of the Applicant’s business is derived in the form of dividends and interest.

  1. THE FACTS GIVING RISE TO THIS CASE

(i) Acquisition of the J film library

In 1981 the general manager of one of the film distribution companies which the Applicant represented as agent in Australasia, (that distributor company is referred to as “SR”), indicated to Mr A that it held the rights to a number of films, but that it desired to terminate its activities in the film distribution business. SR owned, at that time, in respect of 175 films, limited rights due to expire for the most part by the end of 1986, as well as perpetual rights to a collection of 42 films, being (most of) the J film library. The films in the J film library are mainly black and white films which were produced between the years of 1945 to 1956 (the majority of the films having being produced in 1948). SR had acquired the rights to the J film library in 1967, under an agreement which is referred to as the “1967 agreement”, from the American company (“J Company”) which had produced the films. Under the 1967 agreement, which was entitled “Licence Agreement for Television Motion Picture Exhibition”, SR acquired “all rights” in the films “with the exception of remake film rights”; these rights were given to SR “forever and in perpetuity” for the territories of Australia, New Zealand, Hong Kong and the Philippines.

In consequence of negotiations between the Applicant and SR in late 1981, it was agreed that the Applicant would acquire the rights which SR had in respect of the J film library, as well as the other 175 films (a total of 217 films), in consideration of a payment of $1,250 and the acceptance by the Applicant of an obligation by SR to certain third parties under a prior contract.

That agreement was formalised on 29 September 1981; for this purpose the Applicant (as assignee) and SR (as assignor) entered into a Deed of Assignment (“the Deed”). The recitals to the Deed are as follows:

WHEREAS:
  1. The Assignor is the owner of certain rights in the nature of copyright in certain of the cinematograph films listed and described in the Schedule hereto and may be the owner of such rights in others of the said films.
  2. It has now been agreed between the parties hereto that the Assignor shall sell the said rights in the films to the Assignee.

It should be mentioned at this stage that the Schedule to the Deed contains a list of the titles of the 217 films, including, under the heading of “[J] Features”, a list of the 42 titles in the J film library.

The operative term of the Deed is set out as follows:

  1. In consideration of the sum of $1,250.00 paid by the Assignee to the Assignor (the receipt of which is hereby acknowledged) the Assignor to the full extent that it has power so to do assigns to the Assignee all rights which the Assignor possesses (if any) in each and every of the films and whether in respect of Australia or any other country or countries in which any such right may TO HOLD the same unto the Assignee for the respective period or periods in which the rights or any of them shall enure...

The Deed does not actually specify the nature of the rights (either by reference to the countries or the periods of time) which were being assigned to the Applicant. Instead, clause 2 of the Deed provides that the Applicant accepts the assignment on the basis that it had made a “full enquiry” (although the nature and extent of that enquiry is not specified). It seems clear, however, that the Applicant was familiar with the geographical circumscription and duration of the rights it was acquiring.

As mentioned above, part of the consideration for the assignment was acceptance by the Applicant of an obligation which SR owed under a previous contract. The acceptance by the Applicant of this obligation was contained in clause 5, and was in the following terms:

  1. The Assignee agrees to hold the Assignor indemnified in respect of all actions claims costs and demands which have been or which may at any time be made or brought against the Assignor out of or relating to the contract entered into between the Assignor and [a television station] dated 8 October, 1971 under which the Assignor assigned 125 [R film library] films and in particular arising out of any failure by the Assignor to supply the prints of any of those films.

It is important to explain the nature of this indemnity and the circumstances under which it arose and was ultimately discharged; I will undertake that explanation shortly.

The somewhat inexpensive purchase price of the rights to the total 217 films, and the perpetual rights to the J film library in particular (which Mr A said is a rare acquisition in the distribution business), arose for a number of reasons. For one thing, as has been mentioned, SR was looking for a way to exit the film distribution market. More importantly, SR had previously had some difficulty licensing the J film library. This in turn arose because, firstly, Mr A suggested, television stations were reluctant in the early 1980’s to run black and white films and the few colour films in the library were made using an older and inferior colouring technique such that the dyes had largely faded, and secondly, the copies of the films (or “prints”) of which SR had actual possession were themselves of poor quality and unsuitable for broadcast.

After having acquired the rights to the J film library, and with the object of turning them to account, Mr A embarked on a worldwide quest in search of better quality prints. After some time he was able to acquire better prints from the owner of the perpetual rights to the J film library in the United Kingdom, and thus was able to ensure that the perpetual rights which the Applicant had acquired were supported by prints of transmittable quality.

In December 1988, Mr A was approached by a principal at J Company, and informed that there were two more films in the J library which did not form part of the 1967 agreement with SR. The Applicant was offered the perpetual rights for Australia, New Zealand, Hong Kong and the Philippines, to the two extra films, for a consideration of US$5,000. Thereafter, under a contract (which took the form of a ‘Licence Agreement’) dated 22 December 1998, the Applicant acquired the “exclusive and unrestricted” rights; those rights were expressed to be “continuous in perpetuity”.

(ii) The Applicant’s indemnity obligation under the 1981 agreement

It is convenient at this point to provide some of the detail and factual surroundings of the indemnity obligation accepted by the Applicant under the 1981 agreement.

On 8 October 1971, SR entered into a deed of assignment whereby it assigned rights in perpetuity to a collection of 125 films forming part of the R film library to an Australian television station. The territory covered by the rights was limited to four Australian capital cities. Under that assignment, SR had an obligation to provide quality prints which the television station could use for its broadcasts. As at September 1981, SR had managed to supply to the television station the prints for all but 11 of the films. As part of the 1981 agreement, therefore, SR required the Applicant to assume the cost of fulfilling the obligation to supply the remaining prints. Mr A estimated that the cost of supplying the prints would have been between $26,000 and $28,000. It is significant to note that the Applicant was the natural candidate to take over the obligation because, in 1977, it had acquired from SR the perpetual rights over those films for the remainder of Australia.

Ultimately, however, the Applicant never had to supply those prints, as Mr A was able to come to a separate arrangement with the television station concerned in satisfaction of the obligation to supply the prints. That arrangement involved a film (“G”) in respect of which the Applicant had independently acquired a limited licence in 1981, but had been unable to on-licence. Mr A therefore approached the television station and offered to exchange the rights to G in return for a waiver of the television station’s remaining rights to the prints; this was subsequently agreed.

One further fact is significant. Although the circumstances are not at all clear from Mr A’s evidence, (and in fact are somewhat odd) it appears that at some later time, Mr A gave (free of charge), to that television station, the perpetual rights over the 125 films in the R library, for the rest of Australia.

As an aspect of the Applicant’s position as the sole agent in South East Asia for the English company, it was Mr A’s custom to travel each year to various South East Asian countries. From 1982, Mr A would go to 10 or 11 countries in that region for a period of three to four weeks each year. His main object while in these countries was to negotiate licence agreements for the program material of the English company (for which, it will be recalled, he received a commission from the English company). However, he also used that opportunity to attempt to exploit the films for which the Applicant owned licences in its own right. He therefore maintained, in the film catalogue which he would present each year to representatives of the television stations in South East Asia - together with the programs of the English company - films and programs for which the Applicant had acquired both limited and perpetual rights. Mr A’s evidence was that he was occasionally able to achieve a licensing arrangement in relation to some of the films and series for which he held limited rights, but it was far more difficult to achieve licensing arrangements for the films for which he had perpetual rights. A sub-licence in South East Asia in respect of a film from one of the perpetual rights libraries was rare, but was nevertheless acheived on occassion; however, his evidence was that he never succeeded, in the entire period during which he held the rights, to sub-licence a film in South East Asia from the J film library. This was because, as Mr A explained, during the 1980’s and early 1990’s the television producers in South East Asia were simply not interested in black and white films.

(iv) Disposal of the J film library rights in Hong Kong and Philippines

From the beginning of the 1990’s, Mr A became increasingly dissatisfied with his endeavours in South East Asia. He resented the compulsory travel, the pressure which attended those business trips, and the time spent away from his family in Australia; he began to seek ways of ending his agency for the English production company in South East Asia and of concentrating his efforts on the Australian and New Zealand markets. He communicated this dissatisfaction to an individual named S, who in the past had been the chief executive officer of the English company, but who had since started up his own film and television distribution company, called “CF”. The Applicant had had close dealings with CF in the past (going back to 1985) by way of joint distributorships and otherwise. In October 1992, S offered to buy two out of the three ‘perpetual rights libraries’ which the Applicant owned. Mr A did not want, however, to sell any of the perpetual rights for Australia or New Zealand, but made it clear to S that the J film library rights for Hong Kong and the Philippines were available. In April 1993, Mr A and S had reached agreement that CF would acquire from the Applicant the perpetual rights of the J film library for Hong Kong and Philippines for the sum of US$350,000.

The following extracts contain explanations by Mr A as to how that price was arrived at, and of the market forces of that time which produced the very substantial sum:

South East Asia was becoming the new frontier in international broadcasting and it had become the flavour of the month for everyone in broadcasting. The countries in South East Asia were developing at a rapid rate, there was a burgeoning middle class with money to spend and broadcasting and advertising was booming. Prices for programs and movies were beginning to skyrocket due to increased competition... Product that had not been saleable for years was now being voraciously consumed... [Witness Statement, paragraph 15]

...Asian television was absolutely booming... in Hong Kong, which for decades had... two free to air English and two free to air Chinese stations, a licence was given to Wharf Cable to cable Hong Kong and they were staring out with 16 stations... In the Philippines, which had a limited amount of free to air VHF television, the government started granting UHF licences. Cable started in the Philippines. Satellite delivery started in the Philippines; the stations were starting everywhere, and at that stage Hong Kong were paying round US$5,000 to US$7,500 for movies for TV for two runs. Philippines round the same... So here was a deal in perpetuity, forever, so that seemed to me to be a reasonable price. [Transcript, p 40-1]

...They always buy two runs, two years in the Philippines and in Hong Kong [and]... the benchmark price is about 5,000 to 7,500... Well mine were rights in perpetuity. So I said, okay, at the lower end of the scale a picture is worth, say, 10,000 in both territories and hence I hit a figure round about that mark, 8000 or so, [per film] as a starting point... [Transcript, p 58]

There are a number of points to be made about this agreement. The first point is that the shell agreement (including the recitals but excluding the Rider) was a standard format which Mr A used throughout the business, and pulled ‘off the shelf’ for this purpose. The second point, which follows on from the first, is that it contains a number of conflicting terms, especially as between the recitals and the small print on the one hand, and the Rider on the other. This reflects the fact that Mr A did not read the small print, nor, he said, did he intend that the terms would apply to the agreement between the Applicant and CF (and he testified that S would be of the same opinion). In this respect, he said:

...all I can say is that in almost the 40 years that I’ve been in the program distribution business and in the station business in Australia, I can’t remember one single time that the fine print of contracts has been referred to in the interpretation of who’s got what rights. [Transcript, p 45]


Therefore, by way of example, although the fine print refers to delivery by the ‘Licensee’ of prints of the films, clause 2 of the ‘Rider’ makes it clear that if CF wanted prints it was to obtain them at its own cost; and although the recitals refer to the disposal of exhibition rights only, the Rider suggests that all the rights that the Applicant had were disposed of, that is, all the rights in the films excluding the film remake rights. It also appears that the use of the word “episode” in the table (qua ‘No. & Duration of Episodes’ and ‘Licence Fee payable Per Episode’) was a misnomer, in so far as it was a collection of feature films which were the subject of the agreement and not a series of episodes from a television program.

The Applicant contended, however, that the contract contains a far more fundamental misnomer, namely, that although it was in the form of a ‘licence agreement’, and although the parties are referred to as ‘licensee’ and ‘licensor’, in fact it was intended (as referred to in the Rider) as a “purchase”, or in legal terms as an assignment - or in any event, an outright disposal by the Applicant such that it retained no rights at all in the J film library in relation to the territories of Hong Kong and the Philippines. It will be necessary to make some comments on the legal characterisation of this agreement at a later stage of these Reasons.

The last point to be mentioned about the 1993 agreement is that it contains a significant error. In the table under the heading ‘No. & Duration of Episodes’ it was stated that the agreement concerned 40 feature films, whereas in fact the Rider listed 44 titles of films which were the subject of the agreement. I mention this because the Respondent put it to Mr A that if the correct number of films was 44, and if the “Licence Fee Payable Per Episode” was $US8,750, then the true “Total Licence Fee” should have been $US385,000 (and hence that that was the amount which should be assessable). However, Mr A replied, and I accept, that it was the total consideration ($US350,000) which had been agreed upon with S, and that the amount per film was calculated in error as a consequence of the fact that the number of films had been typed incorrectly.

(v) Applicant’s accounting treatment in relation to the J film library

When the Applicant received the first instalment under the 1993 agreement in July 1993, it treated that receipt, in both its financial accounts and later in its tax return for the relevant year, as a receipt on income account. Indeed, the evidence indicates that throughout the Applicant’s dealings with the J library, the library was treated, for accounting purposes, as being on income account. Thus, both the original $1250 which was used to acquire the initial 42 films of J library in September 1981, and the later US$5,000 used to acquire the subsequent two films of the J library in 1988, were expensed and claimed as a deduction in their respective years of income. Furthermore, in its financial accounts for the period during which it held the rights to the J film library, the Applicant did not record the J library as an asset.

The Applicant contended that this previous accounting treatment was in error, and in particular, that its initial return of the first instalment as income for the 1994 year was merely because entries were made by an “inexperienced accounts clerk”.

  1. THE ARGUMENTS

The question to be determined is whether the proceeds from the disposal of the J library under the 1993 agreement are properly assessable in the hands of the Applicant.

(i) The Applicant’s argument

The Applicant argues that the proceeds from the disposal of the J library are properly to be regarded as capital receipts. Its essential argument is that its business consists in granting and on-licensing short term licences over films and television programs, and not in “selling” perpetual rights to libraries of films. This argument was summed up as follows:

[The Applicant] has never been in the business of purchasing films for resale, and has never sold film investments. [The Applicant] is... in the business of licensing and on licensing television programs, but this business ... does not involve the acquisition or disposal of perpetual rights over films... In fact in the 26 years of [the Applicant’s] existence the [J] sale has been the only disposal of a film library; all other activities in that period have related to commission or licence income and not to the sale of film rights. (T Documents, p 51)


Indeed, the Applicant claims that the perpetual rights which it has acquired over the years should be regarded as part of the framework or foundation of its business - they are assets which enable it to grant short term licences and hence derive income - and so the disposal should be regarded as the realisation of a structural asset.

Furthermore, the Applicant asserts that it did not acquire the library for the purpose of sale, or as part of a profit-making scheme or undertaking. It claims that the only reason why it acquired the library was to derive film licensing income by way of granting short term licences over individual films in the library.

(ii) The Respondent’s argument

The Respondent, on the other hand, submits that the proceeds are assessable under either s25(1), s25A(1), or s26(f) of the Act.

The Respondent argued that the proceeds were income according to ordinary concepts because the disposal of the J film library was an activity forming part of the normal course of the Applicant’s business. Its submission was that the 1993 agreement should be regarded (in accordance with its express terms) as a licence agreement (albeit for a different nominal term than usual) and hence that it was an activity of the kind typically engaged in by the Applicant - ie licensing films. Alternatively, the Respondent argued that, even if the 1993 agreement was to be regarded as an outright “sale” of the relevant rights, that act was still one which was part of the normal business, or at least an ordinary incident of the normal business, of the Applicant - which, it was submitted, was properly characterised as a motion picture distribution business concerned with the taking and granting of rights to films and television programs, in whichever way that could be done. Its argument in this respect was summed up as follows:

If the 1993 licence agreement is construed as the sale of rights to broadcast, it has not only fulfilled the taxpayer’s activity but also yielded a profit. As the sale of the rights to broadcast was part of the taxpayer’s business in carrying on the distribution of films, it is considered that the agreement was made in the course of the taxpayer’s business. [T documents, p 61]


The Respondent also contended that when the Applicant acquired the J library, its purpose was to make a profit from the films, and therefore (following Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Myer Emporium [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 163 CLR 199; 87 ATC 4363), even if the disposal in 1993 is to be regarded as falling outside of the ordinary course of business, the proceeds therefrom are nonetheless assessable.

The Respondent further argued that the proceeds were caught under the second limb of s25A(1); that the circumstances of the Applicant’s acquisition of the J library, together with the efforts of Mr A in finding quality prints with which to exhibit the films and its final disposal of the rights, indicate a scheme for profit making, and hence that the proceeds are assessable under that section.

I should note that the Respondent also claims that the proceeds are assessable according to s26(f) as a royalty under the extended meaning of that term in s6(1) of the Act. The Respondent has not sought to argue, however, that the sums received were royalties according to the ordinary meaning of that word under the common law.

  1. THE LAW

(I) Significance of Applicant’s accounting treatment

Before I move on to the principles which apply to this matter, I should address one of the submissions of the Respondent.

The Respondent placed weight on the Applicant’s own initial characterisation of the J film library and the way it treated the receipt of the first instalment. Counsel for the Respondent submitted that in initially treating the J films as income assets, the Applicant had “nailed its colours to the mast”, and that it was possible to infer that the Applicant is now merely trying “an ex post facto re-characterisation of the receipt in hope of obtaining a tax advantage by claiming the receipt to be capital”. The Respondent further submitted that “the Applicant, having so nailed its colours to the revenue mast from the outset, and then loyally stuck to them consistently throughout the period of ownership, is bound to treat the proceeds of disposal as income” (Respondent’s Outline of Submissions).

With respect, I have never understood the law to be that a taxpayer is bound to the characterisation of a receipt simply because the receipt is reflected as such in its accounts. The task of characterisation is to be carried out according to the tests enunciated in the decided cases and can not be affected by any ‘estoppel’ as such. As Lord Gorell said in Edinburgh Life Assurance Co v Lord Advocate (1909) 5 TC 472 at 491:

...the rights of the Crown [do not] depend upon the book-keeping of the company;... nor do I think the liabilities of the company can be made to depend upon the system of accounts.


(See also Collins (HM Inspector of Taxes) v The Firth-Brearley Stainless Steel Syndicate Limited (1925) 9 TC 520 at 569, per Pollock MR.) It may even be putting it too highly to say that a taxpayer’s own characterisation is a matter amongst many to be taken into account when ascertaining the true character of a receipt. We are not here concerned with a generally accepted accounting practice, or rule of thumb, which suggests what the legal characterisation should be; in such circumstances the accounting practice may be considered as relevant in determining the legal characterisation: Federal Commissioner of Taxation v James Flood Pty Ltd [1953] HCA 65; (1953) 5 AITR 579 at 585. Rather we are concerned with a decision made by a particular taxpayer which is now claimed to have been made in error. The taxpayer is bound to pay tax only on that which is duly assessable, and, as the courts have said, the Commissioner is entitled to receive “not a penny more and not a penny less” (Lighthouse Philatelics Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 91 ATC 4942) than the amount correctly ascertained by law. Furthermore, I think that it is a clear inference from s170(6) of the Act, which contemplates the amendment of an assessment by the Commissioner as a result of an application by the taxpayer, that an assessment may be amended notwithstanding that the taxpayer had previously adopted a position in relation to its taxable income from which it later (but within the prescribed time) seeks to resile.

In short, I do not think that anything at all turns on the fact that the Applicant initially regarded its receipt from the disposal of the J film library as income or expensed its cost, or on the fact that the Applicant appeared to treat the J film library, through the years, as a revenue asset. Whether it is, or is not, assessable depends on the principles as they have been distilled in the relevant cases. It is to those cases to which I now turn.

(ii) Assessability according to ordinary concepts of income

The starting point, when considering the disposal of an intellectual property right, is the dictum of Lord Denning MR in the case of Murray (Inspector of Taxes) v Imperial Chemical Industries Ltd. (1967) 1 Ch. 1038 (at 1052):

...it seems to me fairly clear that if, and in so far as, a man disposes of patent rights outright (for example, by an assignment of his patent, or by the grant of an exclusive licence) and receives in return royalties calculated by reference to the actual user, the royalties are clearly revenue receipts. If, and so far as, he disposes of them for annual payments over the period, which can fairly be regarded as compensation for the user during the period, then those also are revenue receipts... If, and so far as, he disposes of the patent rights outright for a lump sum, which is arrived at by reference to some anticipated quantum of user, it will normally be income in the hands of the recipient... But if, and in so far as, he disposes of them outright for a lump sum which has no reference to anticipated user, it will normally be capital...


In the present case, despite the fact that the relevant sum was calculated with an eye to what the films were worth in terms of their licence fee capacity, I think it is quite clear that the proceeds of the disposal were calculated without reference to any anticipated amount of future use by CR. But (as is clear from the above passage) this does not automatically result in the conclusion that the proceeds are capital. As Hill J said in Henry Jones (IXL) Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 91 ATC 4663 (at 4674):

If an industrial property right is licensed for a lump sum payment the question whether the lump sum is income or capital will depend on all the circumstances.


One of the central circumstances affecting the characterisation of the receipt is the relationship between the asset disposed of and the business of the taxpayer. Thus while it may be that, prima facie, the disposal of an intellectual property asset for a lump sum without reference to user is capital, “it is equally well established that enhanced values obtained from realisation... may be assessable, where what is done is not merely a realisation or change of investment, but an act done in what is truly the carrying on, or carrying out, of a business”: California Copper Syndicate v Harris (1904) 5 TC 159.

It is clear, therefore, that if a taxpayer disposes of intellectual property rights, even for a lump sum without reference to user, in a transaction which is properly to be regarded as being carried out as part of its business, then the proceeds from that transaction will be received as income.

In my view the concept of a transaction which is part of the “carrying on, or carrying out, of a business”, as that phrase was used in California Copper (supra), has come to be understood by the courts as embracing two essential notions. The first is the concept of a transaction which is within the “ordinary course of business”; the second (deriving specifically from the decision of the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Myer Emporium [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 163 CLR 199; 87 ATC 4363 is the concept of a transaction which, while outside the ordinary course of business, is still considered to be part of the “carrying out of business” because, at the time it was entered into, the taxpayer had the intention or purpose of making a ‘relevant’ profit.

The essential fabric of both of these two notions, however, and the reason why each are considered to give rise to gains which are income, is their association with a purpose of profit-making on behalf of the taxpayer: Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Spedley Securities Limited 88 ATC 4126 at 4130.

Where, on the one hand, a transaction is found to be outside the taxpayer’s ordinary course of business, it is imperative to make a positive finding of fact that the transaction was attended with the ‘relevant’ profit making purpose. What is that “relevant profit-making purpose”? The cases make it clear that the proceeds of the sale of an asset which is sold in a transaction outside the ordinary course of business will only be assessable if that asset was acquired with a purpose of profit making “by the very means by which the profit was in fact made”: Westfield Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 91 ATC 4234 at 4243; see also Myer (supra), at 210; SP Investments Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 93 ATC 4170 at 4176-7; Montgomery v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 98 ATC 4120 at 4139. In other words, there must be an identity between the purpose for which the property was acquired and the actual method of its ultimate disposal. It may still be possible, however, to find this identity in a scenario where, upon acquisition, the taxpayer intended to profit from acquisition of the asset but had not yet determined the precise means by which that was to be done. In such circumstances, the courts appear to make a distinction between two situations: (1) where the taxpayer could be said to have had, as one of number of alternate purposes of acquisition, a purpose to profit by the means ultimately utilised; and (2) where the taxpayer acquired the asset for purposes other than profit-making by the means ultimately used, albeit at the time of acquisition, the taxpayer was aware of the possibility that the asset could be disposed of in that way. In the latter situation the gains ultimately made are not assessable, while in the former situation, they are assessable. This distinction is, in my view, apparent from the following passage in the judgment of Hill J (with whom Lockhart J and Gummow J agreed) in Westfield, (supra), (at 4243):

While a profit-making scheme may lack specificity of detail, the mode of achieving that profit must be one contemplated by the taxpayer as at least one of the alternatives by which the profit could be realised... But, even if that goes too far, it is difficult to conceive of a case where a taxpayer would be said to have made a profit from the carrying on, or the carrying out of a profit-making scheme, where, in the case of a scheme involving the acquisition and resale of land, there was, at the time of acquisition, no purpose of resale of land, but only the possibility (present, one may observe, the case of every acquisition of land) that the land may be resold. The same may be said to be the case where s. 25(1) of the Act in involved.


Where, on the other hand, the transaction which gives rise to the profit is found to be part of the “ordinary course” of the taxpayer’s business, it can be validly inferred (without the necessity of making a specific finding) that the purpose of profit-making is present; that is because the identification of the transaction with the normal business activity of the taxpayer (which is assumed to be carried out with a view to a profit) ‘stamps’ the transaction with the purpose of profit-making: Myer, (supra) at 209; Westfield, (supra) at 4242. One question which arises, however, is this: where a transaction is found to be within the taxpayer’s ordinary course of business, is it necessary (as with the “isolated transaction” situation) to demonstrate that when the property was acquired by the taxpayer it was acquired for the purpose of profit-making by the very means by which the profit was ultimately made? In my view this question was addressed, and answered in the negative, by Lockhart J (with whom Jenkinson and Gummow JJ agreed) in CMI Services Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 90 ATC 4428 at 4435 where His Honour said, (referring to the judgment of Jacobs J in London Australia Investment Company Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1977] HCA 50; (1977) 138 CLR 106):

I do not read Jacobs J’s reasons for judgment [in London Australia] as departing from the well established principles which determine whether a taxpayer has derived income according to ordinary concepts pursuant to sec. 25(1) and which do not require that a taxpayer who carries on a business must necessarily have a purpose of resale at a profit when assets are acquired in the course of carrying on the business.


Thus, where it is found that a transaction occurs within the “ordinary course of business”, the proceeds from that transaction will be assessable notwithstanding that the taxpayer may have acquired the asset initially for the purpose of profit-making by means other than the those ultimately utilised.

It is thus necessary to enquire as to when an activity of a taxpayer will be regarded as falling within the “ordinary course” of that taxpayer’s business? It has been made clear in several cases that the phrase “ordinary course of business” is not to be interpreted in a temporal sense: Spedley Securities, (supra) at 4130; Westfield, (supra) at 4242; Lees & Leech Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 97 ATC 4407 at 4414. That is to say, a profit made by a taxpayer, or an activity engaged in by a taxpayer, is not automatically within the “ordinary course of business” (for assessability purposes) simply because it takes place while the taxpayer is carrying on business. The mere propinquity of the activity or profit with the rest of the taxpayer’s activities is not enough to result in its identification with the taxpayer’s “ordinary course of business”. An example of a use of the phrase “ordinary course of business” in this temporal (non-assessability) sense is given by Hill J in Westfield, (supra), (at 4242):

A taxpayer carrying on a business might sell its headquarters in order to move to larger premises and make a profit over historical cost. The transaction of sale may be one which arises in the ordinary course of the taxpayer’s business, but that profit will not ordinarily be income...[Emphasis added]


Distinguishing the temporal sense of the phrase from the use of the phrase for assessability purposes is essential; failure to make the distinction has the result that “any receipt by a business would necessarily be of an income nature, and this would be contrary to authority, to the Act itself and to basic concepts concerning the distinction between capital and income”: per Fox, Fisher and Sheppard JJ, in Spedley Securities (supra), at 4130.

Calculating which activities fall within the “ordinary course” of a taxpayer’s business, therefore, is a matter of ascertaining precisely the scope of its ordinary business. Thus the courts have said that it is necessary to undertake a “wide survey and an exact scrutiny of the taxpayer’s activities”: per Gibbs J in London Australia (supra), at 116, citing Western Goldmines NL v Commissioner of Taxation (WA) [1938] HCA 5; (1937-1938) 59 CLR 729 at 740. It is not possible to state definitively the kind of activities which will fall into that category, and nor is it possible to state an heuristic, or rule of thumb, that the scope of businesses is to be regarded broadly or narrowly; each case is a creature of its own facts - some businesses are narrow in scope, and some are wide in scope. The courts have, however, suggested various factors to take into account when attempting to ascertain whether a particular payment is to be regarded as within the scope of a company’s ordinary business. Thus in London Australia (supra), Gibbs J (citing Rumah Property Co. Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1928] HCA 22; (1928) 41 CLR 148 at 154) stated the relevant factors as follows: (at 116)

...it is necessary to have regard to the nature of the company, the character of the assets realised, the nature of the business carried on by the company and the particular realisation which produced the profit.


The task for the Tribunal in this respect was summarised by Hill J in Lees & Leech (supra) in the following terms (at 4415):

Two questions must be initially asked in a case such as the present. The first is what the payment in question was for. Having answered that question, it is necessary to identify the nature of the taxpayer’s business to determine the relationship between what the relevant payment was for on the one hand, and the taxpayer’s business on the other.


Two further points need to be made in relation to the “ordinary course of business”. The first is that an activity is not to be regarded as falling outside the “ordinary course of business” merely because it is unusual judged by reference to the activities in which the taxpayer typically is engaged, or indeed is the only transaction of its kind in the course of the taxpayer’s history. As Jacobs J said in London Australia, (supra), (at 129):

...it must be made quite clear that frequency of an activity is not synonymous with business. There may be no business despite frequency and on the other hand there may be a business where the activity is an isolated one. Every business must begin with an initial transaction.


In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Hyteco Hiring Pty Limited 92 ATC 4694, Hill J (with whom Black CJ and Wilcox J agreed) made the point that it is not determinative that the transaction occurs only once (at 4700):

While regularity of receipt may often indicate that particular amounts have the character of income,... mere regularity on its own will seldom be determinative... Likewise, a single payment may, in a particular case, be income, notwithstanding that it be not repeated: cf Smith v FC of T 87 ATC 4742; [1987] HCA 48; (1988) 164 CLR 513.


Reference may also be made to the recent High Court case of Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Orica [1998] HCA 33, wherein Gaudron, McHugh, Kirby and Hayne JJ said in the context of a consideration of income according to ordinary concepts (at para. 82):

The present transaction was a singular transaction, not part of the regular means whereby the taxpayer obtained returns. It must be acknowledged, of course, that the fact that it was a singular transaction is important but, standing alone, is not determinative of the characterisation of a “profit” or “gain” under consideration.


The second point to make in relation to the “ordinary course of business” is that a transaction or operation may be regarded as within the “ordinary course of business” if, notwithstanding that it is not directly the main business activity engaged in by the taxpayer, it is a transaction or operation which is an ordinary incident of the main business activity. Thus Hill J said in Westfield, (supra), (at 4242):

...where the transaction is an ordinary incident of the business activity of the taxpayer, albeit not directly its main business activity...[t]he profit-making purpose can be inferred from the association of the transaction... with that business activity.


Justice Hill revisited this statement in Hyteco Hiring (supra). That case concerned the characterisation of payments received from selling forklift trucks where the main business of the taxpayer was in hiring the trucks. The sales were made when the trucks were unsuitable for further hiring. The Commissioner argued that the sales of the ex-hire trucks was an ordinary incident of the taxpayer’s hiring business. The Full Federal Court, however, held that the business of the taxpayer was in hiring trucks and not selling them, and therefore the proceeds from such sale were non-assessable. In dealing with the submission of the Commissioner, Hill J sought to clarify what he had said in the above passage in Westfield. He explained that the “ordinary incident” concept derived from a consideration of the banking and insurance cases, where although the main business of the taxpayer was banking or insurance, it was found that engaging in investments was “so integrally related to the business that it may truly be said that the act of realising those investments is an act done in the carrying on of the banking or insurance business” (92 ATC at 4703).

His Honour continued (at 4703-4704):

It may well be that the proper characterisation of a banking business, for example, is both a banking and investment business. But whatever be the correct characterisation of a banking or insurance business, the making and realising of investments is so integral to the banking or insurance business that a profit on realisation will form part of the income of banking or insurance business. It is in that sense that I said in Westfield that the profit-making purpose inherent in the realisation can be inferred from the association of the investment activity to the main business activity.... However, in the sense in which I used the words in Westfield, the sale of forklift trucks at a profit is not an ordinary incident of the business activity of a company hiring out such trucks, any more than the sale of redundant typewriters or word processing equipment is an ordinary incident of the business of a solicitor or accountant.


The import of that passage, as I understand it, is that the “ordinary incident” concept is not to be understood as an extension of the “ordinary course of business” idea; ‘ordinary incident’ refers to those occasions where the activity is supplementary to the main business but still regarded as being included within the scope of the business for income taxation purposes. In other words, that “incidental activity” would still fall within what would be properly ascertained as the scope of the business through a “wide survey and exact scrutiny” of the taxpayer’s activities. The notion of “ordinary incident” indicates that activities which are perhaps outside the immediate description of the business can still be regarded as part of the ‘ordinary course of that business’ if its relationship to the main description is close enough to be identified with it, or as part and parcel of it, or as a natural (Hill J uses the word “integral”) aspect of it.

On the other hand, it is to be noted that not all authorities regard the notion of the ‘ordinary incident’ as being a step towards concluding that the activity is within the “ordinary course of business” (see, for example, Montgomery v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 98 ATC 4120, per Lockhart J at 4139), preferring to regard it as a third notion within the California Copper concept of “carrying on, or carrying out, of a business”. It is likely that nothing much turns on this difference in categorisation. In respect of both categorisations, it will be still necessary to find that the relevant activity was within the scope of the business according to the “survey and scrutiny” and the factors previously mentioned.

(iii) Some relevant cases closer to the facts of the present case

Although it is well settled that each case depends on its own facts, it is instructive to consider some of the cases which have dealt with the disposals of intellectual property rights. I might say at the outset that each of these cases indicate just how crucial it is to ascertain precisely the scope of the business carried on by the taxpayer.

The first case to consider is Collins (HM Inspector of Taxes) v The Firth-Brearley Stainless Steel Syndicate Limited (1925) 9 TC 520. In that case the taxpayer company was formed for the purpose of exploiting various patents over an invention of a process to make stainless steel, and the company’s business consisted mainly in licensing the rights to use that patent. The company, however, assigned, or ‘sold’, its patent rights on two occasions; on one occasion it sold the Japanese rights to a Japanese company and on the other it sold the American rights to an American company. The issue in the case was whether the proceeds from those sales were income. The company argued that the patent rights were capital assets and that therefore the disposal of those rights were not assessable. The Inspector of Taxes took the view that the sums were assessable on the grounds that the taxpayer was carrying out a business of disposing of patents and inventions. The General Purpose Commissioners found that the amounts were on capital account, and not assessable. The Inspector of Taxes appealed to the High Court.

Rowlatt J at first instance held that the amounts from the sales were assessable on the ground that it was part of the company’s trade to sell the patents it had acquired. The Court of Appeal overturned the decision, for two reasons. Firstly, it decided that the effect of Rowlatt J’s judgment was to overturn a factual finding of the Commissioners which itself was not impeachable; the Court of Appeal found that there was no error of law by the Commissioners and there was abundant evidence on which to base their finding (at 568-9, 570, 573). Secondly, the Court of Appeal found that the sums were capital in any case. The court was of the view that, on the facts of that case, the proper scope of the company was not to be regarded as encompassing the outright sale of its patent rights in a particular country. Lord Pollock, Master of the Rolls, said (at 569):

... if the business of the company was to buy here there and anywhere all and sundry patents and then to sell those patents at a profit it would be clear that they were carrying on the business of acquiring and selling patents. But it does not appear to me that that would be a true statement of what their business is. It appears to me that they had got one patent which is protected in different countries, but it is a single patent, and they have not got and I cannot see the intention on their part of acquiring patents many and sundry.


Lord Justice Atkin also characterised the business in this way; emphasising the fact that the patent rights were the company’s “one capital asset”. He said (at 573):

It appears to me that the Commissioners, who seem to have been experienced business men, took the right view in this case, and came to the conclusion that this company was formed for the purpose of acquiring these patent rights as its one capital asset and that it was not to make money by retailing them or peddling them, but to treat the one asset that it acquired as its capital and to use it accordingly...


Lord Justice Atkin was of the view also that it was easy to conclude that the various sales were mere conversions of one capital asset into another, since (with at least the American sale) the sale was in return for a forty (or so) percent interest in the American company, which had the effect that it would continue receiving income from the relevant patent (at 575).

A similar fact situation arose shortly after Collins in the case of Ducker v Rees Roturbo Development Syndicate (1928) 13 TC 366. In that case the taxpayer company was incorporated for the purpose of exploiting patent rights over a group of inventions in relation to centrifugal turbine pumps. The Company owned the majority share in various patents over those inventions, including in the United Kingdom and various overseas countries. Its usual course was to licence the patents to other companies to manufacture and sell the relevant invention. Most of the licensees however, refused to take the licence unless it was accompanied by an option to buy the patent outright. The company, which preferred to retain the patents, would therefore set the option price at a prohibitive level. In 1912 the company granted the patent rights for USA to an American company with an option to sell. The American company eventually exercised that option, and the Company was assessed for the profits of the sale. The Company disputed the assessment on the grounds that it was a sale of a capital asset. The Special Purposes Commissioners (deciding the issue at the time after Rowlatt J’s judgment in Collins but before the judgment of the Court of Appeal) found that the amounts from the sale were assessable on the grounds that the sale was part of the business of the taxpayer.

Rowlatt J at first instance, regarded himself bound by the factual finding of the Commissioners, which he interpreted (they were somewhat ambiguous) as being that the sale was made in the ordinary course of the taxpayer’s business. He regarded that finding to have been made with evidence to support it, and so felt he could not overturn the decision (even though he expressed some consternation at the fact that Collins was “decided one way and this case [was] being decided the other way - the facts are so very nearly the same”: at 381).

The majority of the Court of Appeal reversed that decision on the basis that Rowlatt J misinterpreted the factual findings of the Commissioners; it was said that they did not so much make a finding that the sale was within the ordinary course of business, as that they attempted to exploit an ambiguity in Rowlatt J’s judgment in Collins, and that the proper finding was that the sale was not in the ordinary course of business - as the Court of Appeal had found in Collins, which was regarded as indistinguishable from that case. Both Hanworth MR and Sargent LJ thought that the assets were clearly capital assets (see p 386, 394). Lord Justice Scrutton dissented, agreeing with Rowlatt J that the Commissioners had made a finding on fact on evidence which justified that decision and that therefore there was no appeal. Interestingly, however, Scrutton LJ mentioned that the Commissioners in both that case and the Collins case could have justifiably reached a contrary decision to what they in fact reached (at 392).

The House of Lords, however, restored the judgment of Rowlatt J. Lord Buckmaster (with whom the rest of the House agreed) took the view that the finding by the Commissioners was not to be regarded as having been based on an erroneous understanding of the law as a result of the decision of Rowlatt J in Collins, and hence, as a result of both that and the fact that there was “abundant evidence upon which their conclusions of fact could be supported”, their factual findings were entitled to have been given their full weight. Lord Buckmaster, moreover, referring to a finding by the Commissioners that the possibility of sale was always contemplated by the Company, went on to say that the transaction in that case was not (at 398):

...a mere accidental dealing with a particular class of property, but... was part of the Company’s business which, though not of necessity the line on which it desired its business most extensively to develop, was one which it was prepared to undertake.


This passage raises an interesting question insofar as interpretation of the judgment is concerned: was this reference to “part of the Company’s business” a reference to a finding that the transaction occurred within the “ordinary course” of the business? After considering the above passage from Lord Buckmaster, the High Court in Myer Emporium (supra), said the following: (163 CLR at 211)

The important proposition to be derived from California Copper and Ducker is that a receipt may constitute income, if it arises from an isolated business operation or commercial transaction entered into otherwise than in the ordinary course of the carrying on of the taxpayer’s business, so long as the taxpayer entered into the transaction with the intention or purpose of making a relevant profit or gain from the transaction.


A possible inference from this passage in Myer is that the High Court interpreted Rees Roturbo as being a case concerned with a gain made outside of the “ordinary course of business”, but which was nonetheless assessable due to the presence of the requisite profit-making purpose. Interpreting Rees Roturbo in such a way may make the case more easily reconcilable with Collins, but I do not think that is so easily done; it is quite possible to regard Myer as not having interpreted Rees Roturbo so narrowly.

The main point to be stressed about these two cases, despite the difference in final result (and however Rees Roturbo is interpreted), is that each turned on the specific finding of the Commissioners as to what the proper scope of the taxpayer’s business was. The ultimate task, in the Court of Appeal and in the House of Lords in each of Collins and Rees Roturbo respectively, was primarily a consideration of whether, in each of the factual situations, the Commissioners had accurately defined that scope.

Another case where the scope the taxpayer’s activities was crucial in the context of disposals of intellectual property was Mackenzie v Arnold (1952) 33 TC 363. In that case an author who typically received royalties for his books entered into an agreement with a publishing house for the partial assignment of the copyright in a number of those books. The full Court of Appeal (Somervell, Denning and Romer LJJ) held that the sums were assessable. In an argument that parallels somewhat one made by the Respondent in this case, Romer LJ said:

An author may derive profit from the exercise of his skill and experience as a writer by receiving royalties from his books, by selling the copyrights of his books, or partly by doing the one and partly by doing the other. It seems to me that whichever way is adopted by the author it has the similar characteristic of being a way of exploiting and bringing to the notice of the public, for the benefit of the author, his works, and the profit which comes to him from whichever of the ways he adopts is to be regarded as a profit or gain for the year of the assessment in which he receives it, arising or accruing to him from his profession... at first sight it might seem wrong to some minds, and undoubtedly has, that what would from some points of view appear to be a capital asset, namely, the copyright of a book, the exclusive right to use it for all kinds of purposes, should if sold, be treated as income. When one realises that that is only one way of making a profit from the exercise of a writer’s profession I think really that those who believe it is unjust might cease to do so.


In that case the author had, in previous years, in fact sold the outright copyright on three separate occasions, but the Court of Appeal’s decision did not turn on this fact. Their Lordships were of the view that the profession of the taxpayer was not the receiving of royalties, but the making available of his works to the public, “in whichever of the ways he adopts” to do so.

  1. APPLICATION OF THE LAW TO THE PRESENT CASE

It is now imperative to consider whether the 1993 disposal of the J library is to be regarded as an activity which falls within the scope of the Applicant’s business. I propose to set out the relevant findings according to the factors which the cases make clear are required for consideration. To reiterate, these factors are: (1) the nature of the company and the nature of the business carried on by the company; (2) the nature and character of the assets realised; and (3) the nature of the particular realisation which produced the profit.

Before I embark on an examination of whether the relevant transaction can be said to be within the scope of the Applicant’s business, I should perhaps indicate the opinion of this Tribunal on the question of the Applicant’s purpose at the time it acquired the J library in 1981.

(i) The Applicant’s purpose in acquiring the J film library

The Applicant’s evidence in this regard was that it acquired the J library with one purpose and one purpose only, namely, to earn licence fee income by granting short term (and otherwise limited) licences. In cross-examination, Mr A was asked why he acquired the J library, his response was simply: “I felt at that time that I would be able to make money out of them” (Transcript, p 25). At a later point, there was the following exchange:

Mr Hamilton: ...Would it be accurate to say that licensing was but one of the ways that you might have turned it to account and you did not really have a fixed view one way or the other how you would make money out of it?

Mr A: I only had one view and that was to licence the pictures to free to air TV.

Mr Hamilton: Do you have any other evidence of any sort to indicate that? Have you got any board minutes or any documentary corroboration of that?

Mr A: [The Applicant] is a very small company and its board meetings are the minimum that are required by law.

Mr Hamilton: So it is only your statement of what the intention was?

Mr A: Yes.

Mr Hamilton: And you say that it was the sole intention?

Mr A: Yes. [Transcript, p 28-9]


I should mention at this point that merely because an applicant produces only one source of evidence to establish any particular fact does not mean that that evidence is to be disbelieved. As David Hunt J said in Allied Pastoral Holdings Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 83 ATC 4015 at 4021:

...it is not obligatory for a taxpayer, before he can discharge his burden of proof, to call all material witnesses and to produce all the material documents which support his evidence... It is certainly wiser for the taxpayer to do so in most cases so as to ensure that his own evidence is accepted, but even where he does not do so the tribunal of fact may nevertheless be sufficiently impressed with the taxpayer as a witness that his evidence is accepted without such corroboration or without the whole of such corroboration. If his evidence as to his purpose is accepted, then he has discharged his onus of proof whatever corroborative evidence he has or has not called.


I accept Mr A’s evidence as to his purpose on acquisition of the J library; I am satisfied that when Mr A acquired the J film library for the Applicant in 1981, his sole intention was to earn income by granting limited licences.

(ii) The nature of the company and the nature of the business carried on by the company

Much of the information under this head has been mentioned previously in these Reasons, but it is worth repeating the salient points. The Applicant carries on a business as a film and television program distributor. Its main business is in acting as a commission agent for production or other distribution companies in the licensing of films or programs where the other company owns the relevant rights. A second, but by no means unimportant, aspect of the business occurs where the Applicant itself acquires rights to films or programs and proceeds to licence those films or programs in its own name. Its assets in this respect are films for which it has acquired rights limited by the number of runs and the term of years, and films over which it owns the ‘perpetual rights’. In respect of the manner in which the Applicant typically disposes of the films or programs over which it has acquired rights limited by runs and years, the following extract is apposite: [at p 17 in the Transcript]

Mr Hamilton: So when you, for example, going back to [name of a television series] you might have a licence for seven years and you would then try to sub-licence it, is that...?

Mr A: Correct

Mr Hamilton: And how long would you licence it for?

Mr A: Most of the time would be for the full duration of the rights but the most profitable way in the seven year licence would be sub-licence three years and then go back and sub-licence the next four.

Mr Hamilton: Yes, because you get two bites at the cherry?

Mr A: Exactly.

Mr Hamilton: But most commonly?

Mr A: Easier said than done.

Mr Hamilton: Yes. most commonly?

Mr A: You would sell the duration of your rights. You would licence the duration.

Mr Block: So you get a licence for seven years and in effect you resell that licence for the same period?

Mr A: Yes.

Mr Block: We are not talking about [a film library] where you have got rights in perpetuity?

Mr A: Correct.

Mr Block: But if you have got [a series] for seven years you might well on-sell it for seven years?

Mr A: Yes, invariably.


Typically, therefore, when the Applicant acquired a limited licence for a film or program, it would on-licence the rights for the duration of the period available to it.

In respect of its ‘perpetual rights libraries’, it would grant licences for exhibition of the films for a limited number of years and a limited number of telecasts. Three examples were produced in evidence of licences over films in J film library where the terms were one year (one telecast), three years (one telecast) and seven years (five telecasts) respectively. It is clear, however, that the number of years and telecasts granted was a matter purely of negotiation and commercial expedience; there was no predefined maximum term aside from what would ultimately produce the greatest return on any one film. The Applicant had the perpetual rights to three film libraries, one of which was a library (the R library) of some 700 films. The rights to the ‘perpetual rights films’, including those from the J film library, were licensed predominantly but consistently in Australia and New Zealand, but there were occasions where a licence would also be granted in the South East Asian region; (the evidence was, however, that this did not occur with respect to the J film library in particular).

It was submitted for the Applicant in its original objection that “in the 26 years of [the Applicant’s] existence the [J] sale has been the only disposal of a film library; all other activities in that period have related to commission or licence income and not to the sale of film rights” [T documents, p 51]. In fact, it was revealed during the hearing that this was not strictly true. It emerged that, perhaps in some way related to the satisfaction of its indemnity obligation under the 1981 agreement, or perhaps not (it was not exactly clear), the Applicant disposed of (or more precisely, gave without receiving consideration) its perpetual rights to 125 films in the R library for the whole of Australia with the exception of four capital cities (for which the donee already owned the rights).

(iii) The nature and character of the asset realised

In order to ascertain the nature of the rights which the Applicant disposed of in 1993, it is necessary to inquire what rights were acquired by the Applicant in 1981, and that in turn depends on the nature of the rights created under the 1967 agreement.

The full title of the 1967 agreement is “Licence Agreement for Television Motion Picture Exhibition And for Any and All Other Exhibition Rights”. At first glance, thus, the agreement concerned only the exhibition rights to the listed 42 films. As with another agreement mentioned previously in these Reasons, however, the form somewhat belies the content. A section entitled “Special Notes”, provides: “All rights are given with the exception of remake film rights”. Thus it is clear that the agreement concerned all the rights covered within the ‘bundle of rights’ falling under the rubric “copyright”, with the exception of the right to remake the film. These rights were given to SR for an “unlimited” number of runs and “forever and in perpetuity” for the territories of Australia, New Zealand, Hong Kong and the Philippines.

One issue which arises is whether it could be said that SR acquired the exclusive rights to the films for the relevant territories. The word ‘exclusive’ is nowhere mentioned in the document, but the only other written clause in the contract reads as follows:

It is understood by both parties that there is an existing contract in effect for the area of Australia and New Zealand with the same parties but there is no outstanding contract in the Philippines or Hong Kong with the exception in Hong Kong of the film [AT] which upon expiry of the present contract with the Hong Kong Licensees will revert to [SR].


It was not explained in evidence what the “existing contract... with the same parties” was; nor was there any evidence as to the circumstances referable to the grant of the licence over the AT film in Hong Kong, but I think an ordinary interpretation of the clause makes it clear that the intention of the parties was that SR would, upon expiry of the only encumbrance, receive the sole rights to the relevant films for the territories for the rest of the ‘perpetual’ life of the copyright. I might add that I do not think it is necessary to determine whether the 1967 agreement is to be construed as an exclusive licence or a partial assignment of the copyright in the films. On both analyses, I think it is clear from the evidence that when in 1981 the Applicant received an assignment of the ‘perpetual’ rights to the film library, the assignment (which, it will be recalled, made no mention of the nature of the rights assigned) would have entitled the Applicant to exclusive distribution of the films in the relevant territories.

That raises another issue: what precisely is meant by the ‘perpetual’ rights to a film? The evidence of Mr A was that that term is commonly understood in the film industry to mean “forever”, and indeed, the 1967 agreement contemplates that the rights would be “forever and in perpetuity”. The Respondent pointed out, however, that copyright is not protected “forever”, and hence that a reference to ‘perpetual rights’ to a film protected by copyright amounts merely to a way of saying that the term of the rights is for as long as copyright subsists in the film.

What then was the term of the copyright remaining in the relevant territories at the time the film was disposed of by the Applicant in 1993?

In the “Applicant’s Statement of Facts and Contentions”, the following was said:

During recent discussions with the ATO about this matter the ATO has advised the Company that protection under the copyright law in the Philippines expired 30 years after first publication of the material and in Hong Kong 50 years after first publication of the material. Recent legal advice received by the Company confirms the Philippine period of 30 years. However for Hong Kong the recently enacted Copyright Ordinance 1997 has extended the protection period that applied at the time the Company sold the rights in July 1993. This period is now 50 years from the end of the calendar year in which the author dies rather than 50 years from the end of the calendar year in which the film was first published.

In any event the Company was unaware of this possible impediment at the time of the sale of the [J] Film Library to CF. It is commonly understood in the industry that ‘perpetual rights’ means forever. Whilst this is the way that the industry views these rights... it now appears that this is an erroneous belief at law. [Applicant’s Statement of Facts and Contentions, p 8]


The Respondent’s submission in this regard was that since the films in the J film library were made at the latest by 1956, the result is that in Hong Kong or the Philippines, either no copyright at all still subsists in those films or the term of the copyright is significantly limited. In the above passage the Applicant acknowledged that in all likelihood copyright no longer subsists in the films in the Philippines; and my own research has uncovered that under clause 5 of Schedule 2 of the Hong Kong Copyright Ordinance 1997 no copyright subsists in a film made before 12 December 1972 unless that film is also regarded as an “original dramatic work”. However, there was no evidence before the Tribunal as to whether the films were to be regarded as an “original dramatic work” under Hong Kong law. Indeed, (and notwithstanding the Applicant’s concession with respect to the Philippines) I am of the opinion that ultimately there was insufficiant evidence before the Tribunal to reach any settled conclusion as to whether, in Hong Kong or the Philippines, copyright still subsists in the J film library, or if copyright does subsist, for how long that protection lasts. However, I am prepared to accept that at the time of the disposal of the J film library to CF in 1993, either there was no copyright subsisting in the J film library in the relevant territories at the time of the disposal, or alternatively the term of the copyright was for a period which was limited in years.

In this regard, the Applicant said:

In the industry the Company’s competitors and the American major studios which had or have the perpetual rights or the original copyright, even though it may have expired, market the product (ie issue limited licences) as if the copyright or perpetual right to exclusive licence still exists. This is respected by the television stations which continue to pay for these licences. Clearly they do this for good commercial reasons because, whilst it is now apparent that such licences may not be exclusive, they do give the licensor access to material in pristine condition because it is derived directly from the original negative. This is not the case in the public domain where material is derived from copies and not the original negative and therefore may be unsuitable for commercial transmission. [Applicant’s Statement of Facts and Contentions, p 6-7]


It appears from this passage that, in the context of disposing of rights over which the copyright no longer subsists, the real import of the transaction is in the obligation to supply prints derived from the original negative. This seems to me to be the correct interpretation; the fact that a licence may continue as a matter of practical fact, even though the copyright has expired, is in my view not to the point.

(iv) The nature of the particular realisation which produced the profit

The first question that arises here is whether the 1993 agreement is to be construed as an exclusive licence or as a partial assignment (I say ‘partial’ recalling that the remake rights were originally withheld) of the rights in the films.

The Respondent’s submission was that the 1993 agreement was in form and in substance a licence agreement, which therefore brought it squarely within the Applicant’s ordinary activities. It was contended for the Applicant, on the other hand, that it was, despite its title and the fact that the parties are referred to as “licensee” and “licensor”, an assignment of the rights and not a mere licence, and reference was made to the ‘clear’ reference in the Rider to the fact that what was being carried out was a “purchase”.

It is clear that the fact that the words “licence agreement”, “licensee” or “licensor” are used does not determine the character of the agreement: Messager v BBC [1929] AC 151 at 155-6 (See also, S Ricketson, the Law of Intellectual Property, Law Book Company, 1984, p 359). But I do not think that the word “purchase” seals the issue either. As mentioned previously, the Applicant gave in evidence examples of its usual agreements with television stations, and one of those agreements also uses the word “purchase”, even though the Applicant tendered it as an example of a licence properly so regarded. Furthermore, the evidence of Mr A was that the word “sale” commonly means “licence” in the distribution industry. Terminology, thus, does not advance the matter.

Ultimately, however, I think it is probably unnecessary to seek to characterise the agreement as one or the other. As Hill J said in Wilson v Weiss Art Pty Ltd (1995) 31 IPR 423, the real issue is to ascertain the nature of the rights and not the name of the agreement: (at 433)

Although much was made in argument of the distinction between assignment and licence, I am of the view that in the present case nothing really turns upon that distinction. It is difficult to see for the present purposes what difference there could be between an equitable assignment of a limited right and the irrevocable grant for consideration of an exclusive licence to use copyright in a particular way... The important question in the present case is, if the matter is approached as an equitable assignment, the extent of the definition of the rights assigned or, if the matter is approached as a licence, the terms of that licence.


As set out previously, it is not entirely clear what the true nature of the rights disposed of in the 1993 agreement were. Either the copyright in the films had expired (and the Applicant appears to have conceded this in respect of the Philippines rights), or they hadn’t (which is still questionable in respect of Hong Kong), but as I indicated above, it is not possible for me to come to a conclusion on this point. If copyright no longer subsisted in the films, then, as suggested for the Applicant, the true nature of the 1993 agreement was not a disposal of exclusive rights in the films, but rather a commitment on the part of the Applicant to supply prints derived from the original negatives. If, on the other hand, copyright still subsisted in the films, then the agreement can be regarded as the disposal of all the rights in the films (except remake rights) for the remaining period of the copyright and a commitment to supply quality prints.

The question which remains is whether such a transaction (on either analysis) is to be regarded as falling within the scope of the business of the Applicant.

(iv) Conclusion

I have come to the conclusion that the 1993 agreement is a transaction which must be regarded as falling within the ordinary course of the business of the Applicant. I think this is the case despite the question of whether or not copyright still subsists in the J film library in the relevant territories.

If, and to the extent that, copyright no longer subsists in the films, and the transaction is to be seen as merely a commitment to supply prints, I think that the conclusion must follow that such an activity is to be considered as an ordinary incident of the Applicant’s business. The supply of prints may not be the most valuable commercial benefit of the licences which the Applicant usually supplies, but it is, I think, an integral aspect of them; (see Roadshow Distributors Pty Ltd v Commissioner of State Revenue (Vic) 97 ATC 4271). I think the evidence establishes that it is a feature of most of the agreements which the Applicant enters into with its customers that the Applicant provides a commitment to supply prints. Indeed, as we gave seen, the Applicant’s acquisition of the J film library itself in 1981 was conditional upon its acceptance of an obligation to supply prints derived from the original negatives. The commitment to supply prints may be either as part of the overall fee, or (as with the 1993 agreement) at the cost of the ‘purchaser’ on request, but on either method, if the 1993 agreement is to be seen as such a commitment, the conclusion must be that it is one within the scope of the Applicant’s business.

On the other hand, if, and to the extent that, copyright still subsists in the J films in the relevant territories, I think that when one considers that the rights disposed of are ultimately themselves limited in terms of time, it becomes clear that the same result must follow. A regular aspect of the Applicant’s business was the provision of exclusive rights for limited periods of time over films and television material. I think that the present transaction is in substance of the same kind, the grant of exclusive rights for a limited period of time. Here, of course, the limited time is imposed by the operation of law (ie the expiry of the copyright) and not by a contract with the Applicant, but I think that this is merely a distinction without a difference. The fact remains that the Applicant grants, in both cases, rights over films which are limited in time.

It was submitted for the Applicant that there was a fundamental difference between disposing of rights for a limited term, for example a seven or ten year licence (which it was argued was its ordinary course of business), and the disposal of rights for the longer, albeit also limited, term that ‘perpetual rights’ implies (which, it was argued, was not in its ordinary course of business). The suggested distinction was that the difference in time meant that the Applicant could, as it did in Australia and New Zealand, earn licence fee income from the perpetual rights by sub-dividing the available time and thereafter sub-licensing various portions of that available time, and hence when the rights were disposed of outright, it was the disposal of an income-producing (and therefore, capital) asset. But I do not think that this is a valid criterion of distinction to draw on the facts of the present case. A crucial fact in this context is that when the Applicant obtains rights to a film, television program or series for a particular limited duration of time, it always has the choice of either sub-dividing that available time and granting a licence for a portion of that time, or on-licensing the rights it acquires for the entirety of the period available to it; furthermore, the evidence of Mr A was that, although the Applicant seeks the former course, because it is usually more profitable, it “invariably” adopts the latter. In other words, it was admitted that the ordinary course of business of the Applicant, when it acquires licences for limited terms, is to on-licence the acquired rights for the whole of the term, even when sub-licensing is a possible strategy with the same asset. That is to say, even where that same asset may itself be put to use to derive shorter-term licence fee income, the usual course of the Applicant’s business is to dispose of the entirety of its interest in that asset. To the extent, therefore, that copyright remains in the J library in the relevant territories, I think that the 1993 agreement is to be seen as a transaction which falls into the Applicant’s ordinary course of conduct - namely, the disposal of rights to the extent, and for the duration of the time, available to it.

If one looks at the difference between what the Applicant does with the J film library in Australia and New Zealand (namely sub-licence the rights) against what the Applicant did in the 1993 agreement (namely dispose of them outright) an argument that the proceeds of the 1993 agreement was on capital account can prima facie be advanced. But, if one looks at what the Applicant did in the 1993 agreement compared with what the Applicant does with the other limited film rights it acquires, it becomes clear that the transaction evidenced in 1993 agreement was something which was readily and ordinarily carried out in the business of the Applicant.

I think it is on this basis also that a case such as Collins v Firth Brearly (supra) can be distinguished. It will be recalled that in that case the Court of Appeal made much of the fact that the patent rights there being disposed of were the only asset of the company. It was therefore easier to conclude that the outright sales were outside of the scope of the company, and hence capital, by reference to the usual activities of the company, which were to licence the patent rights for short periods of time. In this case, however, the rights disposed of were by no means the only film rights with which the Applicant dealt; and as the evidence indicated, the Applicant’s transactions with those other film rights - namely, disposal of the entirety of its rights for the whole of the time available to it - were all in substance on a par with what it did in the present transaction.

I should mention that I have reached the above conclusion despite a forceful argument put by Counsel for the Applicant. This argument pointed to a number of further factors which, it was contended, indicate that the disposal was nothing other than a disposal of a capital asset. These factors are particularised as follows.

Counsel for the Applicant pointed out: (i) that the assets disposed of were acquired in 1981 for $1250 and the acceptance of an obligation of indemnity, and disposed of 12 years later for more than half-a-million dollars, where the assets themselves were never used to produce income (that is, in Hong Kong and the Philippines) in their own right - the “sale” was therefore in the nature of a windfall or unforeseen gain; (ii) the disposal was not to the usual purchaser, namely a television station, but rather to another distributor who could do with the assets just as the Applicant could have done, that is, sub-divide the rights and use them to produce income; (iii) this was the only “sale” of perpetual rights that the Applicant had ever carried out in its 24 year (as at the relevant year) history; and (iv) that by the transaction, the Applicant was excluding itself from licensing the J films in Hong Kong and the Philippines, and therefore the transaction could be seen as one whereby the Applicant was effectively removing itself from a market.

While I appreciate the strength of this argument, I do not think that these factors tilt the balance in favour of the Applicant. In respect of each of them: (i) It may be that the consideration for the assets was astounding given their past income producing capacity for the Applicant (which was nil) and compared to the amount for which they were acquired, and in that respect the price paid was indeed a “windfall” or fortuitous gain, but it was so as a result of a transaction which was, I think, within the ordinary course of the Applicant’s business - and I do not think the sheer size of the payment or the length of time between acquisition and disposal negates the placement of the transaction as one within that scope. (In any event the size of the gain is explicable in the light of changes in the structure of the relevant market and in particular the coming of cable television). (ii) I do not think that the identity of the purchaser takes the transaction outside the scope of the Applicant’s business. True it is that the ‘customers’ of the Applicant are usually television stations, and here the ‘customer’ was another distributor, but if the substance of the transaction itself (the disposal of rights in films) is something ordinarily carried out by the Applicant, as I believe is the case, then a different ‘purchaser’ cannot alter the nature of that characterisation. (iii) Although the evidence indicated that the Applicant had disposed of perpetual rights on at least on other occasion, even if the 1993 agreement was the only “sale” (that is, for a price) of perpetual rights in the history of the Applicant’s business, the cases make it quite clear that a transaction which bears unusual characteristics is not thereby to be regarded as outside of the ordinary course of business. In any case, as I have indicated above, I think this transaction is to be regarded as within the Applicant’s ordinary course of business having regard to the ultimate similarity between it and the agreements in which the Applicant is usually involved. In other words, I do not think it, when viewed in substance, can be regarded as a truly “isolated transaction”. (iv) It may be that the Applicant is no longer able to licence films in the J library in Hong Kong and the Philippines, but this is no different from the usual scenario where the Applicant licences films for the entire period for which it holds a film or series. Any exclusive licence for the period for which the Applicant holds rights has the effect of removing the Applicant from that territory in respect of those films. And nothing, of course, prevents the Applicant, if it ever desired, from licensing any of the other films for which it holds the relevant rights, in Hong Kong or the Philippines.

In conclusion, therefore, I think the 1993 agreement was a transaction which fell within the ordinary course of the Applicant’s business, and was therefore properly assessable under s25 of the Act.

Having reached this conclusion, it is unnecessary to deal with the alternate submissions of the Respondent, namely in respect of s25A(1) and s26(f), or according to s25(1) and the rule in Myer Emporium on the basis of the Applicant’s intention at the time of its acquisition of the J film library.

Accordingly, the objection decision under review is affirmed.
