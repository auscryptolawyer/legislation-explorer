Re MJ Goldman and Commissioner of Superannuation [1984] AATA 386 (10 September 1984)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: M.J. GOLDMAN

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. W83/134

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Hon. G.D. Clarkson QC (Senior Member)

Dr. A.H. Marsh

Dr. J.G. Billings

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - determination of retirement benefits - whether failure to furnish full information - whether false information furnished - issue of benefit classification certificate

Superannuation Act 1922

Superannuation Act 1976 s.184

R M (A83/28 16 April 1984)

Watts 3ALD 267

Mackrell (December 1981 unreported)

Z (16 September 1983 unreported)

Fry (31 July 1984 unreported)

HEARING

PERTH

10:9:1984

ORDER

The decision of the Tribunal is to set aside the decision under review and to remit the matter to the Commissioner for Superannuation for further consideration in accordance with the direction that at or in connection with the medical examinations which M.J. Goldman underwent for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1922 that person did not fail to furnish any information required to be furnished by him nor did he furnish false information.

DECISION

The applicant was born on 28 March 1957. When fifteen years old he applied to join the Postmaster General's Department and sat for an entrance examination. At the end of that school year he was informed that he had passed the examination and would be accepted by the Department as an apprentice telecommunication tradesman in June 1973.

On 11 May 1973 before commencing employment he underwent a medical examination by a Commonwealth medical officer, Dr. Brazowski.

The examination consisted of a physical examination by the doctor and the completion by the applicant of a form containing a number of questions.

As a result of the examination, Dr. Brazowski recommended "that this candidate be deferred for eleven months". This was because the applicant as a child had suffered from asthma. A note on the form stated "deferment should only be recommended where ultimate acceptance is likely". The reason which the examining doctor gave for the deferment was "history of asthma".

The applicant commenced work on 13 July 1973. On 22 May 1974 he was again examined by Dr. Brazowski who recorded that there was no evidence of asthma and recommended that the applicant be accepted for the Superannuation Fund.

The applicant was then accepted for permanent employment to the Public Service and as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund.

It was common ground that the applicant had entered this scheme as a contributor under the Superannuation 1922 Act and that he came under the Superannuation 1976 Act as an "existing contributor" when it commenced operation on 1 July 1976.

He was retired on 15 June 1981 on the grounds of invalidity.

Under the 1976 Act contributors suffering from a relevant disability are given a benefit classification certificate specifying that disability and where premature retirement occurs by reason of that disability, benefits may be reduced.

On 12 January 1982 a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation gave a decision, the effect of which was to limit the benefit payable under the Superannuation Act 1976 to the applicant, to a benefit substantially less than he might otherwise have received.

The proper construction of the relevant provisions has caused difficulties but we see no reason to depart from the careful analysis of para. 184(5)(d) of the Superannuation Act 1976 in the decision of the Tribunal in R.M. and Commissioner for Superannuation A83/28 16 April 1984, paras. 64-69.

Adopting that view we think the primary question at issue here is whether this Tribunal, acting in the place of the Commissioner, is satisfied "that at or in connection with a medical examination which the person underwent for the purposes of the superseded Act, the person failed to furnish any information required to be furnished by him or furnished false information".

Depending on the answer to that question a further question may arise under sub-para.184(5)(d)(ii) for consideration.

The primary question involves a consideration of the applicant's state of health in the period up to the first medical examination in May 1973 and then to May 1974 and then until his retirement in June 1981.

The applicant is now an adult, twenty seven years of age, who suffers from a rare and serious illness, sclerosing chomangitis. As an intelligent man concerned with his health, he has gathered much information and obviously has considerable understanding of his present condition. It is necessary to remember, however, that the medical examination with which we are principally concerned took place when he was sixteen years old.

As a child, until aged about twelve years, the applicant suffered from asthma. He had periodical attacks and on occasions was taken to a doctor but was not hospitalized. According to his mother, he first showed signs of asthma when about twelve months old and it commenced to clear up when he was about eight or nine. By the time he was eleven or twelve he ceased having attacks and appeared to be in good health.

In 1970 in the first week in his first year in high school he injured his elbow playing sport and his mother took him to see a general practitioner. The applicant remembers only that the doctor prescribed some exercises for his elbow.

His mother says she took her son to see the doctor regarding his elbow. The doctor apparently suspected the child had hepatitis and ordered blood tests and prescribed rest. On an occasion some time later, he developed an abdominal pain and the doctor admitted him to the Mount Hospital.

Apparently there was some suggestion that he might be suffering from appendicitis. However, the pain disappeared and he returned home.

Whilst in the Mount Hospital, he was seen by Dr. Cohen, a specialist physician. After the applicant's discharge from hospital he was taken on a number of occasions to see Dr. Cohen.

His mother, speaking of these visits, said:

"Can you tell the tribunal what sort of

general impression you were left with from Dr

Cohen about Mark's condition?---Dr Cohen told

me that Mark had hepatitis, as I recall, at

that time. I cannot really - it was mainly

hepatitis. He just told me it was hepatitis.

I thought hepatitis was a disease; I had not

heard much about it but it is something that

takes longer than the normal diseases to get

over and eventually Mark would get over this

too. That was in the early stages.

The admission to the Mount Hospital appears to have been in April 1970.

Thereafter during that year the applicant was admitted on three occasions to St. John of God Hospital.

The first admission to St. John of God Hospital was for a liver biopsy performed by Prof. Joske. Nothing abnormal was disclosed, the pathologist's comment being "I see no diagnostic features here".

Late in 1970 he was again admitted to St. John of God Hospital in order that an exploratory operation could be performed. After admission he felt acute pain. His appendix was found to be acutely inflamed and was removed. Goldman says he was told after the operation that "they had removed a burst appendix and that was the reason for the pain".

Describing this admission Goldman said:

"What in fact happened?---I was well for a

while until one day I felt quite weak on the

way to school, and when I got to school I

suppose someone rang my parents and I think

it was my mother who picked me up from

school. She took me to see, I think, Dr

Cohen, and after seeing him that evening I

went in to St John of God Hospital again.

You were admitted to St John of God Hospital

that evening, straight away, were you?---Yes.

What happened then? Were you in any pain or

what did you understand you were being

admitted to the hospital for?---At the time I

do not remember exactly why I was admitted,

but I know now it was for a blood

transfusion.

But you did not realise that at the

time?---It is hard to say what I realised at

the time. Actually, I am not sure if you are

asking me what I realised at the time or even

what I realised at the time of my

examination. I mean, I know a lot more about

what happened then through reading medical

notes and asking questions now, and I do know

that it was for a blood transfusion."

Thereafter he was again admitted to hospital late in 1970 when it was

suspected that an abscess may have formed at the site of the earlier operation. Apparently he was discharged on the same day.

During 1971 his condition appeared to improve. He paid only two visits to Dr. Cohen for check-up, on 10 June and 17 November 1971.

It is clear that Dr. Cohen did not diagnose the applicant's condition in any discussions with him.

In 1972 there was only one visit to Dr. Cohen. Indeed in 1971 and 1972 the applicant attended school regularly with no major absence.

Thereafter there was no visit to Dr. Cohen until a check-up in 1974, well after the medical examination by Dr. Brazowski.

Goldman's illness was not diagnosed until he saw Prof. Sherlock in England in 1980.

We return now to 11 May 1973 when Goldman was examined by Dr. Brazowski. He was then sixteen years old. As previously indicated, following the removal of his appendix in 1970 the applicant's visits to Dr. Cohen decreased and in April 1972 Dr. Cohen had noticed a "distinct improvement" with no further pains, no asthma and eating well.

For the purpose of the medical examination in May 1973, the applicant completed a form containing a number of questions. The relevant questions and answers are as follows:

"Have you now or have you previously had any

of the following conditions?

1(i) Attack of unconsciousness or weakness?

(Answer) no.

3c. Disease of the liver? (Answer) no.

3e. Jaundice? (Answer) no.

4a. Asthma? (Answer) yes.

4b. Bronchitis? (Answer) yes. Against this

Dr. Brazowski noted 'asthma until twelve

years of age. No symptoms now'.

11. Details of any complaint, illness or

injury not mentioned above. (Answer)

none.

12. Details of absence from school or work

due to sickness over past two years.

The original answer, 'none' is crossed

out and 'eight days, colds' substituted.

13. Details of all accident, surgical

treatment or operations. (Answer)

'appendectamy'. (sic)

14. Are you in good health now? (Answer)

yes.

15. When did you last receive medical or

surgical treatment and for what

condition? (Answer) approx. eighteen

months ago for sprained fingers.

Against this Dr. Brazowski noted

'recovered'."

Dr. Brazowski, not surprisingly has no present recollection of the

examination, although he was able to explain what his usual procedure was.

There are two matters arising from the examination which should be noticed. The first is that Goldman maintains that he explained to Dr. Brazowski that the scar on his abdomen was the result of an exploratory operation which was undertaken to find out what was wrong with him and which resulted in his appendix being removed. The size and position of the scar are unusual, but Dr. Brazowski did not pursue the matter further.

The second is that it is quite clear that when the examination took place Goldman's spleen was grossly enlarged, but Dr. Brazowski did not detect that this was so.

As a result of the examination Dr. Brazowski recommended that the candidate be deferred for eleven months. The reasons for this as previously noted was "history of asthma".

Goldman saw Dr. Brazowski again on 22 May 1974 with the result already indicated. Neither party has any clear recollection of this interview or whether a physical examination took place.

The Full Court of the Federal Court in Fry (31 July 1984 unreported) has said that there is no justification for approaching the obligation placed on an employee by s.16(11)(d) of the Superannuation Act on the basis of the knowledge which might fairly have been attributed to him or upon what he should have known, and that an employee complies with the section if he answers the questions truthfully according to his actual knowledge and state of mind.

Section 184(5) of the Act which is in similar terms provides in effect that where the Commissioner is satisfied that a contributor "at or in connection with a medical examination which the person underwent for the purposes of the superseded Act, the person failed to furnish any information required to be furnished by him or furnished false information . . . the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the person a benefit classification certificate . . .".

The important question to consider is the extent to which Goldman was aware of the nature of his illness and the facts in relation thereto which could have been disclosed by him at his medical examination.

The Acting Commissioner in his decision said that having regard to certain evidence and medical reports which he specified he was "satisfied that at or in connection with the medical examination undergone by Mr. Goldman on 11 May 1973 he failed to furnish information required to be furnished by him or furnished false information in that he did not provide any information about his chronic illness and treatment over two years, symptoms of which subsided only one year before the examination".

To reach a state of satisfaction now as to the extent of Goldman's knowledge in May 1973 regarding his illness and treatment in 1970, 1971 and 1972 is a difficult task.

The Acting Commissioner's statement that symptoms of Goldman's chronic illness had subsided only one year before the medical examination is no doubt based on Dr. Cohen's statement, that it would be reasonable to assume that Mr. Goldman would have known that his hospitalization in 1970 "and the continuing illness over the next two years were connected".

However, the evidence shows that this conclusion is open to question. Goldman's symptoms subsided quite substantially after the operation late in 1970 and in 1971 and 1972 no medication was prescribed and he attended school regularly. As mentioned earlier, he saw Dr. Cohen only twice in 1971 and once in 1972 for "check-ups".

It is important to bear in mind that Goldman turned thirteen years of age in March 1970, the year in which he suffered considerable ill health and underwent surgery. At the time of his first medical examination by Dr. Brazowski he was sixteen years old and when giving evidence in May 1984 he was twenty seven years of age and being asked to remember what he knew and heard of his illness fourteen years before when aged thirteen.

The evidence to which the Acting Commissioner has referred in reaching his decision is set out as follows:

"(d) Dr Cohen's opinion has been obtained on

Mr Goldman's awareness of his condition on

two occasions. On 8 Sep 1982 he reported

that the nature of the illness was not

discussed with Mr Goldman, but that his

mother was fully informed. He commented, 'It

seemed surprising that he should have been

unaware of the nature of his illness at the

time of his medical examination in 1973 but I

suppose it is possible that if his mother had

played down the nature of the illness and its

significance, he may have effaced the

relevance from his mind'.

Dr Cohen amplified this opinion in response

to a request from this Office in a report

dated 23 Jun 1983 which indicated that Mr

Goldman underwent two liver biopsies in 1970

(the second concurrent with an appendectomy).

He further indicated that Mr Goldman was

treated by him with Cortisone and

hospitalised in the management of his

'intense itching' (Pruritis). Dr. Cohen

concluded:

'4. The liver biopsies were performed

during 1970 at which time Mr Goldman was

around 12 years of age. In my opinion,

because of the presence of his jaundice

and itching, the repeated concentration

by his medical advisors on the

examination of his liver and spleen, the

necessity for a low fat diet and the

discussions which took place with his

mother, I would be surprised if he was

not aware, even at that age, that there

was something wrong with his liver nor

would he fail to recall that a liver

biopsy had been performed. However,

with regard to the latter comment,

children often undergo quite painful and

elaborate investigations which they

successfully sublimate with the years.

I do believe, however, that it would be

reasonable to assume that Mr Goldman

would have known that his

hospitalisation in 1970 and the

continuing illness over the next two

years were connected.'

(e) In view of this report I accept that Mr

Goldman may have been aware only of the

appendectomy and not the concurrent liver

biopsy in October 1970. However the fact

remains that he was hospitalised on two other

occasions in relation to his illness.

Similarly, the severity and two-year duration

of his illness, requiring modification of his

diet, together with the associated Jaundice

and Pruritis indicate to me that he should

have furnished these details to Dr Vrazowski

(sic.) on 11 May 1973 when examined for the

purposes of the superseded Act. This would

have enabled the examining medical officer

and the delegate of the Superannuation Board

to exercise their respective powers and

functions at the time.

(f) The Acting Assistant Director,

Department of Health, Perth (Dr Lee)

expressed his opinion on this point in a

letter to my Office dated 9 Sep 1983 stating

inter alia "If Mr Goldman was unaware of the

diagnosis of his condition at the time of his

entry medical examination in 1973, even

though the diagnosis was thought to be

hepatitis of a chronic aggressive nature, it

is difficult to assume he could also be

unaware that he had suffered a chronic

serious illness with jaundice associated with

persistent itching". Dr Lee was, for this

reason, of the opinion that "Mr Goldman

appears to have failed to furnish information

relating to his illness between 1970-1972, in

connection with his entry medical

examination."."

The applicant's contention is that as a child of thirteen who was given

little information by his mother and none by Dr. Cohen, he had the most meagre knowledge of his illness in 1970 which in any event appeared to him to have been cured by the appendectomy in late 1970. In consequence, it is said, he knew nothing of the rare liver condition from which he was in fact suffering when he underwent the medical examination by Dr. Brazowski in May 1973, that he answered the questions put to him fully and truthfully and submitted himself to a physical examination which should have alerted the Commonwealth medical officer that as an applicant for admission to the Superannuation Fund he was a bad risk.

The passage quoted above from the Acting Commissioner's decision refers to a number of matters which require further examination.

Apart from what Goldman may have been told there are certain symptoms and treatment which would have been significant at least to a medical practitioner and it should be borne in mind that the original "working diagnosis" was hepatitis - which was wrong.

Dr. Cohen refers to intense itching (pruritis), jaundice and the two liver biopsies. In addition, there was the enlargement of the spleen and - for a time - of the liver, blood tests and references to a low fat diet.

One would naturally expect that a child suffering an illness would obtain information about his condition from his parents and Dr. Cohen said that he did not discuss his diagnosis with Goldman. Having heard both parents give evidence we are satisfied they did not gain the impression which Dr. Cohen attempted to give them.

Dr. Cohen said he spoke to Mrs. Goldman "outlining what I thought of the matter at that time probably conveying to her my uncertainties about the ultimate diagnosis but I do not think at any time was I in any doubt that it was the liver which was the outstanding problem".

Mrs. Goldman's attitude is best illustrated by part of her evidence referring to the year 1970 and then parts which refer to later years.

Referring to the period after the operation, Mrs. Goldman's evidence was as follows:

"Can you recall subsequent visits to Dr Cohen

after that operation?---Yes.

Again, did you talk to Dr Cohen?---Dr Cohen

used to examine him. He told me his spleen

was enlarged. I presumed, there was sort of

no explanation but I presumed, at the time,

as Mark was going to get better his spleen

would diminish. That was the idea I had at

that time. He used to go for a blood test

and - - -

What made you think that Mark was going to

get better? Was it as a result of something

Dr Cohen said or as a result of something

else?---I believed that he had hepatitis and

it was a disease that you eventually get

over. I did not realise at the time he had -

- -

You see, you heard Dr Cohen give evidence

today, did you not?---Yes.

You were in the room. He said that he was

always very frank with his patients and

family?---Yes.

That if he had followed his normal course he

would have told you what he perceived the

problems to be?---Yes.

I take it that included that it was unlikely

there was going to be a rapid recovery. That

does not seem to accord with your

impression?---I did not think it would be a

rapid recovery but I definitely thought it

was something he would overcome. Put it this

way, I was not given the impression that it

was something he would not overcome.

What about diet? Was that discussed with

you?---Yes: he was on a low fat diet - that

was already at Mount Hospital; he was already

on a low fat diet.

Why did you think he was on a low fat

diet?---Because of the disease.

You thought it was connected, did you?---Yes.

In the subsequent visits after that year was

over, in 1971 and 1972, we have heard you

went twice in 1971 and once in 1972 to see Dr

Cohen. Do you recall those visits?---I know

I went to him but I cannot say I recall them.

Were you left with an impression at the end

of those visits as to Mark's condition and if

so, what was that impression?---Dr Cohen used

to tell me the result of his blood test; that

it had improved. He used to tell me his

spleen was slightly enlarged and there was

sort of nothing else, really, that he could

tell me. Just that his blood was - the

condition of his liver was improving, yes.

In 1971, 1972 how did you perceive Mark's

condition to be, from the family point of

view?---He was coming on well. He was going

to school; he did not miss any school; he did

not have to make up any time. There was no

recurrence of asthma or anything like that.

So what did you think?---I thought he was -

the hepatitis was going. I had heard since

then that some people have hepatitis for 12

months, some 6 months; this is the impression

I had at the time, that it is something that

takes longer than normal - mumps or measles -

to get over.

From that period until when did you think he

would - when, in the seventies or eighties

did you think he had recovered?---I really

thought at the end of 1970 that was it. I

thought, "Now he's on his way coming back to

health."

Her evidence regarding the liver biopsy is as follows:

"Did you understand him to be having a liver

biopsy at one stage when he was in

hospital?---Yes; yes, they told us he was

having a liver biopsy.

What did you understand that involved?---That

they would take part of the liver and test

that to see how it was; that they would take

a small portion out of the liver.

Did you hear the result of that?---Yes.

What was your understanding of those

results?---Well, they did not find anything,

so I presumed his liver was well."

Regarding jaundice and the tentative diagnosis of hepatitis, Mrs. Goldman

said:

"What about his colour. Doctor Cohen has

indicated that he had fairly severe jaundice.

Was this manifested in any way by his

complexion? Did it change as a result of

this illness in 1970?---When it was brought

to my notice I perhaps remember his eyes

being more yellow than a normal person's but,

other than that, at that time his colour was

not as bad as it is now.

Was your son aware that he was diagnosed as

having hepatitis at that stage?---I could not

say. Perhaps he heard it.

But you cannot recall explaining why he was

being subjected to all these hospitalisations

and tests?---No.

I mean, did he ask you? Was he

concerned?---Perhaps I just told him that he

had a disease, hepatitis. Whether he

actually recalls that I do not know.

And you cannot recall?---I am sure I must

have told him, but I just cannot recall the

time or what I told him.

You said that Dr Cohen told you at the

consultations that the blood tests indicated

his health had improved in check-up periods.

Is that correct?---Uh huh. Yes."

The applicant's father gave similar evidence regarding 1970 an extract from

which is as follows:

"It was your impression, was it, during the

course of that year that Mark suffered from

hepatitis?---And that it would eventually go.

That it would eventually go? That is right,

yes.

Do you recall any specific details being made

in relation to Mark's diet?---No; only that I

have recently heard my wife mention that she

recalled no fats, or a low fat diet, I think,

that Cohen mentioned. (it)

We have heard that Mark was not well during

the course of that year; that must have been

evident (to) you?---Right.

Did you perceive him as being

jaundiced?---No, not at all. After the

biopsy we were sort of - we breathed a sigh

of relief when they found no problem with the

liver. We assumed that that that was the end

of that part of the story.

That was the biopsy carried out by Professor

Joske?---Joske, right."

With the parents having the impressions they have recounted and Dr. Cohen

naturally enough not attempting to explain the position as he understood it - and there was of course no firm diagnosis - to the child, it is not surprising that the applicant could form a different impression of his state of health from that which Dr. Cohen held. It is clear from the applicant's evidence that he maintains that at the time of the medical examination in May 1973 he did not know of his liver condition nor did he know of it until some years later.

Some of the matters which Dr. Cohen assumed would be likely to indicate to the applicant that something was wrong with his liver are not as persuasive as Dr. Cohen apparently thought. A paragraph in Dr. Cohen's report of 23 June 1983 already quoted goes to the heart of the matter and bears repetition:

"4. The liver biopsies were performed during

1970 at which time Mr. Goldman was around 12

years of age. In my opinion, because of the

presence of his jaundice and itching, the

repeated concentration by his medical

advisors on the examination of his liver and

spleen, the necessity for a low fat diet and

the discussions which took place with his

mother, I would be surprised if he was not

aware, even at that age, that there was

something wrong with his liver nor would he

fail to recall that a liver biopsy had been

performed. However, with regard to the

latter comment, children often undergo quite

painful and elaborate investigations which

they succesfully sublimate with the years. I

do believe, however, that it would be

reasonable to assume that Mr. Goldman would

have known that his hospitalisation in 1970

and the continuing illness over the next two

years were connected.

This view assumes that Goldman was aware of the conditions referred to and

that he had sufficient knowledge at the age of thirteen to appreciate that these conditions suggested a liver disorder.

Goldman says he was unaware that he was jaundiced in 1970. His parents say they did not notice it and there seems to be no reason to suggest that Goldman at that stage would have known more than his parents. In his evidence, the applicant speaking of his knowledge of his jaundiced condition in 1970 said, "no I do not recall knowing that I was jaundiced or noticing it in myself. In fact the word jaundiced was not even a term I was familiar with".

Dr. Cohen's reference to severe itching indicates that its existence should have pointed to a liver problem. There is no evidence that Goldman or his parents knew this and more importantly Goldman said:

"Do you recall having the condition pruritus,

the itching?---I do not recall actually being

itchy, though I do remember sort of having

the actual marks from where I had been

scratching. I think that comes about because

I know now it was something that I used to do

at night that was in a semi-conscious state.

I suppose, possibly, your skin irritates you

and that is when I would scratch myself. I

do not remember being itchy during the day

and having discomfort from the itching."

Dr. Cohen refers also to "the repeated concentration by his medical advisers

on the examination of his liver and spleen". We understand from the evidence that a person may have an enlarged liver and spleen and yet be unaware of it. The only "concentration" by medical advisers was in 1970 and his ill health in that year resulted in an exploratory operation and appendectomy. We do not feel confident that a thirteen year old would detect the difference between an examination of his abdomen directed at his liver and spleen and one directed at a suspected appendicitis.

As previously noted, apart from a total of three visits to Dr. Cohen in 1971 and 1972, there were no other visits to doctors in the period from the end of 1970 to May 1973.

We do not recall any evidence that Goldman was aware that he was on a low fat diet, but in any event again we think it unlikely that a thirteen year old would see it as an indication that he suffered from a liver complaint. His mother said she thought he was on a low fat diet from early in 1970 because of hepatitis.

Dr. Cohen commented that Goldman would not fail to recall a liver biopsy had been performed. He was obviously referring to the first biopsy and not the second which occurred when Goldman was under a general anaesthetic. Goldman's evidence relating to the first biopsy was as follows:

"Do you recall hearing Dr. Cohen say that you

had a liver biopsy in, I think, the second

admission? Do you recall that?---Yes. I do

not think I understood at the time it was a

liver biopsy; but, though Dr Cohen once again

said it was quite painful, I do not recall it

as being painful. It was a fairly short

procedure by memory in which they sort of

just put a needle into me and withdrew it. I

suppose it could have been painful but, once

again, I was used to having injections.

You do not now remember it as being painful.

Is that what you are saying?---That is

correct, yes. I know that sounds unusual,

because I had a biopsy when I was in England

which was quite painful - in fact, just to

explain, I was quite surprised when I had

that biopsy in England, in November 1980,

under the supervision of Professor Sherlock.

I was quite stunned that I actually had this

pain after the biopsy.

. . . However, having read that I had a biopsy

before, I said, "It cannot be too bad; I have

had one before and I do not remember it being

painful." I was quite surprised that it was

painful."

It should be noted also that the results of the blood tests which Dr. Cohen

arranged to be done were not communicated to Goldman. The pathologist's report went to Dr. Cohen who said his practice was to inform Mrs. Goldman in general terms of the result on her next visit.

To these comments should be added Dr. Cohen's view already quoted that children often undergo quite painful and elaborate investigations which they successfully sublimate with the years. Counsel for the Commissioner suggested that the issue relating to Goldman's knowledge of his liver condition and jaundice depended on whether the evidence of Dr. Cohen or of the applicant was to be preferred. We do not find the answer so clear cut because what the applicant knew of Dr. Cohen's views depended on how Mrs. Goldman translated to the applicant what she thought Dr. Cohen had said to her.

Whatever information and advice Dr. Cohen gave Mrs. Goldman and however much of it she comprehended, the general effect of it as it was understood by Goldman himself, then thirteen or fourteen years old, was described by him as follows:

"Can you now recall what impression you were

left with as a result of having those

check-ups?---Not really much impression at

all because I think during those days I

attended Dr Cohen's rooms with my mother and

Dr Cohen never really discussed my health

with me. I remember I used to always ask my

mother after we came out, "Well, what did he

say?" I think this was due to the fact that

after the medical examination, I suppose,

where I had to get dressed, Dr Cohen would go

in and have a few words with my mother, which

was not in my presence, but my mother usually

said, "Well, he said you're getting better"

and that is about it."

We are prepared to accept that Dr. Cohen tried to be both sympathetic and

frank with Mrs. Goldman about Goldman's condition in the period from early 1970 to the visit in 1972. This must have been difficult for him because it is now known that the tentative diagnosis of hepatitis was wrong and that Goldman suffered from sclerosing cholangitis which was not recognized by the members of the medical profession investigating Goldman until well after 1973, and as Dr. Cohen himself has said, he was "non-plussed" by some of the incidents of the child's illness.

To this we add that Mrs. Goldman obviously had difficulty in reducing what she was told to some simple explanation for her schoolboy son. She had no medical or even first aid experience to help her do so and was as prepared, as her husband and son were, after Goldman's apparently substantial improvement in health after the appendectomy in late 1970 to conclude that the operation had removed the main cause of the problems experienced during 1970.

We are quite satisfied that Goldman himself honestly held that view from 1971 onwards for several years and for some considerable time after the medical examination by Dr. Brazowski in May 1973 and that it was well after that event before he was brought to the realization that he suffered a serious and apparently progressive disease or disorder of the liver.

Having reached that view it is now necessary to consider the reasons on which the Commissioner relies for saying that Goldman failed to furnish information or furnished false information.

The fact that Goldman's illness was initially wrongly diagnosed as hepatitis is not to the point. As the Acting Commissioner properly says, the medical examination form which the applicant completed does not require the applicant to diagnose his own condition. He is, as we have previously said, required to answer the questions put to him truthfully according to his actual knowledge and state of mind.

Counsel for the Commissioner relied on the answers to questions 13, 15 and 1(i) in addition to 3(c) and (e) which we have already discussed.

Question 13 and the answer read as follows:

"Details of all accidents, surgical treatment

or operations.

Appendectamy." (sic)

It was said that this answer was not sufficient in that it did not amount to

a full disclosure because it indicated "he had only received surgical treatment or operations for an appendectomy".

Counsel did not specify what the applicant should have said. There is no suggestion of any relevant accident not disclosed; the only matters disclosed by the evidence which might fall within this question are the two liver biopsies and any surgical procedures which occurred during the second admission to St. John of God Hospital in October 1970. This we understand to have been an exploratory operation during which a badly inflamed or burst appendix was removed. There was a later admission in November 1970 to St. John of God Hospital because of the possible formation of an abscess at the site of the earlier operation. Whether any surgical procedure occurred we are uncertain. Goldman himself when asked for how long he was in hospital on this occasion said, "I think it was only about half a day because the abscess which did arise from the operation disappeared so they immediately discharged me from hospital". In any event this was clearly a complication associated with the operation.

We did not understand it to be contended that either liver biopsy by itself constituted a surgical treatment or operation. The first was merely part of the diagnostic process on the applicant's admission to hospital for tests and the second was an incident of which the applicant was unaware in the later exploratory operation.

The real criticism we presume is that the only answer to the question was "appendectamy" (sic) when what occurred was an exploratory operation.

We cannot conclude that for a sixteen year old boy to describe an operation he had when thirteen by its result rather than its purpose can produce the state of satisfaction required by s.184(5)(d) of the Superannuation Act. As we have already said we accept that the applicant believed the cause of his troubles disappeared with the removal of his appendix and that, in the circumstances of which he was aware, it was reasonable for a youth of his age and experience so to believe.

But in addition the applicant says that he told Dr. Brazowski of the wider purpose of the operation when Dr. Brazowski queried the position and size of the scar on the applicant's abdomen.

Dr. Brazowski has no present recollection of his examination of Goldman and can describe only the practice which he followed on similar examinations. Whether he followed that practice on this occasion or not we do not know but it is beyond doubt that he made a substantial error in this examination by failing to detect the applicant's grossly enlarged spleen and in our view he was unable satisfactorily to explain the error. This raises a doubt whether a satisfactory procedure was followed.

We think it probable that the applicant did tell Dr. Brazowski the purpose of the operation he had in late 1970 and that Dr. Brazowski, accepting the explanation of the scar and the highly inflamed appendix, saw no reason to note anything further.

The next matter raised is question 15 and its answer:

"When did you last receive medical or surgical

treatment and for what condition?

Approx 18 months ago for sprained fingers."

Against this Dr. Brazowski noted "recovered". This answer is relevant to the

finding of the Acting Commissioner that Goldman did not provide information about " his chronic illness and treatment over two years, symptoms of which subsided only one year before the examination".

This raises two questions, firstly, when the symptoms of the illness subsided and secondly, when the last medical or surgical treatment was received.

To say that the symptoms subsided only twelve months before, the medical examination implies they continued up until about May 1972. Again, we must consider the allegation in respect of a sixteen year old youth with the knowledge, or lack of, it of his state of health which has already been discussed.

The applicant was satisfied that his problems had been solved by the appendectomy in late 1970. Thereafter he commenced what he described as the healthiest years of his life:

"How did you feel in 1971-72?---I felt

perfectly healthy. You know, I was leading

what I suppose was a normal life. I think I

used to ride a bicycle to school on some

occasions; if not, I would catch a bus by

myself. I think it was pretty normal.

Did you miss much school in 1971-72?---I do

not believe I missed any school. If I did,

it was for the occasional cold or - - -

And at the time that you went for your

examination in 1973 in May, how did you

assess the state of your health?---Well, I do

not remember actually making an assessment of

my state of health at that time, but I

remember it to be well. In retrospect now I

would have to say that the period of time

between, say, the end of 1970 and up until

1980 must have been the healthiest years of

my life in actual fact, because since 1980 I

have been unwell and prior to 1970 I suffered

from asthma and the initial effects of this

condition.

Goldman's only contact with the medical profession in the period from the

beginning of 1971 to May 1973 consisted of two visits to Dr. Cohen in June and November of 1971 and one in 1972 when Dr. Cohen recorded that in general Goldman had been well, was eating better and had no abdominal pain. He had grown four inches in twelve months and gained fifteen pounds. Any abnormality in the size of the spleen or liver and the subsequent results of the blood test taken were, as previously noted, not communicated to Goldman who was approaching his fifteenth birthday.

In our view the evidence as it ultimately emerges does not support the contention that within Goldman's knowledge he was suffering a chronic illness when he was examined by Dr. Brazowski or earlier, or that the symptoms of which he was aware had subsided only one year before the examination.

The further issue regarding treatment said to have occurred in the preceding period of two years is referred to later.

The conclusion we have reached regarding Goldman's lack of knowledge of his liver disability is sufficient to dispose of the allegation that he did not properly answer the enquiries in para. 3(c) & (e) of the medical examination form relating to "diseases of the liver" and "jaundice". It is therefore unnecessary to examine the doubt raised by the applicant himself in evidence whether his liver condition is in fact a "disease" of the liver.

The original decision of 12 January 1982 of the Commissioner's delegate relied on an alleged failure to furnish information required to be furnished by him or furnished false information by reason that:

"a) Mr Goldman answered "No" in writing in

response to the question:

"have you now or have you previously had

any of the following conditions?"

Q.3(c) Disease of the liver.

Q.3(e) Jaundice.

b) Mr Goldman also answered "None" in

writing to the question:

Q11. "details of any complaint, illness

or injury not mentioned above"."

The Acting Commissioner when he reviewed the earlier decision referred

expressly to the applicant's answers to paras. 3(c) & (e) and questions 11, 12, 13 and 15, but as appears from the extract of his decision already quoted he did not assign a particular failure to inform or a false statement to each of these provisions.

At the hearing before this Tribunal, counsel for the Commissioner did not address the Tribunal regarding questions 11 and 12 and the answers given to them.

Presumably the allegation in respect of the answer to question 11 is the failure to provide information about his chronic illness. This involves substantially the same allegation as in respect of 3(c) & (e).

We are not altogether clear what failure is alleged in respect of question 12 which reads:

"Details of absence from school or work due to

sickness over past two years.

8 days colds."

It will be seen that the applicant first gave the answer "None" and finally

"8 days colds". The alteration was probably made after discussion with Dr. Brazowski.

There is no evidence that between 11 May 1971 and 11 May 1973 the applicant was absent from school for any other reason and presumably because of this counsel for the respondent did not address the Tribunal on this issue.

The respondent contends that the visits to Dr. Cohen by Goldman on 17 November 1971 and in April 1972 being within eighteen months of the examination on 11 May 1973 should have been disclosed in answer to question 15, but the question calls for the "last" occasion on which treatment was received, so that the respondent's submission should be limited to one or the other of them.

It is not clear whether on either occasion Goldman presented himself for examination or came in response to a suggestion by Dr. Cohen made at the previous visit. Clearly on each occasion there was a physical examination and questioning. The blood sample was not taken by Dr. Cohen but at the pathologist's premises. On each occasion the applicant was taking no medication and none was prescribed.

Counsel for the respondent argued that "if you go to the doctor you go for treatment". This view is with respect untenable; for instance, Goldman did not attend Dr. Brazowski for treatment.

Watts (3 ALD 267) to which we were referred is clearly distinguishable. There the applicant failed to disclose a number of occasions on which she visited a medical practitioner complaining of various symptoms e.g. headache and stomach pains, and when she was hospitalized for appendicitis and on another occasion to receive treatment for injuries received in a car accident.

Reliance was also placed on Mackrell (December 1981 unreported.) In that decision the Tribunal said: "We understand a doctor would regard the checking of vision and prescription of spectacles as "medical treatment" but the Tribunal finds no difficulty in accepting that the applicant (and indeed many possibly most laymen) would regard such a visit to a doctor as not involving "medical treatment".

In relation to this question and answer the applicant said:

"---Well, what I was going to say, in my mind

also the question seems to relate to me as

treatment, and I would say that I was treated

for the sprained fingers whereas I would

regard my check-ups with Dr Cohen as merely

that - a check-up. So I would say, to answer

the question, I interpreted it as treatment

for sprained fingers.

So you do not consider that going to see the

doctor is medical treatment?---No, I do not

because, as I have previously stated, during

that period of time I was quite healthy and

the visits to Dr Cohen were a matter of

routine. I think Dr Cohen himself said it

was a matter of just monitoring the

situation, he was not in fact treating me. I

was not on any drugs or taking any medicines

at the time."

On the information supplied to Goldman and in the state of health in which

he saw himself from the end of 1970 to May 1973, the answer which he gave was we think full, fair and truthful.

This leaves for consideration the issue raised for the first time before the Tribunal that the answer to question 1(i) was false or constituted a failure to provide required information.

Speaking of this occasion Goldman's evidence was:

"Tell me, prior to attending the hospital in

October for the purposes of the third

admission, which was the exploratory

operation and the appendicitis, are you able

to say now what your then understanding of

your condition was?---Yes. In that I have

done some research for myself on my medical

notes - - -

No; I am not asking you what now your

understanding is; I am asking you what then

was your understanding - in 1970?---In 1970?

What did you understand before you went to

hospital for the purposes of the operation I

have mentioned, to do with your appendix and

so forth?---I know I was at school

immediately prior to that - I was attending

school. I believe on the day I was admitted

that on the way to school I felt quite weak

and when I eventually got to school - I

suppose it was my mother who was called to

take me home. That is the event leading up

to me going into hospital.

Are you able to say now whether or not you

understood what was happening to you in that

year?---Not really. I suppose I was

unconcerned as to what was happening. I did

not feel very ill in myself, though I realise

now I must have been quite ill. That may

have been because having asthma previously as

a child I was to a certain extent used to

being ill and used to seeing doctors,

etcetera."

The following passages occur in the applicant's cross-examination:

"Mr Goldman, do you recall in giving evidence

a little earlier you mentioned you were

hospitalised following an attack of weakness

at school, or something of that

nature?---Yes.

Could you perhaps explain why you did not

disclose this for the purposes of the medical

examination in May 1973?---I would say the

only reason why I did not was because it was

something that was not on my mind, or that

did not appear relevant. I suppose a lot of

things that happen to people, such as falling

over and grazing their knee or even worse

accidents that happen to you which do not

seem relevant.

Are you saying you forgot, or are you saying

that you decided it was not relevant?---I do

not think that I decided it was not relevant

nor that I forgot. I would have been more

likely to say that it was something that did

not come to mind at the time.

If you go over to the right-hand block, you

see (g), (h) and then (i), which is "Attack

of unconsciousness or weakness"?---All I can

say is that I obviously failed to recall that

incident."

Counsel for the respondent argued that an attack of weakness precipitated

hospitalization, on the occasion when the exploratory operation was performed.

The argument was that the test to be applied was that set out in "Z" (16 September 1983) (sub. nom. Fry (supra.))

"36. The duty of disclosure is not absolute,

but it is high, and it cannot be stood aside

on the grounds of forgetfulness or of

misjudgment."

It should also be noted however that the Tribunal had earlier said:

"33. But the question is not what would have

been known had all been revealed. The

question is what information should have been

furnished by the applicant in the light of

his own knowledge of his history and medical

state."

The argument was, whether the applicant had forgotten or misjudged, there

was a failure to disclose or a furnishing of false information which was inconsistent with the applicant's obligation.

This is contrary to the view of the Federal Court in Fry (supra.) set out earlier. Further when speaking of the contention that "false" in the section should be interpreted as meaning false in fact, not false to the knowledge of the employee, so as to create an absolute liability or duty upon the employee, Sweeney J. with whom the other members of Court agreed said:

"An employee who answered in the negative a

question whether he suffered from a

particular condition would fail such a test

if at the time of the examination he in fact

suffered from that condition. It would not

be an answer to show that he was not then

aware of it, or that no medical examination

would then have revealed it, as in a case

where a later examination or an autopsy

disclosed facts which justified a finding

that the condition must have been present at

the time of the examination. Plain words

would be required to justify so harsh a

consequence and in my opinion they are not to

be found in the section."

This is not a case where there has been a deliberate concealment of what was

appreciated to be relevant information. Goldman when asked whether his answers on the medical examination form truly reflected his understanding of his condition at that time replied:

"---Yes. I believe now, I suppose then as

well, that I filled in the form honestly. I

suppose the only way I can sort of exemplify

that point was that I was surprised to find

out that I had supposedly put in misleading -

omitted to put in something in the form. I

was very surprised to hear that because I

believed that I filled out the form to the

best of my knowledge and honestly."

On a consideration of the whole of the evidence we accept that statement as

true, but the question still remains whether there was a breach of duty by the applicant in answering 1(i) as he did.

We are not satisfied that Goldman ever saw this feeling of weakness as anything other than the commencement of the day's events which finished with the operation or even that in May 1973 he had an independent recollection of it. He must find it very hard as he indicated to distinguish what he himself observed in 1970 from what he has learnt in the intervening 14 years. As previously set out, - when he first referred to the incident in evidence he said "one day I felt weak on the way to school" but he then went on to indicate he had little recollection of subsequent events on that day - he "supposed" someone rang his parents, he "thinks" his mother picked him up and took him, he "thinks", to see Dr. Cohen before his admission to hospital.

Later he said "I believe on the day I was admitted that on the way to school I felt quite weak . . . " which suggests a lack of any independent recollection but a knowledge of information gleaned from notes of his medical records. It would not be surprising if a child of thirteen had an emergency operation in which a highly inflamed or burst appendix was removed, and who had intense abdominal pain in hospital, should subsequently have no recollection of a temporary feeling of weakness or at least merge it into what he subsequently knew to be an attack of appendicitis, nor - anticipating a question which could later arise - is there any evidence to suggest that if Dr. Brazowski had known that the exploratory operation and appendectomy had been preceded on that day by a feeling of weakness, that the subsequent events would have been any different.

In the present case we conclude that Goldman was not in breach of his obligations when in May 1973 he failed to disclose that on the day of the emergency operation in 1970 he had a feeling of weakness which preceded the operation. Nor do we think that if he had, the result of Dr. Brazowski's enquiries would have been different.

It follows that we are not satisfied that in relation to his medical examination the applicant's conduct falls within s.184(5)(d)(i) of the Act.

We should not leave this review without commenting on a paragraph of Dr. Cohen's report of 9 October 1981 which reads as follows:

"As perhaps a gratuitous addendum to this

report, it must be stated that Mark admitted

'to the presence of a liver disorder at the

time that he was accepted into Commonwealth

employment and it is to be assumed that the

risks and consequences were evaluated at that

time before his actual acceptance."

Dr. Cohen's evidence was as follows:

"Are you able to explain the source of that

statement?---Well, I can only assume that it

was given to me by Mark - I would have no

other source of information - and it was

probably occasioned by my surprise that he

had been accepted into the service with such

a large spleen and with illness of such

manifest kind throughout his adolescence. I

cannot recall, but I assume that that must

have been at least the impression I gained

from Mark, that the doctor was aware that he

had this disorder."

The applicant's evidence was as follows:

"You say you cannot remember at that time

whether you - - - ? ---It would seem probable

that I had probably had advice that the

superannuation had been rejected and that the

discussion went something along the lines as

to why and I probably stated that I could not

understand why because I remember answering

the questions on the form, the medical

examination form, honestly. I may have asked

Dr Cohen's opinion as to, you know, why they

would not have picked up that there was

something wrong with me at the time of the

medical examination. That was all. I think

it was a pretty small, sort of - It was a

very short discussion. Most of the

discussions I have with Dr Cohen are fairly

short.

Just the words used, "It must be stated that

Mark admitted to the presence of a liver

disorder at the time he was accepted into the

Commonwealth employment". You can recall

having such a discussion with him?---I do not

think I used words that were quite that

strong as to blatantly say, "Yes, I admitted

to a liver disorder", because I do not think

at the time I realised I had a liver

disorder. I think what I said was that

having answered the form honestly and putting

down the relevant information, in particular

the fact that I had an operation which

resulted in an appendectomy, being all that I

could recall of the situation at the time - I

think, maybe, Dr Cohen for some reason

obviously thought he should put that

paragraph in that letter. I do not know

why."

Goldman does not claim to have told Dr. Brazowski of his illness in 1970 in

the explicit terms which Dr. Cohen uses.

Bearing in mind the view we have formed of the applicant's credibility we think it likely that Dr. Cohen's statement arises from some misunderstanding between him and Goldman.

It seems likely that Dr. Cohen's statement was prompted by his mistaken belief that Goldman had known he had a liver problem and his acceptance of Goldman's assurance that he had made a full and honest disclosure. This would lead him to infer that Goldman had told Dr. Brazowski that he had a liver disorder.

In view of the conclusions reached so far it is unnecessary to consider the further question under sub.para. 184(5)(d)(ii) to which we earlier referred.

For the reasons we have given we would set aside the decision under review.
