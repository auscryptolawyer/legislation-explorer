Re Taxation Appeals [1993] AATA 119; (1993) 25 ATR 1107 (15 April 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. NT92/156

AAT No. 8650

Number of pages - 6

Income Tax

[1993] AATA 119; (1993) 25 ATR 1107

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

P Gerber(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Audit - Alleged undisclosed income Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1988, sec 190(b)

HEARING

SYDNEY, 17 December 1992

15:4:1993

ORDER

The objection decision is set aside and the applicant's objection allowed in full.

DECISION

In this case, the respondent issued an amended assessment for the year ended 30 June 1988 as a result of an audit. The respondent concluded that, applying the source and application of funds method of accounting, there was an excess of $36,478 of funds expended over funds available, which he treated as having a revenue source and increased the applicant's taxable income by said amount. In addition, he imposed additional tax of 45% of the tax allegedly avoided ($13,293). The applicant has a simple explanation for the excess - the amount treated by the Commissioner as taxable income was, in the main, made up of loans from family members, the monies coming mainly from Italy.

2. The applicant, unemployed as at the date of hearing, was in the relevant year working as a labourer with Cockatoo Dockyard Pty Ltd. His nett income from that source was roughly $20,000.

3. The family's wealth is said to have its origin in Italy. The applicant's father, an Italian migrant, died in this country in 1977, leaving a widow, six sons and farming land in Italy. The mother returned to Italy soon after her husband's death and sold some of the land from 1982 onwards. In his evidence-in-chief, the applicant stated that:

I think that there was money given to the kids in patches as the

land was getting sold ... when she sold the land in Italy as she

sold each section so much money was coming in from different

sources over there and it was coming in in patches, like sometimes

my brother might bring over a certain amount, $6000, $7000. I might

go over there and see my mother and bring something back and it all

sort of built up from that in the space of so many years. (tr p 5.)

When asked how the money came to Australia, he replied: "In cash, it was all done in cash. I brought it in cash and just changed it over here". Some of the money was also allegedly brought back by Salvatore, one of the applicant's brothers.

4. The money thus accumulated over the years was claimed to have been kept in a safe at the applicant's home ("occasionally my family might ask for a loan or whatever, just to help them out a bit, and I would give them something and they would pay it back when they have got it, like it has always been in the family"). (tr p 6).

5. Turning to the calculation of the amended taxable income set out as "T 7" at p 14 of the "T" documents (exh "A"), the respondent arrived at "funds available" in the sum of $217,499, which included, amongst other things. $185,333 described as "sale of real estate" and an amount of $6,267 interest. On the other side of the ledger, dealing with "funds expended" he included, inter alia, an IBDs of $100,000 and rolled over interest of $6,267 on the $100,000 IBD, three loan repayments totalling $55,650, legal expenses $8,188, purchase Naval Pde $30,250, expenses Naval Pde $1337, two payments to Trivett Classic Pty Ltd (a car dealer) totalling $21,999 and overseas travel $7,000. Under "other expenses" he included Bankcard $9,885, NAB $4,575, proceeds from sale of a property at Church Street $11,485. The total (including some minor items not listed) comes to $262,256. The difference between "funds expended" and "funds available" amounts to $44,757. Of this amount, $36,478 was attributed to the taxpayer as taxable income, the balance ($8,279) to Frances, the taxpayer's wife.

6. Mr Conomos, the tax agent, who represented the taxpayer at the hearing and acted for him through most of the investigation, got his client to agree in the witness box that the two IBDs were put in his account at the National Bank. The taxpayer went on to explain that the amount of $55,650, shown as "loan repayment", were made up of three separate loans (set out in the T-account as $40,160, $10,000 and $5,496). It seems the $40,160 was paid into his home loan account at the National Bank from funds received from the sale of the Church Street property ($185,333); (ii) $10,000 was said to have been brought over from Italy in 1987 and handed over to the taxpayer allegedly in the relevant tax year. I do not know what the balance - $5496 - relates to. In his evidence-in-chief, the taxpayer gave a most confused explanation relating to the $10,000; it became somewhat clearer later in his evidence. It seems that his brother Rocco:

had a bit of a domestic problem overseas with his wife and

whatever, and they sold one of their apartments and apparently

someone from overseas bought it and he has come over to Australia

to live from Melbourne and he did not get on in Melbourne. The

gentleman who bought the actual apartment from Rocco, he was living

in Hay Street Leichardt. Now my brother had a bit of problems over

here and he had to pay so much to my brother - he owed my brother a

certain amount of money - and because my brother had all the

troubles, I took the $10,000 off this man called Firenzi I put it

into my bank account ... because my brother was in gaol at the time

and he had three kids overseas, he left his wife over there, she

could not have any way of getting any money over there because

everything was under my brother's name and it was like a family

sort of ... (tr p 7).

When asked when he received that money, the applicant replied:

In that period. I do not remember the exact date but it was in that

period, and as soon as I receive that $10,000 which I had more

money owing to me by my mother overseas, I have put this one in the

bank and they have taken the one from over there. (tr p 8).

Since the $10,000, given to him by his brother, was clearly paid into the applicant's home loan account with the Leichardt branch of the National Bank on 7 March 1988, this money was used to reduce the applicant's home loan.

The "legal expenses - $8,188" - were said to be the legal expenses and real estate's commission relating to the sale of the Church Street property.

7. The reference to "Naval Parade" relates to the acquisition of a block of land ("we had a bit of money in the safe and there was a block of land down the coast that was up for sale at a good price and I spoke to my wife and my brother at the time and we purchased the land" - transcript p 9). The amounts shown in the T-account re this item - $30,250 and $1,337 - relate to the purchase price and expenses relating to that purchase. The taxpayer alleged that the property was purchased in the name of his wife with funds derived from the sale of a property at Frazer Street, Leichardt, which was owned by the taxpayer's brother Salvatore, the gentleman with the domestic problems and currently in goal.

8. When I queried the taxpayer why a house, paid for with funds provided by Salvatore, was put in the name of his wife answer came through a glass darkly: It seems that Salvatore:

had a lot of personal problems with his wife and for not for his

wife to receive any of this money it was sort of given to me at the

time, sort of not to know where the money went to. And if I would

have bought it (the Naval Parade property) under my name at the

time probably she would have sort of put a claim on with that

money, with that property, so it was put under my wife's name at

the time. ... We rang up Mum at the time - we've still got

properties over there and land would be sold, it would be sort of

arranged in that way that he'll sort of benefit when he gets back

overseas. (tr p 11)

The money for the purchase of Naval Parade was paid by Salvatore to the taxpayer "in dribs and drabs" and put in the taxpayer's safe:

He (Salvatore) has had a few problems, domestic and personal

problems, and at that time he had to sell the property and also the

furniture and everything what is inside the house. And when he did

sell the property for not for his wife to receive any of that money

at the time and make out he had spent all the money, he was giving

it to me in dribs and drabs, something to around about $30,000

worth. (tr p 10)

9. The taxpayer kept no records about the dribs and drabs as they came in. When asked whether he had ever repaid his brother, the answer was even more obscure:

When he went over there (Italy) I still had some money to me by my

mother, and I've given him in dribs and drabs when he was over

there, but not the amount of $30,000. (tr p 12)

10. The Naval Parade land was sold the following year for $85,000. What became of the proceeds is unknown save that the taxpayer admits that Salvatore is still owed "about $10,000".

11. The reference in the T-account to "Trivett Classic P/L $21,999" relates to the purchase of a new 6 cylinder BMW motor car. This car was bought from Trivett for $68,367 and paid for as follows:

Deposit 3,000

Trade-in allowance 41,367

Balance 27,000

12. Exh "C" consists of two receipts from Trivett, one for "$3,000 cash, and the other for "$470.07 cash", and "$18,529.93 paid by cheque". Next to the latter amount is a handwritten notation: "from sale of Church St, Leichardt". I am satisfied that the amount of $18,529.93 was indeed the endorsement of a cheque for that amount received as a deposit for the sale of the Church Street property which had been released prior to settlement. I am likewise satisfied that the $40,160.37 credit which appears on the applicant's bank statement on 29 June 1988 was part of the proceeds of the sale of the Church Street property and extinguished the loan account. Earlier on 7 March 1988, the applicant paid in $10,000, reducing his debit balance to $39,046. Against this last transaction appears a handwritten notation "Firenzi" (a reference to the $10,000, referred to in para 6 above, said to have been brought out from Italy in 1987 and allegedly handed to the taxpayer by a Mr Firenzi.

13. Summarising the car transaction, the order form (undated) sets out the terms of payment. Having traded in his earlier BMW model for a new one and paid some $22,000 in two payments of $3,000 and $19,000 and still owed Trivett $5,000 which, according to the "Details of Settlement" on the Order Form (exh "C") was payable by 14 June 1988. The Commissioner accepts that this amount was never paid to Trivett and made the appropriate adjustment to the alleged "understatement" of income.

14. Turning to the sale of the Church Street property, the applicant agreed that the payment into his National Bank account of $40,160.37 on 29 June 1988 extinguished the home loan. The T-account shows that an amount of $100,000 was put into the IBD shown and this was not challenged. The taxpayer was asked about the overseas travel claim - $7,000. He agreed that he went overseas in the relevant year but denied having spent anything like $7,000.

15. The amounts of $9,885 and $4,575 "NAB Bankcard" were not disputed.

16. The next item - "Proceeds from sale of Church St - $11,485" was admitted as being spent on personal living items.

17. Mr Conomos finally asked the taxpayer to explain to the Tribunal the source of the $36,478 which the Commissioner had treated as undeclared income. His explanation did not waiver - the money came from Italy:

Well, it come from the sale of land from '82 to such time to '87.

There was land being sold in Italy by my mother and the money was

come in dribs and drabs because over there, it is more by word of

mouth than there is straight on contract when they sell a block of

land or apartment or whatever, and as the money was coming from

relatives who Brought the properties over there, once it was

available I made every effort to bring the money to Australia.

That's where the money come from. I mean, even the paperwork I've

shown them (i.e. the Commissioner) tells you that. I've still got

two properties there and there was land being sold in a document I

showed them.

MR CONOMOS: They are not yours, are they? They are your mother's,

are they not?---No, not mine. It's my mother's

MR CONOMOS: That's what you will inherit?---That's what I will

inherit, what's on the document there, that's correct. (tr p 17)

18. Unequivocal evidence was tendered that the applicant did indeed receive in Italian lira amounts equivalent to some $70,000 from his brother Rocco via four bank drafts between November and December 1992, all of which were paid into a Westpac account in the name of the applicant.

19. Having been advised that the taxpayer's mother was old, ill and illiterate, with little comprehension of the English language, that concluded the evidence-in-chief. Mr Conomos asked no questions about a "loan" of $30,000, said to have been made to a brother-in-law, one John Roma the money allegedly coming from the proceeds of the sale of the Church Street property. This "loan" was adverted to by a previous tax agent in a letter to the respondent dated 12 December 1989 (exh "E").

20. The applicant was cross-examined by an officer of the Tax Office and taken carefully through the record of interview with Miss S, the auditor. Thus prior to the commencement of the audit, in a letter written to the Commissioner on behalf of the taxpayer by a Mr Mangraviti, the tax agent referred to in the previous paragraph, it was said that (i) that Salvatore advanced $30,000 by way of loan to the taxpayer, the proceeds from the sale of the Frazer Street property; (ii) that the taxpayer lent some $30,000 to John Roma (iii) $7,000 was spent on an overseas trip and (iv) that $10,000 was given to the taxpayer "by his brother Rocky via an acquaintance named Antonio Firenzi who owed the taxpayer's brother this sum".

21. Dealing with the "loan" of $30,000 received by the taxpayer from Salvatore in "dribs and drabs" ("when he was selling the property at the time, he sold his furniture, sold the kitchen, material in the kitchen, refrigeration, TV sets, the tables, the best suite, some clothing that Mum gives ..."), it was put to the Taxpayer that, in fact, Salvatore received the proceeds from the sale of Frazer Street in two cheques - one for $29,700 and the other one for $531.84 from the solicitor acting in the transaction. Vouching established that on 27 April 1987, $10,500 of the cheque for $29,700 was cashed, $19,000 being deposited into an IBD and $200 into an Advantage Saver Account. On 1 June the IBD was terminated and the $19,000 deposited into the Advantage Saver Account. The admission of the solicitor's letter to Salvatore was objected to. Since the issue of the "loan" by Salvatore of $30,000 goes to credibility, I indicated that I would attempt to arrange a telephone hook-up with Salvatore at the Oberon prison farm. This course met with the approval of both parties, I adjourned the hearing briefly to arrange for the hook-up and the letter was admitted over Mr Conomos' objection (exh 2).

22. Whilst the taxpayer's evidence-in-chief left much to be desired, he fared better in cross-examination, to the point where he has persuaded me that there was family farming property in the South of Italy which came to the mother via the father. This land was practically worthless as farming land when the family migrated to Australia in 1957. However, as a result of the urban sprawl, the land gradually gained in value as it began to be bought up for building sites for apartments and the like and I accept that it was indeed sold off in the manner deposed to.

23. On close cross-examination about the Firenzi payment of $10,000, it turns out that Firenzi is a first cousin of the taxpayer who had bought an apartment in Italy belonging to Rocky, the taxpayer's brother. It was not fully paid for. Rocky, it seems left his wife and five children "for another woman" and came to Australia:

And naturally this bloke had to pay off whatever he sold the

property over there, and he had the money here. He said to me:

"would you give that to me brother?"- this is to me - and the money

was overseas owing to me for the sale of the land from my mother,

right? Well, Mum - and I'll put it this way, and my mother will

give the $10,000 to my sister-in-law over there with the kids. (tr

p 33)

... Firenzi was living in Leichardt and because he still owed

money to the Property Board over there off me brother when he was

living in Italy, all right. He still owed it, so he gave $10,000 to

me brother, which brother said to me: "I will give $10,000 to (my)

wife" - that's my sister-in-law. So I told - that was all

transferred between the families, saying, "look, I've got the

$10,000 here. The $10,000 what my mother's still got over there is

entitled to me. Take it out of that". And that's how it was done.

(tr p 34)

24. Dealing with the "loan" to the brother-in-law (Roma), a matter left unexplained in evidence-in-chief, this was allegedly a loan made to Roma before 1987. The taxpayer claimed that he was mistaken when he told his Tax Agent about this loan. I accept the taxpayer's explanation about this loan and that it was lent before the period under review, the funds coming via his mother in Italy.

25. After the luncheon adjournment, contact was able to be made with Salvatore by telephone. At the outset, I am satisfied that this hook-up was totally unexpected by him and that Salvatore had had no prior indication that he would be giving evidence. After taking an affirmation, he was asked by Mr Conomos what he did with the proceeds from the sale of the Frazer Street property. He replied:

What I did with them? I gave to me brother John. (a nickname for

the applicant)

He was then asked how much he gave to his brother:

I can't remember. I remember I had to give a fair whack to the bank

and that. Not really off the top of me head, no. It would have been

about $30,000, $40,000. I can't remember. It depends what I gave

the bank. I,m not too sure about that, to tell you the truth. With

that I paid back the bank some money on loans I got off them. ... I

can't remember the amount to tell you the truth. No, because we got

property overseas that me mother's given it to us. And I was just

going to take one of his houses over there when me mother died -

this was like, you know, when I'd inherit it, you know, so I've got

a bit of property over there. ... It would have been over $40,000

because I got about $80,000 for the house if I remember correctly.

I'm not too sure. (tr pp 45-46)

When asked whether he was sure that he gave his brother approximately

$40,000, the witness replied:

Yes, that would have been what was left over from what the bank

give me, yes. ... I withdrew it out in cash, I think I remember,

and I took it out of the bank bit by bit; $5000, $10,000 here and

there. (tr p 46)

26. The witness was rigorously cross-examined. When asked why he gave the money to his brother, he replied with disarming frankness:

I was into drugs at the time and I was going to spend it, so

I - you know, just thought I'd have something there in case

anything ever happened. ... I told him to mind it for me and I said

if things come to things, I've got me property in Italy - well we

got two houses that our mother has left us, and I'll take one of

his houses over there when me mother dies. (tr p 47)

He was asked whether he gave (i.e. gifted) the money to John or gave it

to him to take care of. He replied:

I gave him the money to do what he wanted, and if things - he

couldn't pay it back, I was to have one of his houses. (tr p 47)

27. Salvatore stated that none of the money he gave to the taxpayer had been repaid, that he expects to return to Italy at the completion of his sentence: "You see, we've got - our mother has given us two houses each overseas, right? And she is living, and until she dies we don't get them". Whilst some of this evidence does not sit easily with the evidence given by the taxpayer, given the lack of any documentation between members of this family, on balance, I am satisfied that the applicant's assertion that he has repaid all except approximately $10,000 is likely to be more accurate given the transactions that occurred between the applicant and his mother in Italy.

28. At the conclusion of the cross-examination, I asked the witness some questions (tr p 50) about the sale of the Frazer Street property based on a letter from the firm of solicitors acting for him as vendor and obtained by the Commissioner (exh 2). Unfortunately, the second page of this letter is missing. It is clear that the Frazer Street property was sold for $75,000 to Mr and Mrs Roma (Mrs Roma being Salvatore's sister). There was a mortgage of $34,788 on the land which was discharged from the proceeds of sale. The first page of the letter states:

As you are aware, pursuant to the arrangements entered into between

yourself and your mother, you were to receive the sum of $6,000

from the proceeds of sale of the property. ...

On settlement, we obtained a cheque in favour of this firm for

$27,227.52. In accordance with the arrangements entered into with

your mother, the sum was invested in a term deposit with

...../2.

The rest is silence. It is clear from the letter, however, that the solicitor made a request to the mother that the proceeds be released to Salvatore. When I put the contents (or what there was of it) to the witness, he recalled some details about a term deposit, but was adamant that he took out all the money.

I ended up putting that much because I ended up giving about 10

grand to my brother before then, and I put 27,000 in a term deposit

and ended up taking it out about, oh, can't remember how long

after, couple of months after.

He was adamant that the money did not belong to his mother, that it was his.

The arrangement I had with my mother was, I had to pay my brothers

so much money, right? I did that because my mother sold some

property for me overseas, right?"

The overseas property referred to by the witness is the property sold to Firenzi and his wife:

"They bought the property off me in Italy ...(inaudible) ... that

my mother had given me, right?

Since this matter was not touched on by either party, I did not see it as my role to explore this any further.

29. I must say that I was impressed with Salvatore's evidence, coming as it did, "out of the blue"; it certainly gave no hint of collusion or prior consultation with the taxpayer. Bearing in mind that both these brothers come from an unsophisticated farming family from the backblocks of Southern Italy (albeit Salvatore was, in fact born in Australia), their confusion about financial dealings is understandable. It is certainly true that the family dealt with each other in a very nonchalant fashion and that no attempt was made to document the various transactions, let alone obtain receipts. Thus, whilst I was left in considerable doubt as what occurred at the conclusion of the taxpayer's evidence, his brother was able to shed much light on events that would otherwise have remained a mystery. I am also mindful that the investigator did not have the advantage of interviewing Salvatore. This is in no sense intended as a criticism of the investigation, but it does explain why I differ in my conclusion from the decision of the respondent and place less emphasis on the lack of documentation and/or receipts.

30. Ms S, the auditor was called and told me how she set about arriving at the T-account. Thus she stated in evidence that the T-account did not balance, that "only one loan was declared at the initial interview and two more during the course of the audit. One to (the taxpayer) of $30,000 is negated in a T-account by another loan of $30,000 by (the taxpayer) but ... the validity of these loans is doubted. The $10,000 loan from Salvatore only came to light when the agent was questioned. The availability of Rocky's funding has not been scrutinized due to cost effectiveness, but in light of the two previous loans and the late disclosure, the loan has not been accepted." The purchase of a new BMW also aroused a natural suspicion, the more so since it was not disclosed.

31. Ms S rightly ignored the "loan" to Roma ($30,000), said to have been made by the taxpayer from the proceeds of the sale of the Church Street property, on the basis that it was "impossible". She refused to accept the loan of $30,000 from Salvatore to the taxpayer re the acquisition of the Naval Parade land and rejected the $10,000 "loan" from brother Rocky. Satisfied that some unexplained cash was used to acquire the BMW car, Ms S arrived at an understatement of $44,757.

32. Ms S conceded in cross-examination that the taxpayer had told her during the interview that his mother had funds in Italy for him and that he had received funds from that source and that some documents (in Italian) were given to her. Ms S had these documents translated but did not refer to them in her evidence.

33. I can well understand that the taxpayer did not create a good impression during his interview with Ms S and that she would be singularly unimpressed by undocumented loans within the family, lacking any kind of corroboration or documentation. Indeed, if the taxpayer's evidence had stood alone, I may have come to much the same conclusion as the investigator. However, once I accept the taxpayer and his brother as witnesses of truth, which I do, and am satisfied that the amount of the "understatement" has its source in family property and resultant loans, I am satisfied that the amount of alleged undisclosed income arrived at as a results of the audit cannot survive.

34. In the result, the objection decision is set aside and the applicant's objection allowed in full.
