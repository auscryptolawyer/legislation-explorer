Marsh and Commissioner for Superannuation [1995] AATA 508 (27 April 1995)

Marsh and Commissioner for Superannuation [1995] AATA 508 (27 April 1995)

Last Updated: 24 September 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISIONS

SUPERANNUATION - late election - whether to be treated as within time - discretion - applicant not aware of possibility of preserving benefits - no information provided by employer - reasons for delay - no prejudice to respondent - commitment to superannuation - requirements of justice - decision set aside and remitted



Superannuation Act 1976 s 137(1) 157(1)



Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 7 ALD 315

Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1993) 33 ALD 420

Ward and Commissioner of Superannuation (unreported AAT No 9987 of 6 February 1995)

Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1993) 123 ALR 239



AAT Decision No 10153



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No: A94/185

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )



Re: MARGARET RUTH MARSH

Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal : Ms G Ettinger, Senior Member

Mr N J Attwood AO, Member

Mr H G Julian DSC, Member



Date : 27 April 1995



Place : Canberra



Decision :



The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and remits the matter to the Respondent for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the election made by the Applicant on 21 July 1992 under sub-section 137(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that sub-section of the Act.



G ETTINGER

...............................

Senior Member



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No: A94/185

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )



Re: MARGARET RUTH MARSH

Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION



Ms G Ettinger, Senior Member 27 April 1995

Mr N J Attwood AO, Member

Mr H G Julian DSC, Member

  1. The application for review before the Tribunal concerned a decision by a delegate for the Commissioner for Superannuation dated 6 September 1994 which affirmed an earlier decision dated 24 March 1994 made under sub-section 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (“the Act”) not to allow the election made by the Applicant on 21 July 1992 under sub-section 137(1) of the Act to be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that sub-section of the Act.
  2. The Applicant represented herself and the Respondent was represented by Ms V Guthrie of the office of the Commissioner for Superannuation. Oral evidence was given by the Applicant, Ms Sonja Jakacic, Mr Evan Bomford and Mr Ian James Rosie. The Tribunal accepted into evidence as Exhibit R2, documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (T1-T53) and the following:

Supporting Statement of Applicant undated A1

Statutory Declaration of Applicant 24 February 1995 A2

Applicant’s original S2A Form 9 October 1985 R1

Statement of Evan Bomford 20 February 1995 R3

ISSUE BEFORE THE TRIBUNAL

  1. The issue to be considered by the Tribunal was whether it was desirable pursuant to sub-section 157(1) of the Act to recognise Ms Marsh’s election on 21 July 1992 pursuant to sub-section 137(1) of the Act to preserve her superannuation entitlements as if her late election had been made within the period allowed by that sub-section of the Act.

THE LEGISLATION

  1. Sub-section 137(1) of the Act provided as relevant in 1985:
“137. (1) Where a person ceases to be an eligible employee and, upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit...he may, not later than 21 days after he so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner, that this Division apply in relation to him.”
  1. Sub-section 157(1) of the Act provided as relevant in 1985, a discretion vested in the Commissioner for Superannuation to recognise a late election:
“157. (1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly.”

EVIDENCE BEFORE THE TRIBUNAL

  1. Ms March was born on 8 September 1952. In July 1974, she joined the then Department of Manufacturing Industry (T7/34), later to merge as part of the Department of Defence. From the 11 November 1974, the Applicant became an “eligible employee” under the Act, by virtue of her permanent appointment as a Clerical Assistant Grade 1. After working for seven months in Woomera Village, approximately 480 kilometres north-west of Adelaide, the Applicant worked at the Joint Defence Research Facility at Nurrungar, approximately fifteen kilometres from Woomera.
  2. In June 1985 the Applicant took long service leave and travelled to the United States where she married her American fiancee. On 24 September 1985 (T26), Ms Marsh sent a letter of resignation to the Department of Defence and enclosed a completed form S2A for processing in relation to her superannuation (Exhibit R1). At the time of her resignation, Ms Marsh had reached the level of Clerical Assistant Grade 5.
  3. The Applicant told the Tribunal that she had inquired on three occasions about superannuation in relation to resignation before the age of retirement. Ms Marsh said the first occasion occurred during the latter half of the 1970’s. She had consulted her supervisor Mr B McCarthy, whom she reported as follows:
“...he thought one received a refund of contributions and interest, but cautioned that he wasn’t sure...and suggested that I contact the personnel section at Woomera for more information...”
  1. The Applicant described her work environment as unique because she and her supervisor were the “only two employees of the Australian Public Service based permanently on a site of several hundred employees” of military members or civilian contractors to the United States Air Force. Ms Marsh said that she and her supervisor were attached to a regional office in Adelaide and a personnel unit at Keswick, Adelaide. To save the cost of a long distance call to Adelaide, enquiries of a general personnel nature were usually made to the personnel section at Woomera, approximately fifteen kilometres from Nurrungar. Ms Marsh said that as a clerical assistant she did not have the authority to make long distance calls, nor did she have immediate access to long distance telephone lines. She reported that all such calls went through an operator and were documented.
  2. Ms Marsh said the second inquiry she made occurred when she telephoned the Defence personnel section at Woomera shortly after speaking to her supervisor in the late 1970’s about the status of superannuation refunds upon resignation. The Applicant said that she had spoken to a man. She could not however recall his name. She said she was told that upon resigning, a person would receive his or her own contributions and interest. Ms Marsh told the Tribunal that the option to preserve one’s superannuation was not mentioned in this conversation.
  3. The Applicant said that the third inquiry was made in 1985, prior to her departure from Woomera and subsequent resignation from the public service. Ms Marsh said she telephoned the personnel section at Keswick and spoke to a man whose name she could not recall. Ms Marsh said that she had made general enquiries about her superannuation in the context of her “tentative plans” to travel overseas and resign. Ms Marsh said that she was told she would “‘want the cash’ ”. Ms Marsh told the Tribunal that the personnel officer had not informed her of other options such as preservation, nor was she given any other information or offered any form of briefing in relation to her resignation.
  4. Ms Marsh said that she received the form S2A (Exhibit R1) in America. The Tribunal notes that the form S2A was headed “APPLICATION FOR REFUND OF ACCUMULATED CONTRIBUTIONS/LUMP SUM BENEFIT OR ELECTION FOR PRESERVATION OF SUPERANNUATION RIGHTS” and provided three options to the Applicant in respect of her superannuation; a refund of accumulated contributions, a payment of a lump sum benefit and preservation of superannuation rights. The Applicant’s form before the Tribunal indicated that the option to receive a refund of contributions had been chosen. Ms Marsh told the Tribunal that although she could not recall crossing out the last two options, she would have elected the first believing that this was the only option available to her upon resignation.
  5. It is also noted that the back of the Applicant’s form S2A provided for a former contributor to be “advised of Preservation Rights and Form S302 issued...”. This was to be signed and dated by an officer of the Australian Government Retirements Benefits Office. The Tribunal observes that this section of the Applicant’s form S2A was neither signed nor dated.
  6. Ms Marsh said that although her “ultimate intention” was to remain in America, she had wanted to leave her options open and did not wish to resign from the public service before she left. The Applicant said that she was a sole parent at the time, responsible for her daughter and receiving no maintenance form her first husband:
“...I wanted to have that option to come back [to Australia] and have a job to come back to...I’m a very security conscious person...there was no certainty that we were always going to live in America. My [American] husband was in the military...There was always a possibility we could be re-assigned back to Australia...”
  1. The Applicant’s accumulated superannuation contributions and interest were paid into her Australian bank account on 6 November 1985 (Exhibit A2). Ms Marsh told the Tribunal that she had not resigned to gain access to her superannuation money, stating that the cash payment was “a result of my resignation and not vice versa.”
  2. The Respondent inquired about the Applicant’s transfer of assets in relation to her property settlement with her former husband which was finalised in March 1985. Ms Marsh told the Tribunal that she was not required to transfer any money to her ex-husband, saying that she had in fact received a cash transfer from him.
  3. The Applicant rejected the Respondent’s suggestion that she had elected the refund of her superannuation to cover the expenses of moving from Woomera to America, the marriage to her American fiancee and their subsequent honeymoon. Ms Marsh said during 1984 and 1985 she had budgeted for expenses such as legal costs in relation to her divorce and her move to America. Prior to receiving her superannuation refund, Ms Marsh said that she owned a rental property and shares, and held a term deposit for an amount similar to the superannuation refund in Australia, and was therefore not “short on the money...”
  4. Ms Marsh returned with her husband to live in Australia on 30 June 1991. She successfully undertook both the entry examination for the public service “at the first opportunity” in December 1991, and the Graduate Administrative Assistant program examination in May 1992. Whilst in America, Ms Marsh had gained a Bachelor of Science Degree in Business. The Applicant told the Tribunal that she commenced employment in the Department of Employment Education and Training (“DEET”) in Adelaide in 1992, before moving to Canberra and working as an Administrative Service Officer Grade 6, for the Department of Social Security.
  5. Prior to her re-entry into the public service, Ms Marsh said she had gained temporary and permanent positions in the private sector. Ms Marsh told the Tribunal that she had taken a substantial drop in salary to return to the public service because she felt her longer term options were better, she enjoyed her work in the public service and felt it provided her with the opportunity “to make a contribution”.
  6. The Applicant told the Tribunal that she first learnt of the option to preserve superannuation at an induction briefing with DEET in Adelaide, given by Mr Evan Bomford on 7 July 1992. On 21 July 1992 Ms Marsh applied to the Commissioner for Superannuation to make a late election to preserve her superannuation rights following her resignation in 1985 (T29).

EVIDENCE OF MS SONJA JAKACIC

  1. Ms Sonja Jakacic told the Tribunal that she had been working as one of three staff clerks in the personnel section of the Department of Defence at the Keswick Base in Adelaide in October 1985. Ms Jakacic said her base administered three people at Nurrungar, a clerk and two clerical assistants Grades 3 and 5.
  2. When asked about the level of knowledge in relation to the preservation of superannuation on resignation, Ms Jakacic said “we didn’t know very much about preservation but it was given as an option when people inquired.” Had someone required more information about preservation, Ms Jakacic said she would have contacted the Retirement Benefits Office. Ms Jakacic said that working with a small number of officers meant that she had a general understanding of personnel issues but did not focus on any one in particular.
  3. Ms Jakacic said that the usual procedure in relation to resignees was a form of interview, where the resignee would come to the section and actually complete the superannuation form with a personnel officer. Ms Jakacic could not recall personally attending to a resignee who was unable to present him or her self at the personnel section. She said that she was unaware of any information sheet in relation to superannuation which was handed to resignees. Ms Jakacic said that of the approximately fifteen resignations she had dealt with, none had elected to take the preservation option, nor had they raised questions about it as they went through their forms.

EVIDENCE OF MR IAN JAMES ROSIE

  1. The Tribunal notes that Mr Ian James Rosie had been identified by the Respondent as an officer in the personnel section of Keswick at the time the Applicant commenced her long service leave in 1985. Mr Rosie told the Tribunal that he had worked in that section for four months.
  2. The Respondent referred Mr Rosie to document T20, which he identified as a file note he had written dated 19 June 1985, signed on 20 June 1985. This detailed salary and leave calculations in relation to the Applicant, then known as Ms Mullins.
  3. Mr Rosie told the Tribunal he could not specifically recall the conversation he had with the Applicant. However according to his file notes which he regarded as comprehensive, they had spoken about the ceasing of union and rental deductions, annual recreation leave and long service entitlements. Mr Rosie said that he would have recorded any discussion of superannuation if they had raised the topic. He could find no reference to superannuation in his notes. It was possible, Mr Rosie agreed, that specific superannuation calculations were not made because this was not necessary until the S2A form had arrived and a resignation tendered.
  4. Mr Rosie said that he had a particular interest in superannuation. When asked whether he knew that preservation existed as an option in June 1985, he replied:
“If I’d had the [superannuation] course before then I’m sure I would have. If I hadn’t I’m still uncertain. I possibly could have, because as I said I had an interest in the preservation of benefits...”
  1. On cross-examination Mr Rosie agreed that the personnel section was small and his duties covered a variety of areas. Mr Rosie found it hard to estimate the time he spent advising people about resignations. He said that he had briefed approximately three to five people about resignation and could not recall if any had elected preservation or made enquiries about it.
  2. The Tribunal notes the evidence of Mr Rosie; however it finds it of little assistance in determining what had actually been said to the Applicant, and by whom, at the time she made enquiries with the personnel section at Keswick.

EVIDENCE OF MR E BOMFORD

  1. Mr Evan Bomford told the Tribunal that he had commenced working in the personnel section of the public service in September 1980. In September 1985, upon his transfer to the Department of Defence, he began conducting seminars on superannuation and later wrote articles and co-authored a book on the subject.
  2. Between 1987 and 1989 Mr Bomford stated that he had conducted retrenchment information sessions at Keswick (Exhibit A3). Mr Bomford said he found that the staff were not “well versed in superannuation matters generally”. In cross-examination Mr Bomford said that he had not worked in either the Keswick or Woomera personnel office and did not know any of the Keswick or Woomera staff personally in October 1985.

SUBMISSIONS

  1. Ms Marsh submitted that the circumstances surrounding her late application for preservation should attract a favourable operation of the discretion under sub-section 157(1) of the Act. She submitted that she had lived and worked in a remote location and had had difficulty in accessing information in relation to personnel matters. Further, on three separate occasions when she had made enquiries, Ms Marsh said that she had not been informed of the option to preserve her superannuation.
  2. Ms Marsh submitted that she elected a refund of her superannuation money because she understood that this was her only option. She did not need access to her funds at the time of resignation. Ms Marsh submitted that had she been informed about the option to elect preservation of her superannuation in 1985, she would have done so. The Applicant submitted that she had invested her refund of superannuation, stating that she did not need it for any other purpose and had always intended that it remain invested. Her commitment to the notion of superannuation, Ms Marsh submitted, could be evidenced by the establishment of her own superannuation fund upon her return to Australia in 1991.
  3. Ms Marsh submitted that there was a reasonable explanation for the consequent delay of six years and ten months between the date of her resignation and her application to preserve her superannuation contributions. Ms Marsh submitted that it took this length of time before she was advised comprehensively of her rights of preservation on resignation. When informed about the option to preserve superannuation, it had taken the Applicant only two weeks to apply for a late preservation. Ms Marsh submitted that this indicated that she had not “slept on her rights.”
  4. According to Ms Marsh, resignees who did not attend an exit interview at the personnel section were disadvantaged. She submitted that the form S2A had been sent to her in America without any instructions about its contents or how to complete it. Ms Marsh rejected the Respondent’s suggestion that she should have telephoned from America to make enquiries about the S2A form, saying that she had made her enquiries in Australia prior to leaving. After receiving the same reply with regard to superannuation three times, Ms Marsh submitted that to the best of her knowledge she knew the correct answer. Ms Marsh referred to the cases of Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1993) 123 ALR 239 and Ward and Commissioner of Superannuation (unreported AAT No 9987 of 6 February 1995) to support her contention that there was no onus upon her to make further enquiries when she did not know there were further enquiries she could make.
  5. The Applicant submitted that she could not recall the names of the two male personnel staff she spoke to at Keswick and Woomera and queried whether Mr Rosie had been one of them.
  6. Ms Marsh submitted that the Respondent had not demonstrated how her late election could jeopardise the orderly administration of the superannuation scheme. However the disadvantage the Applicant would suffer if her late election were not accepted was considered by Ms Marsh to be significant. The Applicant submitted that she was disadvantaged as an individual small scale investor in comparison to the Respondent in such areas as investment fees, investment opportunities, preferential treatment and specialised advice. Ms Marsh submitted that it was relevant to have regard to the totality of her service. The ten years of prior service in the public service would be discounted in relation to her retirement income, Ms Marsh said, and she would be further disadvantaged by receiving a reduced pension on retirement.
  7. Ms Guthrie for the Respondent, referring to the decision of Von Doussa J in Boardman (supra), submitted that there was an evidentiary burden on the Applicant to place information before the Tribunal sufficient to satisfy it that a late election should be recognised. The Respondent emphasised to the Tribunal that a lapse of over six years had occurred between the date of the Applicant’s resignation and that of her late election, creating difficulties in establishing a factual base to justify a late election. Ms Guthrie directed the Tribunal to the decision of Von Doussa J in Boardman (supra), where the lapse of time had been traversed.
  8. The Respondent submitted that the Applicant would not have elected to preserve her superannuation contributions in 1985, even if she had been fully informed of her options. Ms Guthrie said the Applicant’s permanent departure from Woomera, coupled with her move to America and marriage to an American, demonstrated that she had no intention of returning to Australia and resuming her employment with the public service. The only reason she returned, submitted the Respondent, was because her husband suffered back complaints and was discharged from the Air Force. Further, the expenses involved with departing Woomera, moving to America, getting married and supporting her daughter without maintenance from her ex-husband, indicated that the Applicant would not have elected to preserve her superannuation.
  9. The Respondent submitted that Ms Marsh had demonstrated that she was a prudent and capable manager of her financial affairs. Ms Guthrie contended that the Applicant’s investment of her money in short term deposits in Australia was indicative of her need to have the money at call should the need arise. According to the Respondent, the Applicant could only say that she had no need of the refund and was able to keep it invested with the benefit of hindsight. The Respondent also submitted that little weight could be attached to the private superannuation fund the Applicant joined on her return to Australia. Ms Guthrie contended that this did not indicate what the Applicant would have done in 1985, as awareness of the importance of superannuation in the 1990’s was greater than it had been in the 1980’s.
  10. Ms Guthrie submitted that on the balance of probabilities, there was no evidence beyond that given by the Applicant that she had not had information in relation to the preservation of her superannuation. Ms Guthrie submitted that the Applicant should not have relied upon the information of her supervisor who was not versed in the area of superannuation on resignation, citing the Tribunal decision presided over by Bulley J in Chalk and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 278; 30 ALD 537.
  11. Ms Guthrie submitted that the Applicant’s poor memory of her conversation with an officer at the Woomera personnel section demonstrated that there was no reliable evidence before the Tribunal that any conversation took place. The Respondent submitted, based upon the file note at T20 and the evidence before the Tribunal that Mr Rosie was the officer to whom the Applicant spoke when she telephoned Keswick. Ms Guthrie referred the Tribunal to Mr Rosie’s evidence which suggested that superannuation had not been discussed and that Mr Rosie had been capable of giving advice in relation to the preservation option. Further the Respondent submitted that Ms Jakacic had given evidence indicating that she was aware of the option and would have contacted the relevant parties on behalf of the Applicant should the need for more detailed information have arisen.
  12. The Respondent submitted that when filling out the form S2A, the Applicant had not relied on any information she had received from Mr Rosie. Ms Guthrie submitted that the Applicant had already decided the option she would take on resignation, based upon the conversation with her supervisor and with the personnel officer at Keswick, which she simply later confirmed in her conversation with Mr Rosie.
  13. Ms Guthrie refuted the Applicant’s claim that geographical isolation created difficulties. This was demonstrated she said, by the Applicant’s ability to overcome the approximately 500 kilometre distance by telephoning personnel at Keswick before she left for America. The Respondent submitted that assuming the Applicant had struck out the preservation option on the form S2A, Ms Marsh could have similarly overcome the geographical distance between America and Australia by making a telephone call to personnel to seek clarification of the meaning of preservation on the form.
  14. Finally it was submitted that the Respondent did not owe a duty of care to the Applicant. Ms Guthrie cited the case of Chalk (supra) which, she observed, put to rest the issue of the Respondent’s legal duty.

CONCLUSIONS

  1. The Tribunal has reviewed all the evidence before it and must determine whether it is desirable to recognise the Applicant’s late election to preserve her superannuation entitlements pursuant to sub-section 157(1) of the Act, as if it had been made within the period allowed under sub-section 137(1) of the Act.
  2. The Tribunal is mindful of the principles as expressed in the decision of Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Development Pty Limited v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 7 ALD 315, and is further assisted by the later Full Court judgement of Black CJ, Davies and Cooper JJ in Chalk (supra), Von Doussa J in Boardman (supra) and the Tribunal decision of Ward (supra).
  3. In its consideration of whether it is “desirable” in all the circumstances of this matter to recognise the Applicant’s late election to preserve her superannuation entitlements, the Tribunal is mindful of the decision of Von Doussa J in Boardman (supra) where he stated:
“...Whether a late election is ‘desirable’ is to be judged objectively having regard to all the circumstances of the case...whether the recognition of a late election is ‘something worthy of achievement’ must depend on what is fair in order to do justice between the person who seeks to make the late election and the requirements of the administration of the Fund...”
  1. The Tribunal recognises the importance of statutory time limits. However it agrees with Von Doussa J in Boardman (supra) that there will be deserving cases where this limit may be relaxed in fairness to the Applicant. The Tribunal concurs with the Full Court in Chalk (supra) where their Honours stated:
“Most provisions which authorise an extension of time are instances of beneficial legislation which, accordingly, should be applied beneficially...”
  1. The Tribunal is mindful of the precedent which suggests that it may be of little consequence whether an employee did not, or decided not to, inquire about her right to elect under section 137 of the Act. Von Doussa J in the decision of Chalk (supra) observed that “...Section 157 confers a discretion which operates in either such case.”
  2. The Tribunal also acknowledges the difficulty in determining factual circumstances as they existed over six years ago. However the Tribunal accepts the evidence given by Ms Marsh and finds that she made enquiries on three separate occasions about her superannuation rights upon resignation. The Tribunal accepts the Applicant’s evidence that on all three occasions she was not made aware of the option to preserve her superannuation and had no reason to suspect that she may have needed to inquire further.
  3. The Tribunal finds that the Applicant has provided a reasonable explanation for the delay and accepts that Ms Marsh departed for the United States of America believing that her only option on resignation would be a refund of her superannuation contributions. The Tribunal accepts that it was not until her return to Australia and her resumed employment with the public service, that she learned of her rights in relation to superannuation preservation. The Tribunal notes that on discovering information about these rights, the Applicant wasted little time in making an application for a late election to preserve her funds. Therefore the Tribunal finds that the Applicant did not “sleep on her rights.”
  4. The Tribunal finds the Applicant to be a prudent and security conscious person. It accepts the evidence of the Applicant that she wanted to keep her options open. By force of circumstance, the Applicant had been geographically removed from the public service in Australia, but demonstrated a commitment to return to it, should the opportunity arise as it did in 1992.
  5. The Tribunal finds that at the time of her resignation from the public service, Ms Marsh had no need of her superannuation funds. The Tribunal accepts that at the time of her resignation the Applicant had other assets and had financially accounted for her various expenses without factoring in her superannuation refund as a means of financial assistance.
  6. The Tribunal accepts that the Applicant invested her superannuation refund in short term accounts, an acceptable practice at the time if the level of interest rates in the 1980’s is recalled. The Tribunal notes that the Applicant had another “nest egg” of invested money and a property returning rental which she could call upon if that had been required. The Tribunal finds that just as in the case of Boardman (supra), this was not a case where the Applicant immediately invested her superannuation in a business enterprise, suggesting that the pursuit of this enterprise was a reason for her resignation and that a deliberate decision to reject the preservation option had been made. In this case, the Applicant’s continual investment of her superannuation refund in a savings bank account and later investment in shares, coupled with Ms Marsh’s later entry into a private superannuation policy, indicated to the Tribunal that she would have elected the option to preserve had she been informed of it when she made her enquiries and resigned.
  7. The Tribunal has noted the evidence of the witnesses Ms Jakacic, Mr Rosie and Mr Bomford. It notes that the evidence of Ms Jakacic supports the view that no information about the election to preserve superannuation benefits was supplied. Turning to Mr Rosie’s evidence; the Tribunal notes that although Mr Rosie claimed to have a particular interest in superannuation, there is no evidence that this dated back to 1985 when the Applicant discussed leave with him. The Tribunal accepts from the Applicant’s evidence that he did not inform her of preservation of her superannuation benefit. As to Mr Bomford; the Tribunal notes that his knowledge of superannuation was not of particular assistance with regard to the matter before it as he had not worked in either the Keswick or Woomera personnel offices and did not know any of the Keswick or Woomera staff personally in October 1985.
  8. The Tribunal has considered the question of prejudice caused to the Respondent by the recognition of a late election to preserve her funds. The Tribunal concurs with the decision of Von Doussa in Boardman (supra) where he stated:
“...a favourable exercise of the discretion would extend...the benefit of additional years of service, but this liability would have arisen in any event had the election been exercised in time. ...the benefit of years of service actually performed is consistent with the policy and objects of the Act...The time limit has its purpose, but the Act recognises that there will be deserving cases where fairness to the applicant requires that the time limit to be relaxed...If the proper application of s.157(1) to individual cases results in many people obtaining recognition of late elections, so be it, that is the law.”
  1. Referring to the decision of Chalk (supra), the Tribunal considers it fair to the employer, the employee and the Scheme to calculate the benefit of the Scheme by reference to the totality of the employee’s service and contributions, including any amount that must be refunded. The Tribunal has looked to the totality of Ms Marsh’s service in the public service prior to her resignation and finds that she has forfeited a significant sum of money in employer contributions to the Scheme. The Tribunal finds that the Respondent has not made out a case of any particular prejudice to the Scheme if the late election were recognised in the case of this Applicant.
  2. The Tribunal notes the submissions made by the Respondent that the existence or not of fault on the part of the Respondent bears little weight in relation to the question of whether it is desirable to allow a late election to preserve superannuation entitlements. This principle was discussed by their Honours and Cooper J in particular in the decision of Chalk (supra).
  3. The Tribunal is satisfied that in all the circumstances of this matter there is sufficient information before it to satisfy the Tribunal that it is desirable to recognise the Applicant’s late election. The Tribunal finds that it is fair and just in all the circumstances of this matter, that the exercise of this discretion be made.
  4. Therefore the Tribunal decides that the decision under review be set aside. The matter is remitted to the Respondent with the direction that pursuant to sub-section 157(1) of the Act the election made by the Applicant on 21 July 1992 pursuant to sub-section 137(1) of the Act be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that sub-section of the Act.
