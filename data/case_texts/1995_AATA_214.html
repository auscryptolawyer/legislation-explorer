Karen Joy Jenkin and Commissioner of Superannuation [1995] AATA 214 (3 August 1995)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

KAREN JOY JENKIN v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V94/534

AAT No. 10337

Number of pages - 18

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

J. HANDLEY (Senior Member), G. WOODARD (Member) AND W. McLEAN (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Eligible employee committed suicide - wife left matrimonial home in previous month - prior history of instability within marriage - whether absence of wife at date of death was temporary - whether applicant deemed to be the "spouse".

Superannuation Act 1976 s.3

Pearce v Hornsey and Anor. (1991) 29 FCR 239 Re Hornsey and Commissioner for Superannuation and Pearce (1991) 22 ALD 85

Tulk v Tulk [1907] VicLawRp 14; (1907) VLR 64

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 17 March 1995

3:8:1995

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr Pinner

Solicitor for the Applicant: Slater and Gordon

Counsel for the Respondent: Ms Munoz

Solicitor for the Respondent: Australian Government Retirements

ORDER

The decision of the respondent of 26 May 1994 is affirmed.

DECISION

J. HANDLEY, G. WOODARD AND W. McLEAN This is an application for review of a decision of a delegate of the respondent made on 25 June 1991 and affirmed on reconsideration on 26 May 1994, that the applicant is not a person to whom the definition of "spouse" contained in section 3(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") applies.

2. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by Mr Pinner of counsel, and the respondent was represented by Ms Munoz. The evidence before the Tribunal consisted of the documents lodged with the Tribunal pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and the clinical notes of Dr Vinod Thacore. In addition, the applicant gave oral evidence and called a number of witnesses: her husband, Stephen Olesnicky; her sisters, Sheryl and Kerrie Smith; and a former work colleague, Michelle Davidson. The respondent called Colin Eade, a former colleague of Peter Jenkins ("the deceased"); William Emerson, the deceased's brother-in-law; and Dr Vinod Thacore.

Relevant Legislation

3. The definition of spouse in s.3(1) of the Act was repealed and substituted with what are now sections 8A and 8B of the same Act, by the Commonwealth Superannuation Schemes Amendment Act, 1992 ("the Amending Act"). The Amending Act however contains the following relevant saving provision:

"s.10(2) The Superannuation Act 1976, as in force immediately

before 25 June 1993, continues to apply in relation to any

benefits payable under that Act in respect of a deceased

person who was an eligible employee or retirement pensioner

(within the meaning of the Act), and who died before

25 June 1993".

4. In this application, the deceased eligible employee died on 11 June 1989. Therefore the applicable provisions are sections 3(1) and 3(2) of the Act which provide as follows-

"s3(1) In this Act, unless the contrary intention appears-

...

"Spouse", in relation to a person who has died and was, at the

time of his death, an eligible employee or a retirement

pensioner, means-

...

(a) a person who was legally married to the deceased person at

the time of the person's death and who, at that time was

living with the person on a permanent and bona fide domestic

basis;

(b) a person who was legally married to the deceased person at

the time of the person's death but who was not living with the

person on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis at that

time, and who in the opinion of the Commissioner, was wholly or

substantially dependent upon the deceased at that time;

...

(2) Where the Commissioner is of the opinion that a person

would, but for a temporary absence or an absence resulting from

illness or infirmity, have been living with another person at

any time on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis, the first

mentioned person shall, for the purposes of the definition of

"spouse" in subsection (1) of this section, and of section 9,

be deemed to have been living with the other person on a

permanent and bona fide domestic basis at that time".

5. The applicant was not living with the deceased at the time of his death. Section 3(1)(a) therefore does not apply.

6. It was conceded by counsel for the applicant at the commencement of the hearing, that the applicant was not wholly or substantially dependent upon the deceased at the time of the death (refer s.3(1)(b)).

7. The only issue to be considered in these proceedings therefore is whether s.3(2) applies. Greater consideration of this sub-section, in the context of the evidence heard in the proceedings, will be discussed later in these reasons.

The Facts

8. The deceased was born on 17 September 1959 and became a contributor to the Superannuation Fund established under the provisions of the Act on 24 October 1984, following his commencement of permanent employment with Telecom.

9. He died on 11 June 1989 with the cause of death being certified by the Coroner as "consistent with hanging/electrocution" (T11/32).

10. In September 1989, the applicant applied to the Respondent for spouse's benefit. In her application she declared that she was legally married to the deceased on 5 October 1985 and stated that she was living with her husband at the time of his death. She gave her address as the address of the matrimonial home.

11. In accordance with the definition of "spouse" set out in subsection 3(1) of the Act, the respondent initially accepted the applicant as an eligible spouse and pending a medical investigation under subsection 16(11) of the Act, she was paid an interim standard pension and a refund of the deceased's accumulated contributions.

12. In the course of the medical investigation, however, doubts were raised as to whether she had in fact been living with the deceased at the time of his death and her pension was subsequently suspended. The applicant then wrote to the respondent on 4 September requesting re-commencement of her pension payments.

13. On 25 June 1991 a delegate of the respondent was satisfied that the evidence submitted by the applicant did not show that she met the requirements of s.3(1) of the Act.

14. The applicant applied to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review of that decision. The AAT however had no jurisdiction to review the decision as, at that stage, there had been no reconsideration in accordance with subsection 154(2) of the Act.

15. The applicant then applied to the respondent under s.154 of the Act and on reconsideration it was found that the she was not a person to whom the definition of "spouse" provided in s.3(1) of the Act applies (T2/2).

16. On 22 June 1994, the applicant applied to the AAT for review of that decision.

Applicant's Evidence.

17. The applicant said that she first met the deceased in 1979 when she was 19 years old at Ballarat, Victoria. Initially the applicant described her relationship with the deceased as a "long distance" one because the deceased was working in Benalla and she was working in Kerang. She said they saw each other at weekends and had telephone calls during the week. In July 1982 they both moved to Melbourne when the deceased obtained employment with Telecom and the applicant obtained work with the City of Essendon. They then cohabited in a flat in Essendon and subsequently married on 5 October 1985 and bought a house in Surrey Hills.

18. Thereafter, the applicant was employed by various city councils as an accountant (she is currently unemployed) and the deceased continued working for Telecom.

19. In July and August of 1988, the applicant and the deceased travelled overseas together. When they returned, the applicant characterised their relationship as being "okay".

20. The applicant then began post graduate studies in market modelling at Swinburne University. She said however, that the hours she needed to spend at her studies, in addition to the time she was spending at work, had a negative impact on her relationship with the deceased. By the end of 1988 she said she "was definitely starting to get disillusioned" with (her) marriage.(p7). The overseas trip and her study had widened her horizons and tensions were building up at home. She said that some of the main tensions concerned the issue of having children and the fact that the deceased was so emotionally dependent on her (p.7).

21. In January 1989 she said that she needed a break from the deceased and left the marital home to stay with a friend in Greensborough. She stayed away for about a month but remained in contact with the deceased by telephone. She returned to the marital home in February 1989.

22. By March 1989 the applicant was studying for her last two units of the post graduate course and had also obtained a more senior position at the City of South Melbourne, which demanded even more of her time. On 14 May 1989, approximately one month prior to the deceased taking his life, the applicant again moved out of the marital home.

23. The applicant said that leading up to this time-

"Tension was building up in terms of the time I was spending

at work and with study and, you know, why aren't we doing more

things together, so in the end it just got too much for me...

In the week before I left Peter- I discussed with Peter that I

had to get away because I couldn't concentrate on doing the

assignments that I had to get in the next six to eight weeks"

(p8).

24. The applicant said that she explained to him that she "needed more time", that she "couldn't cope" and that she "had to get away". She said that the marriage wasn't working satisfactorily and she asked him to move out of the house to give her some time alone. The deceased however refused.

25. It was in that week that the applicant was reacquainted with Stephen Olesnicky (her present husband) at a council work function. The applicant apparently told Mr Olesnicky about her situation and he offered her the use of his second bedroom in his flat in Richmond.

26. The applicant said that she first met Mr Olesnicky when she was at the City of Essendon in 1985. She had applied for a job as an accountant at the City of Hawthorn and Mr Olesnicky interviewed and subsequently gave her that position.

27. She admitted that in December 1988 she and Mr Olesnicky had had a brief sexual relationship and that this was probably the cause of some of the underlying tensions in her marriage to the deceased "but certainly not all of it." On 14 May however, she said that her relationship with Mr Olesnicky was not the impetus that led her to leave the deceased. She said the relationship with Mr Olesnicky was "casual", as he was then still married to his first wife. She said that she "certainly didn't have any long term plans with (him)". She said that she found it easy to talk to Mr Olesnicky because of their long work association and that his offer of the second bedroom was convenient because she only needed 6-8 weeks to "sort things through" and therefore did not want to commit herself to a long term lease. She also said that his flat was close to where she worked and the accommodation was satisfactory.

28. She said that she was honest with the deceased "as that was the only way to make (her) relationship (with him) work" and that the deceased knew about her relationship with Mr Olesnicky. In the week leading up to her move, she said that she had discussed with the deceased how she wanted a break for six or eight weeks to enable her to finish her assignments and that the she would then be able to put everything in perspective. She said that she "needed that space, that breathing space, to get away and he reluctantly agreed to that..." She added that he probably didn't have a lot of choice in the matter but "it was fairly amicable."(p10) She said that he knew where she was going to live and that they had gone out to dinner the night before and had discussed how they would review the situation in six to eight weeks time.

29. When she left on 14 May, the applicant said that the deceased would ring her constantly at work, at least once and sometimes three or four times each day. He did not however have the telephone number of the flat in Richmond. She said that during these conversations, she would often close her office door and that she spent a lot of time talking to him. She said that he would always ask her the same line of questions, why had she left and when was she coming home?

30. The applicant said that she never indicated to the deceased that the marriage was at an end.

31. On 10 June, the deceased rang the applicant and "begged" her to meet him at the Surrey Hills house. She agreed but asked her sister, Sheryl Smith, and her brother-in law, Bill Emerson, to attend with her so it "wouldn't get too heavy and he wouldn't put too much pressure on (her) to go back."

32. When she arrived at the house, the applicant spoke privately to the deceased for about 20 minutes. He again apparently implored her to come back and said that they should have a child together. She said that he had been drinking but was reasonably coherent.

33. When her sister and brother in law came into the house they tried to talk about other, more general things, such as what they were going to do on the weekend and what was happening in world affairs. The Tiananmen Square massacre had just occurred and she said that his attitude was that it was their (the Chinese's') problem and that she shouldn't worry about it. She said that she found his attitude "difficult to cope with".

34. During the conversation, Mr Emerson tried to persuade him to go to the football, to get him out of the house, to be with other people and to stop him drinking. The applicant said she tried to reinforce this idea, but he replied "No, I'm not going because you might ring me." She said that this was the last occasion she ever spoke to him.

35. At the hearing, the applicant was again asked whether she had ever made comments that, so far as she was concerned, might indicate that the marriage was at an end. She said "I may have, I can't say a hundred per cent that I didn't say that". In cross examination she explained further that at the time of his death, she was not 100% certain that she would have gone back to the deceased-

"One minute I thought I could, next minute I couldn't, and the

constant pressure of the phone calls, I thought I might end up

having to go back because of the guilt and everything." p17

36. The applicant was also asked whether she had done any positive act to return to the matrimonial home from the time she left the deceased on 14 May 1989 until the time of his death. She answered-

"I certainly hadn't shifted back into the marital home but by

the same token I hadn't made any decision to finally end the

relationship, because if I had, I wouldn't have been taking

the phone calls. I would've taken more possessions. I

would've started sorting things out but I wasn't near that

decision".

37. The applicant said that she had only taken her clothes and her study books when she went to live in Richmond.

38. The applicant said that she believed that she had left the deceased with the belief that there was a possibility that she may have come back. At page 23 of the Transcript she said that-

"there was still a strong possibility I believed that I could

have gone back, that I would have gone back. But even if- I

probably even if I had have felt that way I couldn't have, I

would never have said it to him".

39. Notwithstanding that she is now married and has two children with Stephen Olesnicky, the applicant said that at 11 June 1989 she was still trying "to have the breathing space of about six or eight weeks to assess the situation". In hindsight, she said, "Obviously...I should never have left." P16

Evidence of Stephen Yaromir Olesnicky

40. Mr Olesnicky corroborated the applicant's evidence of their first meeting in the mid 1980s and their brief affair in 1988 after which they did not see each other again until May 1989 when they re-met at a local government work function. He said that on that occasion the applicant had indicated that she was not getting on well with her husband.

41. He said-

"She was thinking of how much passion was there in the marriage.

See, she always uses that word, and I think she was having a

lot of stress too at work..."p26

42. The end result, he said, was that he offered to give her a spare bedroom at his flat in Richmond. The applicant moved in in mid-May and "things developed from then."

43. He said that they had not discussed whether they would set up as a de facto couple because he had gone through a separation with his previous wife, and-

"the furthermost thing from my mind was having a permanent

relationship and I could sort of see it in Karen too, that she

was going through the same thing, and really it- I don't think

one can say that at that time it was our intention to enter

into any sort of permanent relationship at all." (p. 27)

44. He denied that his relationship with the applicant was responsible for his separation from his first wife.

Evidence of Sheryl Maree Smith

45. Evidence was given by the applicant's sister, Sheryl Maree Smith, who boarded with the applicant and the deceased for approximately two years in the late 1980s. Ms Smith said that until about September 1988 she had considered her sister's marriage to be "for the most part, happy and contented" but in the period leading up to her sister's departure in 1989, the marriage had had a "few problems"

46. She said that the applicant had said that the problems could only be sorted out if she had a trial separation and that this was why she moved out of the marital home in the first instance in January 1989. Ms Smith said that the deceased made a lot of contact with her sister during this period, and that she "did not view it really as a separation."

47. When the applicant moved back to the Surrey Hills home, the witness recalled her sister working long hours at her job with the South Melbourne council, in addition to her studies at Swinburne Institute. On top of this she was also trying to deal with the problems in the marriage. Ms Smith said that during this period, the deceased was "demanding" and was not supportive of the applicant working and studying long hours.

48. Ms Smith left the Surrey Hills house in April 1989 but remained in contact with the deceased who rang her nearly every day, sometimes more than once. She also saw him personally on a couple of occasions during that period. She said that he would always ask questions such as- "Where is Karen? Why isn't she coming back?" She said that she attempted to explain to him that her sister needed time to finalise her studies and to concentrate on her work. He did not, however, seem to comprehend this and would repeat his line of questioning.

49. On 10 June 1989, Ms Smith went to the Surrey Hills home to visit the deceased, along with Bill Emerson and the applicant. She said that-

"Peter would not let Bill in to the house and when I arrived

Peter opened the door so that Bill and I could have access to

the house and he let us in the house and the four of us sat

down in the lounge room, just talked about day to day

conversation topics. At one stage, Bill and I left Karen

alone with Peter and then we returned. Peter was not in a

stable condition, obviously been drinking quite heavily."

50. The last time Ms Smith spoke to the deceased was on the evening of 10 June 1989. She said that he rang her and had again asked the same sorts of questions.

51. Ms Smith was asked whether she was aware of her sister's relationship with Stephen Olesnicky at any time in 1989, before the deceased's death. She answered that she knew that her sister was staying at Stephen's place, but she was not aware of any relationship that was "more than just friends."

Evidence of Kerrie Ann Smith

52. The Tribunal heard oral evidence from another of the applicant's sisters, Kerrie Ann Smith.

53. Ms Smith who lives in Ballarat, said that during their marriage, she mainly had contact with the deceased and applicant at family gatherings such as Easter, Christmas and birthdays. She also saw them when she came to Melbourne for weekend shopping trips.

54. She recalled that in 1989, the applicant and deceased came to Ballarat for an Easter camping function and that during that time their relationship was-

"a bit tense but certainly not anything overtly obviously

wrong. I thought that they were working at resolving any

difference they might have had. I knew that Peter didn't have

a lot of outside interests and he was and I knew that Karen

had to be the instigator of any social events or what they

would do for a weekend or whatever." p36

55. She said that her sister was studying for her post graduate degree and wanted to stick to her study timetable and that she had started a new job at South Melbourne and wanted to make a good impression. She said that she had never discussed with the deceased or applicant where their marriage was heading as the deceased "did not like things like that being discussed: "(He) was not one to openly display what he was thinking or feeling."

56. She said that when the applicant left the marital home in May, she was only aware that her sister and Stephen Olesnicky had "a work relationship". Although the applicant told her that she was moving in to Stephen's flat in Richmond, Ms Smith did not question her about the extent of the relationship because she believed her sister had separated from the deceased "so she could cope with the exams that were coming up in June."

Evidence of Michelle Ann Davidson

57. Evidence was heard from Michelle Ann Davidson, who worked with the applicant at the South Melbourne Council. The applicant commenced employment with the Council in March 1989 as the city accountant and EDP manger and Ms Davidson was the accountant reporting directly to her.

58. Ms Davidson said she had a fair bit to do with the applicant as they were both new in the job. She said that the applicant was studying and was-

"more stressed than you would normally be in a new job and I

asked her, you know, was there anything I could do or was

anything the matter and she confided in me that in fact she

had recently left her husband."

59. In relation to the applicant's work behaviour during this period, Ms Davidson said-

"She was very stressed. ... I wouldn't actually say confused

but there was a lot of turmoil during this time and she would

frequently be in her office with the door shut "sort of

opting out of the office environment. She was receiving a

lot of phone calls. I tried to shield her as much as I could.

I was in fact the only person in the office that she had told

that she had recently separated from her husband so I sort of

was there as a bit of support as well within the office for

her."

60. Ms Davidson said that the applicant had never spoken about her marriage "being over", but had mentioned that there were some problems and that she needed breathing space. She said there was also an issue in relation to children and that the applicant and her husband had disagreed on whether or not they should have them. Ms Davidson said "It was all sort of- all a bit much at the time and that that's why she (the applicant) had moved out." P40

Evidence of Mr Colin Eade

61. Mr Eade has been employed by Telecom Australia for 12 years and had known the deceased for nearly 4 years. He said that, as work colleagues, their relationship was "relatively close". He described the deceased as a very quiet, reserved man, who usually kept to himself but was very forthcoming if anyone asked or wanted help for anything.

62. The last time Mr Eade saw the deceased was the Friday night before he committed suicide. They were both at a social function after work and had had a couple of beers together. He said that the deceased at that stage "seemed very happy, very relaxed with himself, and seemed to be progressing onwards; to be over his recent problems."

63. Mr Eade said that the deceased's job at Telecom had required a lot of technical intellect and that he was very proficient in the area of his expertise which was to provide and maintain data transmission parts for corporate customers for high flows of data transfer.

64. In the months before he committed suicide, Mr Eade said that the deceased went away on annual recreation leave and that when he came back he was very vague. He said that he learnt why the deceased was acting vaguely at some time between the deceased returning from recreation leave in February/March 1989 and his eventual suicide. He was unable to recall the exact date but remembered the deceased telling him that he was separated from his wife. He said that when the deceased informed him of this-

"It was a bit of (a) surprise to me. Actually it came out of

the blue that he said he was separated....He kept his private

life virtually to himself and to suddenly reveal something of

that nature was a little bit outstanding, I suppose."

65. Mr Eade said that they did not discuss the separation in any great depth and the deceased did not qualify whether it was a permanent or temporary separation.

66. On the Friday night when he last spoke to the deceased, Mr Eade said there was no further discussion about the separation or the state of the marriage. He said that the deceased "appeared to be back to his normal self and was happy with himself."

Evidence of William John Emerson

67. Mr Emerson is the deceased's brother-in-law. He is married to the deceased's sister Christine and knew the deceased for approximately 15 or 16 years. He has known the applicant for approximately 14 years.

68. At the hearing Ms Munoz asked Mr Emerson to read over his deposition for the Coroner's inquest (refer T21/55). In part, that deposition stated as follows-

"The marriage between Peter and Karen seemed very stable. In

fact so stable that my wife and I thought of asking them to be

godparents for my daughter.

Well, on Mother's Day in May, Peter said that they... were

having problems and had already had a trial separation in

January....This came as a complete shock to everyone as we all

thought that they were an ideal couple.

I believe the reason for the marriage- for the breakdown in the

marriage was that Karen went off with another bloke, but we

have not seen Karen for a couple of months so we did not know

if that was the situation or not."

69. At the hearing, Mr Emerson affirmed this statement.

70. He said that in the period leading up to the suicide, the deceased's family were concerned about him. Mr Emerson's wife in particular kept in close touch with the deceased and Mr Emerson spoke with him over the phone on a regular basis.

71. During that period, Mr Emerson described the deceased as being very vague. He said that he "tended to have, what I consider, flashes of reality, and then he would sort of drift away".

72. Before the deceased committed suicide, he confided to Mr Emerson that he was separated from the applicant and that he "didn't know what he was going to do."

73. He said-

"I can remember him saying things like "I don't know what I'm

going to do" and that sort of thing so I took that to mean

with the rest of his- his life"

74. In relation to the question of why the deceased and applicant had separated, Mr Emerson said that he was aware that the applicant "had left to be with somebody else" but he could not recall how he became of aware of this.

75. On 10 June, he went to the Surrey Hills house, at the applicant's request. He said she had rung him on the Friday night and told him that she needed his support. When he arrived at the house, he said that it was apparent that the deceased had consumed a lot of alcohol and was uncharacteristically aggressive. Mr Emerson said that the deceased was not pleased to see him and he got the "impression ... that (the deceased) did not want anybody there other than Karen."

76. In his deposition to the Coroner's proceedings, Mr Emerson said that-

"I would have got there about ten (10) am on Saturday, I

knocked on the door and Peter only opened it slightly just

enough to get his head around.

We chatted for a while and he said that he did not want to go

to the football with me so I went and sat in my car for about

ten (10) minutes.

Karen's sister then arrived and then Karen came out of the

house and we all went back inside. We all chatted together

and Karen and her sister left and I stayed and spoke to Peter

for about twenty (20) minutes."

77. At the hearing, Mr Emerson said that in that period of twenty minutes the deceased did not explicitly state that the applicant was not coming back. However, from his observations of the deceased during that time Mr Emerson said that-

"My opinion of what Peter thought was that he didn't have too

much to live for as far as his relationship with Karen.

Peter never actually said- he ranted and raved a bit, but

nothing -he never said to me that she wasn't coming back or

anything like that, but I got the impression that that's what

he felt and I tried to be positive to him as far as saying,

well, you know, keep your chin up, that sort of thing".

Evidence of Vinod Thacore

78. Dr Thacore is a psychiatrist who has been practising psychiatry since 1960. He has practised in India, England and for the last 20 years in Australia.

79. At the hearing he was shown a copy of a statement he prepared on 22 May 1990 (T - 30/77) in relation to his examination of the deceased and the report he prepared to the Commissioner of Superannuation dated 5 August 1993. That report, states as follows:

"...Mr Jenkins was first seen by me on the 19th May 1989, along

with his brother in law. I formed the opinion that he was

suffering from a dissociative state and had been distressed for

a few days before, due to problems in his marriage, and had

attempted to take his own life by gassing himself a couple of

days before....

On the 23rd May he informed that in the previous week he and

his wife had discussed to separate amicably. He said that he

was still confused about his wife and that he did not know

where she was and could not remember that she had been in

touch with him on the phone.

Mr Jenkin was admitted to hospital the following day where

under abreaction he recalled that he was upset at his wife

leaving him with another man. She had been in touch with him

and had expressed uncertainty about her leaving him. When

seen on the 1st June, he told me that his marriage was

finished, as his wife had told him that she did not love him

anymore. He was unable to pinpoint as to where things had

gone wrong with his marriage. .....

"Mr Jenkin had difficulty in dealing with the break-up of his

marriage and I reckon it was the finality of the separation

which induced him to attempt suicide in the first place, soon

after she left him on this occasion, and life without his

wife became intolerable for him when he decided to end his

stress by taking his own life..."

80. At the hearing he was asked to describe how he saw the deceased on 19 May as against 1 June. He answered that the deceased seemed to be a bit more relaxed and that he talked a bit more on the latter occasion. The deceased also apparently told him that he had had an earlier telephone call from his wife, and that she was indecisive. Dr Thacore however said that on that day-

"He said that he felt that the marriage was finished, and that

she had told him so because she didn't love him. And he was

confused as to where things had gone wrong".

81. He said-

"I think that he believed that the marriage was over. He

wanted to live his life with his wife; didn't know where

things had gone wrong but he did not express any suicidal

feelings. That is where deception can come in; that he may

have made up his mind that he wants to do and that itself

removed the conflict he was in and gave him that feeling of

calm that he had made up his mind- this is it he's going to

do, and then he would look at an opportune time to do it".

82. Dr Thacore said that the deceased was a very quiet person with a fragile ego. He said that it was possible that he may have misinterpreted his wife but "if you go on what he has said I would believe that he felt the marriage was finished."

Closing Submissions

83. The hearing concluded late in the day of 17 March and the representatives for both parties were invited to make submissions by way of closing summary.

84. Counsel for the applicant indicated that the evidence in the application could be distinguished from the decision in Re Hornsey and Commissioner for Superannuation and Pearce (1991) 22 ALD 85. However, no submission was given, either then or subsequently, as to why Hornsey might be distinguished.

85. Counsel for the respondent requested the opportunity to prepare and subsequently file and serve written submissions, having regard both to the effluxion of time on the day allocated for the hearing and to counsel's intended absence from Australia commencing on the day after the hearing. Subsequently, comprehensive and competent submissions were filed on behalf of the respondent.

Findings and Conclusion

86. As discussed earlier, the applicant and the deceased were not living together at the date of the deceased's death. Section 3(1)(a) of the Act therefore does not apply.

87. It was conceded that the applicant was not wholly or substantially dependent upon the deceased at the time of his death and we are satisfied that this concession was properly made. Section 3(1)(b) consequently does not apply.

88. Whether the applicant is a "spouse" must therefore be determined under the deeming provision in s.3(2). Under this provision the Tribunal is required to form an opinion as to whether the absence was temporary and that but for the absence the spouses would have been living together on a permanent and bona fide basis. The sub-section is curious when compared to the language used under sub-sections 3(1)(a) and (b) because it does not use the words "legally married" when referring to the relevant parties and does not restrict eligibility to the date of death but rather entitles an overview of the relationship "at any time". The words "at any time" despite the inelegant drafting of the sub-section, appear to refer to the point in time at which the Commissioner deems that a person is a spouse, and should not be confused with the broader period of time contemplated by the words "at any time".

89. Sub-section 3(2) offers considerable latitude, rightly in our opinion, to assess eligibility in circumstances that may arise in relationships where there may be absences for a multitude of reasons not restricted to illness or infirmity and being circumstances which if not permitted to be considered could cause considerable hardship and would plainly be unfair.

90. There is no requirement to determine whether the marital separation was contributed to or caused by the temporary absence having regard to the presence of the words "but for" as appear in s.3(2) (refer Pearce v Hornsey and Anor (1991) 29 FCR 239 at pp 244, 245). We are accordingly obliged to consider all the circumstances of the application excluding the absence by the applicant from the deceased in the circumstances previously recorded and then to form an opinion of whether the applicant and the deceased would have been living with each other "at any time" on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis. The words "but for" compel this approach.

91. In the law of negligence this approach when the issue of causation is considered is sometimes referred to as a negative approach "yet has an important role to play in the resolution of the question" (refer March v E and M H Stramere Pty Ltd and Anor [1991] HCA 12; (1991) 99 ALR 423 at 430).

92. That said, the "resolution of the question" of whether the applicant and the deceased would be living in a permanent and bona fide domestic basis excludes consideration of the actual absence at the date of death, but does entitle consideration of the alleged reason for the absence, as given by the applicant, that is "breathing space".

93. The Tribunal in Re Hornsey and Commissioner for Superannuation and Pearce (1990) 22 ALD 85 considered the absence resulting from illness and the prospects of resuming co-habitation at the time of death and at p 95 said that-

"It is not sufficient to find that an absence from such a

relationship began six months prior to death or that it began

as a result of illness. The crucial question is the

formation of an opinion as to the prospects of such a

relationship existing at the time of death but for an absence

from illness."

94. And at p.96:

"What must be remembered is that we are not endeavouring to

ascertain how many causes there were for the existence of an

absence from cohabitation at the time of death, but whether in

our opinion there was at that time a likelihood of resumption

of cohabitation if absence resulting from illness were removed.

If it is conceded that when the separation began there was an

immediate "absence resulting from illness", it still remains to

determine whether the addition of other factors contributing

materially to the continued absence precludes the possibility

of a finding that but for the absence resulting from illness

the necessary state of cohabitation would have been resumed.

An answer to that question must depend on the nature of and the

weight to be attributed to the additional factors."

95. The Tribunal's decision was affirmed on appeal to the Federal Court in Pearce and Hornsey and Anor (1991) 29 FCR 239.

96. For the purposes of these reasons, we are satisfied and accordingly find as a fact, that the absence was not because of "illness or infirmity".

97. Following the approach in Hornsey, it is not enough to examine whether the absence was undertaken with the intention that it should be of a short or of a definite term, it is necessary to establish whether factors existed immediately prior to the time of death that indicate whether, irrespective of the original reason for separation, resumption of cohabitation was likely. The task necessarily involves taking account of all the circumstances that are relevant to that question, including the history of the marriage.

98. In the present matter, the applicant claimed that the separation was not final and that this was evidenced by the fact that she was still taking all the deceased's phone calls and that most of her personal possessions were still at the matrimonial home at the time of her husband's death. We do not find however, that this on its own leads to the conclusion that the applicant intended to resume cohabitation with the deceased. It may have been that it was convenient for her to leave her belongings at the home.

99. In Tulk v Tulk (1907) VLR 63 Cussen J at p. 65 and 66 provides a guide to the interpretation of the term "temporary absence" namely-

"In deciding whether there was at any specified date an

existing matrimonial relationship, it is, I think, right to

say that such a relationship does not end so long as both

the spouses bona fide recognize it as subsisting, and in

particular it does not end by reason of a separation brought

about by the pressure of external circumstances such as

absence on professional or business pursuits, or in search of

health, or, it may be, even of pleasure. Marital intercourse,

the dwelling under the same roof, society and protection,

support, recognition in public and in private, correspondence

during separation, making up as a whole the consortium vitae

which the old writers distinguish from the divortium a mensa

et thoro, may be regarded separately as different elements,

the presence or absence of which go to show more or less

conclusively that the matrimonial relationship does or does

not exist. The weight of each of these elements varies with

the health, position in life, and all the other circumstances

of the parties".

100. The "breathing space" sought by the applicant was, in our view, not only to pursue studies but to escape the deceased's emotional needs. This latter reason was the impetus for the previous separation. We are not satisfied that the applicant would have returned to the deceased on the completion of her studies.

101. Whilst reluctant to do so, having regard to the tragedy of the circumstances giving rise to this application, we do treat the applicant's evidence with some caution. The falsity of her representations to the respondent as evidenced by the answers given on the form at T-13 p.36 do not convince us that she was at all times honest in answers to questions asked of her. Her prior separation from the deceased, her previous sexual relationship with Mr Olesnicky and her subsequent resumption of her relationship with him, in addition to the erratic behaviour of the deceased, are all factors which satisfy us that the relationship would not have resumed.

102. Whilst the absence prior to the deceased's death was relatively short, it does not persuade us that there would have been a resumption of co-habitation. The duration of the absence was determined only by the suicide of the deceased and our opinion would be no different even if the suicide occurred on the day after the commencement of the absence - applicant's absence was significant because it was the second separation within the 4 years of the marriage and both separations occurred in the period 6 months immediately prior to the deceased's death. Within that period of 6 months the applicant had also had a sexual relationship with Mr Olesnicky.

103. In her evidence the applicant said she had initiated the discussion concerning the separation with her husband and had in fact asked him to leave - which he refused to do. Whilst she denied she subsequently made a decision not to resume co-habitation she said she remained uncertain.

104. These events and explanations in addition to the admitted tensions between the applicant and the deceased, the applicant's "disillusionment" from late 1988, her widened horizons by the overseas trip and her career and academic progress paint a picture, in our opinion, of the relationship not likely to resume.

105. Remarkably, and perhaps consistent with our conclusions, is the absence of any professed wish on the part of the applicant or the deceased to seek or at least discuss the possibility of marital therapy or counselling. If this had been considered either before or after the separation we might have attached favourable weight to it.

106. The applicant left the matrimonial home because the deceased would not, and thereafter she lived in a flat rented by Mr Olesnicky, as she would have us believe, as his boarder. We are not satisfied that the relationship between the applicant and Mr Olesnicky was simply one of landlord and boarder.

107. Further we give weight to the evidence of William Emerson who said that the deceased had announced at a mother's day function in May 1989 that the applicant and he had separated earlier that year. The existence of the relationship between the applicant and Mr Olesnicky earlier that year was also spoken of by the deceased to persons present at that function.

108. Similarly we note the evidence of Kerry Smith, one of the applicant's sisters, who observed tension between the applicant and the deceased during Easter of 1989, approximately 1 month before the separation.

109. We are also persuaded by the evidence of Colin Eade and Dr Thacore. Both of these witnesses are independent of the applicant and the deceased. Although Mr Eade could not recall the exact date, he remembered the deceased informing him that he had separated from his wife at some time between the deceased returning from recreation leave in February/March 1989 and his eventual suicide.

110. Dr Thacore gave his evidence frankly and in our opinion honestly. His reference to his notes of conversations with the deceased provided a useful and historical account of the belief of the deceased that the marriage was finished and his belief that his wife no longer loved him.

111. Whilst it may be inferred that the deceased took his own life because of the ending of the marriage (such was the fragility of his ego and inability to emotionally maintain himself) it was an act, in our opinion, consistent with the deceased being of the belief that his wife's absence was not temporary.

112. The words "temporary absence" as appearing in s.3(2) should not in our opinion be construed as amounting to the temporary absence of one person only without an examination of the belief as to whether the absence was temporary in the mind of the other party to the relationship.

113. We are not prepared to deem that the applicant's absence was temporary. Additionally, we are left with no doubt that the deceased was of the opinion that the absence was not temporary and this belief was probably the catalyst for his suicide. As was decided in Re Hornsey at p 97-

"the Tribunal could not conceivably find a likelihood of a

resumption of the marriage if it were satisfied that one party

to the marriage had in reality so firmly rejected any

reasonable possibility of its continuance".

114. The deceased had, we are satisfied, firmly rejected any reasonable possibility of the marriage continuing. The applicant's absence could not therefore be temporary because the deceased was of the opinion, as may be gleaned from the evidence of Dr Thacore, that the marriage had ended.

Decision

115. The deeming provisions of s.3(2) come into force if the Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant would, but for a temporary absence, have been living with the deceased on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis.

116. Having regard to the above reasons we are satisfied that the applicant is not a person who can obtain the benefit conferred by s.3(2) of the Act.

117. The decision under review must therefore be affirmed.
