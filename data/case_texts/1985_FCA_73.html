Re Peter Clyne v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1985] FCA 73; 1985 Bankruptcy 5 FCR 1 (12 March 1985)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: PETER CLYNE

And: DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. G14 of 1985

Bankruptcy

5 FCR 1

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Fisher(1), Morling(1) and Wilcox(1) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Bankruptcy - Petition for sequestration order - Opposition to order on basis of "other sufficient cause" not to make order - Material relating to assets produced to creditor with offer to submit to enquiry under Income Tax Assessment Act - Whether this material capable of constituting other sufficient cause - Discretion of Court.

Bankruptcy Act 1966 s.52(2)(b)

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s.264

Bankruptcy - Petition - Whether "other sufficient cause" to decline to make sequestration order - Bankruptcy Act 1966 (Cth), s 52(2)(b). Evidence adduced by the judgment debtor relating to his financial dealings and current assets, the fact of disclosure of those dealings and assets to the petitioning creditor, a proposal by the debtor to submit himself to examination by the petitioning creditor under s 264 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), and to the debtor's state of health was not evidence of matters capable in the context of these particular proceedings of constituting "other sufficient cause" within the meaning of s 52(2) of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 (Cth) so as to ground a refusal to make a sequestration order.

HEARING

Sydney, 1984, March 12. 12:3:1985

PETITION AND APPEAL

Petition and Appeal from judgment and orders of St John J.

D. G. Hill QC and T. F. Bathurst, for the petitioning creditor/appellant.

The judgment debtor/respondent, in person.

Cur adv vult

Solicitor for the petitioning creditor: Australian Government Solicitor

GFV

ORDER

1. The appeal be dismissed.

2. The appellant pay to the respondent his costs of the appeal Orders accordingly

Appeal dismissed with costs

DECISION

The previous history of the bankruptcy proceedings involving these parties is summarized in the decision of the High Court of Australia in Clyne v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1984] HCA 44; (1984) 58 ALJR 398 and in the reasons for judgement of the members of a Full Court of this Court in relation to a case stated by St. John J upon the further hearing of the Deputy Commissioner's petition: see Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Clyne, 16 November 1984, not reported. Following upon the delivery of those judgements, the hearing of the creditor's petition proceeded before St. John J. Mr Clyne raised a number of matters in opposition to the making of a sequestration order but these matters were all decided adversely to him and, on 22 January 1985, a sequestration order was made.

2. Mr Clyne now appeals against the making of that sequestration order. He argues that his Honour erred in rejecting certain evidence tendered by him in support of an argument that there was "other sufficient cause", within the meaning of s.52(2) of the Bankruptcy Act 1966, for the Court not to make a sequestration order. That sub-section reads as follows:

"(2) If the Court is not satisfied with the

proof of any of those matters, or is

satisfied by the debtor -

(a) that he is able to pay his debts; or

(b) that for other sufficient cause a

sequestation order ought not to be made,

it may dismiss the petition."

3. The material rejected by St. John J, and in respect of which complaint is now made, consisted of paragraphs 21-26, 36-38, 44 and 49-51 of the affidavit of the debtor sworn 9 January 1985 together with various documents which were marked for identification 1 to 5. It is not necessary to set out this material in detail. It is sufficient to say that it related to four matters: the financial dealings and current assets of the debtor, the fact of disclosure of those dealings and assets to the petitioning creditor, a proposal made by the debtor to submit himself to examination by the petitioning creditor under s.264 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 and the debtor's state of health. The debtor submitted to the learned judge that the material demonstrated on its face that he possessed no assets - except for three identified assets worth $42,000 - additional to those which had been received by Mr W E Andrew, the trustee appointed pursuant to the sequestration order made on his own petition. The assets held by Mr Andrew are now held, pursuant to an order made by Sweeney J at the time of annulment of that sequestration order, by Mr J W O'Brien, a registered trustee who had consented to act as trustee of the estate in the event of a sequestration order being made upon the petition of the Deputy Commissioner. The appellant submitted that the material indicated that he had already given to the petitioning creditor full information relating to his affairs, during any possible period of relation back, so that the Deputy Commissioner might satisfy himself that the making of a sequestration order would not be likely to result in additional assets being recovered on behalf of the estate. However, Mr Clyne conceded before his Honour that the Deputy Commissioner was unlikely to accept without investigation the accuracy or completeness of the material he had supplied. He, therefore, sought to prove that, in November 1984, he had proposed that the Deputy Commissioner require him to submit to an examination under s.264 of the Income Tax Assessment Act. That section is as follows:

"264(1) The Commissioner may by notice in

writing require any person, whether a

taxpayer or not, including any officer

employed in or in connexion with any

department of a Government or by any public

authority -

(a) to furnish him with such information as

he may require; and

(b) to attend and give evidence before him or

before any officer authorized by him in

that behalf concerning his or any other

person's income or assessment, and may

require him to produce all books,

documents and other papers whatever in

his custody or under his control relating

thereto.

(2) The Commissioner may require the

information or evidence to be given on oath

and either verbally or in writing, and for

that purpose he or the officers so authorized

by him may administer an oath.

(3) The regulations may prescribe scales

of expenses to be allowed to persons required

under this section to attend."

4. The material in relation to the debtor's health consisted of various medical reports indicating that Mr Clyne was under treatment for hypertension and mild angina.

5. The contest before St. John J, in relation to the matters raised before us, revolved around the question of admissibility. His Honour was, in effect, invited to determine that issue by reference to the question whether the material was capable of constituting "other sufficient cause", within the meaning of s.52(2). He held that it was not so capable and, therefore, that the material was irrelevant to the proceedings before him.

6. Mr Clyne has submitted that his Honour should have admitted the evidence, as being relevant to the matter of "other sufficient cause" which he sought to argue, and, at the end of the case, should have determined whether - considered as a whole - it met that test. Certainly, this course might have been taken but the course that was taken by his Honour was a reasonable and natural response to the issue in the circumstances in which it arose. His Honour did consider the whole of the evidence tendered to him before rejecting any of it as being incapable of constituting 'other sufficient cause'. In any event, nothing now turns on that procedural point. The material rejected by St. John J has been read by us. If, properly considered, the material could have constituted "other sufficient cause" it would have followed that it was wrongly rejected by his Honour and that the discretion vested in the Court under s.52(2) had miscarried. Under those circumstances it would be incumbent upon us to exercise the discretion afresh and to make such order in relation to the petition as is appropriate: see House v The King [1936] HCA 40; (1936) 55 CLR 499 at pp 504-505 and Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 s.28(1)(b). If, on the other hand, the material does not constitute "other sufficient cause", it would follow that his Honour rightly rejected this ground of opposition and, this being the only ground argued before us, that the appeal should be dismissed.

7. The circumstances which may constitute "other suffficient cause" for dismissing a bankruptcy petition are extremely variable. It is not appropriate to attempt to catalogue or circumscribe them: see Cain v Whyte [1932] HCA 6; (1933) 48 CLR 639 at p 645. But we are not persuaded that the material relied upon by Mr Clyne is capable of constituting sufficient cause. It is true that the material relied upon by Mr Clyne, if accepted, established that he had no assets other than those now vested in Mr O'Brien and the identified three items. It is also true that it indicated that a deal of information had been supplied by Mr Clyne to the Deputy Commissioner. However, as Mr Clyne recognised, the Deputy Commissioner was not bound to accept the accuracy of his assertions as to his assets or the adequacy of his information. On its face the information leaves many matters unanswered. It was for this reason that Mr Clyne volunterred to be examined under s.264 but that section is a poor substitute for an examination of Mr Clyne under s.69 of the Bankruptcy Act and, perhaps, for the examination of others under s.80. The scope of the enquiry is more limited; s.264 is concerned only with a persons "income or assessment" whereas s.69 extends to "conduct, trade dealings, property and affairs". Section 69(2) provides that, unless the presiding officer otherwise directs, a bankrupt shall not be excused from answering questions by reason of the fact that the answer may incriminate him; it is not clear that a person would be denied this privilege in an enquiry under s.264. The sanctions for failing to provide material or to answer questions at a s.69 examination are more stringent than at a s.264 enquiry. Although Mr Clyne offered an undertaking that he would take no point at a s.264 enquiry on the ambit of questions or in relation to self incrimination and that he would co-operate in respect of documents and answers, this is not a satisfactory substitute for the wider provisions of the Bankruptcy Act. If the undertaking were broken the only redress for the Deputy Commissioner would be to renew his application for a sequestration order - supposing the petition to have been adjourned rather than dismissed - when arguments would arise, no doubt, as to the reasonableness of the debtor's conduct. Furthermore, Mr Clyne cannot bind others who may, properly, be required to give evidence or to produce documents.

8. There are more fundamental objections to a substitution of proceedings under s.264 for a public examination under s.69. An examination under s.264 is conducted in private, an examination under s.69 in public. Public awareness of the examination may result in information becoming available to the trustee or to creditors in relation to matters the subject of the examination. This is, no doubt, one reason for the requirement of an examination in public. Moreover, Mr Clyne's major creditor, according to the evidence, is a Commonwealth officer acting on behalf of the public. The contest in which these parties have been engaged, over a number of years, has attracted a certain amount of public attention. It would be incorrect, in our view, without strong reasons to accede to a course which would conceal from the public knowledge of what might turn out to be an important aspect of their engagement. Finally, there are other known creditors apart from the Deputy Commissioner; still others may yet emerge. Although the amount owing to the known other creditors is small, they have the right to participate in an examination of a bankrupt under s.69 as does his trustee. They would have no such right under s.264. The course suggested by Mr Clyne is not a satisfactory alternative.

9. Although Mr Clyne relied upon the medical reports he very fairly accepted that his state of health could properly be given little weight in the choice between a s.69 examination and an enquiry under s.264. It does not affect our view as to the course that ought to be taken.

10. The appellant referred us to authority for the proposition that a bankruptcy court should exercise its discretion against the making of a sequestration order where the debtor has no assets which may be recovered on behalf of the creditors. However, this course has been regarded as appropriate only in cases where the lack of assets is clear beyond question so that the presentation of the petition amounts to oppression: see In re Betts, ex parte Betts (1897) 1 QB 50, Re Somers ex parte Union Creditor Bank Limited (189) 4 Mans. 227. It is not enough that the Court has no information to indicate that there are assets: see Black v Blake [1909] HCA 61; (1909) 9 CLR 360 at p 364 and In re Leonard; ex parte Leonard (1896) 1 QB 374, in both of which cases it was pointed out that when the petition is presented it will not - normally, their Honours meant - be possible to say whether there will prove to be assets or not. Even an appearance of absence of assets after investigation has been held to be an insufficient reason for refusing to make a sequestration order : see Re Field (1978) 2 All ER 981.

11. The present case is not one of absence of assets. The appellant has, and has had in the past, considerable assets. Mr O'Brien already holds some $175,000. The debtor has identified a further $42,000 which might be expected to be recovered in due course. In the absence of a sequestration order, or perhaps a composition under Part X of the Bankruptcy Act which has not been suggested, those assets would have to be returned to Mr Clyne; the order that Mr O'Brien hold the assets depends upon the pendency of this petition. Furthermore, there is, in this case, a real prospect that the Court would accede to an application by the trustee for an order under s.131 of the Bankruptcy Act requiring the debtor, after bankruptcy, to make contributions out of his income to his estate. In his affidavit in these proceedings Mr Clyne expressly conceded that a requirement of a contribution of $420 per month would be reasonable. There is material to suggest - we express no view about the matter - that an order to pay a much higher sum may be appropriate.

12. Finally, there are consequences of a sequestration order which may - again we express no view - turn out to be significant; such as proceedings to set aside settlements or preferences, or for the prosecution of the debtor for offences against the Bankruptcy Act. The possibility of such proceedings should not be denied by the adoption of a procedure intended to serve a purpose altogether different from that which was intended by Parliament to be followed under the present circumstances.

13. For the above reasons we are of the opinion that his Honour was correct in holding that the material sought to be relied upon by the appellant was not capable of constituting "other sufficient cause". If, contrary to that view, the material was wrongly rejected it would be necessary for us, upon consideration of the material, to determine for ourselves the manner in which the discretion of the Court ought to be exercised. For the reasons we have expressed, that discretion should be exercised in favour of the making of a sequestration order.

14. Mr Clyne took two additional points in his Notice of Appeal which he recognised to be concluded against him, in this Court, by the decisions in the Full Court of 16 November 1984: that the petition had lapsed and that the annulment order of Sweeney J was wrongly made. No argument has been addressed to us on either of those matters.

15. In our opinion St. John J was correct in proceeding to make the sequestration order of 22 January 1985. The appeal should be dismissed with costs.
