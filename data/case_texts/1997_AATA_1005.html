Harts Pty Limited and Tax Agents' Board of Queensland [1997] AATA 1005 (10 October 1997)

Last Updated: 23 January 2009



TAX AGENTS - company registered as tax agent - cancellation of registration on basis company did not have a qualified director - whether qualified director beneficially owns shares carrying not less than 25% of the voting power in the company - decision affirmed.

Decision The Tribunal affirms the decision of the Tax Agents’ Board of Queensland dated 3 September, 1997.

Deputy President



REASONS FOR DECISION



On 5 September, 1997, the applicant, Harts Pty Ltd (“Harts”), applied for review of a decision of the respondent, the Tax Agents’ Board of Queensland (“the Board”). The Board’s decision made on 3 September, 1997 had been to cancel Harts’ registration as a tax agent. It did so on the basis that Harts did not have a qualified director within the meaning of section 251BA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”) and that it was required to cancel its registration under sub-section 251K(3D).



2. At the hearing, Harts was represented by Mr Hastie of counsel and the Board by Mr Robertson of counsel. The documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 were admitted in evidence together with an affidavit of Mr Steven Irvine Hart sworn on 1 October, 1997 and an affidavit of Mrs Laura Hart sworn on the same day. No oral evidence was given.



THE ISSUE



3. The issue in this case was whether Harts had a qualified director within the meaning of section 251BA of the Act. In particular, the issue was whether a director who is a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers, beneficially owns shares in Harts carrying between them the right to exercise not less than 25 per cent of the voting power.



LEGISLATIVE FRAMEWORK



4. Part VIIA of the Act provides for the registration of tax agents. It sets out the requirements for the original registration of a tax agent whether that applicant is a natural person, partnership or a company. The Board must register the applicant if certain specified requirements are met. If those requirements are not met, the Board must refuse registration and give the applicant reasons for its decision (sections 251J and 251JA).



5. Once registered, a tax agent may apply to the Board for re-registration. Generally, the application must be made during the period commencing 60 days before and ending 30 days before the day on which its existing registration ceases to be in force. The Board may allow the application to be made at a later time but before the existing registration ceases to be in force. (section 251JB)



6. The Board must re-register an applicant as a tax agent if the Board is satisfied that the applicant meets certain requirements. Different requirements are specified according to whether the applicant is a natural person, a partnership or a company. Where those requirements are not met, the Board must refuse to re-register the applicant. The Board must give reasons for a decision to refuse to re-register an applicant. (section 251JC)



7. The requirements specified in relation to a company are the same as those specified for the original registration of a company as a tax agent and are:



“(i) a person employed by the company and specified

in the application as the original nominee of the company is a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters;



(ii) each executive officer of the company:

(A) has attained the age of 18 years at the date on which the application is made; and



(B) is of good fame, integrity and character;



(iii) if the company is a non-exempt company - the company is a company in which qualified directors have a substantial interest; and



(iv) the company has not gone into liquidation; ...” (paragraph 251JC(c))



8. Whether or not a person is a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income matters is determined by section 251BC.



9. Sub-paragraph 251JC(c)(iii) is relevant to a company such as Harts. It applies to a “non-exempt company”. A company is a “non-exempt company” at a particular time unless either paragraph 251BB(a) or (b) applies to the company. Harts does not come within either for it is not an authorised trustee company (paragraph 251BB(b) and section 251A) and it has not been registered as a tax agent at all times since the commencement of section 39 of the Taxation Laws Amendment Act (No.2) 1988 (“the Amendment Act”) on 24 June, 1988 (sub-paragraph 251BB(a)(ii)).



10. Sub-paragraph 251JC(c)(iii) requires that a non-exempt company is a company in which qualified directors have a substantial interest. For the purposes of Part VIIA, which includes this provision and also section 251K to which I will shortly come,



“... a company shall be taken to be a company in which qualified directors have a substantial interest if:



(a) shares in the company carrying between them the right to exercise not less than 25 per cent of the voting power in the company are beneficially owned by a director who, or by directors each of whom, is a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters; and



(b) the voting power in the company, to the extent to which it relates to those shares, is not controlled, by a person or persons other than the director or directors concerned.



(2) A reference in subsection (1) to control of the voting power in a company is a reference to control that is direct or indirect and includes a reference to control that is exercisable as a result or by means of arrangements or practices:



(a) whether or not having legal or equitable force; and



(b) whether or not based on legal or equitable rights.”

(section 251BA)



11. The registration of a tax agent continues in force for a period of three years unless terminated, cancelled or surrendered under the Act (sub-section 251JG(2)). Cancellation of the registration of tax agents is dealt with in section 251K. A number of circumstances are identified in which the Board must cancel or suspend a tax agent’s registration. Those circumstances include the conviction of the nominee of a tax agent of specified offences, the falsity in a material particular in a return unless the tax agent satisfies the Board that he, she or it had no knowledge of the falsity and it was not due to his, her or its inadvertence, the tax agent’s neglect of the business of a principal and the tax agent’s misconduct as a tax agent.



12. Also of relevance in this case are paragraphs 251K(2)(c) and (f) which provide that the Board may suspend or cancel the registration of any tax agent if it is satisfied that:



“(c) a registered nominee of the tax agent is not a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters;



...



(f) if the tax agent is a company - an executive officer of the company:



(i) has not attained the age of 18 years; or



(ii) is not of good fame, integrity and character.”



13. Sub-section 251K(3D) provides that:



“A Board shall cancel the registration of a tax agent that is a non-exempt company if the company ceases to be a company in which qualified directors have a substantial interest.”



14. It is this provision under which the Board cancelled Harts’ registration as a tax agent. In determining whether the company has qualified directors having a substantial interest, reference must be made again to section 251BA (see paragraph 10 above).



CONSIDERATION



15. The structure of Harts and related companies is set out in the affidavit of Mr Hart. On the basis of that evidence, I find that, at the time of the Board’s decision, Mr Hart was registered as a tax agent and also registered as a nominee of Harts. As he was registered, I have taken Mr Hart to be a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income matters. That is so for he was required to meet that criterion before he could be registered as a tax agent (section 251JC) and as a nominee (section 251KC). As I find that Mr Hart was a director of Harts, he was a director of Harts who was a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters. It follows that it is to Mr Hart’s beneficial ownership and to his control of the voting power that I must have regard in determining whether Harts meets the requirements of section 251BA.



16. At the time of the cancellation, all of the shares of Harts were owned by Harts Australia Limited. Since 1987, over fifty per cent of the shares in Harts Australia Limited have been held by Steve Hart Family Holdings Pty Ltd. The remainder of the shares have been held by other shareholders who were not identified for the purposes of these proceedings.



17. There are two shares in Steve Hart Family Holdings Pty Ltd. One is owned by Mrs Hart and the other by Ms Shirley Petersen. Mrs Hart and Ms Petersen each holds her share on trust for Mr Hart. Each executed a declaration of trust in which she declared that she held the share



“... and all dividends and rights pertaining thereto UPON TRUST for STEVEN IRVINE HART (hereinafter called “the beneficiary”) who provided the full consideration for the Trust AND I hereby agree to transfer, pay and deal with the said share in such manner as the said beneficiary shall from time to time direct AND will at the request of the said beneficiary appoint a proxy to attend all meetings of the shareholders and vote at every meeting in such manner as the said beneficiary shall direct in writing ...” (T documents, page 41)



18. Having considered the structure of the shareholding in the companies and the declaration of trusts and taking into account sub-section 251BA(2), I am satisfied that the voting power of Harts is controlled by Mr Hart. He does so as he controls the voting power of the shares held by Mrs Hart and Ms Petersen in Steve Hart Family Holdings Ltd. By virtue of its majority shareholding in Harts Australia, Steve Hart Family Holdings Ltd controls the voting power of Harts Australia and that company controls the voting power of Harts. Clearly, Mr Hart’s control of the voting power in Harts is indirect but is within the limits contemplated by sub-section 251BA(2). The voting power in Harts is not capable of being controlled by any person other than Mr Hart. As Mr Hart is the director who is a fit and proper person as required by sub-section 251BA, Harts meets the requirements of paragraph 251BA(b).



19. Paragraph 251BA(1)(a) is a different matter. It is not looking to the control, direct or indirect, of the voting power in Harts but to the beneficial ownership of shares carrying the right to exercise not less than twenty five per cent of the voting power in Harts. As no reference is made in paragraph 251BA(1)(a) to “control of the voting power”, it would appear that the provisions of sub-section 251BA(2) are not relevant in interpreting that paragraph.



20. Mr Hastie submitted that the Act should be interpreted to achieve its object and referred me to section 15AA of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 as well as to the judgements of the High Court in Cooper Brookes (Wollongong) Proprietary Limited v Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 26; (1980-81) 147 CLR 297 and of Hill J in Hilton v Commissioner of Taxation (1992) 38 FCR 170.



21. Section 251BA was inserted by the Amendment Act together with a number of other amendments intended to modernise and improve the provisions of the Act which dealt with the registration of tax agents and which had remained relatively unchanged since 1943. This is apparent from the Second Reading Speech of Mr Duncan, the then Minister for Employment and Education Services (Hansard, House of Representatives, 14 April, 1988, pages 1624-1625). The Minister addressed the registration of a company as a tax agent when he said:



“The ability of a company to gain registration as a tax agent on the basis of the qualifications of an employee - who need not be a director, shareholder or even a full time employee of the company - has led to exploitation by some unqualified persons.



The Bill will therefore tighten the rules for registration as a company tax agent by requiring that shares carrying at least 25 per cent of the voting power in a company applying for registration be beneficially owned by a director or directors having the necessary qualifications to be registered as a tax agent. ...” (page 1625)



22. The effect of Mr Hastie’s submission was that the Minister’s words favoured my interpreting paragraph 251BA(1)(a) so that I should look to the person who ultimately benefited from at least twenty five per cent of Harts shares. As I understood Mr Hastie to express his submission, I have to look at the overall ownership of shares in the various companies and determine who had the beneficial ownership of at least twenty five per cent of the shares in Harts. I would find that person was Mr Hart and, as he was a qualified director of Harts, that company satisfied paragraph 251BA(1)(a). He distinguished the cases to which Mr Robertson referred.



23. The High Court considered the meaning of the words “beneficially held” in the context of sub-section 80(5) of the Act in Dalgety Downs Pastoral Co. Pty. Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 54; (1952) 86 CLR 335 (Webb, Fullagar and Kitto JJ). That sub-section provided that no loss incurred in any year prior to the year of income could be an allowable deduction unless a private company satisfied the Commissioner that, on the last day of the year of income, shares of the company carrying not less that twenty five per cent of the voting power were beneficially held by persons who beneficially held shares of the company carrying not less than twenty five per cent of the voting power on the last day of the year in which the loss was incurred. Mr Gerloff had held 9,151 of the 20,305 shares issued by the Dalgety Downs Pastoral Co. Pty Ltd. He held the requisite 25 per cent of the company’s shares on 30 June, 1944 and 1945. In 1948 Mr Gerloff transferred all but 100 of his shares to Mrs Fitzpatrick. He did so by way of security for a sum of £2,750 to be paid on 15 June, 1951 with interest to be paid monthly in the meantime. The issue was whether Mr Gerloff beneficially held the shares he had transferred to Mrs Fitzpatrick by way of security.



24. The Commissioner had disallowed the company’s objection on the basis that Mr Gerloff did not beneficially hold the shares. In order to beneficially held, he had determined, the person’s name had to be entered in the register of members in respect of them and the person had to hold them for his own exclusive benefit. The High Court said:



“... we are of the opinion that the construction of s.80(5) upon which the deputy commissioner acted is correct. Dixon J so held in Avon Downs Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 26; (1949) 78 CLR 353, basing his conclusion upon the view that in the terminology of company law shares are said to be ‘held’ by the person who is registered as a shareholder in respect thereof, and that s.80(5), being concerned with voting power, should be treated as using that terminology. We share this view. Indeed it is not too much to say that the verb ‘hold’ and its variants, when used in relation to shares in companies, normally refers to the legal ownership of the shares according to the register of members. The Companies Acts of the United Kingdom and of the several States of the Commonwealth have uniformly used the word in this sense, and common usage has followed their example. Before a different meaning is accepted, some justification must be found in the context, or the subject-matter. No such justification is provided by the fact that ‘held’ is modified by the adverb ‘beneficially’. This word serves more naturally the purpose of excluding the case of a holding for the benefit of others than the purpose of so broadening the meaning of the word ‘held’ beyond the particular significance which it normally has in relation to shares as to make it equivalent to ‘owned’ in the most general sense of that word.” (pages 341-342)



25. Although this passage is not directly relevant to the interpretation of paragraph 251BA(1)(a), it is important in that it draws the distinction between holding shares in the sense of being entered on the register of shares and holding shares for a person’s exclusive benefit. It seems to me that it is to this latter aspect to which paragraph 251BA(1)(a) is referring when it refers to shares’ being beneficially owned by a person rather than to the name in which those shares are registered. The reference is to the equitable, rather than the legal, ownership although there will be many occasions when they reside in the same person.



26. In this case, there is no question that Harts Australia Ltd holds all of the shares issued in Harts. It is shown on the company extract as the sole shareholder (T documents, page 14). I note that the company extract states that the shares are “beneficially held” by Harts Australia Ltd. There is no evidence that Harts Australia Ltd holds the shares for or on behalf of any other person and particularly not for or on behalf of Mr Hart.



27. Mr Hastie, however, does not argue that shares in Harts are held by Harts Australia Ltd on behalf of another person. He argues that Mr Hart beneficially owns more than twenty five per cent of the shares in Harts because he is the beneficial owner of all of the shares of Steve Hart Family Holdings Ltd which holds more than fifty per cent of the shares in Harts Australia Ltd which in turn holds all of the shares in Harts.



28. If Mr Hastie’s argument is to succeed, the ownership of shares must entitle the shareholder to ownership of the assets of a company. The authorities, however, are against that proposition. It is clearly established that a company and its shareholders are separate legal entities and the inter-relationship was outlined by Lord Sumner in Gas Lighting Improvements Co Ltd v Inland Revenue Commissioners [1923] AC 723 when he said:



”It is said that all this was `machinery’ but that is true of all participations in limited liability companies. They and their operations are simply the machinery, in an economic sense, by which natural persons, who desire to limit their liability, participate in undertakings which they cannot manage to carry on themselves, either alone or in partnership, but, legally speaking, this machinery is not impersonal though it is inanimate. Between the investor, who participates as a shareholder, and the undertaking carried on, the law interposes another person, real though artificial, the company itself, and the business carried on is the business of that company, and the capital employed is its capital and not in either case the business or the capital of the shareholders. Assuming, of course, that the company is duly formed and is not a sham (of which there is no suggestion here), the ideal that it is mere machinery for effecting the purposes of the shareholders is a layman’s fallacy. It is a figure of speech, which cannot alter the legal aspects of the facts.” (page 740-741)



29. Reference should also be made to the earlier case of Saloman v Saloman & Co [1897] AC 22 where the House of Lords considered whether Mr Saloman was liable for the debts of a company in which he and six others were shareholders and which had been formed to purchase Mr Saloman’s business. Lord Macnaghten said:



“... The company is at law a different person altogether from the subscribers to the memorandum; and, though it may be that after incorporation the business is precisely the same as it was before, and the same persons are managers, and the same hands receive the profits, the company is not in law the agent of the subscribers or a trustee for them.” (page 51)



30. The distinction between notions of control and notions of ownership of the assets of a company were clearly drawn by Cozens-Hardy MR in Gramophone & Typewriter Ltd v Stanley [1908] 2 KB 89 when he said:



“The fact that an individual by himself or his nominees holds practically all the shares in a company may give him the control of the company in the sense that it may enable him by exercising his voting powers to turn out the directors and to enforce his own views as to policy, but it does not in any way diminish the rights or powers of the directors, or make the property or assets of the company his, as distinct from the corporation’s. Nor does it make any difference if he acquires not practically the whole, but absolutely the whole, of the shares. The business of the company does not thereby become his business. He is still entitled to receive dividends on his shares, but no more. I do not doubt that a person in that position may cause such an arrangement to be entered into between himself and the company as will suffice to constitute the company his agent for the purpose of carrying on the business, and thereupon the business will become, for all taxing purposes, his business. Whether this consequence follows is in each case a matter of fact. In the present case I am unable to discover anything in addition to the holdings of the shares which in any way supports this conclusion.” (pages 95-96)



31. Where there is a group of companies, the principles remain the same. As Mason J said in Walker v Wimborne [1976] HCA 7; (1975-76) 137 CLR 1:



“The word `group’ is generally applied to a number of companies which are associated by common or interlocking shareholdings, allied to unified control or capacity to control.” (page 6)



His Honour continued:



“In such a case the payment of money by company A to company B to enable company B to carry on its business may have derivative benefits for company A as a shareholder in company B if that company is enabled to trade profitably or realize its assets to advantage. Even so, the transaction is one which must be viewed from the standpoint of company A and judged according to the criterion of the interests of that company.” (page 6)



32. A little later, Mason J considered the legal personalities of the members of a group of companies in the context of the requirement that there be produced group or consolidated accounts. He said:



“However, it can scarcely be contended that the provisions of the Act operate to deny the separate legal personality of each company in a group. Thus, in the absence of contract creating some additional right, the creditors of company A, subsidiary company within a group, can look only to that company for the payment of their debts. They cannot look to company B, the holding company, for payment (see Walker v Wimborn (1976) 137 CLR at 6).



The Companies Act does not, in the case of holding companies, substitute the requirement for group accounts for the old requirement of accounts of the holding company itself. Group accounts are an additional requirement; the holding company is still obliged to lay before its shareholders in general meeting its profit and loss account and balance sheets (s. 162(1) and (3)), containing the information prescribed by the statute and accompanied by the prescribed documents.” (Industrial Equity Pty Ltd v Blackburn [1977] HCA 59; (1977) 137 CLR 567 at 577-578)



33. That these authorities still reflect the law today is apparent from cases such as Briggs v James Hardie & Co Pty Ltd (1989) 16 NSWLR 549 (Court of Appeal of New South Wales) and, more recently, Repatriation Commission v Harrison (unreported, No.NG722 of 1996, 17 September, 1997, Tamberlin J). In that case, Tamberlin J said that



“There are some closely limited exceptions to the fundamental principle enunciated in Salamon. These include specific inroads made by legislation or circumstances where there is a use of the separate entity principle to perpetrate fraud: see Ford, Austin and Ramsay, Ford’s Principles of Corporations Law,at pars4.350ff.” (page 9)



34. This view had been earlier stated by Sheen J in The “Maritime Trader” [1981] 2 Lloyd’s Rep. 153 in considering whether the Maritime Trader, which was owned by Maritime Transport Ship Holding G,m.b.H. (“MTS”) was beneficially owned by Maritime Transport Overseas G.m.b.H. (“MTO”). The shares in MTS were owned by MTO. Having referred to principles to those I have set out, Sheen J said:



“From that starting point there is no way in which it can be said that Maritime Trader was ‘beneficially owned as respects all the shares therein’ by MTO, unless the corporate veil can be lifted. I would not hesitate to lift that veil if the evidence suggested that it obscured from view a mask of fraud rather than the true face of the corporation.



...



My attention was drawn to the judgment of Mr. Justice Slynn in The Aventicum, [1978] 1 Lloyd’s Rep. 184, in which it was held that s.3(4) of the Act intended that the Court should not be limited to a consideration of who was the person having legal ownership of the shares in the vessel. The Court in all cases can and in some cases should look behind the registered owner to determine the true beneficial ownership. I do not dissent from that view. In that case there was much to be investigated in order to see whether each change of ownership was genuine or a sham. Circumstances may justify the lifting of the corporate veil or more than one veil, if that is necessary, to reveal the truth. But I also agree with Mr Justice Slynn that the onus is upon the plaintiffs to show that the person against whom it is sought to invoke the Admiralty jurisdiction by arresting his ship is the person who beneficially owns all the shares in that ship and that he is the person who is liable in an action in personam.” (page 157)



35. Finally, I will refer to the case of Malaysia Shipyard and Another v “Iron Shortland” as surrogate for the ship “Newcastle Pride” (1995) 131 ALR 738. The Iron Shortland was chartered by BHP Transport Pty Ltd (“BHPT”) and was arrested on the basis that it was a surrogate ship for the Newcastle Pride which had also been arrested. The registered owners of both ships were separate companies but both were wholly owned subsidiaries of Capeco Maritime NV (“Capeco”). An issue was whether the Iron Shortland was owned by Capeco.



36. Dealing only with the aspect of Sheppard J’s judgement that is concerned with ownership of assets in a group of companies, I note that he said:



“In relation to the question whether Capeco Maritime was the beneficial owner of the Newcastle Pride, I put the placing memorandum aside because I considered that the statements in it were ambiguous. They were consistent with the ownership of the vessels being in the various subsidiaries so that the usual situation of there being a holding company and a number of subsidiary companies was the one which was contemplated. On the basis of the authorities earlier referred to, particularly Macaura, there is no warrant for holding that the holding company, ie Capeco Maritime, has, upon the basis of the statements made in the placing memorandum, a beneficial interest in the vessels themselves as distinct from the beneficial interest in the shares in the subsidiary companies.” (page 758)



His Honour also considered whether other evidence established that the Iron Shortland was owned, directly or indirectly, by Capeco.



37. Returning to the case of Harts, I have no reason on the evidence to suppose that this is a case in which the use of separate entities may have been used to perpetrate a fraud. Therefore, on the authorities I have no reason, and it is not proper, to attempt to lift the corporate veil. In view of that, I find that Mr Hart is the beneficial owner of both shares in Steve Hart Family Holdings Ltd for they are held on trust for him by Mrs Hart and Ms Petersen. His beneficial interest in those two shares does not give him a beneficial interest in, or beneficial ownership of, the assets of Steve Hart Family Holdings Ltd. In particular, it does not give him the beneficial ownership of the shares which that company holds (as part of its assets) in Harts Australia Ltd. In turn, Steve Hart Family Holdings Ltd does not beneficially own the assets of Harts Australia Pty Ltd and so does not beneficially own the shares which Harts Australia Ltd holds in Harts. It follows that Mr Hart has no beneficial ownership of the shares of Harts and the requirements of paragraph 251BA(1)(a) are not met.



38. Returning to the Minister’s Second Reading Speech to which I referred above, it seems to me that the way in which I have interpreted section 251BA is consistent with his stated intention to tighten the rules for the registration of tax agents. It is apparent from the Minister’s words that the tightened control is to be partially achieved through linking an appropriately qualified director with a significant degree of control of the tax agent through the medium of that director’s beneficial ownership of what may be described as a significant share of the tax agent’s shares.



39. Since the cancellation of Harts’ registration, there have been changes including a variation in the allotment of shares in Harts. These are detailed in the affidavit of Mr Hart sworn on 1 October, 1997. Mr Hastie submitted that I should consider whether Harts’ registration was correctly cancelled in light of these changes. He submitted that administrative decision making is a continuum as described by Davies J in Jebb v Repatriation Commission [1988] FCA 105; (1988) 80 ALR 329 at 333. Freeman and Secretary, Department of Social Security [1988] FCA 294; (1988) 15 ALD 671, Mr Hastie continued, should be distinguished in view of the particular provisions in the Social Security Act 1947 that, once a pension has been cancelled, the previous recipient has no entitlement to restoration until a further claim has been lodged.



40. In an earlier case of Re Ellis and Tax Agents’ Board of Queensland (1992) 92 ATC 2,001, I referred to some of the previous authorities considering the review of cancellation decisions and of entitlement decisions. I did so in the context of whether, in reviewing a decision refusing to register a person as a tax agent, I should consider his employment during the five years preceding the date of the hearing or of my decision or preceding the date of his application. I said:



“On the clear wording of the provision, I consider that I am not entitled to have regard to employment prior to the five year period. However, it is not so clear whether that five year period should be considered from the date of Mr Ellis’s application, from the date of the hearing in this Tribunal or from some other date. Consideration was given to his type of question by the Tribunal in Re Tiknaz and Director-General of Social Services (1981) 4 ALN N44 (Davies J, President, Mr Ballard, Senior Member and Dr Garlick, Member) in relation to an application for an invalid pension. The note of the case reads as follows at page N45:



‘The Tribunal said that, on the facts, it was satisfied that T was permanently incapacitated at the date of the application. Even had this not been so, it was appropriate to take into account the events that had occurred after the application. These demonstrated that the possibility of recovery postulated then did not occur. The Tribunal also noted:



‘As the director-general was authorised to grant a pension to Mr Tiknaz as from the date of entitlement without requiring the lodgment of a further claim form, so also the Administrative Appeals Tribunal, which is empowered by s43 to exercise all the powers and functions which are vested by the Social Services Act in the director-general, may do so if, in the course of the proceedings before it, it comes to the view that there was not entitlement at the date of application but that there was entitlement at a later date.



8. Since then, Tiknaz has been referred to in a number of cases both in the Federal Court and in this Tribunal but I will refer only to Freeman v Department of Social Security [1988] FCA 294; (1988) 87 ALR 506 (Davies J). That case concerned the review of a decision to cancel a widow’s pension and, in the course of drawing a distinction between that type of decision and a decision to grant an entitlement, Davies J elaborated upon he principles in Tiknaz’s case. He said at pages 509-510:



‘The jurisdiction of the tribunal arose from the application made to it to review the decision of the delegate who, on 18 August 1987, affirmed the decision of the officer made on 19 May 1987. The function of the tribunal was therefore to reconsider the decision of 19 May 12987 and to determine whether the decision to cancel Mrs Freeman’s widow’s pension at that time was the correct or preferable decision to have been made. In coming to its decision, the tribunal was entitled to take into account all the facts proved before it. But the issue was whether, having regard to those facts, the decision to cancel made on 19 May 1987 was the correct or preferable decision, not whether Mrs Freeman had an entitlement to a widow’s pension as at the date of the tribunal’s decision.



Regard must always be had to the nature of the decision which is under review. In Re Tiknaz, Re Eaton, Jebb’s case and in McGourty’s case, the decision under review was a decision refusing to grant a pension or benefit that had been applied for. In each case, it was held that there was jurisdiction to consider entitlement not only as from the date of the application but also entitlement up to the date of the tribunal’s decision. This was because the function of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal formed part of an administrative continuum and, in reviewing a refusal to grant a pension or benefit that had been applied for, it was proper for the tribunal to consider the entitlement to the pension not only as at the date of the application for the pension or benefit or at the date of the decision refusing to grant it but also up to the time of the tribunal’s decision.



However, in the present case, the decision under review was not a decision refusing to grant a pension but a decision cancelling a pension as from 19 May 1987. After the decision in Re Tiknaz was handed down, the Act was amended by the insertion of ss158(2) and 159(2). Those sections provide that the grant or payment of a pension shall not be made except upon the making of a claim for that benefit or allowance, which claim is to be in writing and lodged in accordance with s158(1) and s159(1). One effect of these provisions is that once a pension or benefit has been cancelled, the previous recipient has no entitlement to restoration thereof until he or she has lodged a further claim in accordance with s158(1) and s159(1).



The ambit of the jurisdiction of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal in relation to the review of a decision to cancel a pension or benefit is therefore less than would be the jurisdiction of the tribunal in respect of a refusal to grant a pension or benefit or a decision suspending the payment of a pension or benefit. In the latter cases, there may well be an ongoing entitlement to a pension or benefit which the tribunal should recognise when formulating its decision. However, if the tribunal comes to the view that the decision to cancel was the correct or preferable decision, then no further matter remains for the tribunal’s consideration. Any entitlement of the applicant to a pension or benefit at a subsequent time must be the subject of a further claim which, having been made, would only become the subject of review within the tribunal’s jurisdiction once a decision with respect to it had been made by an officer of the Department of Social Security and that decision had been the subject of appeal and reconsideration in accordance with s19.’” (pages 2,003-2,004)



41. In Re Webb and Tax Agents’ Board of Queensland (1992) 28 ALD 464, I added that



“From my reading of Freeman’s case and Jebb’s case as well as the authorities referred to in those cases, it seems to me that the determining consideration in each was whether the decision amounted to the cancellation of an entitlement which a person had been receiving, as in Freeman’s case, or a decision that a person was not qualified to receive an entitlement from a particular time. In deciding whether that is the correct or preferable decision with regard to the particular date of cancellation, it matters not whether the person might, or might not have, an entitlement at a later date. In the second case there is a decision that there is no entitlement at the outset and the issue is whether there should be an entitlement. It was in relation to such cases that, it seems to me, Davies J said in Jebb’s case:



However, the general approach of the tribunal has been to regard the administrative decision making process as a continuum so that, within the limits of a reconsideration of the decision under review, the tribunal considers the applicant’s entitlement from the date of application, or other proper commencing date, to the date of the tribunal’s decision. That function was enunciated in Re Tiknaz and Director-General of Social Services (1981) 4 ALN No 44 at 333.” (pages 467-468)



42. Having considered the issues again, I can see no reason to change my understanding of authorities such as Jebb and Freeman. Applying their principles to this case, I find that I am concerned with a decision to cancel Harts’ registration as a tax agent. In the framework of the registration provisions I have summarised above, cancellation of a tax agent’s registration is a separate matter from whether or not a person is entitled to be registered as a tax agent. The two decisions cannot be treated as the same decision. As the decision under review in this case is a cancellation case, I am limited to considering the facts as they were at the date of the cancellation. I cannot take into account the changes in the shareholding of Harts.



43. For the reasons I have given, I affirm the decision of the Tax Agents’ Board of Queensland dated 3 September, 1997.
