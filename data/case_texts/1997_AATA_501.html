REASONS FOR DECISION



J Block. Senior Member



  1. These matters, heard together, relate to a decision of the Respondent dated 1 October 1996, disallowing objections dated 30 May 1995 against amended assessments issued on 4 April 1995 in respect of each of the years ending 30 June 1990, 30 June 1991, 30 June 1992, 30 June 1993 and 30 June 1994; the years referred to in this clause 1 are collectively referred to as the “relevant years”; the term “1990 year” will be used to refer to the year ending 30 June 1991 and the other relevant years are referred to accordingly.
  2. (a) The Applicant was represented by Miss L. Robinson of Counsel instructed by Johnston Vaughan, Solicitors, and the Respondent was represented by Mr D.B. McGovern of Counsel instructed by the Australian Government Solicitor. The Tribunal admitted into evidence the documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, and in addition a number of exhibits as follows:
    • Exhibit R1 consists of material referable to the Commonwealth Bank of Australia (“CBA”);
    • Exhibit R2 consists material referable to Westpac Banking Corporation (“Westpac”);
    • Exhibit R3 consists of material referable to MLC;
    • Exhibit R4 is a Residential Tenancy Agreement dated 29 January 1992 in respect of which Kristy (White) is the Lessor, and reflecting his address as an address in a western suburb of Sydney;
    • Exhibit R5 is a lengthy typed record of an interview of the Applicant by the police dated 24 March 1994;
    • Exhibit R6 is a bulky file of documents referable to Mastercard;
    • Exhibit R7 is the CBA account produced by the Applicant in answer to a summons issued against him.
  3. I propose to commence by dealing with a number of matters, which might conveniently be characterised as being of a preliminary nature.
  4. (a) The Respondent contended that the objections by the Applicant are invalid in that they do not disclose the grounds on which the Applicant objected to the amended assessments.

(b) The objection (T4 of the T Documents) by the Applicant in respect of the 1990 year (and all of the other objections are in similar terms) reads as follows:

“NOTICE OF OBJECTION AGAINST ASSESSMENT

I John Christopher (White) hereby object against the assessment of income tax based on income derived by me during the year of income ended 30 June 1990 and issued to me by Notice of Amended Assessment dated 4 April 1995 and contend that the taxable income of $147,445.00 shown on the Notice of Assessment should be reduced by the excision of the amount of $135,782.00 included in the assessable income.

In support of my contention I claim that:
  1. My taxable income for the year ended 30 June 1990 was $11,663.00.
  2. Neither the amount of $135,782.00 nor any other additional amount has been derived by me as income within the meaning of the Act for the year ended 30 June 1990.
  3. The Medicare Levy payable by me pursuant to Part VIIB of the Act should be calculated by reference to a taxable income of $11,663.00 or alternately a taxable income less than that shown on the notice of assessment.
  4. The additional tax payable by me should be deleted from the assessment or alternately wholly remitted or alternately reduced to an amount less than that shown on the notice of assessment.
The Statements of Claim contained in the foregoing paragraph represent my grounds of objection for the purposes of Section 14ZU of the Taxation Administration Act, 1953.”

(c) Mr McGovern referred the Tribunal to the decision of the Full Federal Court in Szajntop v Commissioner of Taxation (1993) 42 FCR 318 in which the objection was cast in similar terms; a judgment was given jointly by Black CJ and Burchett J and a second judgment was given by Gray J:


(1) in respect of the judgment of Black CJ and Burchett J, at page 320 their Honours said:

“We turn first to the construction of the letter. The starting point is that the letter was, as appears from its terms, intended to be an objection. It stated “We ... hereby wish to object to the assessment in full” (emphasis added) and it concluded with a request that the assessments be set aside.

The reference in the second sentence of the letter to tax returns lodged on behalf of the appellant should be read having regard to the stated object of the letter, which was to object to the assessments for the years ending 30 June 1985 , 1986 and 1987. It should also be read having regard to the nature and circumstances of the assessments to which it purported to be an objection; they were default assessments based, as both parties obviously then knew, upon an assets betterment statement prepared by the Commissioner’s officers.”;


(2) and at page 322 their Honours said:

“Although such a ground inferentially denies the correctness either of the betterment statement upon which the assessments were based or of the conclusions drawn from the betterment statement, it offers no detail at all as to how the statement is said to be incorrect. The assertion in the letter that the betterment statement was produced “on completely erroneous information” conveys no idea of the particular respects in which it is contended that the statement was incorrect. There are many ways in which a betterment statement may be incorrect: the methodology may be inappropriate, particular facts may be wrong, values attributed to assets may be incorrect or there may be the omission of relevant facts of which the Commissioner was unaware or mistaken. The letter does not come to grips with any of the possibilities; the best it does, apart from saying that the statement is based on “completely erroneous information”, is to say that “absolute proof as to the above facts” is being got together.” (Emphasis added)


(d) In respect of the judgment of Gray J:

(1) At pages 326 and 327 his Honour said:

“Those proceedings were heard and determined by Hill J, who delivered judgment on 1st July 1992, dismissing them. His Honour held that the Court had no jurisdiction to deal with the application under the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977. He determined the appeals on the basis that both parties conceded that only one issue needed to be determined, namely “whether the letter of 11th August 1987 was a valid objection”. As his Honour said:-

“If it was not, then the Tribunal had no jurisdiction to review any decision with respect to it and the applicant’s application to the Tribunal for review necessarily would fail. If, on the other hand, it was a valid objection, then counsel for the Commissioner conceded that the Tribunal erred in law in refusing to grant the adjournment.”

His Honour held that:- “While non technical language may be employed, and while the court should not give a narrow or rigid construction to the grounds employed and in the event of doubt should give the taxpayer the benefit of that doubt, no amount of generosity of construction enables me to conclude that the applicant’s letter in any way set out fully and in detail her grounds of objection. The letter can be construed as involving no more than the general complaint that the asset betterment statement is wrong. Where it was wrong and the extent to which it was wrong remain undefined. Such a general statement in my opinion does not satisfy the language of s185.”

Section 185(1) of the Act provides: “A taxpayer dissatisfied with any assessment under this Act may, within 60 days after service of the notice of assessment, lodge with the Commissioner an objection in writing against the assessment stating fully and in detail the grounds on which he relies.”

Over the years, the courts have tended to substitute for the words “fully and in detail” formulations which appear less stringent than the words of the statute. These attempts go back at least to R. v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation for Western Australia; Ex parte Copley (1923) 30 ALR 86 , at p 87, where Knox CJ said:-

“I think it is effective notice of objection under the Act if the written communication is expressed in words that are reasonably calculated to convey to the understanding of the person to whom it is addressed (1) that the taxpayer contends that the assessment is not in accordance with law; and (2) the grounds on which that contention is based.”

In the same case, at p 88, Starke J pointed out that an objection need not be in formal language, or in language that lawyers would adopt. In A.L. Campbell and Co. Pty. Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1951] HCA 36; (1951) 82 CLR 452 , at p 461, Dixon J (as he then was) said that grounds of objection should not be interpreted technically, narrowly or with rigidity. In H.R. Lancey Shipping Co. Pty. Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1951) 9 ATD 267 , at p 273, Williams J said:-

“The grounds of objection need not be stated in legal form, they can be expressed in ordinary language, but they should be sufficiently explicit to direct the attention of the respondent to the particular respects in which the taxpayer contends that the assessment is erroneous and his reasons for this contention. In each case the sufficiency of the grounds is a matter for the Court. Vague grounds such as that the assessment is excessive are not, in my opinion, a compliance with the Act.””;


(2) at page 329 his Honour said:

“For the reasons which I have given, I am of the view that Hill J reached the correct conclusion, namely that the letter dated 11th August 1987 did not constitute a valid objection. It was not capable of founding any jurisdiction of the Commissioner to give the decision he purported to give, or any jurisdiction of the Tribunal to review that purported decision. The appeal must be dismissed and the taxpayer must pay the Commissioner’s costs of the appeal.”


(e) Mr McGovern gave notice at the commencement of the hearing that he intended to argue that the Applicant’s objections did not disclose the grounds on which he objected to the amended assessments. However, he dealt with this aspect of the matter in his closing address, and not by way of preliminary objection, and thus after the matter had been fully heard.

(f) Having regard to the decision in Szajntop’s Case, and that decision is, of course, binding on the Tribunal, the Tribunal finds that the objections by the Applicant did not disclose the grounds upon which the Applicant objected to the amended assessments.

  1. (a) The second matter of a preliminary nature concerns the use of names. It is quite usual in matters of this nature to use initials in respect of names; this practice, while it preserves anonymity also has the effect of making the decision difficult to read. In this particular instance, the Applicant used variations of his own name and, the Respondent alleges, other names. I propose to refer to the Applicant by the pseudonym “White”, a common name, coupled where relevant with his own Christian names, John Christopher, which are also common enough. Thus, and on this basis, the Applicant sometimes used the name John Christopher White, sometimes Christopher White and sometimes Kristy White.

(b) Another Christopher is relevant to this matter. He too will be assigned by way of pseudonym, another common name, “Black”. There was some evidence before me (dealt with more fully later in these Reasons) as to the fact that Christopher Black does exist as a person separate and apart from the Applicant. The Respondent contended that either the Applicant and Black are one and the same person and that in fact Christopher Black was no more than the Applicant’s alter ego, or in the alternative that if a Christopher Black existed as a separate person, the Applicant used his, Black’s, identity on numerous occasions when it suited him to do so, as his own.

(c) Oral evidence was given by the Applicant and also by two other persons called by the Applicant. I propose to refer to the other two witnesses as “Miss Brown” and “Green” respectively.

(d) The Applicant’s uncle who lives in Galway in Ireland is a relevant person in this case. He is referred to simply by his Christian name, “Joseph” or sometimes as “the uncle” or “the Applicant’s uncle”.

Words or names which appear in these Reasons in brackets denote that they have been edited for the purposes of anonymity.

  1. (a) In addition to the exhibits (which, combined, constitute a considerable quantity of documentation) I had before me two statements by the Applicant, one dated 28 May 1997 (“first statement”) and the second dated 29 June 1997 (“second statement”); the second statement is expressed to be in support of the first statement. It may be noted in this context that the second statement purports to be a Statement of Facts and Contentions, but it is cast in the form of a statement by the Applicant; both statements refer (inappropriately) to Plaint No. NT96390 in the Local Court at Kogarah; it is clear, however, that these were intended for these proceedings given that the Plaint No. does relate to the number allotted by the Tribunal to one of the objections. (The Applicant also filed a separate Statement of Facts and Contentions dated 8 August 1997).

The first statement contains certain statements which are cast in intemperate or inappropriate language. By way of example, clause 7 of the first statement reads as follows:

“7. A previous solicitor acting for me in this taxation matter, Mr (Mack) of (Rose) Partners, ignored what I told him about the cheques coming into my account. I gave him a statement from my Commonwealth Bank account clearly showing one of the cheques going into my account and I also told him about the other 2 cheques. Seemingly, he was structured by the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) to ignore this fact. I have paid Mr Mack over $6,000 to date with no result - either good or bad.”


By way of further, and perhaps more apposite, example, clause 18 of the first statement reads as follows:

“Also, the ATO and one detective from (Police Station), (Jones) structured a story against me and she perjured herself in Court. She accused me of using a Mastercard belonging to (Black). The accusation came about 18 months after a supposed incident. It was a very nasty thing to do, unfounded and cost me over $6,000 in legal fees and lost income. Will the ATO stop at nothing to get my money? I have a transcript from Sutherland Court which states I was not Chris (Black) and the Court found that as a fact in the case.”

(b) The Tribunal was furnished also with a statement by Miss Brown which is short and which (broadly) simply testifies as to the separate existence of White. It too is expressed to relate to proceedings in the Local Court at Kogarah under Plaint No.NT96390.

(c) The statement by Green produced to the Tribunal is even stranger. It is expressed to be an affidavit sworn for the purpose of the Local Court (Civil Claims) issued at the Administrative Appeals Tribunal; moreover the top right hand corner of the first page of that statement contains the following words:

“OPTION A

Before Judgment.”


No explanation of these odd words or their intended purpose was furnished to the Tribunal.

  1. (a) In respect of the amended assessments which are the subject of these proceedings, it is relevant to note that:

(1) the Applicant failed to return certain interest income in respect of the 1990 year and the 1991 year; amended assessments were raised in respect of that interest income;

(2) subsequently there were further investigations in respect of the Applicant which resulted in the betterment statement which is document T6 of the T Documents. That betterment statement was the subject of correspondence between the Respondent and (previous) advisors, and which resulted in certain alterations in respect of the amounts assessed.

(b) The Respondent furnished me with a helpful outline of submissions (“Outline”). Clauses 4 and 5 of the Outline (and Miss Robinson agreed to the procedure set out in clause 4) may be conveniently set out in full in these reasons as follows:

“4. The asset betterment calculations appear at pp 8-11 of the Section 37 documents. The asset betterment has been calculated by determining the increase in assets in each year, adding thereto outgoings not qualifying for deduction (each ordinary living expenses) and unexplained withdrawals from bank accounts (which are presumed to be retained as assets or converted into other assets).

It is agreed between the representatives of the parties that the Tribunal should make determinations of fact in relation to the issues referred to in paragraph 5 hereunder and depending upon the determination of those issues the parties will bring in a formal Short Minutes of order to reflect the impact, if any, on the amended assessments as a consequence of the Tribunal’s determination.
  1. The applicant here seeks to demonstrate error in the assessment by a betterment method by establishing the existence of receipts of a capital nature in the form of an alleged inheritance from his late mother’s estate in Ireland and/or a loan from Chris (Black) which he repaid in Ireland from the proceeds of his late mother’s estate. These facts if established would demonstrate overall error in the assessment by the betterment method (cf Case R 124 84 ATC 793 at 806-807).
The applicant contends for a foreign exchange gain of $40,033 in the year ended 30 June 1991 presumably on the transfer from Ireland to Australia of certain funds. There is no satisfactory factual basis to support the alleged gain. Alternatively, foreign currency specifically within the definition of “asset” for CGT purposes. Any gain arising on acquisition or disposal of foreign can give rise to a capital gain. Taking the amount received for the disposal and subtracting the cost of acquiring the asset gives rise to the capital gain for the year in question. Division 3B of Part III would not apply because the gain is not derived in relation to a borrowing transaction.”

(c) In summary, it was agreed as between Counsel for the parties that it is necessary for the Tribunal to make findings as to three matters only and that in the light of those findings, the parties would bring in formal Short Minutes of Order on the basis set out in clause 4 of the Outline: Those three matters may conveniently be characterised for the purposes of these Reasons as:

(1) the inheritance;

(2) the Black/MLC matter (which is connected with the inheritance); and

(3) the foreign exchange gain of $40,033.

  1. I deal in the first instance with the inheritance and for which purpose it is necessary to refer in brief terms to the Applicant’s evidence. In this context:

(a) The Applicant was born in Ireland in 1949 and grew up in that country. In June 1960, and when he was 11, his mother died, and in consequence of her death, he became entitled to an inheritance of £60,000; (references to pounds or £ are to Irish pounds; the Tribunal was informed that for practical purposes the value of the Irish pound has been, in approximate terms, not materially dissimilar from the value of the pound sterling).

(b) The amount referred to in subclause (a) was in turn referable to an inheritance from some person (unknown) in some part (unknown) of the United States of America. That United States of America deceased person, who may or may not have been a relative of the Applicant, bequeathed an amount of money (not specified) to the Applicant’s mother and her two brothers one of whom was Joseph.

(c) When the Applicant’s mother died in 1960, her share of the inheritance (said to be about £60,000) came under the control or perhaps trusteeship of Joseph. The Applicant contended that Joseph was in effect vested with a power to nominate a beneficiary or beneficiaries from amongst the Applicant and his siblings (two brothers and a sister), and that Joseph exercised his power so as to nominate the Applicant alone in respect of the whole of his mother’s portion, and to the exclusion of his siblings. The Applicant gave evidence in this context as to a close relationship between him and his uncle; in particular, he gave evidence in this context as to the fact that in 1982 while on a trip to Ireland, his uncle told him that he alone (the Applicant) would inherit Joseph’s farm, worth approximately £50,000.

(d) Joseph is now an old man. He is a farmer who has little education and the inheritance referred to previously was invested and generally looked after by a solicitor; however, no evidence was given as to the name and address of that solicitor, who is referred to simply as the “solicitor”.

(e) Although the Applicant could, according to his evidence, have called for payment of his inheritance when he turned 21, in 1970, he did not do so and the inheritance remained under the control of his uncle until 1990, during which time it was invested by or through the office of the solicitor.

(f) There was no documentary evidence of any sort or nature as to the inheritance. In particular the Tribunal was not furnished with any will (or copy of a will) by any person (either the United States of America deceased or the Applicant’s mother) or any accounts or records or tax returns of any kind as to the inheritance or any income referable to it. Nor was there any evidence before the Tribunal as to any enquiries or attempts by any person now representing, or who had previously represented, the Applicant to obtain such evidence.

(g) The Tribunal is prepared to accept (as contended by Miss Robinson) that it is a matter of common knowledge that in the late 19th century and thereafter many Irish people emigrated to the United States of America. It may well be that, as the Applicant said, he has relatives in the United States of America. In these circumstances, one might expect that he could at least name the benefactor in respect of what was clearly a substantial amount. The Tribunal considers that an amount of £60,000 in the early 1960’s to a family living in Ireland in circumstances which were not affluent, would have seemed a very considerable sum indeed. However, the Applicant could not even state whether the benefactor was in fact a relative. His evidence was to the effect that that inheritance came to him through his mother, his uncle having selected him for this purpose. When questioned as to the fact that his uncle in the exercise of the alleged power, appears to have excluded his siblings, his answer was that he had a special relationship with his uncle.

(h) If there was indeed an inheritance as alleged by the Applicant there would undoubtedly have been a will or wills which would have received probate in the United States of America and/or in Ireland. Intestate succession in this context appears to be improbable if only because of the allegation that Joseph had the power to appoint the Applicant in respect of his mother’s inheritance to the exclusion of his siblings. Such a provision would of necessity have been couched in a written and presumably reasonably complex or comprehensive will. There would also, of course, have been accounts and records including returns to the relevant tax authorities as to income derived in respect of the inheritance. Clause 4 of the first statement sets out that by 1990 the inheritance had grown from £60,000 to approximately £98,000, equivalent at that time to over A$200,000.

(i) There are, of course, numerous other questions which arise from these basic aspects. If there was a trust on some basis, when did the Applicant become presently entitled to the income or the corpus? What then are the tax consequences? Was Joseph possessed of a power and why did he exercise it to appoint the Applicant alone? If he did so exercise it, why then did the siblings not complain about their exclusion from participation in what must have been, as set out previously, to them a substantial amount? As a general matter how did it come to pass that the Applicant alone succeeded to the whole of his mother’s inheritance, and what indeed was the nature (vested, contingent or otherwise) of his mother’s or for that matter his, the Applicant’s, inheritance? In respect of all of these questions there were no credible answers.

(j) The evidence of the uncle as regards the inheritance would plainly have been of great importance, but he was not called as a witness, and no statement by him was produced. The Applicant said on a number of occasions that his uncle is elderly and that he did not wish to bother him. There was, however, no suggestion that his uncle is infirm. Moreover, and even assuming that the uncle was not, for whatever reason, able to travel to Australia for the purpose of these proceedings (and there was no such allegation) evidence by video-link might have been a feasible alternative. The uncle is, after all, and in relation to the inheritance, at its core. He was a co-beneficiary with his sister, he was (so it is alleged) the trustee or custodian of his sister’s share, and it was he who instructed the solicitor. The failure to produce any evidence by him must give rise to an adverse inference against the Applicant.

(k) Then there is the question of the solicitor’s records. Miss Robinson said from the bar table, in her closing address, that the solicitor is dead, although there was no such allegation in evidence. But even assuming that the solicitor is dead, his records would not have died with him, and presumably there would have been some arrangement for the custody of those records either by his partners, if he had any, or if relevant by his successor, or failing a successor, some proper authority.

(l) An inheritance of such substance must of necessity give rise to written records. The failure of the Applicant to produce anything at all in corroboration of his vague evidence as to the inheritance must also give rise to an adverse inference against the Applicant.

(m) In summary the only evidence as to inheritance is that of the Applicant; his evidence on this subject was vague and contradictory and cannot in the absence of corroboration be accepted.

(n) The inheritance with accumulated income (and there was no evidence as to whether it bore tax in Ireland or elsewhere) amounted in 1990, as set out previously, to £60,000 or A$200,000, when it was paid over to Black in Ireland. This aspect will be dealt with in more detail when I come to deal with the Black/MLC matter.

(o) It is probably worthy of note that the Applicant’s evidence as contained in Exhibit R6 differs as regards the inheritance from that given by him before the Tribunal. By way of just one example, he said (at page 31):

“Yeah well - there was money there with my uncle’s and - which was, ah, left to my mother from a legacy we used to call, in America, from her relations and I was the oldest in the family so, um - so it came by way. Put it that way.”;

and later on the same page 31 in answer to a question as to how much the inheritance was:

“In Irish pounds, quite a lot any way. I don’t know exactly.

Just a ball park figure?

Would say 100,000 or something or ...”.


The two substantial (and important) differences are as to the amount of the inheritance and, even more to the point, that as set out in his statement to the police he succeeded because he was the eldest and not in consequence of the exercise by his uncle of a power.

(p) In respect of the inheritance there is only one possible finding; that finding must be that there is no credible evidence before me as to the inheritance and accordingly that the Applicant did not discharge the onus on him under Section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act. It must be said indeed that having regard to all of the circumstances, he made virtually no effort to do so.

  1. Before dealing with the remaining matters a description, albeit in brief terms, of the background against which the Black/MLC transaction arose is necessary. In this context:

(a) The Applicant emigrated from Ireland to Australia as an assisted emigrant in 1971. He had previously served his apprenticeship in Ireland and become a plumber.

(b) He said that during the period 1971 to 1989 he worked most of the time, sometimes as an employed plumber and sometimes as a self-employed plumber He said that he lived in a “miserly” fashion. During that period he sent money from time to time (in amounts of $100 approximately each) to his uncle; he stated that his uncle would probably have “drank” it. As to why it was it was necessary for him to send money to his uncle was never made clear (more particularly when one has regard to the fact that his uncle appears to own his own farm and was also the recipient of an inheritance, presumably equal to that of the Applicant’s mother’s inheritance) and more fully referred to previously in these Reasons.

(c) During this period, the Applicant purchased four vehicles, a Volkwagen, two Valiants and a Volvo, the last purchased cheaply (for $2,000) abut ten years ago from Black.

(d) During this period the Applicant also sent money from time to time from Australia to Ireland where it was deposited to an interest bearing account with the Bank of Ireland. He was unsure about how much he had sent precisely but thought that it was in excess of $5,000 per annum.

(e) In 1989 the Applicant became ill, and after a colonoscopy, bowel cancer was diagnosed. An operation was performed and it seems that that operation was successful. About three months after the operation (which took place in 1989), the Applicant went back to work.

(f) The Applicant decided at Christmas time (presumably in 1989) although there was also reference to this regard to a decision in 1990 (which might refer to early 1990) to return to Ireland. He said that he wanted to live with relatives; moreover, and as set out previously his uncle (who had no children) had told him previously that his farm would be the Applicant’s one day (presumably on the death of the uncle).

(g) In 1979 the Applicant had bought a unit (the “Whiteacre unit”) in a suburb referred to as (Whiteacre); the price was $46,000 which was paid in cash. The reference in this context to cash means that the Applicant did not require a mortgage. The Applicant uses that term “cash purchase” in a different sense, so as to refer to a purchase of property with actual currency or notes. He said that one can thereby achieve a lower price.

(h) The Applicant sold the Whiteacre unit in March 1990 for $146,000. Completion took place in May 1990, and the proceeds when received were deposited into one or other of his bank accounts; the Applicant conducted a number of bank accounts, and including, and apart from the Banks previously mentioned, with Advance, St George and ANZ Banks. (The proceeds of the Whiteacre unit may have been paid in two tranches, one referable to the deposit, and the other to the far more substantial balance on completion, but nothing turns on this).

(i) The Applicant stated that he had met Black in Ireland in 1964/65 and indeed that they had attended the same school. They met again in Sydney at the Irish Club in 1974. Black was a gyprocker who was, as he put it, in a de facto relationship with a New Zealand girl and was also, according to the Applicant, in Australia illegally. The Applicant stated that Black came to stay with him in 1979, and stayed with him at various times thereafter.

  1. It is against the background set out in the preceding clause 9 that the Black/MLC matter must be considered.

(a) Clause 5 of the second statement reads as follows:

“On 24 April 1990, C. Black gave the Applicant three cheques for $66,000.00, $66,00.00 and $66,783.54 totaling $198,783.54. Copies of the cheques are attached and marked “A”. A copy of the transaction history is attached and marked “B”.”

Copies of the three cheques were duly annexed; all three cheques were drawn by Australian Funds Management Limited in favour of John Christopher (White) in the case of one and in the case of the other two in favour of Christopher (White).

Annexure “B” to the second statement is a transaction history which commences with 7 August 1989 when the balance was $164,999.62 and ends with 24 April 1990 with entries referable to the three cheques and culminating in a nil balance.

(b) Australian Funds Management Limited was at some point in time acquired or in some sense taken over by or merged with MLC and it is for this reason that it is MLC which is referred to in the context of this transaction.

(c) Exhibit R3 contains:

(1) A letter by MLC dated 11 August 1997, on the subject of the MLC Australia Trust and sets out that “an MLC Australia Trust was opened in the name of Chris (Black) on 18 March 1985 with a registered address of (the Whiteacre unit)”.

(2) A transaction history which is considerably more complete than that attached as Annexure “B” to the second statement; that latter transaction history appears to have been edited, inter alia, by the deletion of a reference to the Contract Number (600006548), and also by the deletion of all entries prior to 7 August 1989. The Exhibit R3 transaction history commences with a balance of $128,063.69 on 26 February 1989 and inter alia refers to a number of unit purchases each for $7,000.00, on dates which, so the evidence revealed, correspond with withdrawals of the same amounts from one or other of the Applicant’s various bank accounts.

(d) The Applicant said that Black gave him the three cheques and that he did not know the source of the money; he said that the three cheques were deposited to three separate banking accounts, one with CBA one with ANZ and one with Westpac.

(e) The Applicant said that he waited for a week or two and then withdrew the money in cash; he then, after digging a hole in the balcony area of the grounds in which the Whiteacre unit is situated, buried it there. He said that he and Black were then contemplating a property purchase for cash as a joint venture from a Yugoslav, and that the Yugoslav would have been prepared to take a lower price if payment were made in the form of cash. The Applicant said that purchases of this nature were not uncommon. (The effect of such a cash transaction as regards considerations of taxation and stamp duty was not canvassed.)

(f) In fact no such purchase eventuated and the Applicant then went to Ireland. In May 1990 he arranged for the transfer to his account with the Bank of Ireland of an aggregate amount of $150,000 from his various bank accounts in three tranches of $50,000 each.

(g) The Applicant explained that he buried the money in this fashion instead of depositing it with a bank for reasons of safety (by which I understood him to refer to his own personal safety) although he never explained what dangers he was seeking to guard against.

(h) Exhibit R5 is the typed record of a lengthy interview with the Police. I do not think that it is necessary for me to set out an analysis of Exhibit R5 or to go into detail as to certain differences between the Applicant’s evidence on that occasion when compared with his evidence before the Tribunal. It may be noted in brief though that Exhibit R5 contains numerous references to moneys belonging to the Applicant held in the boot of a car. See in this context by way of example page 61 of Exhibit R5, and the following interchange between White and the policeman:

“Policeman: There is only five minutes on the tape. Just going back, when you were depositing this money, where was it being kept?

White: Actually in the boot of the car, and sometimes, you know, I would keep it in the unit, you know.

Policeman: You were referring to safety?

White: Mm. Well in the boot of the car, in the sense that I couldn’t leave it in the unit when I went to work, you know. So I had to take a chance.

Policeman: Yes, just that you were referring, you were concerned about the safety?

White: Mm.

Policeman: About not depositing the money in ...(indistinct)..., but you were prepared to keep a large sum in the boot of your car?

White: That’s right.

Policeman: And you had to go, over a series, over the period of months --

White: Sorry, when I go home I take the bag into the house, into the unit, you know. But I couldn’t afford to leave the bag in the unit when I was going to work. So the only option I had was the boot of the car, in that sense.”


It was that interview which led in turn to a prosecution of the Applicant under the Financial Transactions Reports Act; in late 1994 the Applicant was convicted of structuring offences against that Act in respect of a series of deposits below the sum of $10,000 referred to in that Act into bank accounts operated by him at various bank accounts in various names and including a bank account under the false name (“Gregory Duckworth”); (this is also a name made-up for the purposes of these Reasons). The Applicant pleaded guilty; he says that he did so despite his innocence, having been persuaded to do so by his solicitor and barrister at the time, and denied that he and Gregory Duckworth were one and the same person. The Applicant’s denial of his guilt in these circumstances is, for obvious reasons, unacceptable. The Applicant admitted that he was aware of the fact that the Financial Transactions Reports Act specifies an amount of $10,000, and so that in broad terms it is transactions above that limit which are reportable.

(i) The Applicant then in May 1990 travelled firstly to the Philippines where he met Black and then together with Black to the United States and thence to Ireland; while in Ireland he stayed with an aunt in Dublin for 6-7 weeks and during that period travelled to Galway 3 or 4 times to visit his uncle; when visiting his uncle he either went by train or hired a car.

(j) The Applicant stated that it was at or about this time that he repaid Black in respect of the money which Black had withdrawn from MLC and paid to him. Payment was made on his behalf by his uncle out of the inheritance which was still under his uncle’s control and which by this time had grown to £98,000 and which amounted in approximate terms to the aggregate of the sums received by him (so he alleged) from Black.

(k) The Applicant explained that he was in effect assisting Black by paying him (or perhaps more appositely repaying him) his inheritance and in Ireland where Black wanted the moneys. The Applicant said that Black could not himself have transferred his own money from MLC to Ireland, because he (Black) was illegally in Australia and because of his de facto relationship with a New Zealand girl. It was put to the Applicant that neither of these factors had apparently prevented Black from (allegedly) opening the MLC account in his own name or from acquiring units in the relevant trust in his own name. It was put to him also that in 1990 there was no legal reason why Black could not have transferred his own money to Ireland. The Applicant, however, adhered to his version, namely, that he had helped Black in this fashion because of the de facto relationship and because Black was an “illegal”. It may be noted that Black appears to have worked in his trade in Australia before his departure to Ireland.

(l) The Applicant said that he was doing Black a favour because Black had saved his life. It transpired during the course of his evidence that the reference to “saving his life” meant that Black had taken him to the hospital when he became ill. The Applicant’s own relations with Black were, as described by him, decidedly ambivalent. He had done numerous favours so he alleged for Black; nevertheless he described Black in unflattering terms. He said that he had lost contact with Black who is now apparently in either Ireland or the United States of America. He said too that when he last spoke to Black more than a year ago he had told him that he, Black, owed the Applicant half a million dollars. If the Applicant had, as he alleged, paid Black £98,000 out of his inheritance in repayment of the aggregate amount received from Black, then plainly there would have been no debtor/creditor or other contractual relationship between the two and the statement to this effect by the Applicant would have been false. Miss Robinson, however, argued that the statement is capable of another interpretation, and being that the Applicant’s assistance to Black had resulted in a loss to him of a substantial amount (although it is difficult to see how it could have amounted to half a million dollars) and that this then accounts for the statement alleging an indebtedness by Black in the Applicant of this sum. While Miss Robinson’s argument is tenable, it does not answer the basic question which is as to why Black could not have transferred his own moneys himself. No evidence written or verbal by Black was tendered to the Tribunal.

(m) The Applicant testified that he had many years ago obtained an amount of approximately £20,000 in compensation for an accident on the farm, that he spent some of it, lent some of it, and deposited some to the Bank of Ireland. No evidence in this regard was tendered; moreover the amount standing to the credit of the Bank of Ireland account was greatly in excess of any balance of the compensation moneys referred to.

(n) The Applicant returned to Australia in July 1990. He rented a unit in the suburb previously referred to and obtained employment as a plumber. After his return to Australia, he brought an amount of approximately $400,000 back from Ireland. He also dug up the money previously buried in the grounds of the Whiteacre unit and then deposited it (in deposits of approximately $9,000 each) in at least four of his various bank accounts.

(o) Since his return from Ireland the Applicant has purchased residential properties in (Blackacre) (for $209,000) and (Greeenacre) (for $283,000), in each case for a substantial amount, and in each case with moneys available to him, and in other words without the need for a mortgage. Specifically, in respect of the Greenacre property, he has embarked on substantial alterations costing some $50,000.

(p) In 1991 the Applicant commenced work for a plumbing contractor but after less than 2 years was dismissed because, as he put it, he wasn’t sufficiently productive. He then worked for another plumber for about 6 months finishing in November 1995.

(q) An amount of $313,000 was paid to the Respondent out of the Applicant’s St George bank account in consequence of a notice under section 218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act. The Applicant said that he had applied for but was refused a pension (although the nature and grounds of application were not specified) and currently receives a sickness benefit of $300 per fortnight.

(r) The Applicant said on a number of occasions that he does not care about money; however, he plainly resents with great bitterness the taking by the Respondent of the amount of $313,000 in consequence of the section 218 notice. The Applicant regards the Respondent as an office bent on persecuting him.

  1. The Applicant’s belief that the Tax Office is persecuting him, is not by any means the only instance of his belief that he is in somehow and in all circumstances a victim. Specifically in this regard:

(a) During the course of cross-examination, he accused Counsel for the Respondent of being corrupt.

(b) As set out previously, he has said in the first statement that he considers that his previous solicitor was in collusion with the Respondent and thus also, and for this reason, dishonest.

(c) The second statement contains as Annexure “D” a brief record as to three charges laid against him and in respect of which a certain police detective (Jones) was involved. These charges involved the fraudulent use of a Mastercard in the name of Black and an assault on a shop assistant. The charges were dismissed for want of an appearance by the informant. The alleged use by him of a Black credit card must of necessity be considered in the light of the Mastercard records dealt with later in these Reasons. However, and since the changes were dismissed it appears to the Tribunal that it is fair to treat this evidence as having little weight. The allegations of perjury and “structuring a story against me” made against the detective in question, while in character for the Applicant, cannot be treated as having any basis in fact.

  1. Exhibit R6 is a very large file involving Mastercard. It is unnecessary in the circumstances for the Tribunal to set out an exhaustive analysis of this file. However, and in this context Exhibit R6 reveals:

(a) Mastercard accounts were opened by Christopher White and Christopher Black; each of these two accounts bears “very similar information:

(1) same date of birth;

The Mastercard file also refers to debit transactions which took place in the United States of America.

(d) The Mastercard file records also that “The Black (last four digits 2452) was opened July 1991 and was reported lost 16-9-91 - this time a mother’s maiden name of O’Neill was supplied”. Also the application shows the next of kin (father) lives in Dublin which is where the sales on the White MCD came from. The (Black) application states that the employer is John (White) who is really one and the same person.”

(e) It would be possible (but it appears to me to be unnecessary to do so) to go into far more detail in respect of Exhibit R6, which indicates that substantial debit balances were run up on the Mastercards after they had been reported lost.

(f) The Applicant denied that he had given notice of the loss of his card.

  1. There are aspects of the relationship between White and Black which are decidedly bizarre. In this specific context:

(a) Exhibit R5 indicates that the Applicant took out two separate driver’s licences both involving the annexation of a photograph of him. He said that he had given one of those driver’s licences to Black; he indicated that he had done so as a “dare”.

(b) The evidence of both Miss Brown and Green was that Black is a man of about 5ft 10ins in height, that he is bulky and that he is in appearance decidedly different from the Applicant. It must follow then that to give someone else of entirely different appearance one’s own driver’s licence cannot assist the recipient since the production of it would immediately indicate that the holder of that licence is not the person who obtained it.

(c) Each of Miss Brown and Green testified, as set out previously, as to the separate existence of a separate person named Black although neither of them knew him very well. Green characterised Black as a person whom he did not like because he was taciturn.

(d) Specifically in respect of Miss Brown:

(1) The Applicant met her many years ago after he first came to Australia and at church; a relationship developed and that relationship turned into an intimate one and lasted as an intimate relationship for some years; after the intimate relationship ended, Miss Brown and the Applicant continued to be close friends (even after the Applicant’s recent marriage) and indeed Miss Brown has, as the daughter of an apparently wealthy retired businessman, advanced substantial sums to the Applicant; the Applicant said that the advances do not bear interest, whereas Miss Brown said that they do carry interest.

(2) Miss Brown gave evidence that it was she who phoned MLC to ask MLC for copies of the three cheques which are Annexure “A” to the second statement. She said that in answer to her telephone call copies of the cheques were sent. That statement was contradicted by the fact that in order to obtain those cheques it was necessary for the Applicant’s solicitor to issue a summons and it was in answer to that summons, as the Tribunal records disclose, that the cheques were made available. Indeed, the Tribunal finds it hard to believe that MLC would in answer to a telephonic request from a stranger, send confidential information without authority from the account-holder.

(e) The evidence of Green was very short and to the point; he is an unemployed builder’s labourer, also Irish, who associated with the Applicant from time to time.

  1. During the course of his evidence, the Applicant gave other indications of the fact that he believes that he is a victim. He said during the course of his evidence, that Australians hate the Irish, and notwithstanding the fact that, as he must know, a not inconsiderable section of the population of Australia has Irish ancestry.
  2. Exhibit R4 was produced by the Respondent, apparently to demonstrate that the address given in respect of the Applicant on Exhibit R4 is the same as an address given for Black. The Tribunal does not, however, think that anything very much turns on this aspect given that the Applicant has stated that Black did stay with him on various occasions.
  3. The Applicant’s demeanour during his evidence was unsatisfactory and his evidence was vague. He often replied by stating that he could not remember or gave answers which were qualified by words of or to the effect of “to the best of knowledge” when the facts must have been within his own knowledge. As set out previously he was abusive towards the Respondent’s Counsel. The Applicant could not, by any stretch of the imagination, be described as a credible witness, and it follows that his evidence in respect of the Black/MLC matter cannot be accepted. More specifically in this context:

(a) The evidence of the Applicant in particular as regards the burial of the money is simply bizarre and unacceptable. The Tribunal has been asked to believe that the Applicant having sold the Whiteacre unit (and at or about the time of completion of the sale), nevertheless buried a very large sum of money in notes in the grounds in which the Whiteacre unit was situated. It must be remembered that the Whiteacre unit had been sold and that burial of this substantial amount of money is alleged to have taken place at or about the time of completion of sale. Moreover, the Applicant’s evidence was that he was then about to return to Ireland and that his return might be permanent. The story is so inherently improbable that it is not necessary to canvas various aspects of this evidence; by way of example: What if an inquisitive inhabitant of the block of units had watched him in the act of digging the hole and burying the money? What efforts were made or precautions taken to guard against damage from the elements and particularly a heavy down-pour of rain? And in what manner was the precise location of the burial site demarcated for later identification?

(b) The evidence of the Applicant in general as to the whole Black/MLC matter is unacceptable. The Tribunal was asked to believe that it was Black (a separate person) who invested the moneys with the predecessor to MLC, that MLC issued cheques in favour of a third party recipient (although there was no evidence as to any specific authority from Black) and that the aggregate of the amounts paid by Black in this fashion by some curious coincidence amounted more or less to the aggregate amount to which the inheritance in Ireland had grown. All of this is simply incredible; it is equally incredible that Black should have needed White for this purpose in order to transfer moneys out of Australia; there was in 1990 no legal reason why Black should not have been able to direct his bankers to transfer his own moneys to Ireland. Moreover, and in the same context, investments in the MLC account matched withdrawals from the Applicant’s bank accounts. There is only one possible logical conclusion; the MLC account (and all amounts standing to its credit) was the sole property of the Applicant, and the Applicant was entitled to deal with it as he pleased, and indeed he did so.

Somewhat reluctantly he admitted that both of them had a birth date in May, although he said that the exact dates differed. Once again the inescapable conclusion is that if there was a separate Black persona, (and there was evidence before me that there was) the Applicant used the Black identity as his own and in particular he used it for the purpose of the MLC matter. All of these circumstances surrounding the lost Mastercard cards and the debits in the Philippines, the United States and Ireland inevitably leads the Tribunal to the view that at least in relation to the credit cards, as also in relation to the Black/MLC matter, White and Black were one and the same person.
  2. A recent decision by Lockhart J in the Federal Court of Australia in the matter of Commissioner of Taxation v Roger Gareth Munro, Federal Court, 24 October 1997, NG 983/1996, reversing a decision of this Tribunal, is instructive as to the principles relating to onus of proof, which are set out at pages 14, 15 and 16 as follows:
“Principles relating to onus of proof

The taxpayer has the burden of proving that the amended assessment is excessive (s14ZZK(b)(i) of the Taxation Administration Act). That section is expressed in substantially the same language as s190(b) of the Assessment Act which was repealed by Taxation Laws Amendment Act (No.3) 1991 (Cth).

In McCormack v Commission of Taxation [1979] HCA 18; (1979) 143 CLR 284 Gibbs J said that s190(b) of the Assessment Act placed the burden of proving that the assessment was excessive upon the taxpayer and that on ordinary principles the taxpayer therefore bore the burden of proving the facts necessary to make out his or her case (at 301). To discharge the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive the taxpayer in McCormack (a s26(a) case) was required to prove affirmatively on the balance of probabilities that the property was not required for the purpose of profit making by sale. His Honour said that the burden may be discharged by drawing inferences from the evidence, but he rejected (at 303) the argument that a taxpayer can succeed simply because there is no evidence from which it can be concluded that the relevant purpose of profit making existed; since this would mean that the burden of proving the existence of that purpose lies on the Commission. That would, his Honour, said, be to invert the onus of proof. His Honour also said at 303:

“The taxpayer will succeed if the proper inference from the evidence is that the property was not acquired for the relevant purpose, but if there is no evidence as to the purpose for which the taxpayer acquired the property, the appeal must fail.”

Gibbs J agreed with the statement of Mason J a few years earlier in his dissenting judgment in Gauci v Federal Commission of Taxation [1975] HCA 54; (1975) 135 CLR 81 at 89-90 in these terms:

“The Act does not place any onus on the Commissioner to show that the assessments were correctly made. Nor is there any statutory requirement that the assessment should be sustained or supported by evidence. The implication of such a requirement would be inconsistent with s190(b) for it is a consequence of that provision that unless the appellant shows by evidence the assessment is incorrect, it will prevail .. The crux of the matter is that when in a s26(a) case an appellant seeks to overcome the onus created by s190(b) by adducing evidence as to his intentions with a view to establishing the purpose of the acquisition was not a s26(a) purpose and that evidence is not accepted, he has not discharged the onus which he bears. At best, from the appellant’s viewpoint, the evidence stands in a situation in which it is equivocal, neither establishing a s26(a) purpose nor denying the existence of such a purpose. At worst, the judge may, in the circumstances, be able to infer the existence of a s26(a) purpose. In either event the appellant fails to discharge the onus and the appeal fails.”

Stephen J agreed with what was said on this matter by Gibbs J and he too endorsed the statement of Mason J in Gauci (at 306).

In MacMine Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1979) 53 ALJR 362 Gibbs J confirmed the views expressed in McCormack by him about the onus of proof which lies upon a taxpayer by virtue of s190(b) of the Assessment Act (at 366). Stephen J confirmed his agreement in McCormack with what Gibbs J said on the subject of s190(b) (at 371).

Murphy J said (at 378) that s190(b) places on the taxpayer the burden of showing that the assessment is excessive and therefore that the circumstances giving rise to liability to such assessment did not exist. His Honour said that:

“The plain intention of Parliament is to raise a presumption of the existence of the circumstances which attract liability to the tax assessed.”

In McCauley v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 88 ATC 4,605 , Lockhart J at 4,613 stated:

“It is for the taxpayer to prove that the assessment is excessive and the onus lies upon the taxpayer to prove that funds to acquire the assets in question were not derived from undisclosed income. The source of these funds is peculiarly within the knowledge of the taxpayer.”

In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dalco 90 ATC 4,088 the views expressed in McCormack’s Case were followed by the Full Court of the High Court. Brennan J said at 4,093 that ordinarily:

“The Commissioner is entitled to rely upon any deficiency in proof in the excessiveness of the amount assessed to uphold the assessment.”
