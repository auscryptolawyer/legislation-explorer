GHI and Commissioner of Taxation [1997] AATA 834; 36 ATR 1053; 97 ATC 2099 (16 June 1997)

Last Updated: 7 May 2009





Administrative

Appeals

Tribunal



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION NO 11950

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No QT95/162

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re GHI

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

FINDINGS OF FACT

Tribunal Deputy President D.P. Breen, Presidential Member

Mr. D.W. Muller, Senior Member

Dr. E.K. Christie, Member



Date 16 June 1997

Place Brisbane

Findings:      

  1. The sum of $2,251,637 was incurred by GHI in the 1993 income tax year in Supreme Court and in Federal Court proceedings to resist a partial takeover by Freds.
  2. The Board of Directors of GHI expected that the partial takeover would proceed but that the proposed merger would not.
  3. The Board of Directors of GHI decided to defend the takeover by whatever means were available to it.
  4. The Board of Directors of GHI were concerned that if the takeover proceeded, the cost of trading stock to GHI would rise because the rebates to which it had been entitled would cease to be available. This would have had an immediate effect on profitability. It would diminish income. The continued availability of rebates, volume rebates and promotional rebates was a matter of crucial concern to the Board of Directors.
  5. If profitability had been adversely affected there would have automatically been an adverse effect on the future viability of the company, like any business operation, but the immediate concern of the Board of Directors was the maintenance of the income of GHI.
  6. The decision to resist the takeover by Freds was not motivated by a desire of the Board of Directors to stall the takeover to ensure the price to the vendor shareholders was maximised. GHI was to be a continuing company and the Directors were concerned about what would happen to it as it continued. There has never been any suggestion put to the Directors that the price offered by Freds was inadequate.
  7. The Board of Directors of GHI did not initiate proceedings in the Supreme Court and the Federal Court of Australia to ventilate the issues that arise under section 50 of the Trade Practices Act 1974 as a matter of academic interest, nor to preserve any public interest in having section 50 of the Trade Practices Act 1974 complied with. It was to stop a partial takeover because of the consequences they envisaged would flow from that partial takeover.
  8. GHI was never an innocent bystander dragged into proceedings by the Attorney-General.

(Sgd) DP BREEN

PRESIDENTIAL MEMBER

CATCHWORDS

TAXATION - decision of Commissioner of Taxation of 31 July 1997 disallowing amount incurred in legal action to be deducted under subsection 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - whether the legal costs were incurred out of academic interest or to protect public interest or whether they were incurred to oppose a takeover bid.

REASONS FOR FINDINGS

16 June 1997 Deputy President DP Breen

Mr DW Muller, Senior Member

Dr EK Christie, Member



  1. This is an application to review the objection decision of the Commissioner of Taxation, dated 31 July 1995, which disallowed a deduction for legal expenses of $2,251,637 on the ground that “the expenses claimed were expended in defending against a takeover bid and are therefore not claimable under the general principles set out in IT 2656”.
  2. At the hearing the applicant (GHI) was represented by Mr. Russell, QC, and the respondent was represented by Mr. Gotterson, QC, with Mr. Applegarth of Counsel.
  3. During the course of the hearing, agreement was reached between the parties and the Tribunal that the following procedure would be followed:
    • (1) The Tribunal would make the findings as to primary fact and then publish its findings;
    • (2) The parties would then co-operate in the drawing and settling of a case stated to a Full Federal Court.
    • (3) The questions which the parties envisaged the case stated would ask for the opinion of the Full Federal Court were:
      • (i) Is the sum of $2,251,637 for legal expenses incurred by the applicant in the Federal Court proceedings referred to in paragraph 13 of the statement of agreed facts (exhibit 3) in the 1993 income tax year, an allowable deduction pursuant to section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.
      • (ii) Or, does the determination of that question depend on further findings of fact, and if so, what findings of fact?
    • (4) Once the parties were satisfied with the form of the case stated, they would submit it to the Tribunal for its approval of the form.
    • (5) Once the Tribunal’s approval was secured, the case stated would then be filed in the Federal Court of Australia.
  4. The following facts are not in dispute between the parties:
    • (1) At all material times GHI was a company listed on the Australian Stock Exchange Limited.
    • (2) At all material times GHI and its wholly owned subsidiaries carried on the business of wholesaling and retailing grocery products in Queensland and northern New South Wales.
    • (3) On 28 May 1992 Freds Holdings Pty Limited (Freds) lodged a purported Part A Statement with The Australian Securities Commission. GHI was to be the target company of a takeover offer by Freds.
    • (4) GHI instituted proceedings in the Queensland Supreme Court seeking (briefly):
      • (i) A declaration that the statement was not a Part A Statement because it did not comply with certain provisions of the Corporations Law.
      • (ii) A declaration that the dispatch of the document would constitute misleading and deceptive conduct.
      • (iii) An order restraining Freds from dispatching the statement to any member of GHI.
    • (5) On 12 June 1992 White J granted an interlocutory injunction, restraining the dispatch of the Statement.
    • (6) On 17 June 1992 a new purported Part A Statement was lodged with the Australian Securities Commission. It was registered by the Australian Securities Commission on 18 June 1992.
    • (7) On 29 June 1992 GHI commenced proceedings number QG3012/1992 in the Federal Court of Australia against Freds and nine subsidiaries of Freds (the “GHI Action”). The relief GHI sought in the Federal Court action included:
      • (i) that the acquisition of shares by Freds in GHI would involve a contravention of s 50 of the Trade Practices Act 1974;
      • (ii) that there was a triable issue in relation to non-compliance with s. 750 clauses 17, 20(1) and 20(2) of the Corporations Law; and
      • (iii) there had been misleading and deceptive conduct on the part of Freds pursuant to s 995 of the Corporations Law.
    • (8) On 3 July 1992 Heerey J of the Federal Court of Australia ordered in the GHI Action that Freds be restrained until judgment or earlier Order from sending or dispatching to any member of GHI the document lodged with the Australian Securities Commission on 17 June 1992 and from sending or dispatching any offers to any members of GHI under or pursuant to that purported Part A Statement as it was arguable that the purported Part A Statement did not contain material information as required pursuant to s. 750 of the Corporations Law.
    • (9) On 22 July 1992 Cooper J. of the Federal Court of Australia heard a Motion by Freds’ subsidiaries to strike out parts of the GHI application which sought injunctions requiring Freds’ subsidiaries to lodge annual returns in the prescribed form.
    • (10) During mid-1992 GHI made representations to the Attorney-General of the Commonwealth regarding the proposed takeover by Freds, including representations that the Attorney-General should commence proceedings against Freds in the Federal Court of Australia for injunctions to restrain Freds from acquiring a majority of the issued shares of GHI. The Attorney-General was responsible for the administration of the Trade Practices Act 1974.
    • (11) On 13 August 1992 the Attorney-General of the Commonwealth commenced proceedings number NG575/1992 in the Federal Court of Australia seeking an injunction restraining Freds from acquiring or offering to acquire directly or indirectly any shares in or assets of GHI (the A-G Action).
    • (12) In August 1992 the Attorney-General applied for and was granted an injunction until trial preventing Freds from acquiring or seeking to acquire any further shares in GHI. GHI being the target company was a necessary formal participant in the proceedings. GHI was also made a respondent because of the company’s interest in the matter, including the fact that it was at the time also seeking relief in the Federal Court of Australia in the GHI Action in respect of Freds’ takeover. Throughout the conduct of the litigation GHI exhibited a greater than formal interest in the outcome of the matter.
    • (13) In October and November 1992 there were a number of interlocutory proceedings.

7.10.92 - Directions Hearing before Cooper J.

20.10.92 - Directions Hearing before Cooper J.

30.10.92 - Directions Hearing before Cooper J.

4.11.92 - Discovery application before Cooper J.

9.11.92 - Return of subpoenas before Spender J.



(14) The GHI Action and the A-G Action were case managed by the Federal Court of Australia by way of directions hearings so that both actions were heard together. A large body of affidavit evidence was filed in the proceedings. The evidence filed by GHI included affidavits sworn by its senior executives. These included an affidavit by its Chief Executive Officer, sworn on 15 October 1992, affidavits by the Financial Controller of GHI, sworn on 30 June 1992 and 15 October 1992 and affidavits by the Marketing Manager of GHI, sworn on 15 October 1992 and 5 November 1992.

(15) In conducting the case for the Attorney-General and in making submissions, the Attorney-General’s legal representatives relied upon affidavits filed by GHI. There was also material including affidavits from retailers and two economists.

(16) The affidavit material filed and relied upon by GHI in both matters was directed to defining the market in which a merged GHI/Freds entity would operate in the event of a Freds takeover scheme proceeding and whether such a merged entity would be, or be likely to be, in a position to dominate that market.

(17) The evidence relied upon by GHI in the GHI Action and GHI in the A-G Action was directed to establishing that a merged GHI/Freds operation would be, or would be likely to be, in a position to dominate a market to the detriment of the “consumers” of its facilities and services.

(18) The case was conducted by GHI on the basis that in the event that Freds was permitted to proceed with its takeover offer, and that offer was successful, the merged entity would be the only wholesaler of groceries to independent retailers in Queensland and northern New South Wales. The case was conducted on the basis that if the takeover succeeded, all of GHI’s customers, and all of Freds’ customers in Queensland and northern New South Wales, would be exposed to the potentially adverse effects of the pricing policy of the merged entity, which would be in a dominant position in the market.

(19) The case was conducted on the basis that if Freds was successful in its takeover offer of GHI, then the merger would be effected by GHI purchasing the business operation of Freds, as proposed in clause 12.1 of Freds’ Part A Statement. Freds’ takeover scheme contemplated that after the merger GHI’s business would continue and that the merged entity would service both GHI’s existing customers and additional customers. It did not contemplate that GHI and Freds’ Queensland operation would remain as competitors in Queensland. Instead, it contemplated a merged operation being the only wholesaler of groceries to independent retailers in Queensland and northern New South Wales. It also contemplated that the merged operation would be in a strong position to resist entry into the market even from an established wholesaler through its ability to cut prices on a retaliatory basis.

(20) The proceedings were not concerned with the rights or interests of the minority shareholders in GHI following a takeover, but were concerned with preventing such a takeover because of its effect upon the market in which the merged GHI/Freds operation would operate in Queensland and northern New South Wales. Both GHI and the Attorney-General submitted that such a takeover would be a contravention of s.50 of the Trade Practices Act 1974.

(21) Between 11 and 19 November 1992 the trial was conducted before Spender J in the Federal Court of Australia. The proceedings which occurred thereafter can be conveniently summarised as follows:

20.11.92 to - Trial before Spender J.

(22) On 30 April 1993 Spender J. in the Federal Court of Australia gave reasons for judgment in the GHI and A-G Actions. According to those reasons, Freds’ takeover scheme, if implemented, would have created a merged entity which would have been in a position to have “a commanding influence on its retail customers”. According to His Honour the takeover scheme contravened s.50 of the Trade Practices Act 1974. His Honour made Orders:
  • (i) declaring the acquisition by Freds as proposed in the Freds takeover scheme would be a contravention of s.50 of the Trade Practices Act 1974;
  • (ii) that Freds be restrained by itself, its servants or agents, or otherwise, from directly or indirectly acquiring the majority of the issued shares of GHI.


(23) On 22 April 1994 the Full Court of the Federal Court of Australia dismissed an appeal by Freds from the judgment of Spender J.

(24) In the 1993 income tax year GHI incurred legal expenses of $2,729,893. The breakup of this amount was as follows:

Amendment to articles $ 4,115

Share Option Plan $ 339

Change of Name $ 1,027

Takeover Defence costs $ 472,775

GHI and the A-G Action $2,251,637



(25) The sum representing the applicant’s action and A-G Action comprised:

Professional Fees of Solicitors $1,219,339

Counsel’s fees $ 747,268

Outlays $ 285,030



(26) In a letter to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, Brisbane, dated 23 March 1995, GHI made a valid objection request against the assessment for the income year ended 30 June 1993, asking for the assessment to be amended to allow a deduction for the above legal expenses amount pursuant to sub-section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

(27) The Deputy Commissioner of Taxation denied the objection request in a letter to GHI dated 31 July 1995 stating that “the expenses were expended in defending against a takeover bid and are therefore not claimable under the general principles set out in IT 2656”.
  1. The Tribunal had the following material placed before it:
    • (1) The section 37 statement.
    • (2) A chronology of events leading to the dispute.
    • (3) A statement of agreed facts.
    • (4) Affidavit plus oral evidence from a former Company Director of GHI.
    • (5) Affidavits plus oral evidence from the Financial Controller of GHI from 1986 to 1995.
    • (6) Affidavits of a Barrister-at-Law, Acting Principal Legal Officer in the Office of the Australian Government Solicitor in 1992.
    • (7) The first and second purported Part A Statements.
    • (8) The judgment of White J.
    • (9) The minutes of GHI’s Board of Directors’ meetings held on 1 June 1992, 11 June 1992 and 23 June 1992.
    • (10) The record of proceedings before Spender J in the Federal Court of Australia.
  2. The prospect of GHI being taken over by Freds was of general concern to the Directors of GHI. In particular the following clauses in the Part A Statement caused alarm.

“11.2 Sell down of GHI Shares

If Freds’ Takeover Scheme is successful, it proposes, following completion of its Takeover Scheme, to dispose of GHI Shares to which it becomes entitled in excess of approximately 55% of GHI to institutional investors.

However, no agreements or arrangements for such disposals have yet been made. If it becomes necessary, Freds will consider how it will dispose of the GHI Shares it does not require following completion of its Takeover Scheme.

Freds’ Intentions

12.1 GHI’s Business and Assets

12.1.1. Merger of GHI and Freds Queensland

Following completion of the Takeover Scheme and subject to requisite shareholder approval, Freds proposes to procure the merger of GHI and Freds Group’s existing Queensland grocery and liquor distribution and cash and carry operations (‘Freds Queensland’). The merger would be effected by GHI purchasing the business operations of Freds Queensland. Freds does not propose to redeploy GHI’s fixed assets.

12.1.3 GHI Shareholder Approval

If GHI remains a listed company at the time of its purchase of Freds Queensland, completion of the purchase of Freds Queensland will be subject to the approval of GHI’s shareholders in general meeting in accordance with the ASX Listing Rules. In particular:

* Freds and its associates would not be permitted to vote at the meeting;

* An independent expert would be appointed by GHI to advise GHI’s shareholders on whether the terms of the purchase of Freds Queensland are fair and reasonable.”

The Directors of GHI accepted that Freds could end up holding 55 per cent of the shares in GHI but they were confident that no merger would take place because they believed that they had enough “friendly” shareholders to prevent Freds’ shareholding going much higher than 55 per cent, certainly not the required 75 per cent. (Freds’ 50 per cent vote could not be counted and consequently there were the practicalities of requiring a further 25 per cent at least to be able to carry a majority at a shareholder meeting.

  1. Mr. K. was the Financial Controller of GHI from 1986 to 1995 and he was a Director of GHI from November 1992 to November 1993. He was able to inform the Tribunal about the circumstances existing in the market place at the time of the takeover bid; the climate in which decisions were made. In his affidavit sworn on 5 December 1995 (exhibit 8) he said (among other things):
    • (1) “On May 29, 1992 Freds announced that it intended to make takeover offers for all of the shares in GHI. When the announcement was made a sub-committee of the members of the board of GHI was formed. The role of that sub-committee was to advise on all matters relevant to the takeover. Outside legal and financial advisors, the Chief Executive of GHI and I attended the meetings of that sub-committee throughout 1992 and early 1993.”
    • (2) “At the time of the takeover offer GHI was operating in Queensland and Northern New South Wales and to a lesser extent in the Northern Territory. Freds had operations in Queensland, New South Wales and Victoria. Freds was a member of the Freds Group of companies which were a complex web of private companies structured through trusts and exempt proprietary companies. Financial information for the group was not made public.”
    • (3) ”In 1985 Freds Group of Companies attempted to takeover GHI but was unsuccessful. After that failed takeover the Freds Group commenced operations in Queensland. There was intense competition between Freds and GHI. That competition exists today.”
    • (4) “In 1992 the Freds Group had a total turnover in the order of $2.3bn whilst GHI had a total turnover of approximately $578m.”
    • (5) “The offer by Freds’ Holdings for GHI was complex. It was for a consideration of $6.75 per GHI share and was subject to a minimum acceptance condition of 50.1%. The offer documentation stated:
      • (i) on acquiring GHI, Freds proposed that GHI buy Freds Queensland operations for an undisclosed sum. The reference to Freds Queensland Operations was not fully defined. At that time GHI understood it to be the operations then conducted by Freds in Queensland. No valuation was provided for the Freds Queensland Operations;
      • (ii) Freds intended to acquire control of GHI. If Freds acquired more than 55% of GHI it intended to sell down to hold no more than 55% of GHI;
      • (iii) Freds intended that GHI continue as a listed company;
      • (iv) the merging of GHI and Freds Queensland would lead to cost savings in the order of $3m-$5m through rationalisation.”
    • (6) “There was a concern in 1992 that what was proposed by Freds would potentially damage GHI’s business. One way in which that could occur is through manipulation of rebates. That is, the sums of money paid by suppliers to grocery wholesalers as volume and co-operative advertising rebates. There was a concern that the volume and co-operative advertising rebates could be manipulated to the advantage of the Freds Group of companies and to the detriment of GHI. It was possible for this to be done in such a way that it would be extremely difficult for GHI to identify what was happening. If Freds was successful with its takeover of GHI it would have led to the following results:-
      • (i) If the acquisition of Freds Queensland Operations was unsuccessful, GHI and Freds Queensland Operations would have remained as competitors in Queensland.
      • (ii) If the acquisition of Freds Queensland was successful, GHI would have operated in competition to Freds New South Wales operations.
      • (iii) It would have been very difficult for GHI to compete as effectively against Freds if Freds was its major shareholder for the following reasons:-

- Freds would effectively control the make up of the GHI board;

- It would be difficult for GHI to negotiate on a confidential basis with its manufacturers/suppliers since the information would be held by both GHI and its only competitor, Freds Queensland. This would put GHI in a disadvantageous position in relation to its negotiation with the manufacturers;

- commercial sensitive information about the market and GHI’s future plans in the market would be held by its only competitor in the market for supplying independent retailers - this would remove any commercial and competitive advantage which GHI had developed;

- GHI’s dealings with its customers would be within the knowledge of its competitor which would render it commercial (sic) impossible for GHI to retain any confidentiality between it and its clients;

- GHI would have difficulty in expanding what was then its program for the development of retailer outlets.”



  1. Mr. K. agreed with the following propositions put by Mr. Gotterson in cross-examination:
    • (1) The context in which the Federal Court proceedings arose were that if Freds implemented its takeover scheme, that would have affected the ownership of shares in GHI.
    • (2) There would have been a new Freds dominated board for GHI.
    • (3) What was going to happen and what everyone at GHI saw happening, was that Freds would use a GHI entity to buy up the business operations of Freds Queensland.
  2. Mr. K attended meetings of the Board of Directors of GHI in June 1992 in which strategies were devised to fight off the Freds bid.
    • (1) On 1 June 1992 a “Task Force” was given the authority “to discuss, recommend and formulate policy relating to the takeover offer received from Freds Holdings”.
    • (2) On 11 June 1992 the Board of GHI “endorsed and ratified all actions taken to date by the Task Force including, but not limited to, the decision to litigate against the Part A offer from Freds and the preparation of a submission to the Trade Practices Commission.”
    • (3) On 23 June 1992 the Board decided to go ahead with proceedings under the Trade Practices legislation.
  3. The Mr. M., Director of GHI in 1992 and Chairman of Directors of GHI as at 6 March 1996, gave evidence to the Tribunal by affidavit and orally. In his affidavit he said:

“The offer announced by Freds was a complex one. Freds did not, pursuant to the terms of the offer, seek to acquire 100% of GHI. Indeed, on the basis of the announcement Freds only wished to acquire no more than 55% of GHI.

The directors of GHI instructed its advisers to take all steps necessary to defend GHI against the proposed acquisition. The main reason for this was that the directors, including myself, were very concerned as to what Freds was likely to do to the particular aspects of the business, particularly rebate income of GHI if its takeover bid was successful. There was a concern that the acquisition of control by Freds of GHI would result in GHI being placed in a position where it would not share in the better prices that might otherwise be available for the acquisition of goods from suppliers. The directors were concerned that Freds might very well aggregate the purchasing power of GHI with the purchasing power of Freds so as to ensure that items were able to be acquired from suppliers at a lower cost per unit. I and my fellow directors were concerned that this may result in the lower cost per unit being made available to the Freds Group of Companies and not to GHI. The directors were also concerned about the possibility that Freds would seek to take from GHI the better aspects of its business and leave behind only the poor quality business. Neither I nor any of the other directors believed that there was any part of the day to day business of GHI which was not likely to be adversely affected by an acquisition of GHI in the terms proposed by Freds.”

  1. In his oral evidence to the Tribunal Mr. M. said that the Board of GHI had decided in 1992 to defend the takeover at any cost. The Board asked the company solicitors “to take whatever action there was to defend it by any means”. He said that one of the means to be used to resist the takeover was to institute the action in the Federal Court of Australia using the Trade Practices legislation. Mr. M. agreed with Mr. Gotterson that there was concern amongst the Board of Directors of GHI that “Freds might pick the eyes out of the GHI business and somehow or other transfer them over to its business, and leave GHI, if not as a dud, as a much depleted company”. Mr. M. said that the Board was confident that although the takeover could be successful (that is the acquisition of 50 to 55 per cent of shares), a merger of the two companies would not succeed (acquisition of 75 per cent of shares) because of a significant number of loyal shareholders. Mr. M. told the Tribunal that the Board accepted the advice of their solicitors that various methods of fighting the takeover were available including the Supreme Court action relating to the Part A statement, the involvement of the Federal Attorney-General and the Trade Practices proceedings. The effect of Mr. M’s evidence was that the whole of the litigation in the Supreme and Federal Courts was a tactic to defeat the takeover. They were not really concerned about breaches of the Corporations Law nor of contravention of the Trade Practices Act 1974.
  2. As part of the defence strategy, Mr. K prepared affidavits for the Federal Court proceedings to the effect that the takeover would result in a reduction in competition in the wholesale grocery market. The following excerpts have been taken from Mr. K’s Federal Court affidavits to illustrate the point.
    • (1) “Broadly speaking, there are two kinds of grocery and foodstuffs retailers in Queensland:-
      • (i) the major chains, comprising Coles, Woolworths and Franklins; and
      • (ii) the independents.”
    • (2) “None of the owners of the independently operated stores (the smaller corner-store type operation) acquire their stock from the major chain warehouses. There are only two major grocery wholesalers who supply stock to the independents (in Queensland) - GHI and the Freds Group. Conversely, neither GHI nor the Freds Group supplies stock to any of the Coles, Woolworths or Franklins stores. There is fierce competition between the three chain operators. There is also fierce competition between GHI and the Freds Group.”
    • (3) “At present, in terms of value, the major chains of retail stores have about 76% of the retail market and the independent retail stores have about 24% of the retail market. On the total retail market GHI supplies about 15% and Freds supplies about 9% of that market. In terms of the independent retail grocery market GHI supplies about 70% and Freds about 30% (excluding distribution to the Fli-Blo chain).”
    • (4) “In addition to competing in Queensland, GHI competes in northern New South Wales with the New South Wales and Queensland operations of the Freds group.”
    • (5) “In 1985, GHI defeated the Freds Group in a takeover battle for the GHI Group (formerly Luckies Wholesale Distributors Limited). Since then, there has been an intense battle between the two groups for retail business. Indeed, it was only following its defeat in that takeover battle that the Freds Group commenced operations in Queensland.”
    • (6) “I find it difficult to envisage any element of the business of GHI which would not be affected if it were in a position where it had to compete with Freds Queensland whilst the Freds Group held a controlling interest in GHI.”
    • (7) “If the takeover of GHI proceeds, the merged GHI/Freds will be the only wholesale supplier to independent retailers in Queensland and Northern New South Wales of a range of groceries of the kinds referred to in Doc. # 3 (which for convenience is referred to below as ‘the market’). In my opinion, it would be extraordinarily difficult for other wholesalers or potential wholesalers to enter the market, either by establishing a warehouse within Queensland or attempting to supply from outside this State. GHI/Freds would be in a strong position to resist entry into the market even from an established wholesaler through its ability to cut prices on a retaliatory basis.”

Findings of Fact

  1. The Tribunal had the opportunity to observe Mr. K and Mr. M give their evidence. Their evidence was not contradicted by any other evidence. There was nothing inherently improbable in what they said. The Tribunal accepts Mr. K and Mr. M as honest and reliable witnesses.
  2. The sum of $2,251,637 was incurred by GHI in the 1993 income tax year in Supreme Court and in Federal Court proceedings to resist a partial takeover by Freds.
  3. The Board of Directors of GHI expected that the partial takeover would proceed but that the proposed merger would not.
  4. The Board of Directors of GHI decided to defend the takeover by whatever means were available to it.
  5. The Board of Directors of GHI were concerned that if the takeover proceeded, the cost of trading stock to GHI would rise because the rebates to which it had been entitled would cease to be available. This would have had an immediate effect on profitability. It would diminish income. The continued availability of rebates, volume rebates and promotional rebates was a matter of crucial concern to the Board of Directors of GHI.
  6. If profitability had been adversely affected there would have automatically been an adverse effect on the future viability of the company, like any business operation, but the immediate concern of the Board of Directors was the maintenance of the income of GHI.
  7. The decision to resist the takeover by Freds was not motivated by a desire of the Board of Directors to stall the takeover to ensure the price to the vendor shareholders was maximised. GHI was to be a continuing company and the Directors were concerned about what would happen to it as it continued. There has never been any suggestion put to the Directors that the price offered by Freds was inadequate.
  8. The Board of Directors did not initiate proceedings in the Supreme Court and the Federal Court of Australia to ventilate the issues that arise under section 50 of the Trade Practices Act 1974 as a matter of academic interest, nor to preserve any public interest in having section 50 of the Trade Practices Act 1974 complied with. It was to stop a partial takeover because of the consequences they envisaged would flow from that partial takeover.
  9. GHI was never an innocent bystander dragged into proceedings by the Attorney-General.
