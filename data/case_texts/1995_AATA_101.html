Solar-Mesh Pty Ltd and Commissioner of Patents [1995] AATA 101 (13 April 1995)

SOLAR-MESH PTY LTD v. COMMISSIONER OF PATENTS

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

S A FORGIE On 12 September, 1994, the applicant, Solar-Mesh Pty Ltd ("Solar-Mesh"), applied for review of a decision of a delegate of the respondent, the Commissioner of Patents ("the Commissioner") dated 29 August, 1994. The delegate's decision was to grant Phifer Wire Products Inc ("Phifer Wire"), Windsor Products Pty Ltd ("Windsor Products") and Cyclone Hardware Pty Ltd ("Cyclone Hardware") an extension of the time within which they could file statements of the grounds of their opposition to the grant of a standard patent to Solar-Mesh and the particulars of each ground.

2. At the hearing, Solar-Mesh was represented by its Managing Director, Mr Kenneth Ivory, and the Commissioner by Mr Hanson of the Australian Government Solicitor's Office. The documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") ("the T documents") were admitted in evidence together with an entry from Mr Ivory's diary for 16 November, 1993, documents relating to Solar-Mesh's products and a letter dated 9 January, 1994 from Messrs Shelston Waters, Patent and Trade Mark Attorneys ("Shelston Waters") to the Tribunal. Oral evidence was given by Mr Ivory and Mr Platt in support of Solar-Mesh's case. No oral evidence was given on behalf of the Commissioner.

THE PARTIES

3. On 19 October, 1994, Phifer Wire, Windsor Products and Cyclone Hardware lodged applications to be made parties to the proceedings. After a hearing on 17 November, 1994, they were joined as parties. At the same time, an order was made on the application of Solar-Mesh to stay the operation or implementation of the decision under review until the determination of the application or until further order. A later request made on behalf of Phifer Wire, Windsor Products and Cyclone Hardware that the proceedings be heard in Sydney was refused.

4. A few days before the application was heard, Shelston Waters advised that their clients, Phifer Wire, Windsor Products and Cyclone Hardware, withdrew from the proceedings. They went on to say that

"... the withdrawal from the appeal proceedings by the opponents

should not be construed as any form of admission, express or implied,

that the appeal by Solar-Mesh Pty Ltd has any merit whatsoever in law

or in fact. The reasons for withdrawing at this stage are essentially

of a commercial nature, and we submit that this should not prejudice

the position of the opponents as far as the present appeal proceedings

are concerned." (letter dated 21 February, 1995)

BACKGROUND

5. I have found various facts to have been established by the written material and I will set those matters out in the following paragraphs together with references to the evidence on which I have based those findings. Some of the material on which I rely appears in more than one place in the T documents as it relates to more than one of the subject areas into which those documents have been divided. Where that occurs, I will give only one reference to it.

6. On 19 May, 1993, Solar-Mesh lodged an Application for a Standard Patent of Addition under the Patents Act 1990 ("the Act") (T documents, pages 18-20). It was requested that the Patent be granted as a Patent of Addition to the Patent applied for on Patent Application No. 60906/86. The application became Patent Application No. 639894. A complete specification was also lodged on 19 May, 1993. In due course and after various steps had been taken in relation to the application, its acceptance was advertised on 12 August, 1993.

7. On 12 November, 1993, Phifer Wire, Windsor Products and Cyclone Hardware ("the opponents") gave notice that they opposed the grant of a patent in respect of Patent Application No. 639894 in the name of Solar-Mesh. They did so under section 59 of the Act by each filing a notice of opposition. Under that section, they could

"... oppose the grant of a standard patent on one or more of the

following grounds, but on no other ground:

(a) that the nominated person is not entitled to a grant of a patent

for the invention;

(b) that the invention is not a patentable invention because it does

not comply with paragraph 18(1)(a) or (b);

(c) that the specification filed in respect of the complete

application does not comply with subsection 40(2) or (3)."

8. In order to oppose the grant of the patent, the opponents had to comply with Chapter 5 of the Patents Regulations ("the Regulations") dealing with opposition generally under the Act. Their notices of opposition had been filed within the time limits set out in sub-regulation 5.3(1) as they had been filed within three months of the publication of the notice of acceptance of the patent application pursuant to section 49. Pursuant to sub-regulation 5.3(7), the opponents were also required to serve a copy of the notice on Solar-Mesh as soon as practicable. Shelston Waters claimed that it had sent copies of the notices of opposition to Solar-Mesh by letter dated 12 November, 1993 (T documents, pages 229-231).

9. On 16 November, 1993, Mr Ivory attended the Commissioner's office and said that he wanted to obtain the certificate for the sealed patent i.e. the Deed of Letters Patent. He spoke with Mrs Pat Butt, who told him that she could seal the Deed either on that day or the next day provided no notice of opposition had been filed. Mrs Butt checked in the office and its computer system and then advised Mr Ivory that no notices of objection had been filed. On re-checking the system, Mrs Butt found that the notices of opposition filed on 12 November, 1993 had been entered on the system. She tried to contact Mr Ivory to advise him of the correct situation and also sent him a facsimile message advising him that the Deed could not be sealed at that time. These findings are consistent with Mr Ivory's oral evidence, his diary note of 16 November, 1993 (Exhibit A) and Mrs Butt's notes (T documents, pages 226 and 227-8).

10. The Commissioner's office then sent three letters to "Australian Solar-Mesh Sales Pty Ltd" dated 19 November, 1993. Those letters advised that the opponents had lodged notices of opposition under section 59 of the Act and that they would be advertised in the Official Journal on 9 December, 1993. The letter asked for advice as to the date of service of the notices of objection.

11. On 22 November, 1993, Mrs Butt sent a facsimile message to Mr Ivory in relation to Patent Application No. 639894. She advised him that the specification was being prepared but that the fee of $90 was still payable before it could be forwarded (T documents, pages 238-240).

12. Mr Ivory responded to this letter on behalf of Solar-Mesh on 21 December, 1993. Apart from enclosing the fee, he dealt with the address to which the Commissioner had written when he said:

"... on most letters we get from the Patent Office they still address

instead

(T documents, page 249)

14. In response to the Commissioner's letter of 19 November, 1993, Mr Ivory wrote on 23 December, 1993. He stated that Solar-Mesh had not been served with the notices of opposition as claimed by the opponents. In view of the opponents' failure, Mr Ivory asked that the Deed be sealed forthwith (T documents, pages 244-249).

In support of his argument, Mr Ivory referred to incidents which he said had occurred in the past. Those incidents related to claims, which Mr Ivory believed to be false, that one of the opponents had prior art.

CONSIDERATION

43. It was agreed between the parties that the Statements of Grounds and Particulars had not been served within the time permitted under sub-regulation 5.4(1). The question whether the notice of opposition had been served on Solar-Mesh was not an issue at the hearing. I am, in any event, satisfied on the basis of the evidence as it stood at the hearing that it had been served.

44. The issue I must decide is whether the opponents are entitled to an extension of the time allowed under that sub-regulation. Section 223 of the Act provides that an extension of time may be given in certain circumstances. Sub-section 223(1) relates to the situations in which the Commissioner has made an error or omission and provides:

"Where, because of an error or omission by the Commissioner, a Deputy

Commissioner or an employee, a relevant act that is required to be

done within a certain time is not, or cannot be, done within that

time, the Commissioner must extend the time for doing the act."

45. Sub-section 223(2) relates to the errors or omissions of those other than the Commissioner or to certain circumstances and is relevant in this case. It provides:

"(2) Where, because of:

(a) an error or omission by the person concerned or by his or her

agent or attorney; or

(b) circumstances beyond the control of the person concerned;

a relevant act that is required to be done within a certain time is

not, or cannot be, done within that time, the Commissioner may, on

application made by the person concerned in accordance with the

regulations, extend the time for doing the act."

46. Sub-section 223(11) provides that

"'relevant act' means an action (other than a prescribed action) in

relation to a patent, a patent application, or any proceedings under

this Act (other than court proceedings), and includes the making of a

Convention application within the time allowed for making such

applications."

47. Sub-regulation 22.11(3) provides that:

"For the purposes of the definition of 'relevant act' in sub-section

223(11) of the Act, each of the following actions is prescribed:

(a) an action or step prescribed in Chapter 5 ('opposition'), not

being an action or step taken under regulation 5.3 ('filing of

opposition') or paragraph 5.4(1)(a) ('filing of statement');

(b) paying a fee in a period that is extended because of subregulation

13.3(3) ('prescribed period: continuation fees') or subregulation

13.6(4) ('time for payment of renewal fee for standard patent')."

48. In this case the step or action which has not been carried out in the time allowed under the Act or Regulations is the service of the Statement of Grounds and Particulars following the opponents' filing their notices of opposition. As the service of the Statements of Grounds and Particulars is a step or action required under paragraph 5.4(1)(a) it is not a prescribed action within the meaning of sub-regulation 22.11(3) and so is a relevant act within the meaning of sub-section 224(11). Consequently, the Commissioner has the power to grant an extension in relation to that step or action provided the criteria set out in section 223 are met.

49. In support of his contention that the opponents should not have been granted an extension of time, Mr Ivory put forward six main arguments. They were that the opponents could not have had prior art, the opponents had filed their Statements of Grounds and Particulars late, no objections were revealed in Mrs Butt's search of 16 November, 1993, the opponents' sole reason for lodging the notices of opposition was to evade the consequences of their having breached the Solar-Mesh trade mark, the Statements of Grounds and Particulars had still not been served and Phifer Wire was not a company registered in Australia.

50. I will start with Mr Ivory's second ground i.e. that the opponents have filed, and presumably served, their Statements of Grounds and Particulars late. As I find on the basis of the Commissioner's date stamps on the documents, that the notices of opposition had been filed on 12 November, 1993, the opponents had until 12 February, 1994 in which to serve their Statements of Grounds and Particulars on Solar-Mesh. As 12 February, 1994 fell on a Saturday, they had until the following Monday (14 February, 1994) by virtue of sub-section 36(2) of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901.

51. The manner in which service is effected under the Act and Regulations is dealt with in section 221 which provides:

"Where this Act provides for a document to be served on, or given or

sent to, a person and the person has given the Commissioner an address

in Australia for service of documents, the document may be served on,

or given or sent to, the person by post to that address."

52. There is nothing in the Act that suggests that the address must be a residential or business address and it would appear that a post office box is acceptable. That interpretation certainly would seem to accord with the Commissioner's practice as revealed by the documents and his acceptance of post office box addresses in the past (see, for example, paragraph 26 above). It also accords with the conclusion reached by Master Lee, as he then was , in a different context in Her Majesty's Theatre Pty Ltd v Starstruck Pty Ltd (1982) 6 ACLR 535.

53. The evidence as to the service is not entirely clear. I am satisfied that Shelston Waters sent the Express Post letter on 10 February, 1994. I am also satisfied from the photocopy of the envelope that they addressed it to Solar-Mesh at its post office box address at Archerfield. On the evidence of "Cheryl" and Express Post's Delivery Record, I find that the letter reached the Archerfield Post Office on 11 February, 1994. I also accept, however, the evidence of Mr Ivory, supported as it is by the evidence of Ms Eyles and also by the file note of Mr Herald made on 24 February, 1994, that the Archerfield Post Office did not place it in his post box until 16 February, 1994. The end result is that the letter was not properly served in this case as it was not placed in the post office box.

54. If that were all that were involved in this case, I would conclude at this point that the opponents' failure to serve Solar-Mesh with the Statements of Grounds and Particulars was due to circumstances beyond their control or that of their attorneys, Shelston Waters. Shelston Waters sent the letter on 10 February, 1994 which was, in the age of express delivery, quite sufficient time to get the documents to the post office box by 14 February, 1994. That is so even though they could not have sent them until after 14:49 on 10 February, 1994 as that is the date on the top of the printout from the Patent Administration System. The fault did not lie with them but with Australia Post over whom they had no control. Therefore, it could be concluded that the opponents were unable to serve their Statements under paragraph 5.4(1)(a) of the Regulations within the time allowed because of circumstances beyond their control.

55. Before that conclusion can be reached there is, however, another aspect to consider. Even if the package had been delivered to the post office box, the covering letters were addressed to "Australian Solar-Mesh Pty Ltd" and not to Solar-Mesh Pty Ltd, which had some time before, become the applicant for the patent. Mr Ivory has argued that, as the covering letter was not addressed to Solar-Mesh, he had no right to look at it at all. He could not look at the contents, he said, and would have been committing an unlawful act had he done so.

56. The Statements of Grounds and Particulars were correctly headed with Solar-Mesh's name. The letter was, according to the Commissioner's computerised Patent Administration System, headed with the proper address for service even if it did not bear Solar-Mesh's name. Its reference at the heading of the text of the letter clearly showed that it referred to the patent application of Solar-Mesh as did the enclosed Statements of Grounds and Particulars. When regard is had to all of those matters and the fact that the envelope was addressed to Solar-Mesh and not to Australian Solar-Mesh Pty Ltd, I find that the letter and its contents were indeed sent to Solar-Mesh at its address for service. The fact that the name of Solar-Mesh, as the entity being served, was omitted from the address at the top of the covering letter does not detract from that conclusion. Quite apart from the terms of the covering letter, the Statements of Grounds and Particulars which were enclosed were patently directed to Solar-Mesh as was the text of the covering letter. The fact that the name "Australian Solar-Mesh Pty Ltd" appeared in the address at the top does not detract from that conclusion for it is part of the address for service shown on the Commissioner's Patent Administration System.

57. If that address for service is the incorrect address for service, that is a circumstance beyond the control of the opponents. They have simply followed the information given in the Commissioner's records.

58. It is relevant to ask whether the address for service is incorrect for if it is and it is incorrect because of an error in the Commissioner's office, then it may be open to argue that service could not be effected within the time allowed in any event. If that argument were accepted then the Commissioner would be obliged to extend the time for service under sub-section 223(1).

59. In all of the material, I have found two occasions on which Solar-Mesh communicated with the Commissioner's office regarding the way in which material was addressed to it. The first was in December, 1993 when it wrote to say that most letters were addressed to Australian Solar-Mesh Sales Pty Ltd and asked that the computer be changed to delete all reference to that company. The second occasion was on 14 March, 1994 and so after the date with which I am currently concerned. The copy of the complete specification for a standard patent shows various changes of address for service, none of which seems to mirror precisely the address for service shown on the computer record (see T documents, page 18).

60. In the absence of the whole of the Commissioner's file and any other relevant evidence from Mr Ivory, none of which I require, I am unable to find whether Solar-Mesh has or has not changed its address for service or whether the Commissioner has or has not recorded any such change correctly.

61. That I cannot reach a conclusion on that issue simply means that I cannot find that the opponents' failure is due to an error or omission of the Commissioner. There remains my finding that the opponents have been unable to serve the Statements of Grounds and Particulars through circumstances beyond their control. That means that I should consider whether the discretion under paragraph 223(2)(b) should be exercised.

62. Guidance as to the circumstances in which it is appropriate to exercise the discretion is found in Vangedal-Nielsen and Others v Smith (Commissioner of Patents) and Another [1980] FCA 163; (1980) 33 ALR 144 (Bowen CJ). Sub-section 59(1) of the Patents Act 1952 ("the 1952 Act") provided that a person might lodge a notice of opposition to the grant of a patent. He or she had three months from the date of acceptance, or such further period not exceeding three months as the Commissioner allowed, in which to lodge the notice of objection. Bowen CJ said:

"... it will be noted that no express guidance is afforded by s.59(1)

of the basis on which the extension of time is to be allowed or

disallowed. Where this is so, one has to consider the purpose and

scope of the provision in order to determine what are relevant or

irrelevant considerations in dealing with an application for an

extension (Water Conservation and Irrigation Commission (NSW) v

Browning [1947] HCA 21; (1947) 74 CLR 492 at 496 and 504; Administrative Decisions

(Judicial Review) Act 1977 s5).

The right to lodge a notice of opposition within three months is

clearly given mainly in the interests of the person wishing to oppose

a patent on any one or more of the grounds stated in s59(1). No doubt

there is also a public interest involved in ensuring that worthless

patents are not granted because insufficient opportunity has been

afforded of raising those grounds (Kaiser Aluminium and Chemical

Corporation v Reynolds Metals Co [1969] HCA 7; (1969) 120 CLR 136). Three months

has been considered by Parliament to be an appropriate period to allow

for this. But it has been recognized that cases may occur where, for

one reason or another, three months may prove insufficient.

Accordingly, it has been provided that further time may be allowed not

exceeding a further three months. The Commissioner is interposed as

the arbiter whether such an extension should be allowed and how long

it should be. Clearly, the Commissioner will have to consider the

interests of the prospective opponent who, for some good reason, has

not been able to mount his opposition within the initial period of

three months. The Commissioner will further have to have in mind,

where a serious opposition is foreshadowed, the public interest which

has been mentioned, but he will have to require to be satisfied by an

applicant for an extension that a proper case has been made out

justifying an extension. It would be wrong if he granted an extension

simply because no one had raised rather exceptional circumstances why

it should not be granted. Reasons why this is so include the

desirability of operating the system efficiently and without

unreasonable delays and also the interests of the applicant for a

patent which are also clearly involved." (pages 149-150)

63. Mr Hanson referred particularly to a further passage in the Kaiser Aluminium case where Kitto J said:

"The respondent contended that the power to admit further evidence

implies a discretion, and that I should exercise the discretion by

refusing to allow the appellant to depart from the notice of

opposition and to make here a case which it did not choose to make

before the Deputy Commissioner. In my opinion, however, it is in the

public interest that a serious opposition by a person entitled in fact

to oppose the grant of a patent should be dealt with on the merits,

rather than that it should be shut out in consequence of a failure in

procedure, lamentable though the failure may be." (page 143)

64. In considering whether the discretion should be exercised under paragraph 223(2)(b), I do not consider it relevant to take into account the events which surrounded the oversight that occurred in the Commissioner's office in failing to find a record of the notices of objection filed on 12 November, 1993 and, as a consequence, incorrectly advising Mr Ivory that none had been filed when he enquired on 16 November, 1993. These proceedings are concerned with the next step in the proceedings. That is an entirely different step and, in the circumstances of this case, the error of the Commissioner's officers in relation to an earlier and separate step cannot influence my decision in relation to a later step.

65. Prior to the hearing, Mr Ivory had questioned the status of the opponents. In response to that, Shelston Waters, who represented the opponents in the earlier proceedings, lodged a letter dated 9 January, 1994 together with copies of certificates of incorporation relating to each opponent (Exhibit C). On the basis of that information, I find that Cyclone Hardware was originally registered as a company on 9 December, 1990 under the name of Eastern Quarries Pty. Limited. Over the years, it has changed its name on numerous occasions (with the change to its current name occurring on 27 September, 1993) but it remains the same company and, therefore, the same legal entity.

66. On the basis of the certificate of incorporation issued on the change of a company's name in Exhibit C, I also find that Windsor Products was originally registered as a company on 29 June, 1951 under the name of Reg. Swan (Tamworth) Pty. Limited. On 4 September, 1963, it changed its name to its current name but, again, it remains the same legal entity as it was before the change of name.

67. Phifer Wire was incorporated in Delaware in the United States of America and another company, PWP, Inc. was incorporated in Alabama. PWP, Inc. was incorporated on 23 November, 1987. Phifer Wire merged into PWP, Inc. on 31 December, 1987 and PWP, Inc. then immediately changed its name to Phifer Wire Products, Inc. The certificate of incorporation of PWP, Inc with its name duly amended was the certificate of incorporation of the surviving company. The date of incorporation of the surviving company, now known as Phifer Wire, was 23 November, 1987.

68. Mr Ivory has argued that Phifer Wire cannot lodge a notice of opposition as it is a foreign company. When this arose at the hearing, Mr Hanson undertook to make further written submissions which he did on 3 March, 1995. He submitted that, as a body corporate, Phifer Wire was a person who could oppose the grant of a standard patent under section 59. He relied on section 22 of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 ("the Acts Interpretation Act"). As it had been incorporated outside Australia, it was a "foreign company within the meaning of the Corporations Law 1988 ("the Corporations Law"). As a foreign company, Mr Hanson submitted, it could not carry on business unless certain criteria were satisfied. He did not canvass whether those criteria had been satisfied but submitted that Phifer Wire does not carry on business in filing a notice of opposition a Statement of Grounds and Particulars. He referred to Hungier v Grace [1972] HCA 42; (1972) 127 CLR 210 (Barwick CJ, Menzies and Walsh JJ) per Barwick CJ at 217.

69. I accept Mr Hanson's submission that a company is a person within the meaning of section 59 of the Act for paragraph 22(1)(a) of the Acts Interpretation Act provides:

"(1) In any Act, unless the contrary intention appears:

(a) expressions used to denote persons generally (such as 'person',

'party', 'someone', 'anyone', 'no-one', 'one', 'another' and

whoever'), include a body politic or corporate as well as an

individual;"

70. Phifer Wire is also a foreign company within the meaning of the Corporations Law for it has been incorporated outside Australia (Corporations Law, section 9). Under section 343 of the Corporations Law, a foreign company must not carry on business unless it is registered under Division 2 of that Act or it has applied for registration and its application has not been determined (section 343). As I have no evidence as to whether or not either of the criteria has been fulfilled, I cannot find whether or not Phifer Wire is entitled to carry on business within Australia.

71. Even if it is not entitled to carry on business in Australia, is it doing so when it files a notice of opposition and serves a Statement of Grounds and Particulars under the Act through its patent attorneys? In the case of Hungier v Grace, the High Court considered whether Mr Hungier was carrying on the business of money-lending. Mr Hungier, an electrical contractor, had lent money to Mr Grace, a timber merchant, on more than thirty occasions over a six year period. Mr Grace set his own schedule for repayment and it was agreed between them that they would divide between them equally the net profit from each transaction to which each borrowing related. Barwick CJ said at pages 216-217:

"The decision whether a person is one 'whose business (whether or not

he carries on any other business) is that of money-lending' can only

be reached after a close examination of the facts in each particular

case. It is not enough merely to show that a person has lent money to

another. As McCardie J said in Edelow v. MacElwee (1918) 1 KB 205,

at p 206:

'There must be more than occasional and disconnected loans. There

must be a business of money-lending, and the word "business" imports

the notion of system, repetition and continuity ... The line of

demarcation cannot be defined with closeness or indicated by any

specific formula. Each case must depend on its own peculiar

features. It is ever a question of degree.'

Whilst no doubt system and regularity are involved in the carrying on

of a business, it does not necessarily follow that one who has

transactions of the same kind systematically or regularly is carrying

on a business in those transactions. One may systematically make

regular deposits to a bank account but not be carrying on a business

of doing so. In other words, system and regularity of making

transactions are not in themselves definitive in this field. Their

absence may well deny that a business is being carried on but their

presence does not necessarily establish that it is."

72. The meaning of the words "carrying on business" was again considered by the High Court in Hope v Bathurst City Council [1980] HCA 16; (1980) 29 ALR 577 (Gibbs, Stephen, Mason, Murphy and Aickin JJ). It was considered in the context of whether rural land was used wholly or mainly for carrying on the business of grazing. That question arose under sub-section 118(1) of the Local Government Act 1919 (NSW). Mason J said:

"Although it has been common ground that 'business' is used in its

ordinary meaning in s118(1), the courts below have refrained from

saying what the meaning is. This perhaps is understandable because,

as a glance at the Shorter Oxford Dictionary will show, the word has

many meanings. Ironically it is the last meaning given by the Shorter

Oxford Dictionary: '19. A commercial enterprise as a going concern',

that comes closest to the popular meaning which the courts appear to

have acted on in the present case. In truth it is the popular meaning

of the word as used in the expression 'carrying on a business', rather

than the popular meaning of the word itself, that is enshrined in the

statutory definition. It is the words 'carrying on' which imply the

repetition of acts (Smith v Anderson (1880) 15 Ch D 247 at 277, 278)

and activities which possess something of a permanent character. This

conclusion serves to emphasize that it is necessary to engage in a

process of construction in order to arrive at the meaning of the word

in s118(1).

I accept, then, that 'business' in the sub-section has the ordinary or

popular meaning which it would be given in the expression 'carrying on

the business of grazing'. It denotes grazing activities undertaken as

a commercial enterprise in the nature of a going concern, that is

activities engaged in for the purpose of profit on a continuous and

repetitive basis. Putting aside the question whether the activities

have a 'grazing' character, the critical issue for decision is whether

the material before the court reasonably admits of different

conclusions on the question whether the appellant's activities

constitute a 'business'. On the facts as found, I conclude that the

appellant's activities amounted to a business and that no other

conclusion was reasonably open. In this respect I agree with what

Reynolds JA said in his dissenting judgment. Transactions were

entered into on a continuous and repetitive basis for the purpose of

making a profit. The activity had a permanent character in that it

had been carried on without interruption since 1965. The appellant

sought customers by advertising and kept appropriate financial

records. The land, though small in area, was put to its best

potential use and the pastures were improved and facilities including

fences were provided for that use. There is nothing to suggest that

the activities were other than genuine and real." (pages 582-583)

73. In light of the views expressed by the High Court, I have no evidence on which I can find that Phifer Wire is carrying on business in Australia. The filing of a notice of opposition and the serving of a Statement of Grounds and Particulars are, on the evidence, steps in a "one-off" action. That action may possibly represent an aspect of Phifer Wire's business activities when viewed as a whole and taking into account those activities outside Australia but it does not indicate that there is any business being carried on in Australia. There is no evidence of any commercial activities being undertaken in Australia on a continuous or repetitive basis. It follows that I have concluded that there is no evidence at this stage of the proceedings that Phifer Wire is precluded from taking the steps it has under the Act.

74. Mr Ivory has submitted that his patent application has a priority date of 6 August, 1985. In view of the fact that Phifer Wire was not incorporated until after that date, he has argued that it cannot claim prior art. He put the same submission in relation to Cyclone Hardware but I have already found that Cyclone Hardware was incorporated long before 1985 although under another name.

75. The opponents would seem to have framed their Statement of Grounds and Particulars on the basis that the 1952 Act applied. Whether or not that is the appropriate legislation in view of the transitional provisions in the Act (see Chapter 23 of that Act) and the date of lodgement of the application is not a matter for me to decide. Whether the 1952 Act or the Act applies, both allow the grant of a standard patent to be opposed on the basis that it is not novel and does not involve an inventive step when compared with the "prior art base" as it is described in the Act (paragraphs 59(1)(b) and 18(1)(b) and section 7) or having regard to what was known or used in Australia before the priority date of the claim (paragraphs 59(1)(g) and (h) of the 1952 Act).

76. Whichever is the applicable Act, there is no requirement in either of them that those opposing the grant of a standard patent must themselves be the possessor of the prior art base (under the Act)or have the prior knowledge or use (under the 1952 Act) prior to the priority date of the claim. It follows that the date on which the opponent, be it a company or an individual came into existence, is not relevant - only the date of the priority of the claim. A grant of a patent of addition may be opposed by Phifer Wire regardless of the date of its incorporation.

77. The same reasoning leads me to conclude that it is not relevant to consider whether or not Windsor Products has ever woven its own products. Whether or not there is prior art is not determined solely by reference to what Windsor Products may do or not do. Regard may be had to knowledge other than that held by Windsor Products and the other opponents.

78. It follows that I do not consider that the opponents' claims are, on their face, "frivolous and vexatious claims" as claimed by Solar-Mesh. Whether or not they are in fact validly made on the merits is a different question. It is not something which is in my power to decide and, even if it were, it would not be appropriate to do so on an application for an extension of time.

79. To date, I have considered each of Mr Ivory's submissions individually. Looking at them overall together with the submissions made by Mr Hanson, I have concluded that the decision to extend the time was the correct and preferable decision in this case. The opponents tried to serve their Statements of Grounds and Particulars within time and allowed themselves plenty of time within which to do that. It was not a case of leaving their attempt to the last minute. They attempted to give Solar-Mesh notice of the Statements by sending them by facsimile. Solar-Mesh's facsimile machine was not operating correctly. Had the opponents succeeded, I would have had to decide whether that amounted to service. As it turned out, they were unsuccessful. At the end of it all, the opponents were prevented from effecting service or even giving notice through circumstances beyond their control.

80. Solar-Mesh, I find, was aware of the Statements of Grounds and Particulars at the latest by 11 February, 1994. Mr Ivory said that he had attended the Archerfield Post Office because he was expecting mail from Sydney and attended each day thereafter until 16 February on the same mission. Apart from the fact that Solar-Mesh would obviously have preferred that the Statements had not been served at all and that there had been no opposition to the grant of the patent of addition to it, there is no evidence of any direct prejudice to it. It was on notice about the Statements. It effectively received the Statements on 16 February, 1994 even though Mr Ivory, for reasons he thought were valid, felt constrained not to read them. The opponents have since effected service and have done so on or before 9 May, 1994.

81. On the evidence I have, there is nothing which shows that the opponents obviously have no ground for their opposition. Taking all of the circumstances into account, it is appropriate that the merits of their case should be considered after all relevant evidence has been submitted. Consequently, the time within which they might serve Solar-Mesh should be extended to and including 9 May, 1994. That was the date on which the opponents served the Statements of Grounds and Particulars on Solar-Mesh.

82. In reaching this conclusion, I have not taken into account the motives of the opponents. Mr Ivory has ascribed certain motives to them but, for the purposes of this application, they are not relevant to consider. The Act and Regulations permit the grant of a patent to be opposed on certain grounds which they specify. It is only those grounds which are relevant. The reasons why a person chooses to exercise the right given by section 59 of the Act to oppose a grant has no place in itself in determining whether or not the ground has been established.

83. The delegate has made certain consequential decisions relating to the time permitted under sub-regulations 5.5(1) and 5.8(1) and did so under regulation 5.10. As the powers of this Tribunal are limited to those set out in the Act and Regulations, it only has power to review those decisions specifically referred to in either section 224 of the Act or regulation 22.26 of the Regulations. As neither gives jurisdiction to review decisions under sub-regulation 5.10 generally or under 5.5(1) or 5.8(1) in particular, I am unable to review those decisions.

84. That leaves the question of costs which the delegate ordered against Solar-Mesh. Again, I do not have power to review that decision. The Commissioner's powers to award costs against a party to proceedings before him arises from section 210 of the Act. Section 210 is not a section to which section 224 refers. Consequently, I have no power to review decisions made under it.

85. For the reasons I have given, I affirm the decision under review.
