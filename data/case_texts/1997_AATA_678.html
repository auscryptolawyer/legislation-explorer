REASONS FOR DECISION

7 March 1997 Senior Member M.D. Allen



  1. By notice lodged 18 September 1995 the Applicant sought review of a decision by the Respondent made 7 August 1995 refusing his application to be registered as a Tax Agent in New South Wales.
  2. The grounds for the Respondent’s decision were that the Applicant was not a fit and proper person as that term is defined by paragraph 251BC(1)(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. That paragraph of the said Act states inter alia:

“Without limiting the generality of an expression used in this Part, but subject to this section, a person is not a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters, as at a particular time, if:

(d) the person is not of good fame, integrity and character;”

  1. Paragraph 251JA(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act states:

“The Board shall register the applicant as a tax agent if the applicant satisfies the Board that:

(a) if the applicant is a natural person:

(i) the applicant is a fit and proper person to prepare income tax returns and transact business on behalf of taxpayers in income tax matters; and

(ii) the applicant is not an undischarged bankrupt;”

  1. Pursuant to a Direction made by me on 9 May 1996 the Respondent on 24 May 1996 served upon the Applicant and lodged with the Tribunal particulars of the grounds upon which it contended that the Applicant was not a fit and proper person to practice as a Tax Agent. On 14 June 1996 I directed that in order to save hearing time this matter would proceed initially upon three only of the allegations raised by the Respondent concerning the Applicant’s fitness to practice, those allegations being (using the numbering in the Statement of Contentions):

(1) That the Applicant was not of good fame, integrity and character within the meaning of section 251BC(1)(d) because:

(i) he has made threats to a former employee Mr Ross; and

(ii) he has made threats to former clients including Barry Radnidge from Bradmere Engineering.

(3) The Applicant is not a fit and proper person to prepare tax returns as required by S251JA because he could not satisfy the Tribunal that he would adhere to professional standards. In particular there is evidence that the Applicant, while not registered as a Tax Agent prepared tax returns for a fee in contravention of S251L of the Act.

  1. The matter was originally set down for hearing on 25 June 1996 but that date was vacated at the Applicant’s request, supported by a certificate from his treating specialist, Dr Robbie.
  2. On 21 January 1997 the matter came on for hearing and occupied 21 and 22 January 1997 and 4 February 1997. A hearing into further matters which arose after the termination of the hearing on 4 February 1997 was held by me on 26 February 1997. At that latter hearing I acceded to the submissions of the Respondent that I should restrict my consideration of matters to the evidence which had been adduced on 21 and 22 January and 4 February 1997.
  3. As part of its case the Respondent called Messrs Ross, Radnidge, Hanratty and Askew. Those gentlemen are to be commended in coming forward to give evidence and of necessity exposing themselves and their affairs to questioning by the Applicant which was not always relevant. As will appear from these reasons, their decision to volunteer their evidence was not taken lightly.
  4. I must also commend counsel for the Respondent, Mr Godwin, and his instructing solicitor, Ms Plater of the Australian Government Solicitor’s Office. During the course of these proceedings both were subjected to ongoing abuse and snide asides from the Applicant, yet at all times they managed a proper and dispassionate professionalism in the face of extreme provocation.
  5. During the hearing of this matter, although the Applicant appeared on his own behalf, he had the assistance of Mrs Karageorge, who is a qualified solicitor. A perusal of the transcript will show that she actively gave the Applicant advice during the hearing and she was allowed to examine him in chief and re-examine him. Mrs Karageorge also made the final address on the Applicant’s behalf. From what I observed she also had difficulties in dealing with the Applicant.
  6. At the outset it is appropriate to discuss the onus each party to the review bears. S251JA of the Income Tax Assessment Act requires that the Applicant satisfy the Respondent that he is a fit and proper person. This onus applies in the Administrative Appeals Tribunal as the Tribunal stands in the shoes of the original decision-maker - Cf Ss43(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and Hospital Benefits Fund v Minister for Health [1992] FCA 599; 111 ALR 1 at pp10-11.
  7. Hill J in Casarotto v Australian Postal Commission [1989] FCA 116; 86 ALR 399 at p412 discussed the concept of onus of proof in the Tribunal. After referring to the Full Federal Court decision of McDonald v Director-General of Social Security [1984] FCA 57; 1 FCR 354, he went on to state:
“It may be that what was said by Woodward J in McDonald v Director-General of Social Security should be confined to the context of social security legislation. Thus, in Minister for Health v Thomson [1985] FCA 208; (1985) 60 ALR 701 at 712, Beaumont J, referring to proceedings before the Medical Services Committee established under the Health Insurance Act 1973 (Cth), said:

‘Generally speaking, concepts of onus of proof used in adversary proceedings are inapplicable in administrative proceedings in the social security area: see McDonald v Director-General of Social Security [1984] FCA 57; (1984) 1 FCR 354. However, where, as here, a breach of discipline, or something analogous, is alleged, the onus of proving such a breach lies upon the accuser. The general position is explained by Professor Enid Campbell in Principles of Evidence and Administrative Tribunals, published in Campbell and Waller (ed) ‘Well and Truly Tried’, Monash Studies in Law (1982) p 53:

‘There may be legal burdens of proof to be discharged in administrative proceedings just as much as there are legal burdens of proof in purely judicial proceedings. Sometimes the incidence of the burden of proof is spelled out by legislation, but more often than not it is simply implied in the nature of the proceedings. If, for example, entitlement to grant of a licence or benefit depends on proof that certain qualifications have been met, the burden of proving the relevant facts going to qualifications must fall upon the applicant. Similarly, where the issue to be decided is whether circumstances have arisen which would justify cancellation or suspension of a licence, or a finding that a breach of discipline had occurred, the onus of proving that these circumstances have arisen would devolve on the accuser. This would be so, notwithstanding that the accuser was also, of necessity, the person or body having authority to adjudicate.’

...”
  1. Here the Respondent has raised specific allegations against the Applicant. This aspect was discussed by the New South Wales Court of Appeal in Ex Parte Tziniolis; Re Medical Practitioners Act [1967] 1 NSWLR 357. At p358 Walsh JA said:
“In so far as the decision of a question of fact which the Court has to determine may depend upon the onus of proof, I think it is clear that the onus is on the applicant. This does not mean that, if some particular act or conduct has been alleged as indicating that the applicant is not of good character, but no evidence at all is given in support of the allegation, the applicant has the onus of disproving that particular allegation. If an applicant produces some evidence of his good character, this would not be regarded as displaced or cut down merely because some charge had been made against him without any evidence to support it. But, if there are acknowledged writings or statements of the applicant which in their natural meaning provide evidence that he is not of good character and, if explanations are offered by the applicant designed to show that they do not really indicate that, I think the Court may take such writings and statements into consideration if the explanations are unconvincing, even if the Court may not be able to decide positively and confidently that the explanations are false. The question before the Court is not whether it is satisfied affirmatively that the applicant is of bad character. The ultimate question for the Court is whether it is satisfied that he is of good character.”
  1. The passage quoted above from Ex Parte Tziniolis supra was specifically referred to by Carter J in Re Fredericks (1984) 1 Qd R438, a case in which the Public Accountants Registration Board of Queensland was the respondent.
  2. On the authorities cited above it is my opinion that, although as pointed out in McDonald v Director-General of Social Security supra, no party before the Tribunal bears any formal onus of proof. If, at the end of the day, I cannot be satisfied, that is to say persuaded on the balance of probabilities having regard to the seriousness of the matter raised (Cf Briginshaw v Briginshaw [1938] HCA 34; 60 CLR 336 and see Ooi v Medical Board of Queensland (Unreported QLD Court of Appeal Judgment CA96/530)) that the Applicant is a fit and proper person then the status quo remains and the Applicant’s registration is denied.
  3. As to what is meant by the phrase “a fit and proper person”, I would first of all refer to the decision of Hill J in Davies v Australian Securities Commission [1995] FCA 1496; 131 ALR 295 at 305-6, namely:
“The phrase ‘fit and proper person’ is a familiar one in the context of qualifications for offices or vocations. Discussing the phrase in the context of licences to use vehicles for the purposes of interstate trade, Dixon CJ, McTiernan and Webb JJ said in Hughes and Vale Pty Ltd v New South Wales (No 2) [1955] HCA 28; (1955) 93 CLR 127 at 156-7:

‘But their very purpose is to give the widest scope for judgment and indeed for rejection. ‘Fit’ (or ‘idoneus’) with respect to an office is said to involve three things: honesty, knowledge and ability; ‘honesty to execute it truly, without malice affection or partiality; knowledge to know what he ought duly to do; and ability as well in estate as in body, that he may intend and execute his office, when need is, diligently, and not for impotency or poverty neglect it’ - Coke. When the question was whether a man was a fit and proper person to hold a licence for the sale of liquor it was considered that it ought not to be confined to an inquiry into his character and that it would be unwise to attempt any definition of the matters which may legitimately be inquired into; each case must depend upon its own circumstances ...’

As I observed in Stasos v Tax Agents’ Board of New South Wales 90 ATC 4950 at 4959, the content of what is necessary to constitute a person a fit and proper person to occupy a particular office or pursue a particular vocation will vary having regard to the office or vocation under consideration. Thus the characteristics required to show fitness as a tax agent were expressed by Davies J in Re Su and Tax Agents’ Board, South Australia 82 ATC 4284 at 4286 as requiring that person to be:

‘... a person of good reputation, has a proper knowledge of taxation laws, is able to prepare income tax returns competently and is able to deal competently with any queries which may be raised by officers of the Taxation Department. He should be a person of such competence and integrity that others may entrust their taxation affairs to his care. He should be a person of such reputation and ability that officers of the Taxation Department may proceed upon the footing that the taxation returns lodged by the agent have been prepared by him honestly and competently.’”
  1. In Australian Broadcasting Tribunal v Bond [1990] HCA 33; 170 CLR 321 at 380 Toohey and Gaudron JJ said:
“The expression ‘fit and proper person’, standing alone, carries no precise meaning. It takes its meaning from its context, from the activities in which the person is or will be engaged and the ends to be served by those activities. The concept of ‘fit and proper’ cannot be entirely divorced from the conduct of the person who is or will be engaging in those activities. However, depending on the nature of the activities, the question may be whether improper conduct has occurred, whether it is likely to occur, whether it can be assumed that it will not occur, or whether the general community will have confidence that it will not occur. The list is not exhaustive but it does indicate that, in certain contexts, character (because it provides indication of likely future conduct) or reputation (because it provides indication of public perception as to likely future conduct) may be sufficient to ground a finding that a person is not fit and proper to undertake the activities in question.”
  1. I was also referred to the decision of the Full Court of the Federal Court in Irving v Minister for Immigration, Local Government and Ethnic Affairs 139 ALR 84. At p94 Lee J said:
“Unless the terms of the Act and regulations require some other meaning be applied, the words ‘good character’ should be taken to be used in their ordinary sense, namely, a reference to the enduring moral qualities of a person, and not to the good standing, fame or repute of that person in the community. The former is an objective assessment apt to be proved as a fact while the latter is a review of subjective public opinion:”
  1. Character is however but one aspect of a non inclusive test of who may be a fit and proper person, see paragraph 251BC(1)(d) above. See also Davies J (with whom Nicholson J agreed) at 139 ALR p87:
“It should also be observed that the term ‘good character’ is not precise in its denotation. In one sense, it refers to the mental and moral qualities which an individual has. In another sense, it refers to the individual’s reputation or repute: see Oxford English Dictionary, meanings 11, 12 and 13; The Macquarie Dictionary meanings 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. ...”
  1. Two of the allegations against the Applicant are that he threatened a former client and a former employee. Although not specified in the written particulars, the threats were implied threats of violence if they did not pay monies the Applicant alleged they owed him and were at that time the subject of Court proceedings.
  2. In Ex Parte Tziniolis supra Holmes JA said at p377:
“A man’s conduct in respects known only to his intimates can evidence his character. ... An inability to withstand the importunings of the evilly disposed would disqualify as would the propensity to exploit the gullible. These and other defects in moral fibre may not be part of a man’s reputation but evidence of these traits must be weighted in estimating character. Even these characteristics cannot be looked at in isolation. The judgment as to character must be arrived at by giving due weight to all features. Finally, the judgment must be made of the man at the time when the Court is asked to consider the application.”
  1. It is also clear from reading Tziniolis supra, especially at p357 where Wallace P quotes from Ziems v Prothonotary of the Supreme Court of New South Wales [1957] HCA 46; 97 CLR 279 at 298, namely:
“conduct may show a defect of character incompatible with membership of a self-respecting profession”

And see Walsh JA at p365 L38 and Holmes JA at p378 that matters need not be criminal in order to amount to showing a person is not a fit and proper person for the purposes of paragraph 251JA(a).

  1. I would only add that although in the course of argument I referred to the well known passage by Lopes LJ in Allinson v General Council of Medical Education and Registration [1894] 1 QB 750 at 763 there is no suggestion that under the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act the conduct judged to be disqualifying has to have been in the pursuit of the Applicant’s profession. It is character generally that is in issue and an applicant may be of impeccable professional integrity but have other flaws of character which would disqualify him.
  2. Furthermore, I hold that a person’s behaviour can be so in breach of conventions on particular occasions that it would be inimical to the proper standards of any professional body to have that person as a member.
  3. Because of the attitude I take towards the issue of threats to Messrs Ross and Radnidge and other matters discussed below, I do not intend to make findings with regard to particular number three, namely that of preparing tax returns for a fee whilst not registered as a Tax Agent.
  4. An issue which has been raised by particular 3 and one which must be addressed by the Respondent is the attitude the Respondent should take to actions such as the Applicant’s in that he used a corporate vehicle, namely Abbotsford Accountancy Services Pty Ltd, with a nominee, to carry on business as a Tax Agent after he resigned as a Tax Agent ahead of being struck off.
  5. As is revealed by Document T25 in the documents prepared for the Tribunal pursuant to S37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, in 1989 the Australian Tax Office conducted an investigation into the income tax affairs of the Applicant, his wife and an associate company, Ajax Anson Pty Ltd. As a result of negotiations between his solicitors and the Australian Tax Office, the Applicant paid to the Australian Tax Office the sum of $570,000-00 in tax and penalties and resigned as a Tax Agent. The Applicant’s resignation dated 17 December 1990 is at Document T11.
  6. During the time the Applicant was under investigation by the Australian Tax Office several of his clients were also investigated by the Australian Tax Office. To assist with these investigations the Applicant engaged Mr Alan Ross, a Chartered Accountant, as a Consultant.
  7. Following a disagreement with the Applicant, Mr Ross entered into partnership with another Accountant, a Mr Hanson. Twenty of the Applicant’s former clients then engaged the services of Mr Ross. Later other former clients of the Applicant found their way to Mr Ross’ practice.
  8. The Applicant issued proceedings out of the Supreme Court of New South Wales claiming $60,000 damages in respect of clients who had transferred their business to Mr Ross. Both parties engaged solicitors to act for them in those proceedings. Ultimately the action was terminated on the basis that each party pay their own costs to date.
  9. Exhibit R1 in these proceedings is an affidavit by Mr Ross deposing as to certain events which are alleged to have occurred on 8 September 1993. The relevant parts of that affidavit read:

“14. Earlier that afternoon, around 1.00pm, I had been contacted by one of my staff at a client’s office. She said words to the effect of: ‘A woman has rung who says she works for Dr Nemis. She says that Dr Peter MacDonald has referred Dr Nemis to me and would like an appointment at 4.30pm.’

I said words to the effect of: ‘Tell her that’s okay.’

15. ...

16. At 5.30pm, when Dr Nemis had not arrived I called Dr Peter MacDonald. I said words to the effect of: ‘Have you referred Dr Nemis to me?’

He said words to the effect of: ‘I have never heard of him.’

17. At about 5.45pm, Mr Jack arrived in the company of two persons. One of these persons was thick set and wore his hair in a pony tail. He sat on the corner of my desk. The other was tall and thinner and was dressed in a suit and tie and sat at the back of the room. Mr Jack sat in a chair opposite me.

18. I said to the two persons accompanying Mr Jack words to the effect of ‘Who are you?’

One of them (the thick set man with pony tail) replied in words to the effect of: ‘We are here on instructions of Tim Bristow to negotiate a settlement between you and Mr Jack. Mr Bristow has been a friend of Mr Jack’s for thirty years.’

19. A conversation to the following effect then took place:

Mr Jack said: ‘You owe me $50,000.00 for clients you took.’

I said: ‘I don’t owe you anything, what do you mean?’

Mr Jack said: ‘The clients you took were worth $50,000.00.’

I said: ‘I don’t owe you anything, if you think otherwise the Court is the place to resolve it.’

Mr Jack said: ‘We don’t need to have the Courts involved in this, we can make arrangements to pay me.’

The man with the ponytail said: ‘You should come to an arrangement because it would be easier for everybody.’

I said: ‘Are you threatening me?’

He said: ‘No, I am suggesting you should come to some arrangement.’

I said: ‘I won’t be intimidated, unless you have something else to discuss you had better leave, otherwise I will call the North Sydney detectives.”

20. They then left and I rang the police.”

  1. The Applicant was cross-examined regarding these allegations and did not deny their truth. In particular he admitted that a member of his staff, on his instructions, telephoned Mr Ross with the false statement that a Dr Nemis been recommended to him and would like an appointment.
  2. Mr Radnidge is the Managing Director of Bradmere Tool and Engineering Pty Ltd. His company in 1982 engaged the Applicant as the company’s Accountant. As a result of finding out that the company was being investigated by the Australian Tax Office and that the company owed between $50,000 to $150,000 to the Australian Tax Office and that the company’s Superannuation Fund was in a “mess”, they transferred in 1991 the company’s business to Mr Ross, and they held the Applicant responsible for the company’s difficulties.
  3. Both Mr Ross and the Applicant rendered accounts to Bradmere Tool and Engineering for work done. As the Directors of the company considered that the work had in fact been performed by Mr Ross, they refused to pay the Applicant’s account. The Applicant issued proceedings against Mr Ross regarding the accounts and the work done and the company had to pay a sum of money into Court, namely the Burwood Local Court.
  4. On a day in September 1993 Mr Radnidge received a visit at the company premises from the Applicant accompanied by two other men. Mr Radnidge’s evidence in chief on this point reads (Transcript p35):
“Anyway these two rather rough looking characters arrived in my office.

One was perhaps my height or a shade taller, perhaps, much more thickset than me, fair sandy straight hair. The other chap was - that first fellow would have been 35 or 40 I suppose and the other chap was about mid-20s, tall, 6’2” or in that area; dark hair, not really heavily built. Dark - I think his hair was a bit wavy or curly, and they intimated that they were from Tim Bristow and they were here to collect money which we owed to Warren Jack and Associates. Then Warren Jack appeared in the doorway behind them. I remember he had a heavy walking stick in his hand which I thought: That’s a bit strange. He doesn’t usually use a stick. But anyway, so these two characters were trying to intimidate me and I was quite taken aback, because it was quite unexpected”

Q: “Why did you think they were trying to intimidate you? What gave you that impression?”

A: “Just their general attitude and: ‘You owe this man money; this man’s done all this work for you; you haven’t paid him; we’re here to collect his money you own him’.”

Q: “Did the name Tim Bristow mean anything to you?”

A: ”Well, I knew Tim Bristow from news reports on television as a sort of a shady private detective-type character who - that is, you know, was involved in sort of underhand/underworld type deals or what not.”

Mr Radnidge’s evidence continued as to a conversation which took place and he concluded his evidence by saying:

“I remember the parting words of the young - it’s just struck me, the taller, younger fellow said in a sort of a menacing way, I suppose: ‘I hope you have a good sleep tonight’ - to me, indicating that I had something to worry about.”

He was then asked whether the Applicant, Mr Jack, was there when the comment was made about having a good sleep and he replied that he was.

  1. Mr Meredith was a Director of Bradmere Tool and Engineering Pty Ltd at the relevant time. He was not present at the time the Applicant and the two other men visited Mr Radnidge. He saw Mr Radnidge later that day and described his state as very emotional and shaken.
  2. During his examination in chief the Applicant was asked about the visit to Mr Radnidge and Bradmere Tool and Engineering Pty Ltd. The following passage occurs in the Transcript (p103):
Q: “Well, do you think he (Radnidge) gave a fairly honest account of what happened as far as you recall.?”

A: “Yes, he did. - as I said I respect Mr Radnidge. ...”
  1. Later, in examination in chief, the Applicant was asked about the two men who accompanied him to Mr Radnidge’s office. He said:
“They were through my association with Mr Bristow. He sent them along, I believed them to be Mercantile Agents.

He was asked:

  1. On the evidence before me I am satisfied beyond reasonable doubt that the Applicant did attend upon Messrs Radnidge and Ross in company with two men who stated that they were associated with Tim Bristow. I am also satisfied beyond reasonable doubt that the reason for the attendance was to obtain monies the Applicant believed were due and owing to him.
  2. Although there was no direct evidence before me as to who the Tim Bristow mentioned and referred to is, I am prepared to take what Flick in his work “Natural Justice” 2nd Edition at p96 refers to as official notice that the name Tim Bristow has received some notoriety in newspapers circulating in Sydney and beyond. According to repute, Mr Bristow holds himself out for hire as a mediator of disputes and collector of debts achieving results for his clients by the threat (implied or otherwise) to inflict physical force. Without putting too fine a point upon it, he is reputed to be what is colloquially referred to as a stand-over man. I have no evidence whether this is in fact so, but I am aware of Mr Bristow’s reputation and there is evidence, which I accept, that Mr Radnidge was aware of Mr Bristow’s reputation “as a shady private detective involved in underhand/underworld type deals ...”.
  3. I am of the opinion and so find that for the Applicant to attend upon Messrs Ross and Radnidge with the two men who stated they were associated with Tim Bristow and demand payment of monies which were then the subject of litigation is conduct which demonstrates that he is not of good character. Specifically, I reject the submission by the Applicant’s solicitor that it was no more than a form of alternate dispute resolution.
  4. To support his claim to be of good character the Applicant called several witnesses. Unfortunately he had not taken the trouble to proof these witnesses and their evidence was not altogether supportive of his case.
  5. The first of such witnesses was a member of the New South Wales Parliament, Dr MacDonald. That witness stated that he had discovered that there were irregularities in the way the Applicant had dealt with his taxation matters and he had therefore ceased to deal with him.
  6. Mrs Bellchambers and her husband had also engaged the Applicant to handle their taxation affairs. In cross-examination she stated that the Applicant, through his actions had caused she and her husband a lot of grief and that she was devastated to find out that they had to pay out a lot of money and were in trouble with the Taxation Department. Thus I do not consider her evidence in any way supports the Applicant’s case.
  7. A Mr Walter Petrovic supports the Applicant. He stated that the Applicant was always honest and above board with him. Mr Petrovic’s evidence was weakened by the inference that he saw nothing wrong with the manner in which the Applicant sought to obtain monies from Messrs Ross and Radnidge. I am therefore not inclined to place much weight upon his estimation of the Applicant’s character.
  8. The Applicant also called his son, Mr Andrew Jack, to support his good character. The evidence of Mr Andrew Jack was more particularly concerned with impugning the credibility of the witness Ross, however, I consider that Ross’ evidence as to the visit to him by the Applicant and the two other men has been corroborated by the Applicant himself in his own evidence.
  9. Mr Indaman Singh was for a period employed by the Applicant, and was the nominee of Abbotsford Accounting Services Pty Ltd. Mr Singh attended the Tribunal in answer to a summons issued by the Tribunal at the request of the Applicant. Although called by the Applicant, he declined to answer directly any questions relating to the Applicant’s reputation and probity.
  10. In the course of his cross-examination Mr Singh stated that when the Applicant had first contacted him by telephone and asked him to give evidence on his behalf, upon his, Singh’s, declining to do so the Applicant had become abusive.
  11. The Applicant also called Mr Bruce Dennis, a solicitor. Mr Dennis had acted for the Applicant and is of the opinion that he is of good fame and character. He is aware of the incidents concerning Messrs Ross and Radnidge and in his view those were isolated incidents brought about whilst the Applicant was under stress and do not represent his character at all.
  12. Notwithstanding Mr Dennis’ views as to the aberrant nature of the Applicant’s conduct in engaging the persons associated with Tim Bristow to assist him collect debts he believed to be owing, I saw nothing in the Applicant’s submissions to this Tribunal and in his cross-examination of witnesses to persuade me that the impugned behaviour was out of character.
  13. I am also satisfied that the incidents alleged against the Applicant regarding Messrs Ross and Radnidge occurred as they said and I am further satisfied that such conduct is sufficient for me to find, as I do so find, that the Applicant is not of good fame and character.
  14. In addition, given the evidence of Dr MacDonald, Mr Radnidge and Mrs Bellchambers, I am satisfied that the Applicant mismanaged the affairs of his clients. This and the fact he resigned his previous appointment as a Tax Agent following investigations by the Australian Tax Office negates any attempt by the Applicant to adduce evidence that he is of good fame and integrity.
  15. Another point of concern, although not raised by the Respondent in its Statement of Contentions but which became apparent during the course of the hearing, is Mr Jack’s ability to represent clients. Part of the duty of a Tax Agent is to prepare Notices of Objection to assessments (see Regulation 156 of the Income Tax Regulations) and, if necessary, to represent his clients upon the hearing of those objections. A perusal of the transcript in this matter will make it apparent that any attempt by Mr Jack to represent anyone in any forum would be counter-productive. For that reason alone he ought not to be registered as a Tax Agent, that is to say he has a present inability to properly represent his clients.
  16. I have a deal of sympathy for Mr Jack. He is currently suffering from a war-caused psychiatric illness and this no doubt explains his behaviour which at times was inappropriate. I can only suggest that he obtains a further report from his treating Psychiatrist, Dr Robbie, a medical practitioner with vast experience in the treatment of veterans, and seeks a pension at the Special Rate pursuant to S 24 of the Veterans’ Entitlements Act 1988.
  17. On 27 February 1997 there was delivered to my chambers by facsimile two written references relating to Mr Jack and a covering letter. As the Respondent has had no opportunity to peruse these references or to cross-examine their makers, I excluded them from my consideration.
  18. As the Applicant is not of good fame, integrity and character as required by paragraph 251BC(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, he is not a fit and proper person for the purposes of S251JA of the said Act and hence the decision to deny him registration as a Tax Agent is affirmed.
