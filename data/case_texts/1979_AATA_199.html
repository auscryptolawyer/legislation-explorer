May and Commissioner for Superannuation [1979] AATA 199 (18 December 1979)

Last Updated: 12 March 2007

Superannuation- refusal to extend period for making election - application for review - jurisdiction of Tribunal - whether there was a "reviewable decision" - whether later Act is amending Act - effect of advice contained in office circular - necessity for "difference" in salary level - advice as to late election - whether there were "special circumstances" for grant of extension of time - exercise of discretion warranted in circumstances.

Acts Interpretation Act 1901, s 15

Superannuation Act 1922, ss 66, 68, 69, 141

Superannuation Act 1971, ss 2, 13, 22

Superannuation Act 1976, s 154

Superannuation Acts Amendment Act 1978, ss 9, 61

Re: LESLIE WILLIAM MAY

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

AAT Decision No 333

DECISION

The Tribunal decides to set aside the decision made by the Commissioner for Superannuation on the 6th August 1979 and in substitution for that decision the Tribunal decides that the period of time for the making by the applicant of an election under section 22(1) of the Superannuation Act 1971 should be extended to 11 December 1973.

DATED this 18th day of December 1979.

) No. 121 of 1979

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: LESLIE WILLIAM MAY
And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

REASONS FOR DECISION

This is an application by Leslie William May for review of a decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation confirming the decision taken "by the Superannuation Board on 13-14 June 1974 under section 22(2) of the Superannuation Act 1971 refusing to extend the period in which Mr May could make an election under section 22(1) of the Superannuation Act 1971. On 28 August 1978, the Commissioner, through his delegate, extended the period specified in section 154(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 within which the applicant might request the Commissioner to reconsider the decision of the Board. Subsequently, on 6 August, 1979, the Commissioner himself, after reconsidering the Board's decision, decided to confirm it. Jurisdiction of the Tribunal

Although the Commissioner did not formally submit that the Tribunal had no jurisdiction to review his decision, he suggested that there were grounds for doubting whether, firstly, he had been correct in extending the time for reconsideration of the Board's decision and, secondly, whether the Tribunal had jurisdiction to review his decision on such reconsideration. Without tracing the argument in detail, we think it is fair to say that the doubts expressed by the Commissioner stemmed from the fact that the Board's decision was made under a section of the Superannuation Act 1971 (section 22) which did not itself directly amend the Superannuation Act 1922.

Section 22 of the 1971 Act provided (so far as relevant) as follows:-

"22(1). A person who became an employee on or after the first day of January, One thousand nine hundred and seventy, and before the commencement of this Act may, within three months after the commencement of this Act, elect that the amendments made by this Act shall have effect in relation to him and, where an election is so made, the Superannuation Act 1922-1971 applies in relation to him, subject to this section, as if the amendments made by this Act had come into operation on the first day of January, One thousand nine hundred and seventy."

(2). The Board may, if it is satisfied that there are special circumstances that justify it in so doing, extend the period for the making of an election under the last preceding sub-section."

The question in the Commissioner's mind was whether a decision under that section was a "reviewable decision" within the meaning of section 154(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976. If it was not, then the consequence followed that the Commissioner had no power to extend the time for reconsideration and the Tribunal had no decision to review.

A "reviewable decision" is defined by section 154(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 as follows:-

"'reviewable decision1 means a decision of the Commissioner or a delegate of a Commissioner under this Act under the superseded Act or under the regulations made under either of those Acts and includes a decision of the Superannuation Board or a delegate of the Superannuation Board (other than a decision under section 141 of the superseded Act)."

The "superseded Act" is defined in section 3 (1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 as meaning the Superannuation Act 1922.

Miss Vardanega, who appeared for the Commissioner, submitted that the definition, despite its somewhat confusing use of the words "means" and "includes" was intended to be exhaustive. On the face of the definition, so far as decisions of the former Superannuation Board were concerned, it was only decisions of the Board under the superseded Act (i.e. the 1922 Act) which were reviewable. As the present decision was taken under the 1971 Act not the 1922 Act, it may not fall within the definition.

We were referred to the provisions of section 15 of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 which provides that:-

"15. Every Act amending another Act shall, unless the contrary intention appears, be construed with such other Act and as part thereof."

Miss Vardanega submitted that, whilst this section clearly applied to those sections of the 1971 Act up to and including section 21 which expressly amended provisions of the 1922 Act, the application of that section to the provisions of section 22 of the 1971 Act was not as clear. The problem was to be resolved, she submitted, by deciding whether section 22 of the 1971 Act was "capable of independent and separate effect": see The Queen v Wheeldon (No 1) [1978] FCA 11; (1978) 33 FLR 402 at p406.

Mr A Stretton, who appeared on "behalf of the applicant, submitted that the whole of the 1971 Act amended the 1922 Act which then became known as the Superannuation Act 1922 - 1971 and that section 22 of the 1971 Act was part of the 1922 Act as so amended. Mr Stretton submitted that the specific reference in the definition to a "reviewable decision of the Superannuation Board or a delegate of the Superannuation Board" should be given its plain meaning and should not be read in any restrictive fashion.

In our view the starting point in the consideration of this question is section 15 of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901. In relation to a provision in identical language in section 12 of the Interpretation Act 1897 (N.S.W.)

Isaacs J. said in Sweeney v Fitzhardinge [1906] HCA 73; (1906) 4 CLR 716 at page 735:-

"That section is a legislative declaration of general application, that when an Act is amended by a later Act the two shall be regarded as one connected and combined statement of the will of Parliament, unless the contrary intention appears in the amending Act. Prima facie, this general rule applies in the case of every amending Act and the onus of displacing it rests upon those who assert the contrary intention; they have the burden of satisfying the Court that, by expressed words or necessary implication, the legislature has indicated in the amending Act its intention to depart from the ordinary rule expressed in the section referred to."

In that case the point had been taken that the appeal provisions in section 108 of the Liquor Act 1898 which referred to "any adjudication of a Licencing Court made under this Act" (emphasis added) did not apply to adjudications made under provisions contained in the Liquor (Amendment) Act 1905. It was argued that the 1905 Act exhibited a contrary intention to the general rule laid down by section 12 of the Interpretation Act 1897 (NSW) on the ground (inter alia) that the provisions of the 1905 Act constituted new legislation on a new subject and was a code quite complete within itself. As no reference could be found in that Part of the 1905 Act to the conferring of any right of appeal against adjudications made under that Act, it was argued that no such right existed. The High Court held however that the amending Act should be construed as part of the principal Act and that the appeal provision therefore applied. Griffith CJ, at p727 summed up his conclusions as follows:-

"Being, therefore, unable to find any indication of contrary intention such as the Court could rest its decision upon, I have come to the conclusion that the Liquor (Amendment) Act 1905 must be construed as part of the principal Act, and as if its provisions were provisions of that Act. When so construed, the appeal given by sec108 from all decisions "under this Act" applies to all decisions given under the amending Act of 1905."

A similar argument was considered by the Supreme Court of Victoria in Georgoussis v Medical Board of Victoria [1957] VicRp 98; (1957) VR 671 where objection was taken to the competence of an appeal to the Supreme Court against a decision of the Medical Board of Victoria refusing to grant registration to the applicant to practice as a physician. The Board's decision was made pursuant to section 2 of the Medical (Registration) Act 1956, section 1 of which provided that:-

"This Act ... shall be read and construed as one with Part 1 of the Medical Act 1928 (hereinafter called the Principal Act) and the Acts amending the same."

The only right of appeal against a decision of the Board was conferred by section 9 (2) of the Medical Act 1933 which referred to a "decision of the Board under Part I of the Principal Act or under this Act." It was argued on behalf of the Medical Board that a decision under section 2 of the 1956 Act did not fall within these words. This argument was, however, rejected. After reviewing the authorities, Smith J concluded (at p 675) that:-

".... when an Act is passed containing a direction that it shall be read and construed as one with an earlier Act expressions such as "under this Act" appearing in the earlier Act must, in the absence of some indication of an intention to the contrary, be given an extended application as from the date of the later Act so as to cover, as from that date, things done under the later Act."

In the light of these decisions the question, to our minds, is whether any reason can be found in the 1971 Act for displacing the application of the general rule provided in section 15 of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 - at least so far as the provisions of section 22 of the 1971 Act are concerned. We should say that it is clear, in our view, that the 1971 Act is in substance "an Act amending another Act" (ie. the 1922 Act) notwithstanding the variety of ways in which the sections up to and including section 21 may effect that amendment (see Beaumont v Yeomans (1934) 34 SR 562 at p569 per Jordan CJ). Although section 22 of that Act was referred to in argument as a "transitional provision", we think that it is more accurately characterised as a section dealing with the application, in certain respects, of the 1922 Act as amended by the 1971 Act. In that regard, the two decisions we have referred to may be distinguishable because in neither case was the Court concerned with provisions of that character. Nevertheless the underlying principle of those decisions appears to us to be entirely applicable.

So far as section 22 (1) is concerned, the expressed consequence of making an election under that sub-section is to apply the 1922 Act as amended to the person making the election "as if the amendments made by this Act had come into operation on 1 January 1970". Such a provision is, in our view, meaningless unless it is construed with the 1922 Act and as part of it. And the same may be said of the other subsections of section 22 to which we do not need to refer in detail.

Despite the form in which section 22 (1) is cast, the substance of the alteration which that section effected upon the rights of the class of employees to whom it was directed, is most informative. Prior to the amendments made by the 1971 Act a former member of the Defence Force who became an employee in the Commonwealth Public Service having, at the time of entry, a vested or contingent right to a pension (other than a widow's pension) under the Defence Forces Retirement Benefits Act 1948, was not required or permitted to contribute for units of pension under the Superannuation Act 1922 unless, there was a "difference" "between the actuarial value of that pension at the time of entry to the Public Service and the benefits which would otherwise be applicable under the 1922 Act (see sections 68 and 69 of the 1922 Act). However, even then, the right to make an election under section 69 (1) of the 1922 Act to come under that Act for the purpose of the "difference", only arose if the right to pension was "commutable for new rights in respect of a less number of units than would be applicable under" the 1922 Act. Effectively, therefore, section 69 (1) precluded the making of an election by an employee in receipt of a pension under the Defence Forces Retirement Benefits Act 1948 unless his salary on joining the Public Service exceeded his salary on discharge from the Defence Force. It mattered not that his Public Service salary might at some later time exceed his salary on discharge. Section 69 (1) only applied if there was a "difference" at the time of entry to the Public Service.

The 1971 Act introduced a number of important amendments to this scheme. In particular, a new Part XA headed "Preservation of Rights of Certain Contributors and Former Contributors" was inserted in the 1922 Act. At the same time consequential amendments were made to the 1922 Act to enable the more beneficial provisions of the new Part XA to apply to employees who may have been disadvantaged by being unable to make an election under section 69 (1) at the time of entering the Public Service because there was then no relevant "difference". Section 2 of the 1971 Act provided that the Act was to come into operation on the day on which it received the Royal Assent, namely, 25 May 1971. However, the application of the new provisions to existing employees was dealt with in two different sections of the 1971 Act:-

(i)Employees who became employees before 1 January 1970

By section 13 of the 1971 Act a new section 66 was inserted in Division 5 of Part IV, subsection (2) of which provided that:-

"(2) An employee to whom section sixty-eight of this Act applies and who became an employee before the first day of January, One thousand nine hundred and seventy, may, within three months after the commencement of this section or within such further period as the Board, in special circumstances, allows, elect to contribute in accordance with Division 2 of Part XA of this Act and, in that case, that Division (other than section one hundred and nineteen M) has effect in relation to him and so has effect as if he had become an employee on the date of the election."

(ii) Employees who became employees on or after 1 January 1970 and before 25 May 1971

It was to this group of employees that section 22 of the 1971 Act applied. In respect of both groups of employees, the clear intention of Parliament was to enable them to take advantage, if they so wished, of the more beneficial provisions as to preservation of rights introduced by the 1971 Act. There is no reason to be found in that Act to suggest that Parliament intended the rights of one group to be determined any differently from the other. More especially, insofar as there were rights of appeal under the 1922 Act, there is no reason to believe that a refusal by the Board to extend the time for making an election under section 66 (2) of the 1922 Act (as inserted by the 1971 Act) was intended to be reviewable but a refusal under section 22 (2) of the 1971 Act was not (see in this regard section 141 of the 1922 Act).

As we are unable to find any contrary intention in the 1971 Act and as there are, in our view, compelling reasons for construing the whole of the 1971 Act with the 1922 Act and "as part thereof", we have concluded that we should apply section 15 of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901. When the 1971 Act is construed in accordance with that section, a reference in the 1922 Act to things done under that Act, must be taken as extending to things done under section 22 of the 1971 Act (see Georgoussis v Medical Board of Victoria - supra). We think that the reference to decisions of the Board or its delegate in the definition of "reviewable decision" in section 154 (1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 should be construed in the same way.

Our reason for so thinking is that section 154 of the Superannuation Act 1976, in its present form, was obviously intended to replace any rights of review which may previously have existed under section 141 of the 1922 Act. Section 141(1) of that Act provided as follows:-

"(1) Any dispute under this Act shall be determined in the first place by the Board:

Provided that any person aggrieved by a decision of the Board may appeal to the High Court constituted by a single Justice of that Court."

This section was repealed by section 19(1) of the Superannuation Acts Amendment Act 1978 (Act No. 17 of 1978) with a saving provision in subsection (2) with respect to any pending appeal to the High Court. By section 61 of the same Act, section 154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 was repealed and the section as presently in force was substituted. Two of the major changes made in the substituted section were, firstly, the inclusion within the concept of a "reviewable decision" of "a decision of the Superannuation Board, or a delegate of the Superannuation Board (other than a decision under section 141 of the superseded Act)"; and, secondly, the express conferral by subsection (6) of jurisdiction on the Tribunal to review not only "reviewable decisions" but also decisions of the Board or its delegate made under section l'4l of the superseded Act. These amendments worked in harmony to transfer to the Tribunal jurisdiction to review a decision of the Superannuation Board or its delegate made under the 1922 Act subject to the following qualifications:-

(i)An appeal instituted in the High Court pursuant to section 141 of the Superannuation Act 1922 by a person aggrieved by a decision of the Board determining a "dispute" under that Act prior to 24 April 1978 (the date of commencement of section 19 of the 1978 Act) was expressly preserved by section 19(2) of that Act

(ii) Where a "dispute" had been determined by the Board in accordance with section 141 (1) of the 1922 Act, but no appeal had been instituted to the High Court prior to 24 April, 1978, the Tribunal's jurisdiction arises directly under section 154 (6) without the necessity for the Board's determination to be first reconsidered by the Commissioner under section 154 (2).

(ii)But where the Board itself had not been called upon to determine a "dispute" pursuant to section 141(1) of the 1922 Act prior to 24 April, 1978, a decision of the Board or its delegate is only reviewable by the Tribunal provided the provisions of section 154(2) are complied with and the Commissioner is first given the opportunity to reconsider the decision of the Board or its delegate. That sub-section provides as follows:-

"(2) A person affected by a reviewable decision who is dissatisfied with the decision may, by notice in writing given to the Commissioner within the period of 30 days after the day on which the decision first comes to the notice of the person, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, request the Commissioner to reconsider the decision."

It was by this path, following an extension of time granted by the Commissioner and following his reconsideration of the decision of the Board that the present application comes before the Tribunal.

Nothing in our review of the legislative provisions by which the jurisdiction of this Tribunal was conferred leads us to believe that Parliament intended to restrict in any way the decisions of the Board or its delegate which the Commissioner and ultimately this Tribunal should be empowered to review. We believe that it was, therefore, competent for the Commissioner to extend the time for reconsideration of the Board's decision and then to proceed to review the decision. Equally, it is, in our view, competent for this Tribunal to review the Commissioner's decision. We proceed accordingly to a consideration of the merits of the application.

The Merits of the Application

The applicant informed us that he joined the army in 1946 as a young man of 17 and rose to the rank of Warrant Officer Class 1 as a specialist in electrical communications by the time of his discharge on 24 July 1970. He joined the Department of Defence in the same specialist field as a temporary civilian employee on 27 July 1970 and was permanently appointed on the 10 February 1971.

Being an employee who had a "vested right" to a pension under the Defence Forces Retirement Benefits Act 1948 the applicant was a person to whom the provisions of section 68 of the Superannuation Act 1922 applied. As his salary on joining the Public Service was less than his Army salary on discharge he was ineligible at that time to make an election under section 69 (1) of the 1922 Act. Following the enactment of the Superannuation Act 1971, however, he was an employee to whom section 22 (1) of that Act applied.

In order to identify the employees who might be eligible to take advantage of the new provisions as to preservation of rights, the Superannuation Board sent a circular number 1971/6 dated 27 May 1971 to all Government Departments and approved authorities explaining the effect of the amending legislation and seeking information as to the employees who may be eligible to make an election under the provisions of s66 (2) of the 1922 Act or s22 (1) of the 1971 Act. Paragraph 36 of the circular stated (inter alia):-

"On receipt of this advice the Board will provide each of these persons with a notice, through the Department, showing the unit equivalent of the pension, the contribution for the "difference" and an explanation of the relevant provisions of the amending legislation."

Paragraph 37 then provided:-

"Departments and approved authorities are asked to ensure that the advice referred to in the preceding paragraph includes the names of all personnel in this category as further action by the Board in relation to its employees will depend wholly on this advice."

On the 15th July 1971 by Office Notice No. 1971/77 the Secretary of the Department of Defence provided an outline of the amendments made by the 1971 Act and indicated in paragraph 5 of the Notice that:-

"(5) It is the Superannuation Board's opinion that the largest categories of persons eligible for the preservation provisions will be:-

(a)Former servicemen contributing to DFRB, and
(b)Persons entering the employment of the Commonwealth at this time."

Paragraph 6 then stated:-

"(6) The Department is required to obtain certain information from officers and employees who were previously not eligible to contribute to the superannuation fund in order to review their eligibility in the light of the amended legislation."

Paragraph 8 indicated that the Superannuation Board would furnish each officer so affected with an individual explanation of his rights. Paragraphs 9, 10 and 11 then provided (so far as relevant):-

"(9) Officers and employees in Central Staff including DSD, Canberra should contact the Personnel Officer in the first instance should an entitlement be thought to exist .... (emphasis added).

(10)Where eligibility exists and to take advantage of the new provisions written elections must be made on the prescribed form SB2A which can be obtained from the Personnel Section. It is emphasised that the restrictive provisions of section 68 will continue to apply to those officers/employees who do not make the appropriate written election.
(11)The time limit for elections expires on 25th August, 1971 and elections must therefore be forwarded to the Personnel Officer by 4th August 1971 to enable examination before submission to the Superannuation Board."

"DSD Canberra", we were told, refers to the Defence Signals Division in which the applicant was employed at the time.

Mr May, who gave evidence in support of his application, said that Office Notice No. 1971/77 came to his attention almost by accident. He was engaged in shift work at the time and happened to see a copy of the Notice on the supervisor's desk. Believing that the Notice could apply to him he discussed the matter with fellow employees and arranged at the first opportunity to speak to the staff clerk in the Department of Defence. As he was working on shift work at the time, it was not until the following week that he was able to discuss the matter with the staff clerk. The staff clerk had informed him that he had nothing to elect for and that he should wait until his public service salary exceeded his army pay on retirement. At the time he had not seen any reason to question this advice. Acting on the advice he had not made any election. Perhaps more importantly, no action was taken by the staff clerk to convey his name and the other requested information to the Superannuation Board. Mr May said that he had been unable to recall the name of the staff clerk and that enquiries he had made through the Department of Defence had been unsuccessful in identifying who the staff clerk then was.

On the l6th November 1972 Mr May was transferred to the Department of Supply. As soon as his salary exceeded his salary at the time of discharge from the army, he approached the staff clerk in the Department of Supply, Mr Ray Zatorski, to see whether he could now join the superannuation fund. Mr Zatorski who was called by the applicant to give evidence, confirmed the applicant's evidence in this regard. He also confirmed that the applicant had told him that he had been advised by the staff clerk in the Department of Defence in 1971 that he was ineligible to join the superannuation fund until there was a "difference" in his public service salary.

As a result of advice given to him by Mr Zatorski the applicant wrote on the 21 March 1973 to the Superannuation Board saying (inter alia):-

"As a permanent employee of the Commonwealth Public Service I request you to consider my eligibility to contribute to the Commonwealth superannuation fund. Previously, because I am in receipt of a defence forces retirement benefits pension I was considered not eligible to join the fund."

By letter dated 18 May 1973 the Secretary of the Superannuation Board acknowledged Mr May's letter and said:-

"I have not as yet completed the examination of all the circumstances of your case but wish to advise that a preliminary check of the records indicates that the Board did not at any time review your eligibility to become a contributor. As soon as I have completed my enquiries I will write to you again."

This letter of course confirms the fact that the Superannuation Board had not received any advice from the Department of Defence that Mr May was within the category of employees who may have been eligible to take advantage of the new preservation of rights amendments.

There then followed a letter dated 10 July 1973 from the Secretary of the Superannuation Board to the applicant which it is desirable to set out in full (other than formal parts):-

"Further to my letter of 18 May 1973 I wish to advise that you are eligible to submit a late election under Section 22 of the Superannuation Act 1971 to become a contributor.

Such an election would however be contingent upon you satisfying the Board that your health and physical fitness is such as to justify your acceptance as a contributor and in this regard you would be required to undergo a medical examination conducted by a Commonwealth Medical Officer. If the results proved satisfactory to the Board, your election would be automatically validated and you would be required to contribute for benefits in the Pension Scheme or the Provident Account.

If your acceptance was for the Pension Scheme, you would be eligible to contribute for so many units as represents the difference between the value of your DFRB pension assessed in terms of superannuation units, and the normal unit entitlement applicable to your salary for superannuation purposes. The position may be clarified as follows:-

Selected Retiring Age

60 years or 65 years

(a) Normal unit entitlement based on salary of $553 42units 42 units

(b)DFRB pension equated as a number of superannuation units 21.23 units 21.23 units
(c)Difference (a minus b) 20.77 units 20.77 units

(d) Fortnightly rate of contribution for number of units stated as the "difference" $14.12 $ 9.35

However, if you are accepted as a provident account contributor you will be required to contribute fortnightly approximately 5% of your salary for superannuation purposes (i.e. $10.70 on $5533).

There is no standard form of election under Section 22 but you should note that it should be made in writing with your intention clearly stated. The election should be signed and dated by yourself and forwarded to the Personnel Section of your Department who will then make suitable arrangements for you to be medically examined.

I am forwarding a copy of this letter to your Department."

Mr May responded promptly to this letter by sending a handwritten minute dated 13 July 1973 to the Personnel Section of the Department of Supply in the following terms:-

"Election for Superannuation Benefits

In accordance with the attached letter from the Superannuation Board I submit a late election to contribute to the Commonwealth superannuation fund.

I wish to contribute for a selected retiring age of 65 years at the rate of $9.35 per fortnight in respect of 20.77 units as calculated by the Board."

On or about the 23 August 1973 the applicant underwent the required medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer, who recommended that the applicant be accepted for the superannuation fund. It appears however that the form of election was mistakenly retained by the Department of Supply and that the medical examination report was forwarded by mistake to the Public Service Inspector's office. It was not until 7 December, 1973, that the medical examination report was received by the Superannuation Board and not until the 11 December, 1973, that the form of election was received by the Board.

A submission was then placed before the Superannuation Board at its meeting held on 24-25 January 1974 recommending that the period of time for making of an election under s22 (1) of the 1971 Act should be extended. The Board considered the recommendation but deferred a decision pending the receipt of additional information

on the reasons for the delay in making an election. By letter dated 19 February 1974 the applicant was requested to provide this information. By a reply 25 February 1974 the applicant stated as follows (omitting formal paragraphs):-

"Regrettably at the time I was advised that I was ineligible because I was in receipt of a DFRBF pension - paid from a higher category than my Public Service category number. Until such time as there was a difference, I could not contribute to the fund and therefore election would be futile.

At the time, my salary was about $3,500, compared to $5,000 (approx.) per annum on discharge from the Army. Salary levels being the rule of thumb, it was expected that I would be ineligible to become a contributor, until such time as my Public Service salary exceeded my army discharge salary. This occurred at about the middle of last year, when I immediately began taking steps to elect myself to become a contributor.

It is unfortunate, but I never realised that regardless of the salary difference at the time, I should have elected then. Therefore, I trust that this explanation will suffice, to remove any further obstacles preventing my election as a contributor."

Mr May informed us that he had a number of telephone conversations with a Mr Antoniak, an officer of the Superannuation Board, inquiring as to the progress of his case and discussing the matter generally. Mr May had no record of his conversations and he was unable to recall the details of the conversations. He was adamant, however, that he would have explained to Mr Antoniak his discussion with the staff clerk at the Department of Defence and the advice that the staff clerk had given him.

Mr Antoniak was not called to give evidence, but the following file note of a telephone conversation between Mr Antoniak and Mr May on 26 March 1974 was before us as part of Exhibit T4:-

"Phoned Mr May and discussed case with him. It appears that Mr May did not at any stage receive personal counsel from his Department in relation to entitlement to become a contributor under the Act. His only information on this matter was extracted from a circular issued by his Department some 3 years ago (probably about the time of the introduction Preservation legislation). It is at this time that he formed the opinion that he could not elect to become a contributor until such time as his Public Service salary became greater than the salary which he was receiving at the time of discharge from the Defence Force. Mr May is endeavouring to obtain a copy of the Departmental Circular referred to above for our office records. He will contact me shortly."

At the foot of the same page was a further note dated 23 April 1974 as follows:-' "Mr May phoned to advise that copies of defence circular found - is sending copy to me - copies received."

The information contained in Mr May's letter of 25 February 1974 together with a precis of Mr Antoniak's record of his telephone conversation with Mr May on 26 March 1974 was incorporated in a further submission placed before the Superannuation Board at its meeting on 14-15 June 1974. Again, there was a recommendation by the Manager, Operations Branch that Mr May be granted an extension of time for making his election.

The Board however determined that it was not satisfied that there were special circumstances that justified it in extending the period in which Mr May could make his election. Whilst there is no record of the reasons for the Board's decision, we can only assume that, based on Mr Antoniak's report of his conversation with the applicant, the Board must have concluded that the applicant failed to seek proper advice as to his rights and had formed his own opinion (erroneously as it turned out) that he was not eligible to make an election. The Board apparently declined to accept, at face value, the applicant's statement in his letter of 25 February 1974 that he had been "advised" that he was ineligible to do so.

Advice of this decision was conveyed to Mr May by letter from the Secretary of the Board dated 18 July 1974. Mr May was not happy with the decision and made further inquiries from the staff clerk from the Department of Supply and from Mr Antoniak at the Superannuation Board. He was told that he could not do anything further about making a late election unless the Superannuation Act was changed. Mr May said that Mr Antoniak also suggested that he might wait for the introduction of the new superannuation scheme which was close at hand and promised to be more beneficial than the 1922 Act. We were told there was no record on the Superannuation Board's file of any such conversation, but we do not find this surprising or of any critical importance.

The new superannuation scheme was not in fact introduced until 1 July 1976. It contained no provisions which assisted the applicant to obtain recognition of his earlier service. Accordingly the applicant made representations through a Member of Parliament to have his case reconsidered by the Commissioner for Superannuation. In support of his application Mr May wrote a letter to the Commissioner dated 30 July 1978 which, so far as relevant, was as follows:-

"As pointed out in previous correspondence, in 1971 I was misinformed about election requirements under Section 22 of the Superannuation Act. On approaching the Superannuation Board in 1974, and more recently your Office in 1978, I was informed that a late election was not possible unless a change was made to the Act. The Officer I spoke to in the first instance was a Mr Antoniak, who also suggested at the time that I wait for introduction of the new Superannuation Scheme; which appeared to be close at hand and promised to be more beneficial than the Act now provides.

I trust you will understand that the thought of trying to change an Act of Parliament for my own personal benefit seemed rather frightening. However, following belated introduction of the present Superannuation Scheme in 1976, and disappointed at the prospect of such dismal accumulated retirement benefits available to me and my family after a whole lifetime of Commonwealth service (age 17 to a possible age 65), on receipt of your letter of 22 June 1978 I sought help from Senator Knight."

As previously indicated, the Commissioner extended the time under s154(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 for requesting reconsideration of the Superannuation Board's decision made at its meeting 14-15 June 1974. On reconsideration, however, the Commissioner refused to grant an extension of time because he was not satisfied that there were special circumstances that justified him in so doing.

Miss Vardanega referred us to a number of authorities where the expression "special circumstances" has been judicially considered. Each of these cases tended, not surprisingly, to turn on its own particular circumstances. The following passage from the judgment of Lopes LJ in Re Norman (1886) 16 QBD 674 at p.677, however, is representative of the views expressed in these authorities:-

"The statute uses the words "special circumstances". Those are wide, comprehensive and flexible words and I think the legislature intended them to be so and that no Court can or ought to lay down any exhaustive definition of them...."

See also Re Welsh and the Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALD 101 at p102 where this Tribunal said, in relation to the same expression appearing in a section of the Superannuation Act 1976:-

"The very use of the word "special" is designed to make it possible for the Commissioner to consider each case individually in relation to its own particular circumstances; cf Ex parte Bucknell [1936] HCA 67; (1936) 56 CLR 221 at 224. Nevertheless, the discretion must be exercised with a view to achieving ends or objects which are conformable with the scope and object of the legislation: see Shrimpton v Commonwealth [1945] HCA 4; (1945) 69 CLR 613 at 620 per Latham C.J."

Having had the benefit of hearing the evidence of Mr May, corroborated as it is by the evidence of Mr Zatorski, we are quite satisfied that there are special circumstances in this case to justify extending the period for the making of an election under s22 (1) of the Superannuation Act 1971. Our reasons for being so satisfied are briefly as follows:-(i) We accept Mr May's evidence that when Office Notice No. 1971/77 came to his attention he sought advice from the staff clerk in the Department of Defence at the first available opportunity concerning his rights under the amending legislation.

(ii) We accept his evidence that he was incorrectly advised as to what his rights were. In particular we are satisfied that he was led to believe that he must still wait until his public service salary-exceeded his army pay on retirement before he would be eligible to make an election. Having regard to the applicant's army background we find it quite understandable that he should have accepted the staff clerk's advice without question. (iii) All the correspondence written by the applicant either to the Superannuation Board or to the Commissioner for Superannuation since the applicant first attempted to join the superannuation scheme is entirely consistent with the applicant's evidence that he was misled by the staff clerk of the Department of Defence as to his rights.

(iii) The only evidence which might suggest anything to the contrary is the file note dated 26 March 1974 regarding Mr Antoniak's understanding of his discussion with the applicant - a note, we might add, of which he was unaware prior to the institution of these proceedings. However, having heard the applicant's evidence, we have concluded that Mr Antoniak placed an interpretation upon the information conveyed by the applicant which did not correctly represent the applicant's position. We hasten to add that we do not suggest for the moment that any-such false impression was conveyed deliberately by Mr Antoniak or that it was conveyed with the intention of harming the applicant's case. (v) We think it is clear that, at all material times since the amendment made by the 1971 Act came into operation, the applicant has indicated his desire to join the superannuation fund if it was open to him to do so. His action in setting money aside to pay for arrears of contributions should his efforts prove successful is, in our view, very significant. But for the incorrect advice given to him by the staff clerk from the Department of Defence and but for the failure of the staff clerk, in any event, to submit Mr May's name and particulars to the Superannuation Board to enable the Board to consider his entitlements, we do not have the slightest doubt that Mr May would have joined the superannuation fund by making an election within the time originally allowed by Parliament. He has been frustrated in that desire through no fault of his own.

Because the superannuation fund established under the 1922 Act is no longer in existence, we were informed that whilst there may now be some administrative difficulties in giving effect to an election by the applicant, these difficulties should not be insuperable. In the circumstances, we have decided that we should set aside the decision of the Board as confirmed by the Commissioner and in place of that decision decide that the period of time for the making by the applicant of an election under s22 (1) of the Superannuation Act 1971 should be extended to the 11 December 1973 being the date on which Mr May's election dated 13 July 1973 was actually received by the Superannuation Board. In so deciding, we leave it to the Commissioner to decide whether effect should be given to the election on the 13 July 1973 being the date on which the election was actually made by the applicant. We leave this question open as we are not aware whether the applicant may be prejudiced in any way by the delay within his own Department in submitting the election to the Superannuation Board. If there is any prejudice we express the view that such prejudice should be avoided if at all possible.
