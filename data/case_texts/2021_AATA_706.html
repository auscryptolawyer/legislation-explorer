INTRODUCTION

  1. The Applicant seeks review under section 30E of the Industry Research and Development Act 1986 (“the IR & D Act” ) of an internal review decision made on 9 August 2016 by a delegate of the Respondent of a decision made on 24 November 2015 under section 27B of the IR & D Act that certain activities were not a “Core R & D activity” for the purposes of the IR & D Act and also that they were not “Supporting R & D activities” within the meaning of the IR & D Act.

BACKGROUND

  1. Mr A is the driving force behind the Applicant.[1] He had for some time prior to the events relevant to this application been employed by a construction and property development company established by his father. This company will be referred to as PLPD. In various projects undertaken by PLPD it had encountered problems with contaminated soil. Apparently, soil contamination is a difficulty frequently encountered in property developments. Contaminated soil is difficult and expensive to manage. The frequency of soil contamination and the experience that PLPD and Mr A had with the problem prompted him to look for more effective, efficient and sustainable ways of site remediation particularly in relation to the treatment of contaminated soils. His research revealed that contaminated land and its remediation in Australia is a billion-dollar industry. Given the recent well-known difficulties that have been encountered with the West Gate tunnel project (amongst others) as a result of significant soil contamination of that site, this is fully understandable.
  2. Mr A formed a belief that it was possible to establish a business based upon remediation of contaminated soils. He commenced detailed technical investigations to find the best soil treatment technologies with a view to bringing them to Australia. During this time, he spent two years in Europe working on construction and waste treatment projects. He met several persons engaged in developing waste and soil remediation processes. Ultimately, he met a German engineer, Mr Von Christen, who was a director of a German Corporation, TechTrade GmbH (“TechTrade”), which has designed and installed plants which apply what is known as “pyrolysis technology”. This included the design and construction of a plant in Herne, Germany, which used pyrolysis to treat contaminated soil.
  3. Pyrolysis is apparently a “very old process” which involves decomposing organic material by heating it in the substantial absence of air. Its application has occurred over many years to different materials. Examples given were of forming charcoal from wood or town gas from coal which was a common method of gas extraction prior to the introduction of natural gas. Another definition was said to be that of a process for disintegration of organic substances at elevated temperatures under an inert atmosphere. The technically preferred “reactor” was described as a heated rotary drum.[2]
  4. After carrying out his investigations, Mr A believed that pyrolysis technology could be used to establish a soil treatment facility in Victoria. Mr A returned to Australia and, with his father, established the Applicant. They have an equal shareholding in it. Mr A is its sole director.
  5. Not much more needs to be said about this in these reasons other than that these endeavours led to the construction and commission of a soil treatment facility in April 2015. Initially, the facility operated under what is known as a commissioning approval issued by the Environment Protection Authority (“EPA”). This continued until 30 June 2016 when the EPA issued a full operating licence.
  6. Relevantly for the purposes of this application, when the soil treatment plant was in the later stages of construction in approximately 2014, Mr A commenced investigating whether pyrolysis technology could be applied to the processing of what is known as municipal solid waste (“MSW”). Most MSW generated in Victoria is disposed of in landfill sites. There is considerable interest, on environmental grounds amongst others, in developing alternate means of disposal.
  7. Apparently, processing of MSW involves a very different application of pyrolysis technology. The main reason for this is because of the differing feedstock, namely MSW, rather than contaminated soil. Also, the different application of technology is required because different outputs, or end products, are intended to be produced using such a process, namely electricity, fuel and what is known as “char”.[3] Naturally, any facility which would process MSW had to be capable of producing sufficient electricity, fuel and char to make it commercially feasible, whilst at the same time maintaining best practice environmental standards.
  8. On 15 August 2014, the Applicant signed a consultancy agreement with PLPD.[4] The terms of the consultancy agreement need not be recorded in detail as it is in evidence before the Tribunal.[5] There are several terms of the consultancy agreement which are not controversial. Under its terms PLPD agreed to provide the Applicant with the following services:

(a) Developing a process to enable the pyrolysis of MSW to produce a synthesis gas (“syngas”) with consistent quality, including:

(i) Designing process parameters with capability for the varying MSW inputs, with each component undergoing varying decomposition reaction pathways; and

(ii) Designing methods for MSW preparation to improve consistency of the input;

(b) Developing a process that can switch the syngas between combustion for electricity generation or cleaning and condensing for fuel production;

(c) Developing a process for cleaning the syngas, which could have varying composition, such that sufficient quality fuel or amounts of electricity can be produced; and

(d) Any other services required from time to time by the Applicant.

  1. The consultancy agreement provided for three phases of work:

(a) Phase 1 - provision of a Preliminary Design Report including Technical Summary and Design Overview and technical specifications for key proprietary equipment;

(b) Phase 2 - provision of a Detailed Design Report including technical specifications for the auxiliary equipment; and

(c) Phase 3 - provision of plant Construction Drawings.

  1. PLPD provided the services required under the consultancy agreement between August 2014 and June 2015.
  2. On 11 November 2014, PLPD produced a report entitled “MSW Waste to Energy Facility Project”, “140,000 t/a”, “Technical Summary and Final Design Overview Report”.[6]
  3. In approximately January 2015, PLPD produced a set of Plant Layout Construction Drawings and Plant Construction Drawings[7] which included the following:

The scoping report and project implementation plan were to be used by the Applicant for the development of the research and development (“R & D”) Project Facility. The various tasks undertaken by Lycopodium were supervised by Paul Morey, a Senior Project Manager. He gave evidence at the hearing of this application both by way of two witness statements and in the witness box.

It includes several appendices comprising an array of plans, specifications and calculations. There are, by way of example, electrical specifications (for such things as installation of electrical systems, electrical systems for packaged equipment and electrical specifications for low voltage electric motors), mechanical specifications and detailed technical information requirements.

  1. On 24 November 2015, the Respondent refused the application for registration under section 27B of the IRD Act on the grounds that:

(a) Activity 1.1 was not a core R & D activity for the purposes of the IRD Act; and

(b) Activities 1.1.1 and 1.1.2 could not be supporting R & D activities.

  1. The Applicant sought an internal review of the decision of 24 November 2015.
  2. The internal review was completed by the Respondent on 9 August 2016 and the decision of 24 November 2015 was affirmed. The Applicant seeks review of that decision in this Tribunal. The grounds relied upon by the Respondent in affirming the 24 November 2015 decision refusing the application for registration were that:

(a) The Applicant had not demonstrated that Activity 1.1 was a core R & D activity, as:[11]

(i) The Applicant had “not demonstrated that the outcomes of its experiments cannot be known or determined in advance on the basis of current knowledge, information or experience” and that the proposal only involved “optimising...... technology and processes [known to work] to account for different composition of municipal solid waste streams”;

(ii) “the project activity relates to operational challenges that a qualified professional could overcome using their experience and knowledge in the field” and the Applicant “has not provided a detailed description of any experiments undertaken to address a scientific theory”;

(iii) The Applicant had “not demonstrated that the activity was conducted for the purpose of generating new knowledge...” and “the knowledge being sought does not go beyond validating existing processes and knowledge in its particular circumstances”; and

(b) As Activity 1.1 was not a core R & D activity, Activities 1.1.1 and 1.1.2 (as the claimed supporting R & D activities for Activity 1.1) could not be supporting R & D activities.

APPLICABLE LEGISLATION

  1. This application requires a consideration of not only sections of the IR & D Act but also the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (“the ITA Act”).
  2. The applicable parts of the ITA Act are found in Division 355 – Research and Development. Sections 355-5, 355-25 and 355-30 are relevant. It is useful to reproduce them in full.

“Subdivision 355-A – Object” of the ITA Act provides:

Section 355-5 Object

355-5 (1) The object of this Division is to encourage industry to conduct research and development activities that might otherwise not be conducted because of an uncertain return from the activities, in cases where the knowledge gained is likely to benefit the wider Australian economy.

355-5 (2) The object is to be achieved by providing a tax incentive for industry to conduct, in a scientific way, experimental activities for the purpose of generating new knowledge or information in either a general or applied form (including new knowledge in the form of new or improved materials, products, devices, processes or services).



“Subdivision 355-B – Meaning of R & D activities and other terms” includes the following sections which provide:

355‑25 Core R&D activities

(1) Core R&D activities are experimental activities:

(a) whose outcome cannot be known or determined in advance on the basis of current knowledge, information or experience, but can only be determined by applying a systematic progression of work that:

(i) is based on principles of established science; and

(ii) proceeds from hypothesis to experiment, observation and evaluation, and leads to logical conclusions; and

(b) that are conducted for the purpose of generating new knowledge (including new knowledge in the form of new or improved materials, products, devices, processes or services).

(2) However, none of the following activities are core R&D activities:

(a) market research, market testing or market development, or sales promotion (including consumer surveys);

(b) prospecting, exploring or drilling for minerals or *petroleum for the purposes of one or more of the following:

(i) discovering deposits;

(ii) determining more precisely the location of deposits;

(iii) determining the size or quality of deposits;

(c) management studies or efficiency surveys;

(d) research in social sciences, arts or humanities;

(e) commercial, legal and administrative aspects of patenting, licensing or other activities;

(f) activities associated with complying with statutory requirements or standards, including one or more of the following:

(i) maintaining national standards;

(ii) calibrating secondary standards;

(iii) routine testing and analysis of materials, components, products, processes, soils, atmospheres and other things;

(g) any activity related to the reproduction of a commercial product or process:

(i) by a physical examination of an existing system; or

(ii) from plans, blueprints, detailed specifications or publically available information;

(h) developing, modifying or customising computer software for the dominant purpose of use by any of the following entities for their internal administration (including the internal administration of their business functions):

(i) the entity (the developer) for which the software is developed, modified or customised;

(ii) an entity *connected with the developer;

(iii) an *affiliate of the developer, or an entity of which the developer is an affiliate.

355‑30 Supporting R&D activities

(1) Supporting R&D activities are activities directly related to *core R&D activities.

(2) However, if an activity:

(a) is an activity referred to in subsection 355‑25(2); or

(b) produces goods or services; or

(c) is directly related to producing goods or services;

the activity is a supporting R&D activity only if it is undertaken for the dominant purpose of supporting *core R&D activities.

  1. The objects contained in section 3 of the IR & D Act should also be considered for the purposes of this application. The section need not be reproduced in its entirety. However, amongst other things, such objects include supporting and encouraging collaboration in the development and delivery of programs relating to industry, innovation, science and research. A further object is the promotion of the development and improvement of the efficiency and international competitiveness of Australian industry by encouraging R & D activities, innovation and science activities and venture capital activities. It is perhaps useful to acknowledge that the language used in drafting this section places some emphasis on the considerations of Australian industry and venture capital activities. The Tribunal considers that the industry and commercial quotient of these objects must be borne in mind when considering the substance of this application. In construing the relevant legislation applicable to this application and applying it to the relevant facts, these industry-based objects direct the decision-maker towards industry and venture capital considerations. These may differ from those that might be applicable to an institutional or academic environment, such as may be the case at the CSIRO or universities. Therefore, the task of construing this legislation must be undertaken with a degree of realism, adopting a beneficial and commercial approach, consistent with the text and objects referred to, for the purpose of encouraging research and development in a commercial setting.
  2. It should be noted that under section 4 the IR & D Act the definitions of “core R & D activities” and “supporting R & D activities” have the same meaning as the ITA Act.
  3. Under section 27A of the IR & D Act, when considering a R & D entity’s application the Respondent must decide whether to register that entity for all or part of the claimed activities as either core or supporting R & D activities.
  4. Several points concerning the construction of section 355-25(1) of the ITA Act warrant mention.
  5. The Respondent contended, and the Tribunal agrees, that the term “experimental” should be given its usual and ordinary meaning.[12] The Applicant also agrees with this approach. In construing the words “experimental activities” in section 355-25 of the ITA Act however, the text, object and purpose of Division 355 of the ITA Act should be recalled.
  6. As noted by the Full Court of the Federal Court of Australia recently in Moreton Resources Limited v Innovation and Science Australia [2019] FCAFC 120 (Moreton Resources FCAFC) (a case decided since this application was heard), the purpose and object of Division 355 of the ITA Act is to:

“encourage industry to conduct research and development activities that might otherwise not be conducted because of an uncertain return from such activities, in cases where the knowledge gained is likely to benefit the wider Australian economy.” Such object is achieved by “providing a tax incentive for industry to conduct, in a scientific way, experimental activities for the purpose of generating new knowledge or information “in either a general or applied form (including new knowledge in the form of new or improved materials, products, devices, processes or services)””.

  1. The Full Court also held that the words “experimental activities” used in section 355-25 are capable of applying, depending on the circumstances, to activities that are conducted for the purpose of generating new knowledge with respect to the application of an existing technology at a new site.[13]
  2. The Full Court of the Federal Court of Australia also noted in Moreton Resources FCAFC, with respect to the words “experimental activities” in the opening line of section 355-25(1), that they have little, if any, work to do beyond reflecting the type of activities described in paragraphs (a) and (b) of that subsection. Therefore, the Full Court opined that given the detail and content of the description in subparagraphs (a) and (b) of that subsection, it would be difficult to envisage activities that would meet the description of those paragraphs and not be considered “experimental activities” in the relevant sense required by that subsection.[14]
  3. The treatment of activities as “experimental” within the meaning of section 355-25(1)(a) of the ITA Act is determined by whether the outcome itself can be known or determined in advance. There can be no core R & D activities within the meaning of the section if the outcome is known, or capable of being determined in advance. Such an outcome also being only capable of being determined by the application of a systemic progression of work, that is based upon principles of established science, and proceeds from hypothesis to experiment, observation and evaluation, and leads to logical conclusions.
  4. The process or progression of the work described, rather than the outcome or result, is the factor which the Tribunal must consider when applying section 355-25(1)(a) of the ITA Act. Such outcome or result however, is relevant by reason of the need to consider whether it could have been known in advance.
  5. Section 355-25(1)(b) of the ITA Act addresses the purpose of the “experimental activities” which prefaced subsection (1). The term “purpose” in the context of this section has been considered in several cases before this Tribunal. In JSLP and Innovation Australia[15] it was considered that the purpose need not be the dominant purpose motivating the activities but must be more than an insubstantial purpose. Deputy President Forgie in Moreton Resources and Innovation and Science Australia[16] (Moreton Resources AAT), after a most helpful analysis which is referred to, adopted a different approach to construing the term “purpose” and considered that ultimately, whether experimental activities are conducted for “the purpose” of generating new knowledge is a matter to be decided on the evidence in a particular case and not according to a characterisation of the purpose as substantial, more than insubstantial and so on. The Applicant contends, and the Tribunal accepts, such contention that either approach to construction of the term “purpose” is unlikely to lead to a different outcome. This Tribunal prefers the approach to construing the term “purpose” adopted by Deputy President Forgie in Moreton Resources AAT. The construction adopted by her conforms with the text of the section which should always assume primacy. The language used in the section does not invite a consideration or introduction of concepts such as “dominant”, “substantial” or “more than insubstantial”. The section is capable of construction and application to a fact situation without the necessity of introducing such concepts.
  6. As the purpose of generating new knowledge within the meaning of section 355-25(1)(b) of the ITA Act does not have to be a dominant one, such purpose is capable of coexisting with other purposes. This approach is also consistent with the industry and commercial quotient of the objects, amongst others, in section 3 of the IR & D Act referred to previously.

THE ISSUES BEFORE THE TRIBUNAL

  1. The issues to be determined by the Tribunal are whether:

(b) the activities comprising Supporting Activities 1.1.1 and 1.1.2 were “Supporting R & D activities” within the meaning of s355-30 of the ITA Act.

OBJECTIONS TO VARIOUS PARTS OF THE EVIDENCE OF PROFESSOR HAYNES AND MR MOREY

  1. The Applicant objected to several paragraphs and parts of paragraphs of Professor Haynes’ first report.
  2. The Respondent objected to several paragraphs of Mr Morey’s second statement.
  3. The parties to the application were content for the Tribunal to rule upon each of the objections as part of its final reasons determining the application.

Objections to the evidence of Professor Haynes

  1. The Applicant objects to several paragraphs of Professor Haynes’ first report. The grounds of the objections have been formulated by the Applicant in a written submission. Helpfully, there is a schedule to the Applicant’s written submission which identifies those paragraphs to which objection is taken. There are two grounds of objection relied upon by the Applicant. The first ground of objection is relevance. The second ground of objection is that the Applicant contends that Professor Haynes reviews documents and purports to express an opinion on their evidentiary value. This is also contended to be a value judgement expressed by him about the utility or weight to be given to other evidence.
  2. In developing its contentions concerning the relevance of the selected portions of Professor Haynes’ first report, the Applicant commences by examining the precise words used in the letter of instruction sent to Professor Haynes on 25 July 2017 and identifying what he was specifically asked to do. It is not necessary for the purposes of these reasons to recount the language of the letter used other than to observe that the first question requested Professor Haynes to identify, with as much precision as possible, what the activities claimed by the Applicant to be “Core R & D Activities” and “Supporting R & D Activities” were, and whether they were conducted between 1 July 2014 and 30 June 2015.
  3. It is contended by the Applicant that Professor Haynes’ answer to the first question contained in the letter of instruction addressed to him on 25 July 2017 contains little more than commentary on the documents and his personal and subjective non-expert observations on their evidentiary value. Further, it is contended that he included arguments about the time when such documents may have been produced and examples of these arguments were given.
  4. The alternate ground of objection raised by the Applicant is that the portions of Professor Haynes’ first report objected to are not within the realms of proper expert evidence as he purports to express an opinion on the utility and weight of other evidence. This is contended by the Applicant as a matter for the Tribunal. An analogy was drawn with the provisions of the uniform Evidence Act 1995 which need not be reproduced here.
  5. The Applicant developed the contention that whilst the “ultimate issue” rule has been abolished, the Tribunal was not assisted by the approach adopted by Professor Haynes. The Applicant contends that Professor Haynes has, in effect, sought to embark upon the function of the Tribunal with respect to fact-finding, as it is the role of the Tribunal to determine these matters rather than the experts. In support of this contention, the decision of Heydon J in Dasreef Pty Ltd v Hawchar[28] (Dasreef) was referred to.
  6. The attack on the evidence of Professor Haynes by the Applicant was resisted by the Respondent for several reasons. These reasons will be briefly summarised hereunder.
  7. The first ground of resistance to the objections taken by the Respondent was to assert that they were mistaken at a conceptual level. This is because the Tribunal is not bound by the rules of evidence and is able to inform itself in any matter it thinks appropriate.[29]
  8. Developing this contention, it is said that the rules of evidence, having been excluded specifically by the Act which governs the function and operation of this Tribunal should not be permitted to "creep back” through the exercise by the Tribunal of a discretion, or domestic procedural rule.
  9. Secondly, it is contended that the approach adopted by the Applicant to, as it was put by the Respondent, overcome the statutory exclusion of the rules of evidence to ascertain the requirements of the principles of natural justice, was an argument that could not be sustained in this case. The contention is that the requirements of procedural fairness or natural justice in this case have been fully observed. The grounds for making these assertions were several, including that Professor Haynes’ report had been served over

    12 months before the hearing. Further, Professor Haynes was open to

    cross-examination, in which he could be challenged on any opinion expressed in such report. Finally, it was open to the Applicant to make any submission as it may be advised concerning the contents of Professor Haynes’ report.
  10. The third ground of resistance from Respondent to the Applicant’s challenges to the relevant portions of Professor Haynes’ first report is prefaced on the fact that in all relevant Australian jurisdictions the “ultimate issue” rule has been abolished by the advent of the Uniform Evidence Acts. By reason of this fact, the appropriately qualified expert may express an opinion about a fact in issue, or as noted earlier the “ultimate issue”. Naturally, this does not mean that the court, or in this case the Tribunal, is bound to accept such evidence. For that matter, it is not obliged to give it any particular weight. The point being it is not inadmissible as noted earlier. Therefore, this is not a ground for rejecting such evidence, or otherwise ruling it inadmissible.
  11. The fourth ground relied upon by the Respondent to resist the Applicant’s challenges to several portions of Professor Haynes’ first report concerns the application of the reasoning of Heydon J in Dasreef referred to earlier. To some extent the grounds as articulated in the Respondent’s submission on this topic overlapped with that of the third ground. It is that paragraph 56 of Heydon J’s decision in Dasreef does not represent, as it was described, “an exclusionary rule” but the role of, and perhaps more accurately, the approach to be adopted concerning expert evidence. It was repeated that the rules of evidence (or more precisely the applicable Uniform Evidence Acts that have been introduced in most Australian jurisdictions) have abolished the “ultimate issue” rule, and permit an expert in certain circumstances to express an opinion on matters that ultimately must be determined by the court or tribunal, as the case may be. To do so requires the expert’s evidence to explain how the field of specialised knowledge in which such witness is expert by reason of training, study or experience, and on which the opinion is wholly or substantially based, applies to the facts assumed or observed so as to produce the opinion propounded.[30] As the Respondent contends (properly relying upon paragraph 56 in Dasreef), frequently this requirement can be met very quickly and easily with little articulation or amplification once the expert witness has identified his qualifications and experience and identified the subject matter about which their opinion is proffered.
  12. The fifth ground of resistance articulated by the Respondent is prefaced upon the question actually asked of Professor Haynes as an expert. It is contended that this question, and the response of Professor Haynes, contained an appropriately identified subject matter about which the opinion was proffered in, as it may be called, the Dasreef sense. This contention is amplified by the Respondent noting that in its view, the evidence available to Professor Haynes at the time he produced his first report was limited in some ways to ways what it described as a level of generality approaching vagary. It also contended that on the information available at the time Professor Haynes prepared his report it was not clear what activities in fact had been undertaken by the Applicant. Therefore, it is contended that once it is accepted that the matters on which Professor Haynes opines were properly the subject of expert evidence, it follows that the opinions are relevant. It was readily conceded that the assessment of evidence is ultimately a matter for the Tribunal. However, given the technical nature of such evidence, it is contended by the Respondent that Professor Haynes’ description of what was done, and what was not done, is of considerable weight.

Ruling on the objections to Professor Haynes’ evidence

  1. In ruling on the Applicant’s objections to the evidence of Professor Haynes, the Tribunal acknowledges that section 33(1)(c) of the AAT Act provides that the Tribunal is not bound by the rules of evidence. It is also acknowledged, as contended by the Respondent, that in any event the provisions of the Uniform Evidence Acts as have now been introduced throughout most Australian jurisdictions have abolished the “ultimate issue” rule. It is also acknowledged that when one considers the decision of Dasreef in the High Court of Australia, Heydon J in his consideration of the applicable principles acknowledged that he was not considering an exclusionary rule, but rather the appropriate role of expert evidence.
  2. Several things should be observed in addressing the thrust of these submissions. It should not be forgotten that the rules of evidence emerged in the common law, various Evidence Acts in common law jurisdictions, and subsequent Uniform Evidence Acts in most Australian jurisdictions for a good reason. They are a refined form of procedural fairness that recognise there must be a limit on evidence received by a court on matters that it is called upon to adjudicate. Such things as the rules against hearsay have evolved for obvious reasons.[31] In other respects such as s 69 of the Evidence Act 1995 (Cth), “Exception: business records”, the rules facilitate the reception in evidence of documents kept by an organisation in the course of, or for the purposes of its business, to facilitate the ease of proof of financial, and other frequently recurring transactions. It is of assistance to the court. Surely, this approach is also a sensible one to adopt in Tribunal procedure.
  3. The provisions of the AAT Act do not give the Tribunal licence to engage in an evidentiary “free for all”. Nor would the Tribunal be assisted by such an approach. The rules of evidence generally in Tribunal practice (and in this Tribunal) should be applied unless there is good reason not to do so. This approach has been enshrined in several leading authorities concerning the practice of this Tribunal over many years.
  4. The Tribunal accepts the Respondent’s submission that, given the provisions of section 33(1)(c) of the AAT Act, the paragraphs in Professor Haynes’ statement complained of by the Applicant should not be excluded. In the view of the Tribunal, it is a question of the appropriate role of the expert, in this case Professor Haynes. It is in this context that the Tribunal accepts the alternative submission of the Applicant that, whilst the “ultimate issue” rule has been abolished, and experts can give evidence on ultimate issues, the Tribunal will not be assisted by the evidence given by Professor Haynes in the paragraphs of which the Applicant complains. Therefore, the Tribunal places no weight upon them.
  5. There are several reasons for reaching this conclusion. The starting point is that of course this is a matter for the Tribunal to decide after having all the evidence placed before it, both documentary and viva voce. Professor Haynes, in making his first statement it is quite apparent, did not have before him all the evidence that was adduced before the Tribunal over four hearing days.
  6. Another reason why the Tribunal cannot place any real weight or utility on those parts of Professor Haynes’ statement objected to by the Applicant is that his report, and the opinions expressed in it on those topics, were based upon a review or consideration of an incomplete part of all the evidence. He was not asked to reconsider the opinions expressed (as contended for by the Applicant) after the Respondent was served with further witness statements of Mr A and Mr Morey, prepared on behalf of the Applicant. Having made this observation, it is of course acknowledged that Professor Haynes prepared a second report (which did not refer to any of the further witness statements of Mr A or Mr Morey), and gave viva voce evidence at the hearing.
  7. The Applicant noted that after receipt of the second witness statements, or more accurately as it describes them, the second series of witness statements, the Respondent filed and served its Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions which did not identify as an issue, or advance any contention (as one would have expected) as to whether the Core R & D Activities were carried out.
  8. The first question in the letter of instruction to Professor Haynes of 25 July 2017,[32] requested that he provide his opinion on the activities claimed by the Applicant to be Core R & D Activities and Supporting R & D activities and whether they were conducted between 1 July 2014 and 30 June 2015, as described in the witness statements of Mr A and Mr Morey dated 12 May 2017. Given that the Respondent has accepted that the activities described in the application for registration dated 19 August 2015 were carried out, the Tribunal accepts the contention of the Applicant that such a question calls for an irrelevant answer with respect to such activities, including the Core

    R & D Activities.[33]
  9. Finally, another reason for accepting the contention of the Applicant that those portions of Professor Haynes’ witness statement are of limited utility or weight, arises from the findings that are made by the Tribunal based on the evidence of the Applicant’s witnesses that the activities, or work, were carried out within the relevant financial year, which are addressed further in these reasons below. Therefore, the Tribunal accepts the contention of the Applicant that, in response to the first question contained in the letter of instruction to him of 25 July 2017,[34] several of the paragraphs objected to contain little more than commentary on documents produced to Professor Haynes, his personal and subjective non-expert observations on their evidentiary value, together with arguments concerning when such documents may have been created.[35] However, it must be stressed that this consideration whilst an important one, is by no means the determining factor.
  10. The Tribunal finds that it is not assisted by an expert such as Professor Haynes conducting a review of the documents and expressing an opinion on their evidentiary status.

Objections to the evidence of Mr Morey

  1. The Respondent objects to paragraphs 16-18, 29-51, and 61-64 of Mr Morey’s second statement.
  2. Similarly, the grounds of objection have been formulated by the Respondent in a written submission. Submissions were made at the hearing from the bar table by Mr Knowles in support of those grounds of objection.
  3. The grounds of objection are twofold:

(a) That the evidence is opinion evidence and it has not been shown that Mr Morey has relevant expertise; and

(b) That Mr Morey purports to give expert evidence outside procedures envisaged by the Tribunal’s Persons Giving Expert and Opinion Evidence Guideline 2015 in circumstances where the independent experts have prepared a joint report and have given concurrent evidence.

  1. In support of the first objection to Mr Morey’s evidence, it is contended by the Respondent that he is a senior project manager with qualifications in mechanical and computer engineering. He is not a chemical or process engineer and has no established expertise in pyrolysis. It is then said that, despite this lack of relevant expertise, he has purported to critique the views of Professor Haynes.
  2. In oral submissions on behalf of the Respondent it was acknowledged and not disputed that Mr Morey has an engineering qualification, and so to the extent that any of his evidence related to the design, for example of machine components, his mechanical engineering degree (and one would also contend his experience) may well qualify him as an expert in respect of those opinions.
  3. The further ground of objection to the specified paragraphs of Mr Morey’s second witness statement arise from the fact that both parties to this application had retained qualified independent experts. Those experts had participated in a conclave, prepared a joint report and given concurrent evidence. It was contended by the Respondent in both its written oral submissions, that the Tribunal’s processes were being circumvented by Mr Morey’s approach, or more specifically, his evidence. This was achieved, it was contended, by the Applicant calling a separate “non-independent expert.” The Respondent submitted that this leads to a distraction and unnecessary imposition on the Tribunal’s time.
  4. The tenor of this submission was also amplified by the Respondent because it said that Mr Morey cannot be independent in circumstances where one of the issues which is raised by the experts is the quality of the work which his firm undertook. Reference was also made in submissions by the Respondent’s counsel to the fact that the Tribunal’s Persons Giving Expert and Opinion Evidence Guideline 2015 requires a person who is giving evidence based upon their special knowledge or expertise not to be an advocate for the party calling them. That practice note also highlights a common feature of codes of conduct for experts in many jurisdictions that they have an overriding duty to provide impartial assistance to the Tribunal. Developing this contention, counsel for the Respondent asserted that Mr Morey had not attempted to display any independence in his statement by, for example, referring to having read the practice note (or any code of conduct for experts) and agreeing to be bound by its contents. If however the Tribunal did not agree with this contention, it was submitted by the Respondent that this factor must nonetheless critically undermine the weight that would be attributed to Mr Morey’s opinions on matters that the joint experts had not addressed.
  5. The Respondent maintained a primary position that the paragraphs it complains of should be ruled as inadmissible. Alternatively, if the Tribunal should find otherwise, it contends that the issue goes to a matter of weight, such that Mr Morey’s evidence is critically undermined.
  6. In response, senior counsel for the Applicant commenced by reiterating that his client was not proffering Mr Morey as an independent expert witness.
  7. Senior counsel for the Applicant in amplifying his response carefully examined the contents of each of the paragraphs complained of by the Respondent. Concerning paragraphs 16-18 of Mr Morey’s second statement, he contended these were statements of fact; the evidence of Mr Morey was to describe what the Aspen+ software does. It is used by his company, Lycopodium. Senior counsel for the Applicant then stated that the documents referred to by Professor Haynes relate to incineration using fluidised bed reactors. As such, it is a different process to pyrolysis by the use of a rotary kiln. The Tribunal accepts this contention. It seems that it is drawing a very long bow to suggest Mr Morey’s evidence falls within the realm of expert evidence. Mr Morey is giving evidence of facts with which he is familiar, that familiarity having been obtained as a result of his involvement in the MSW project on behalf of PLPD. The paragraphs of his second statement objected to by the Respondent do not therefore, have the characteristics of opinion evidence, let alone expert opinion evidence in any sense.
  8. As to paragraphs 29-51 of Mr Morey’s second statement, once again the Applicant contended that Mr Morey had a factual basis for expressing his disagreement with Professor Haynes. It was conceded that, for instance, portions of paragraph 37 of that statement were “a bit argumentative”, but nonetheless, he explained how the proposed process works as a matter of fact. It does appear to the Tribunal that overall these paragraphs were directed to facts rather than an opinion, and to the extent that it did stray into the realm of argument (if indeed it was that), or opinion, an appropriate factual foundation had been laid for it.
  9. With the remaining paragraphs, a similar contention was advanced. It was also contended that Mr Morey is an engineer, admittedly a mechanical engineer by qualification. Nonetheless, he had been working on this project as an employee of Lycopodium. He has experience in such matters. It also should be noted that his experience was verified in his evidence in chief as a result of the objections taken by the Respondent.
  10. The Applicant responded to the contention that it was inappropriate for Mr Morey to give opinion evidence due to the existence of a joint report prepared by Mr Bland and Professor Haynes by pointing out that Mr Morey’s second witness statement was filed before the joint report was prepared. Additionally, the assumptions given to Mr Bland and Professor Haynes prior to their joint conference which led to the preparation of the joint report included assumptions based on Mr Morey’s evidence, including the contents of his second witness statement, and presumably those paragraphs to which objection is now taken.[36] Therefore both experts, being Mr Bland and Professor Haynes, were in a position to deal with Mr Morey’s evidence in the course of preparing the joint report. It was also relevantly noted that, in paragraph 3 of the Statement of Agreed Facts[37] furnished to the experts prior to preparation of the joint report, they were advised that they could still have regard to material provided to them for the purpose of this proceeding, amongst other things. Given this fact, and that Professor Haynes had Mr Morey’s statement and no supplementary expert report was filed by him expressing any opinion on its contents, it was contended that it was at best a matter for cross-examination.

Ruling on objections to the evidence of Mr Morey

  1. The Tribunal accepts the contentions made by the Applicant in relation to this evidence. The impugned evidence of Mr Morey is not an attempt at, or a circumvention of the Tribunal’s procedures. Once again, the factual foundation for the matters Mr Morey gave evidence on in his second witness statement was laid out. The second witness statement was made prior to the commissioning and provision of the joint report of Mr Bland and Professor Haynes. The contents of his second witness statement, and the paragraphs objected to by the Respondent specifically, were both relevant and probative. They ought not be ruled inadmissible. It is of course open to both parties to, as they did, make submissions on such evidence concerning its reliability, the weight to be placed upon the contents, and, where appropriate, identify what probative value such portions of the statements are to the Tribunal in terms of the fact-finding task, amongst other things, required of it.
  2. As to the contention that Mr Morey cannot be independent in circumstances where one of the issues which is raised by the experts is the quality of the work which his firm undertook. Several observations should be made in ruling on this issue. The Tribunal notes that the Applicant did not proffer Mr Morey as a true expert as noted above. Also, it should be repeated that the fact of him being an engineer and familiar with the project should not prevent his evidence on topics with which he is familiar from being received. It is ultimately a matter for the Tribunal to decide whether or not to accept such evidence, as it would with any witness before it. If it does so, it is then a question of weight.
  3. The fact that the quality of the work which Mr Morey’s company undertook may be under scrutiny in this application is beside the point. Naturally, his evidence will be viewed in a different light to that of a truly independent expert who is retained. It does not mean that his evidence should be excluded, nor that, if it is appropriately given and the Tribunal finds it credible, it should not be received and relied upon for whatever purposes are appropriate. It is not uncommon in a variety of different cases in courts and tribunals for a professionally qualified witness to give evidence which may well stray into the domain of opinion on the quality or standard of professional services that they have rendered to a client or otherwise carried out. It is a common feature of professional negligence cases.
  4. The Tribunal acknowledges the force in the Respondent’s contention that a witness being called as an expert, or someone otherwise possessed of special knowledge and expertise, should not be an advocate for the party calling them. It also acknowledges the contention of the Respondent that Mr Morey did not in either of his statements, depose to having read, or be familiar with, the Tribunal’s Persons Giving Expert and Opinion Evidence Guideline 2015. Naturally, this is a factor that the Tribunal must take into account when assessing the evidence of Mr Morey and whether to accept or reject it. Insofar as his evidence strays into the realms of expertise or special knowledge, the Tribunal considers this argument is not one that justifies excluding his evidence but once again raises a question of the weight that should or should not be attached to it.
  5. If the Tribunal is wrong in this assessment, it does nonetheless consider the fact that it is not bound by the rules of evidence from time to time, as has been canvassed earlier in these reasons, justifies permitting those paragraphs complained of by the Respondent to be received in evidence. Once again, many of them do give evidence as to matters of fact, not opinion. If they stray into the realms of opinion or argument, they are, as contended by the Applicant, arguments or opinions for which an appropriate factual foundation has been laid by Mr Morey. More will be said about his evidence later in these reasons. However, he is an experienced engineer who the Tribunal found to be a credible witness. In these circumstances, there seems no reason for his evidence to be excluded in any way.
  6. Therefore, the Tribunal will receive in evidence the paragraphs of Mr Morey’s second statement of 30 October 2017 that were objected to by the Respondent.

CONSIDERATION

The Lay Witnesses

Mr A

  1. As noted earlier in these reasons, Mr A made two witness statements on 12 May 2017 and 1 November 2017, together with annexures, which were tendered in evidence during the hearing of this application. He also gave viva voce evidence to the Tribunal. Some of the contents of those witness statements have already been referred to in these reasons under the heading “Background”. They need not be repeated. The Tribunal accepts this evidence from him.
  2. The initial parts of his first witness statement addressed his professional background, background in pyrolysis technology, incorporation of the Applicant together with the development and commissioning of the soil treatment facility referred to earlier.
  3. His first witness statement then turned to the development of the MSW treatment facility.[38] As noted earlier, he explained that he commenced investigating the application of pyrolysis to the treatment of MSW. He expressed the aim to have the Applicant design the world’s first large-scale commercial waste treatment facility which would process MSW by the use of the pyrolysis process to produce energy, fuel and char.
  4. It should be observed that Mr A’s evidence was that the use of pyrolysis to process MSW required a very different application of that technology. There are two reasons for this, namely: the different feedstock, being MSW rather than contaminated soil; and the intention being to produce different outputs, being electricity, fuel and char. Critical to the commercial viability of such a facility was to have the project develop so that it was capable of enough production of electricity and, what he described as, other by-products to make it commercially feasible. This also had to occur in the context of maintaining best practice standards with respect to environmental considerations.
  5. Whilst his research had revealed that several companies were currently investigating pyrolysis for the treatment of MSW, none had done so in a way that incorporated two key objectives that the Applicant sought to achieve. Those objectives were firstly, the capacity to process 140,000 tonnes of MSW per annum, and secondly, the application of pyrolysis technology to MSW treatment so that electricity, fuel and char were produced.
  6. Mr A gave evidence that the objective (or, as he put it, aim) on the part of the Applicant was to develop an operational plant that could accurately regulate pyrolysis of MSW, remove impurities, then produce electricity, fuel and char. To achieve this objective, several uncertainties identified at the outset needed to be resolved. These uncertainties, or unknown factors, included necessary design parameters for the process, requisite control systems, and equipment specifications to enable input of MSW. The Tribunal accepts this evidence, and also that it identified the objective, or perhaps more accurately for the purposes of this application, the hypothesis, and when it first crystallised.
  7. On the question of a hypothesis or the development of new knowledge, reference should be made to a passage in the cross-examination of Mr A on this topic.[39] He was questioned on the purpose of the “whole project”. It was suggested to him that it was the development and construction of a new commercial scale MSW plant, rather than the testing of a hypothesis or the development of new knowledge. He strongly disputed this and stated that the purpose certainly was, and that the work carried out in the initial phase certainly was, for such purpose otherwise there would be no progress to the next phase. The Tribunal accepts this evidence from him.
  8. The next portion of his first witness statement canvassed the engagement of PLPD. Much of this has already been referred to earlier in these reasons.[40]
  9. Mr A’s first witness statement then provided an account of the nature of the activities carried out by the Applicant.[41] The contents of that portion of the first witness statement made by him are referred to. He gave a summary of the steps taken by PLPD. This involved, as he described it, the design of a suitable process which addressed several variables that arise from the treatment of MSW. These variables included MSW inputs (including MSW composition) and different methods of its preparation.
  10. Mr A stated that the variables were tested using computer-aided design (CAD) software, modelling what he described as various ideas and testing their validity in a number of simulated environments. He further stated that as part of this process PLPD developed a number of hypotheses concerning the workings of the proposed MSW plant and sought to validate such hypotheses so as to produce a feasible design.
  11. Examples were provided by Mr A of modelling using differing equipment sizes and support structures arising from the movement of equipment caused by temperature extremes. Simulations were also undertaken with respect to differing MSW compositions. Experiments were also conducted concerning alternative combinations of construction materials, equipment layouts and specifications. The Overview Report, the Plant Layout Drawings, Plant Construction Drawings and the Project Scope Report referred to earlier in these reasons were said by him to represent the work product derived from undertaking the activities described in, amongst other things, the application for registration.
  12. Additionally, Mr A’s evidence in his first witness statement concerning the nature of activities undertaken by PLPD, and continued by Lycopodium, was directed at testing as he described it “a broader hypothesis”. This broader hypothesis is the question of whether or not it is possible to construct a large-scale MSW treatment facility capable of producing electricity, fuel and char. Modelling undertaken by PLPD established that the additional energy required to condense the syngas and convert it into a saleable and consistent fuel product was unworkable. The Applicant is now concentrating on the production of electricity and char from the operation of a proposed MSW treatment facility.
  13. Some initial observations should be made about Mr A and the evidence that he gave to the Tribunal in the course of this application. The Respondent attacked his evidence both in a general way and also by specific reference to various inconsistencies or deficiencies (sometimes also referred to by counsel for the Respondent as “irregularities”) in his evidence on several subjects. It was contended that the Tribunal should view his evidence with caution and, in relation to the issues in dispute, find it to be unreliable.
  14. The approach on the part of the Respondent was to state that the Tribunal should form an adverse view of Mr A’s evidence as mentioned in the previous paragraph for several reasons. Partly, this was because he was not directly responsible for the performance of the technical works claimed to be registrable activities. It was asserted that Mr A has no engineering qualifications. He did not prepare any of the drawings or designs created by PLPD Developments. He did not prepare any calculations that were in evidence in several spreadsheets that were tendered. It was also contended that he did not supervise the work or contribute to any modelling by suggesting inputs. It was submitted on behalf of the Respondent that on his part as a witness there was a lack of any contemporaneous evidence of the three-step process that he described. This was coupled with the allegation that the drawings were prepared prior to the relevant year and then amended. So, to that extent, his evidence was unreliable. This approach was the subject of some fairly probing, if not vigorous, cross-examination of him when he was in the witness box, of which more will be said later in these reasons.
  15. The Tribunal found Mr A to be a credible witness. He is an intelligent, highly qualified and experienced businessman. It should not be lost sight of that he is a graduate of the University of Melbourne Law School with a Bachelor of Laws and also a graduate from the University of Melbourne Business School with a Master of Business Administration. Whilst he was forthright and direct in the witness box, and may present, as undoubtedly, he is, as a hard-nosed successful businessman, he always answered the questions put to him directly, properly, and where occasion called for it, made appropriate concessions. These are the hallmarks of a satisfactory witness. Despite the careful, probing and thorough cross-examination that he was subjected to by counsel for the Respondent, he left the witness box with his credibility intact. There was no reason for him to be untruthful, embellish or exaggerate his evidence. He did not do so. Therefore, the Tribunal accepts his evidence and finds that he was not an unreliable witness.
  16. Whilst Mr A might not have directly supervised the activities or contributed to any modelling by suggesting inputs to its development as contended for by the Respondent, nonetheless he was intimately involved in the project at all relevant times. He described this involvement being both as a representative of the Applicant, and to a more limited extent, as part of the PLPD project team. It also follows as a matter of logic that his involvement and knowledge of the activities concerned would have been intimate because of his long background in pyrolysis technology from the perspective of technical research that he gave evidence on and of course his genuine business endeavours and the objective of harnessing such technology to realise significant commercial gains. He presented as someone who was highly motivated to understand the project as much as he was able to do so in a technical sense, and from a high level of desire for ultimate commercial benefit to ensure it succeeded. It should not be lost sight of that the Applicant, of which he was sole director, had invested significant sums of money in the project and was likely to continue to do so in the future. They were high stakes that would drive someone in his position to have a detailed knowledge of what had occurred. He had succeeded in developing the soil treatment plant using pyrolysis technology and he wanted it to succeed with the treatment of MSW in the same way. For these reasons the Tribunal accepts his evidence and rejects the contention of the Respondent that it should form an adverse view of his evidence.
  17. Another matter concerning the evidence of Mr A should be addressed. In the course of his cross-examination and submissions, the Respondent contended that it had been misled by reason of the fact that some of the work, or a drawing, had been prepared prior to the income year under review. It was contended that a diagram had been modified to appear that work had been performed in 2015 when in fact it was performed in 2013.
  18. The Applicant in its submission contended that this was tantamount to an allegation of fraud or perhaps a state of mind consistent with acts of moral turpitude. Indeed, at one stage in his closing submissions from the bar table counsel for the Applicant submitted that the Respondent was running the case on the basis that two witnesses had perjured themselves and the documents were “frauds”.[42]
  19. In her closing submission from the bar table counsel for the Respondent specifically stated that in relation to Mr A that the Respondent was not alleging fraud.[43] In the course of such submission she emphatically stated that no allegation of fraud in relation to the evidence given to the Tribunal was being made.[44] The central plank of her attack on Mr A’s evidence went to its reliability. The theme of documents having been created before the relevant period under consideration in this application, and then proffered to the Respondent as having been carried out during the relevant period, were put in several contexts. This will be canvassed further later in these reasons.
  20. Even though the Respondent has eschewed any allegation of fraud, these are, nonetheless, extremely serious allegations. They cannot be made lightly. Indeed, in his closing address from the bar table counsel for the Applicant referred to the well-known case of Briginshaw v Briginshaw[45] and its effect. One need not embark upon an excursion into this well traversed area of the law other than to observe that the more serious the matter, and the gravity of the consequences flowing from a particular finding, the stricter proof required. The Tribunal notes, as was contended by the Applicant, that these allegations were not referred to prior to the hearing of this application. No such assertions were made in the Respondent’s Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions. One would have expected them to have seen the light of day in such a document or, even if one gives the Respondent the benefit of the doubt, a reasonable time before the hearing of this application so that the Applicant had ample opportunity to address such allegations. It is surprising that this did not occur.
  21. One of the specific criticisms of Mr A’s evidence said to justify viewing it with caution, was that his description of the design process adopted by the Applicant consisting of three steps only emerged after Professor Haynes’ first expert report dated 7 September 2017 had been produced and served on its lawyers.[46] The Respondent contended that the three-step process was not referred to in the initial application, Mr A’s first witness statement nor, in the Applicant’s Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions. Further, it was contended that the three-stage process was not documented in any contemporaneous document. Such three-step process was only addressed by Mr A in his second statement of 1 November 2017.[47] The Respondent also contended that a further obstacle to acceptance of the evidence from Mr A concerning his explanation of the three-step process is that it is not documented in any contemporaneous document.
  22. It was also put by the Respondent that Mr A’s evidence concerning a three-stage process was undermined by the fact that the detailed design of the dryer and the dual train plant layout were completed prior to the registration period. Therefore, the Tribunal should find his evidence to be unreliable and of little weight.
  23. The design of the dryer is contained in “MA-13” to Mr A’s first witness statement and dated 30 January 2015. The design was said to have been undertaken by PLPD.[48] The Respondent relied upon what it said was “an identical diagram” provided to it on 10 September 2015.[49] This was said to be by the Respondent, strongly suggestive of the dryer design having been completed by 18 April 2013 which was prior to the financial year under consideration in this application. The Tribunal accepts the contention of the Applicant that such an allegation is a very significant matter given its gravity.
  24. The Applicant countered this allegation in several ways. Mr A disagreed with the proposition put to him in cross-examination that the document was in fact produced in July 2013 by another company “Tech Trade”.[50] He also identified specific distinctions between the drawings which reveal that they are not identical. The Tribunal accepts this contention. An examination of both the drawings does reveal that section Z is different. It is acknowledged that the documents are very similar, however, as noted not identical. One of the drawings[51] showed as an input “waste”. The drawings also showed a dual train layout. Mr A’s evidence was that the two-train plant concept was only developed for the MSW plant after it had been determined in September 2014 that a single train system was not feasible, because the dryer and the kiln would be too big. These design features could not have been prepared for the soil treatment plant. It should not be lost sight of that the drawings, even if dated 2013, were after the commencement of the construction of the soil treatment plant which occurred in 2012. The Tribunal accepts Mr A’s evidence concerning these matters and when the drawings were created.
  25. This is also consistent with the evidence of Mr A in paragraph 69 of his second witness statement, and his viva voce evidence. He stated that PLPD had, as he put it, a bank of knowledge, or design precedents for the type of equipment required for the proposed facility. Some of those precedents were originally created by Techtrade. Mr Morey

  1. The Tribunal found Mr Morey to be a credible witness. The Respondent contended that his evidence had the features and characteristics of being “coloured”, “significantly overstated” and of little weight.[64] The Tribunal did not observe Mr Morey in this light. Mr Morey has been employed by Lycopodium since 2006 as a Senior Project Manager and Principal Mechanical Engineer.[65] He presented as a conscientious and experienced engineer, as he undoubtedly is.[66] Like most engineers, he conducted himself in the witness box as careful, dedicated and conscientious. He was a fair and candid witness. He did not give his evidence in any way that was a reconstruction, an embellishment or otherwise in an unreliable fashion. Concessions were readily made by him when called for. By way of example, in the course of cross-examination on several occasions it was put to him that there were “flaws” or errors in several aspects of the PFD.[67] Mr Morey readily acknowledged these flaws or errors. This is hardly the hallmark of a witness whose evidence is “coloured”, or in some other way unreliable. The Tribunal finds him to be a reliable witness.
  2. The initial part of Mr Morey’s first witness statement provided details of his professional background and Lycopodium, then briefly there was reference to his, and Lycopodium’s involvement in the soil treatment plant for the Applicant. He then referred to the retainer of Lycopodium by PLPD to assist in designing the MSW to energy plant for the Applicant. His evidence was that through this connection he became aware of the association between TechTrade, the Applicant and PLPD. Additionally, he was aware of the role played at TechTrade and Mr Franz-Eicke Von Christen who had a reputation for considerable expertise in the application of pyrolysis technology.

They were said to constitute broad general statements about the use of such software which were not borne out by the evidence. It was contended that his evidence changed significantly in cross-examination. The Tribunal considers that Mr Morey’s responses to specific questions put to him by counsel for the Respondent in cross-examination were providing more detail or supplementing the content of his statement. It did not seem to the Tribunal to represent a significant change. He readily conceded that he could not personally use Aspen+. One would have to observe that, notwithstanding he may not personally be able to use Aspen, he may be well qualified and experienced, as it appeared to the Tribunal that he was, to identify documents and other work product generated using Aspen+. This is what he did in both his witness statements and evidence from the witness box to the Tribunal.
  4. In regard to Mr Morey’s concession in cross-examination that Aspen+ was predominantly used for the steam cycle in the steam turbine, this is not inconsistent with the exhibit “PM-6”, being a copy of a Mass & Energy Balance Review, that he exhibited to his second statement, and the steps undertaken by the use and application of Aspen+. A careful examination of those documents reveals that Aspen+ was used for making other calculations of which there were several. Again, this is not inconsistent with his evidence in the witness statements and the witness box.
  5. The Tribunal accepts the Applicant’s contentions that in oral evidence Mr Morey confirmed the contents of his witness statements, confirmed the various experimental activities that had been carried out by Lycopodium during the relevant period, and described how they generated new knowledge by a systematic progression of work. He also gave evidence concerning the supporting activities and other relevant matters. It may well be contended that he did not directly carry out all the technical works which are claimed to be registrable activities. Nonetheless, even if he is characterised, as the Respondent does, as “essentially a project manager”, it does not prevent him from giving relevant and probative evidence as to what was in fact done from his observations. The evidence given by him on this topic included providing details of, as he described them, the “key persons in the Lycopodium team that worked on the MSW project.”[69] Those persons were identified by name and title or occupation as applicable.[70] He did this and, as noted earlier in these reasons, exhibited several documents to his witness statements which demonstrated the completed work product, and was able to explain how the work occurred. These documents were the Lycopodium Report, the Project Scope Report, the Updated Project Scope Report, the USB containing a 3D model of the plant, the Mass and Energy (“M & E”) Balance Review and several relevant emails. The Tribunal sees no reason why this evidence should not be accepted. It should be repeated that he was found by the Tribunal to be a credible witness.
  6. Another matter arising from Mr Morey’s evidence that the Tribunal accepts is that he stated that, in a commercial environment, items are not always documented to the extent that might occur at a facility such as a university. He observed that the iterations of various calculations and modelling that he referred to in his statements are not generally of interest to clients and, therefore, are not always retained. He stated that, during the design phase, the company does not keep revisions of every single change made. Principally, clients are concerned with the outcome (which he described as the “agreed outcome”). His evidence was that, in his experience,[71] it is not unusual for there to be no direct evidence of the activities undertaken, other than the final result. This is hardly surprising. Along this line, in the course of his evidence he explained to the Tribunal the that engineers have a different approach to projects than entrepreneurs, or as he called them, “commercial people”.[72] Commercial people and entrepreneurs focus on the commercial advantages of getting to market quickly. This causes them to take risks that engineers would not take. He gave an example of a well-known publicly listed company who he described as a very conservative client as far as risk goes. Indeed, he said that one could not find a more conservative client as far as risk goes. However, that client, knowing there was a risk, built a whole plant when it still had not determined what chemical it was going to use. Tension between commercial or industrial demands and best engineering practice, as explained by Mr Morey, is readily understandable. The Tribunal accepts that at times best engineering practices, such as six decimal place accuracy, will not always be achieved in commercial environments. It may not be necessary to achieve such accuracy at certain stages of the project if it can be attended to later in the life of a long project. This evidence from Mr Morey is relevant to understanding the commercial considerations within which this project (and no doubt many others) was developed. Those considerations should be seen in the light of the industry-based objects of the IR & D Act which, as observed earlier, direct the decision-maker towards, amongst other things, supporting and encouraging collaboration in the development and delivery of programs relating to industry and venture capital considerations.
  7. Mr Morey, to his credit, in cross-examination on an aspect of this topic, also conceded that it was not a logical manner of working to complete detailed design of components before settling on design fundamentals. In making this concession, he reiterated that this was not unusual in the commercial environment where scheduling and financial risk considerations assume greater significance, if not primacy, over what an engineer might prefer to do it a perfect world.
  8. Mr Morey readily acknowledged that Lycopodium did not keep interim results of modelling of the various parameters and iterations of calculations. He stated it was really only interested in results and, as he put it, issuing them for review by the client. Typically, in a design phase it did not keep records of daily activities. It was more interested in getting to a “solution”. He stated that the focus was on taking the design that had been provided by PLPD, testing it and satisfying itself of whether or not such design would work. It was then a case of trialling different scenarios with different capacities and seeking to improve such design.[73] In one passage of his oral evidence, that was indeed telling of the demands that are placed upon engineers in a commercial setting, he stated that if engineers couldn’t get six decimal place accuracy on a calculation they might lose sleep at night. Entrepreneurs don’t think about that level of detail.
  9. This evidence from Mr Morey concerning documentation was also consistent with evidence given by Mr Bland. In what he described as an industrial environment, Mr Bland observed that it is not always expected that records would be kept of research conducted or of each step in the process. This is particularly the case where an outcome or result is achieved that is more successful than earlier experiments or previous trials. In the event that there have been successful results achieved from the research undertaken, there is less likely to be a need to keep records of the previous results or steps.[74] He also opined that there was most likely to be retained a written record only of the most successful outcome. There might well be a record of the start and the end but there was no requirement to retain all the intermediate material. Although disagreeing with this contention by Mr Bland, Professor Haynes did concede that in an industrial setting he would not expect details of experiments undertaken to be written down as religiously as might occur in a laboratory environment.[75]
  10. Another relevant matter that Mr Morey gave evidence on concerned the Burgau plant in Germany. He visited the plant in April 2016. He gave helpful evidence of what he observed of the Burgau plant during that visit. Amongst other things, he only saw a pyrolysis kiln there and did not observe a separate dryer. When making a reference to a comparison between that plant and the one designed on behalf of the Applicant, he described it as “not comparing apples to apples”. In response to questions concerning such comment, he identified several key differences. Those key differences included that the Burgau plant used all its flue gas to evaporate water, so any moisture content in its feed stock was evaporated in that phase. The adoption of this process adds to the gas flow rate through the hot gas filter. Therefore, the plant uses energy that could have been used to generate steam for the purposes of creating electricity instead of using the lesser value low-pressure steam for the drying process.[76] Additionally, he observed that the Burgau plant supplemented its energy from landfill gas. That therefore meant that it was not a stand-alone plant. He described the gas cleaning system used at Burgau as very simplistic and old-fashioned. He concluded by stating that, other than the fundamentals of using pyrolysis to treat MSW, there was a great deal of difference in the detail between the two of them. As he put it, “the devil’s in the detail and there’s a lot of difference in the detail.” Insofar as what he observed during his visit to the Burgau plant in April 2016, the Tribunal also accepts his evidence.

The Expert Witnesses

Mr Bland’s first report of 30 May 2017

  1. Mr Bland is a highly qualified and experienced chemical engineer.
  2. In his first report of 30 May 2017,[79] Mr Bland concluded that the outcome of the activities carried out by the Applicant as outlined in its application to the Respondent could not have been known or determined in advance on the basis of existing knowledge, information or experience at the time such activities were undertaken. He reached this conclusion because:

(a) The Applicant found that by combusting the syngas for additional heat and electricity generation, the plant could improve its cost efficiency, but there was insufficient energy remaining in the gas to produce a quality fuel;

(b) The Applicant observed the kiln experienced expansion of up to one metre. Consequently, the Applicant devised new support structures that could accommodate such expansion and contraction of the kiln. This required a determination of which materials to use and which operating temperatures were optimal to minimise kiln expansion. It also observed that the kiln does not expand uniformly as it acts as a combustion chamber. To counter this, the support had to be devised in a way that accommodated for this non-uniform expansion;

(c) The Applicant concluded that a hybrid facility was not currently economically feasible due to the higher capital expenditure required to produce a sufficient quality fuel, but that it may be possible for the MSW treatment facility to generate electricity in a manner that is cost effective;

(d) That rotary kilns used in “slow pyrolysis” situations produce lower yields of condensable vapours. It could not be determined if the low proportion of condensable vapours produced by slow pyrolysis could be condensed by the “quench” method used in fast pyrolysis;

(e) The extent of expansion of the kiln was surprising and not what was expected on the basis of existing knowledge at the time;

(f) The non-uniform expansion of the kiln covered as a result of the experimental work undertaken by the Applicant was certainly new knowledge and the design of the support required to accommodate it may also be new knowledge, given the lack of similar previous experience elsewhere;

(g) It generated knowledge of the specific processes proposed, in particular how the pyrolysis process responded to changes in feed composition, feed moisture content, processing temperature and residence time, and the impact on operations and yields. Such knowledge would allow process improvements such as information that would enable the capacity of the kiln to be determined under a range of operating conditions, and enable the economic value of the kiln to be assessed; and

(h) It generated knowledge of how heat was distributed through the kiln, and how this heat affected the shape of the kiln now. This resulted in an improved device, in particular by the development of new means for supporting the kiln as referred to earlier.

  1. He concluded therefore, by reason of these matters, that new knowledge was generated by the activities as undertaken by the Applicant.
  2. Additionally, in his first report he expressed the belief that the most sensible way to conduct the activities was in fact by the adaptation of the method used by the Applicant. In his view, such a method constituted a systematic progression of work, was based on principles of established science, and proceeded from hypothesis to experiment, observation and evaluation and led to a logical conclusion.
  3. Mr Bland considered the state of technology concerning pyrolysis of MSW. He observed that there is both a great body of academic research and a significant number of projects of various scales. He stated that the success rate of commercial scale projects has to date been low. He referred to the existence of plants in Japan and Germany. There are no identical facilities in commercial operation. The plant at Burgau in Germany appears to be the only example of a pyrolysis plant using MSW as a feedstock[80] that has operated successfully. That plant is much smaller, being approximately one third of the size of the Applicant’s proposed plant, and had been operating since 1989. There were no examples that Mr Bland could find of pyrolysis plants producing liquid fuels. Mr Bland expressed the opinion that any party contemplating building a plant for pyrolysis of MSW would need to ensure that the chosen process was either:

(a) well supported by very similar examples, with a good history of reliable operation at a similar scale, using similar feedstock; or

(b) investigated thoroughly to ensure the process could produce the required outcomes as to specific product deals, reliability and operating costs, from the expected feedstocks.

  1. Mr Bland then canvassed the use of computer simulation. It was his opinion that it was prudent to investigate the process initially using computer simulation to determine:

(a) whether the required outcomes are likely to be achieved; and

(b) whether further work, in the form of laboratory or pilot scale work, is required to investigate any uncertainties that cannot be addressed completely by computer simulation.

  1. To undertake such simulation Mr Bland considered that the Applicant had to build a unique computer model from scratch, using the building blocks contained in a proprietary software package such as Aspen+. He concluded that the results of running simulations with such software, using the range of operating and processing conditions envisaged, would have allowed the Applicant to test its hypothesis and generate new knowledge about pyrolysis of MSW, including new knowledge about production of liquid fuels from the pyrolysis vapours.

Mr Bland’s second report of 3 November 2017

  1. Following the provision of Professor Haynes’ first report of 7 September 2017, Mr Bland produced a second report on 3 November 2017.
  2. Helpfully, Mr Bland provides a summary of his report in part 4. The substance of his second report was that, after reviewing Professor Haynes first report of 7 September 2017, he maintained his earlier conclusions.
  3. It should be observed that Mr Bland maintained his earlier conclusions having had the benefit of the further witness statements from Mr Morey and Mr A. Additionally, he was furnished with the witness statement of Mr G.
  4. The conclusions of Mr Bland in his second report on 3 November 2017 are summarised as follows:

(a) The claimed Core R & D activities did generate new knowledge. He opined that this was described principally in the second witness statement of Mr A, including annexures MA-15 to MA-18. Further, he opined that it was quite clear from the documents provided to him that the Core R & D activities were undertaken;

(b) The claimed Core R & D Activities did proceed logically to test the hypothesis and were substantially directed to testing this hypothesis;

(c) The documents provided by the Applicant evidence that a systematic approach was used for Supporting Activities;

(d) New knowledge was created through the claimed Core R & D Activities and is documented in the Applicant’s application to Innovation and Science Australia;

(e) The claimed Supporting R & D Activities are distinguishable from the Core R & D Activities, and additional documentation provided by the Applicant also evidences that these were carried out; and

(f) Modelling using Aspen+ and Excel was definitely carried out.

  1. Mr Bland disagreed with the conclusions of Professor Haynes that the Core R & D Activities did not generate new knowledge because they did not resolve a fundamental uncertainty. His investigations revealed that this had not been achieved anywhere else previously. He opined that the Applicant, by determining that there is insufficient energy, has removed that uncertainty, thereby generating new knowledge.

Conclusions on the Expert Reports and Evidence

  1. Overall, the Tribunal prefers, in several respects, the evidence of Mr Bland over Professor Haynes. In reaching this conclusion, it is important to state that the Tribunal does not in any way question Professor Haynes’ credibility or reliability. The reasons for preferring Mr Bland’s evidence arise from other matters.
  2. The initial ground for preferring the report of Mr Bland over Professor Haynes arises from the factual findings that the Tribunal has made, which are referred to above.
  3. Paragraph 9.3 of Professor Haynes’ first report observed that there was no direct evidence of the conduct of work referred to in several witness statements (D7,[90] p 54) and D8, p36)) being aspects of equipment design including sizing. The Tribunal finds that this work was carried out.
  4. This approach of Professor Haynes to venture into areas that are relevantly a matter for determination by the Tribunal, which was much criticised by the Applicant, continued into his second report. As noted earlier in these reasons, in relation to the drawing of the Rotary Tube Dryer dated January 2015 referred to in exhibit “MA-13” to the first witness statement of Mr A, Professor Haynes stated boldly and without qualification that the design did not occur in the period 1 July 2014 to 30 June 2015.
  5. Further, it is of some concern to the Tribunal that Professor Haynes also engaged in argument, which is clearly not the role of a responsible expert, to note that there was no patent protection for the Applicant’s tube layout as incorporated in the relevant design drawing.
  6. The Tribunal does not understand Professor Haynes to be an expert in the unquestionably complex law of patents. Further, it does not appear that he was ever instructed to express an opinion on the topic of the tube layout of the Applicant’s design. There was no letter of instruction in evidence before the Tribunal addressed to Professor Haynes prior to him preparing his second report in November 2018. The letter of instruction from the Respondent’s lawyers of 25 July 2017, which is in evidence before the Tribunal,[91] does not refer to nor request Professor Haynes to advise whether the Applicant had sought patent protection as he canvassed in the second report.
  7. In any event, it is at best a matter for submission to be made by the Respondent in the course of the hearing of the application. It is perhaps salient to observe that the point was not taken by the Respondent during the hearing of the application, probably wisely so. It does demonstrate, to some extent, the fact that, contrary to his obligations as a responsible expert as required by the Tribunal’s Persons Giving Expert and Opinion Evidence Guideline 2015, he assumed the role of, or very close to, an advocate for the party who engaged him, namely the Respondent. He certainly stepped beyond what he was asked to do when, in his second report, he addressed the issue of the lack of patent protection by the Applicant for the tube layout. This approach does not assist the Tribunal and in some respects is a factor that is relied upon to prefer the opinions expressed by Mr Bland.
  8. Further, Professor Haynes stated in his first report that, from the evidence provided, he was unable to confirm whether the Core R & D activities were carried out solely in the income year ended 30 June 2015. It is acknowledged that he was requested to do so in the letter of instruction of 25 July 2017. He also stated there is no evidence that those physical activities were undertaken.[92] This observation in his report was also consistent with evidence that he gave from the witness box.[93] In the course of that evidence, amongst other things, he stated that, when he first examined the material with which he was furnished prior to producing his first report, he was of the view that it could well qualify for the kinds of “definitional criteria in the scheme”. However, when he examined such material more closely and, as he put it, dug down into the detail, he felt there was a lack of evidence. In fairness to him, it should be said that he did not, and could not, take into account the contents of the second witness statements of both Mr A and Mr Morey and the statement of Mr G. Additionally, unlike the Tribunal, he did not have the benefit of their viva voce evidence, in which they were searchingly probed about the activities undertaken. The Tribunal has made findings, as noted above, accepting their evidence, and that the work concerned was carried out in the relevant financial year ending 30 June 2015.
  9. Professor Haynes expressed opinions concerning the Supporting R & D Activities claimed by the Applicant. Those activities were said to be firstly, the evaluation and documentation of process and mechanical designs, and secondly, project management including scheduling and procurement as documented in his report as “D6”. He stated that, from the evidence provided (and it should be stressed that once again no reference was made to the second witness statements of both Mr A and Mr Morey and he did not have the benefit of their viva voce evidence), he was unable to confirm whether such activities were carried out solely in the financial year ended 30 June 2015, nor the extent to which computer simulation and/or modelling were employed. Further, he opined that there was no evidence that the physical activities were undertaken. Once again, the Tribunal has made findings that the activities were carried out during the financial year ended 30 June 2015 and were undertaken as described by the Applicant’s witnesses in accordance with the findings made earlier in these reasons.
  10. Another matter that should be observed concerning Professor Haynes’ evidence relates to the use of Aspen+ software. He stated that there was no evidence that the Applicant used a proprietary software package such as Aspen+ or others. The Tribunal accepts the evidence adduced on behalf of the Applicant that Aspen+ was indeed used.
  11. Some observations should be made about Professor Haynes’ opinions concerning new knowledge about the pyrolysis of MSW. Throughout his evidence he tackled the claims of the Applicant and opinions expressed by Mr Bland concerning simulations performed. These simulations were the basis for process design decisions. This was for the purposes of determining how a commercial MSW pyrolysis process might be established, or as he put it assembled, from the integration of conventional process operations such as drying, pyrolysis, treatment of the vapours, combustion and steam raising. His conclusion was that the outcomes from such simulations generated by the Applicant, as the Tribunal has found, did not in his opinion give rise to new knowledge in the sense contemplated by the section 355-25 of the ITA Act.
  12. The Applicant submits, and the Tribunal agrees, that the term “new knowledge” in this section is not narrowly defined. It does include, amongst others, “improved ...processes or services”.[94] It does not mandate the concept of scientific breakthrough, let alone resolution of fundamental uncertainty as was mentioned by Professor Haynes in his first report. Properly construed, the inclusion of the words “in the form of” in the section must lead to an interpretation that captures the improved process itself as being, in whole or in part, within the concept of “new knowledge” in the section, not just any knowledge gained or derived as a result of it. Professor Haynes reached his conclusion regarding new knowledge about the pyrolysis of MSW because in his view there was no “fundamental uncertainty” about these individual processes nor about the general manner of their interconnection in pyrolysis of MSW that could be addressed by process simulation alone.
  13. In the witness box, Professor Haynes went even further than just the resolution of a fundamental uncertainty in his description of the concept of new knowledge. He proposed that, in engineering, it is quite possible for an assembly of existing facts to produce new knowledge. For that assembly of facts to create new knowledge it needs to lead to something which is unexpected, surprising and which is not anticipated in the connection of the existing knowledge.[95]
  14. Professor Haynes use of the term “fundamental uncertainty”, or for that matter something unexpected and/or surprising, does not accord with any proper construction of the text of section 355-25 of the ITA Act. It will be recalled that the experimental activities identified in this section are those whose outcome cannot be known or determined in advance. It is a different concept to a fundamental uncertainty or something unexpected and/or surprising. Fundamental uncertainty or something unexpected and/or surprising requires a higher threshold for an applicant to satisfy. It is not what the section of the ITA Act requires. The Tribunal also agrees with the contention of the Applicant that the fact that the legislature used the term “improved processes” in this context establishes that activities which lead to an improvement on existing processes will qualify as new knowledge for the purposes of this section.
  15. Coupled with this observation, Professor Haynes concedes later in his first report that process innovation can most definitely be derived from simulation. Once again, he states that there must be some fundamental uncertainty that is resolved and goes further and says the outcome must be novel, even surprising, to competent practitioners for it to be considered new knowledge. With respect to Professor Haynes, this opinion does not fit with, or more accurately address, the statutory test required by section 355-25 of the ITA Act, which it will be recalled requires experimental activities, whose outcome cannot be known or determined in advance on the basis of current knowledge, information or experience that can only be determined by applying a systematic progression of work. The statute does not require, as he puts it, for process innovation to resolve a fundamental uncertainty or produce an outcome that is necessarily novel, or even surprising.[96] Indeed, the Respondent, in counsel’s closing submissions, quite correctly stated that novelty itself forms no part of the statutory test.[97] The Tribunal agrees.
  16. It should be observed that the letter of instruction sent to Professor Haynes dated 25 July 2017 did not anywhere use the words “fundamental uncertainty”, “novel”, “unexpected” or “surprising”. These words and/or concepts, referred to in his first report (and later in his evidence), were presumably a product of his deliberations and assessment of the material that he was furnished with. It does reveal a tendency on his part (which is not the role of an expert) to stray into the realms of statutory construction and beyond the strict scope of his letter of engagement and into matters that must be determined by the Tribunal. It is a factor, albeit not conclusive, taken into account by the Tribunal and relied upon to prefer the evidence of Mr Bland.
  17. Mr Bland, in his second report and throughout his evidence, tackled this conclusion in any event. He stated the uncertainty was whether there is sufficient energy in the plant to operate effectively while removing a significant proportion of the energy as liquid fuel. He stated that this had not been achieved anywhere else before. The Applicant, by determining that there is insufficient energy, has removed the uncertainty. This must be a generation of new knowledge that has arisen from experimental activities whose outcome could not have been known or determined in advance on the basis of the then current knowledge, information or experience. It has been achieved by applying a systematic progression of work to achieve that result. This satisfies the requirements of section 355-25(1) of the ITA Act. The Tribunal accepts this conclusion.
  18. Professor Haynes disagreed with what he said was Mr Bland’s conclusion concerning what was described as the “gap between academic studies and what is needed for full scale commercialisation” of a MSW pyrolysis plant. Professor Haynes conceded that there is such a gap. It was said to be operational, commercial and environmental. Mr Bland on the other hand put it differently. He stated that in his view the Applicant’s activities had sought new knowledge to ensure that gaps had been closed for the equipment proposed. He stressed that he was not in a position to say that the gaps have been closed. However, he really relied on the Applicant’s actions of proceeding further with the project and assumed such gaps had been closed to their satisfaction at this stage of the development. Professor Haynes stated that the Technical Summary and Final Design Overview report did not demonstrate that the Applicant had in fact closed the gap. The contents of Mr Bland’s first report speak for itself. The description of them as used by Professor Haynes in paragraph 21.1 of his first report (amongst others) was not strictly correct.
  19. Professor Haynes agreed with Mr Bland that knowledge of how to build a plant for the Applicant’s specific feed mix that would be capable of successful operation in Australia at the scale proposed, involved additional considerations compared to the relevant studies conducted previously and identified in documents 1A and D2 with which he had been furnished in his letter of instruction. He stated that he could not agree that the knowledge sought to be obtained by the Applicant through these considerations did not involve only a simple progression of knowledge contained in existing studies.
  20. The existing studies referred to by both the experts are predominantly derived from the previous design of the plant situated at Burgau in Germany. Mr Bland, in his first report, identified four differences,[98] and he describes them as significant differences, between the Applicant’s design and that of the Burgau plant. The first difference is that the Applicant’s design has a separate feed dryer. At Burgau it was combined with the pyrolysis kiln. Secondly, the Burgau plant did not pre-heat the combustion air prior to entering the “Burning chamber”. The Applicant’s design does. Thirdly, the Burgau plant revealed a cyclone for cleaning pyrolysis vapours leaving the kiln. The Applicant’s design has some sort of filter built into the exit from the kilns. This is a different concept. Fourthly, each of the Applicant’s kilns has more than three times the design capacity of each of the Burgau kilns.
  21. Mr Bland, in his third report, also identified two additional differences between the Applicant’s design and that of the Burgau plant.[99] The first additional difference noted was that the Applicant’s design recirculates dirty flue gas, whereas the Burgau plant recirculates clean flue gas. Consequently, the Applicant’s “baghouse filter” can be smaller than otherwise would have been the case. Mr Bland opined that this is an improvement, though not a particularly innovative one. The other additional difference identified by Mr Bland in his third report is that the Burgau plant uses “gas from landfill” as supplementary fuel for the heating chamber.
  22. The Respondent, in its closing submission, seized upon a passage of

    cross-examination from Mr Bland’s evidence to contend that the basic operational diagrams prepared by the Applicant simply involved an adaptation of existing engineering processes.[100] Professor Haynes, on the other hand, stated that they were an adaptation within the scope of what would be expected from a competent chemical engineer, without what he would describe as innovation. Whilst strictly that submission does record some aspects of the evidence given by Mr Bland and Professor Haynes on this topic, Mr Bland prefaced his observation by stating that the differences between what the Applicant wanted and what the Burgau plant achieved were “significantly different” such as to make quite an amount of work required. The Tribunal does not see this passage of Mr Bland’s evidence as derogating from what he consistently contended throughout the course of his written and oral evidence, namely, that the significant differences between the two designs were such that the development of Applicant’s design did lead to the creation of new knowledge within the meaning of the applicable section of the ITA Act. The Tribunal does not take his “concession” in that section of his evidence to be otherwise.
  23. Professor Haynes touched on the Burgau plant in parts of his first report, particularly in part 11. In paragraph 11.5 of that report, he stated that the heating of the dryer by flue gases was standard practice. When questioned in cross-examination, he stated that this was an error, notwithstanding that when he was affirmed at the commencement of his evidence, he stated that the opinions expressed in his reports were accurate. Upon further probing in cross-examination, he stated that there is no standard practice at that level and that a separate dryer is in the minority. He further conceded, when examining the schematic flowsheet for the Burgau plant, that the burner was partly fuelled with landfill gas which is a difference between it and the plant proposed by the Applicant. When further pressed on this topic he conceded that there were significant differences between the Burgau plant and that of the Applicant.[101] This acknowledgement of the differences between the two plants by Professor Haynes was, to the Tribunal, consistent with the observations of Mr Bland made earlier.
  24. It should also be repeated, as observed above at paragraph 186, that Professor Haynes, in his second report of 14 November 2018, made concessions concerning Mr Bland’s identification of the distinctions between the Applicant’s proposed plant and that at Burgau.
  25. There were several other differences between the two plants conceded by Professor Haynes in cross-examination. They included the fact that the composition of the MSW treated by each plant was different. The Burgau plant had a composition of MSW that included sewage sludge. The Applicant’s does not. It was also conceded that there was no use of steam in the pyrolysis process at Burgau. The Applicant’s plant uses steam to produce electricity.
  26. When it was specifically put to him in cross-examination that, overall, the configuration of the design of the Applicant’s plant is very different to that at Burgau, Professor Haynes conceded that there are significant differences between the plant at Burgau and that of the Applicant.[102]
  27. These concessions made by Professor Haynes are an important factor in the Tribunal preferring the evidence of Mr Bland on this topic and his evidence overall to that of Professor Haynes.
  28. The Tribunal should make some observations concerning this portion of the evidence from Professor Haynes. It is surprising indeed that the error in paragraph 11.5 of his first report exposed in cross-examination did not see the light of day much earlier. Professor Haynes, in response to a question from counsel for the Applicant, stated he was presuming it would come up and that he would say so. This indicates that he had been aware of, and alive to, the error in that paragraph of his report for some time. It seems quite unfortunate, to say the least, that he did not correct this error at the earliest opportunity. One would have expected an eminent expert such as Professor Haynes to have corrected the error at the earliest opportunity after he realised that an error had been made, or, at the very latest, by the time he prepared his second report. The failure to correct this error by Professor Haynes is also amplified by reason of the fact that Mr Morey, at paragraphs 29 and 30 of his second witness statement made on 30 October 2017, reproduced the contents of paragraph 11.5 of Professor Haynes’ first report and disagreed with its contents, providing the reasons for doing so. The Tribunal therefore concludes that Professor Haynes must have been familiar with these paragraphs of Mr Morey’s second statement by the time he prepared his second statement on 14 November 2018. In the face of this reasoned and rational explanation by Mr Morey for disagreeing with Professor Haynes’ opinion expressed at paragraph 11.5 of his first report, it is telling that Professor Haynes did not correct his error until he was cross-examined.
  29. Another matter referred to in Professor Haynes’ first report was also exposed as being wrong in cross-examination. In paragraph 11.3, he stated that the Applicant’s process shown in the PFD includes shredding, drying, pyrolysis, combustion of the pyrolysis vapours and steam generation. He opined that these operations are common to all MSW plants. It was put to him that such an opinion was wrong insofar as steam generation applies and he agreed that it was. As with the observations concerning the incorrect portions of paragraph 11.5 referred to above, it is surprising to the Tribunal that this portion of his report that was conceded by him to be wrong also did not see the light of day earlier.
  30. Even if one were to give Professor Haynes the benefit of the doubt in relation to the two errors identified, it seems inconceivable that these matters were not clarified when he adopted his report at the commencement of his evidence and stated that he believed the opinions expressed in it to be accurate. Overall, his approach to these errors do not reflect well on him as an expert witness. It does not assist the Tribunal.
  31. The other aspect that emerges from this portion of Professor Haynes’ evidence is that it does, the Tribunal finds, identify significant differences between the Applicant’s design and that of the Burgau plant. The Applicant’s design does have the hallmarks of new knowledge as contemplated by section 355.25(1)(b) of the ITA Act.
  32. For these reasons, the Tribunal cannot accept the contention of Professor Haynes that the “additional considerations” and the knowledge sought to be obtained by the Applicant only involved a simple progression of knowledge contained in existing studies. The significant differences between the design of the Burgau plant and that of the Applicant is consistent with Mr Bland’s evidence that such activities can provide new knowledge, and so too can the application, integration and optimisation of these processes’ building blocks. The Tribunal accepts his contention that a sophisticated process plant is not something built by simply taking pieces of pre-existing equipment ‘off-the-shelf’ and bolting them together, not that the Tribunal considers that occurred in this case.
  33. Another matter that should also be raised concerning the differences between the Burgau plant and that proposed by the Applicant, arises from the evidence of Mr Morey. He visited the Burgau plant and inspected it. His evidence concerning what he observed of the differences between the two has been recorded at paragraph 135 above. That evidence has been accepted by the Tribunal. It does, to some considerable extent, corroborate the opinions expressed by Mr Bland and, insofar as it does so, provides another reason for preferring his evidence on this topic over that of Professor Haynes.
  34. In response to Professor Haynes’ opinion on the question that the choice, design and integration are essential to the technical and commercial success of the project, much has already been stated about the sizing of the kiln. Mr Bland observed that sizing of the kiln to ensure that required residence times and heating areas were both met would normally be considered one of the most important things to be checked. He described this as a significant difference, given that the Applicant required a scale up of its kiln when compared with that at Burgau by a factor of nearly 6. He stated that it would have been vital for PLPD and Lycopodium to research a wide variety of variables. Such variables might include residence time, heat transfer area, wall thickness, rotational speed, materials of construction and kiln filling factor.[103] Mr Bland further opined that there can be a considerable amount of work required and new knowledge generated to achieve this end. The Tribunal prefers this contention. Further, in the course of his subsequent evidence before the Tribunal, Professor Haynes did not rebut this aspect of Mr Bland’s evidence either in his several reports or in the witness box.
  35. Mr Bland’s evidence was the subject of robust criticism from the Respondent by reason of his high level or “top-down” approach, as opposed to that of Professor Haynes who adopted, as he described it, a “low-level” approach which broke the activities down into individual steps, or as he put it on one occasion during the course of his evidence, “digging down into detail”. When Professor Haynes did undertake this step, he felt there was a lack of evidence.
  36. The Respondent contended that Mr Bland’s assessment and conclusion was not based upon an analysis of contemporaneous documentation, but rather a willingness to assume the accuracy of statements made in documents, including the application and the several witness statements filed in this proceeding. Alternatively, the Respondent argued that Mr Bland focused on outcomes, which caused him to make assumptions as to the work undertaken to achieve such an outcome. Indeed, the Respondent produced Schedule A to its closing submission which was a table containing examples of assumptions or inferences made by Mr Bland as to the nature of the work performed. The Respondent contended that inferences drawn by Mr Bland and/or those assumptions made by him could not be used to fill gaps in the contemporaneous documentation setting out the conduct of the claimed experimental activities.
  37. The Applicant tackled this contention of the Respondent largely by submitting that, if the evidence of the Applicant’s lay witnesses is accepted, the Tribunal should conclude that the assumptions made by Mr Bland are established. Therefore, many of Professor Haynes’ criticisms fall away. The Tribunal accepts this submission. Findings of fact have been made earlier in these reasons after having had the opportunity of hearing the several witnesses called by the Applicant as well as considering the contents of the documentary evidence that was tendered. Having accepted the evidence adduced, the Tribunal agrees that the assumptions made by Mr Bland by and large were correct.[104] Therefore, it is open to it to accept the “top down” approach adopted by Mr Bland.
  38. It should be repeated that Professor Haynes’ report and, to some degree, his evidence from the witness box is, as contended by the Applicant, irrelevant. The reasons for reaching this conclusion are that the question is one of purpose, rather than the approach that he relied upon which was resolution of fundamental uncertainty or even novelty. On its proper construction and in the light of the decision in Moreton Resources, as observed several times already in these reasons, the words used in the relevant section of the ITA Act are capable of applying, depending on the circumstances, to activities that have the purpose of generating new knowledge with respect to the application of existing technology at a new site. The Tribunal accepts this contention of the Applicant is a further reason for preferring the evidence of Mr Bland over Professor Haynes.
  39. The Tribunal also accepts the contention of the Applicant that Professor Haynes was prepared to express unqualified opinions in his reports that he later corrected in cross-examination. Several of these were highlighted by the Applicant in closing submissions. In his first report, he stated that it could not have been known in advance that there would be insufficient energy in MSW to produce liquid fuel, run the plant and generate electricity. In cross-examination he conceded that there was evidence that this had been achieved in China. However, there was little evidence of this claim. He then sought to argue his evidence that, notwithstanding this concession, the process would not be commercially viable. When further probed, he conceded that there were potential revenue streams but no reliable data costs. Ultimately, both experts agreed that it was not known or determined whether the process would be commercially viable or otherwise. This is another reason for preferring Mr Bland’s evidence or opinions over those of Professor Haynes.
  40. Another feature of Professor Haynes’ evidence which leads the Tribunal to prefer that of Mr Bland arose from his observations in the first report concerning the Burgau plant. There were errors in this report in stating that heating the feed in a dryer using flue gases was standard practice and steam generation is common to all pyrolysis plants. Professor Haynes did not alert the Tribunal to these errors until such time as he was carefully probed in cross-examination by counsel for the Applicant. This is surprising. Experts from time to time for all sorts of reasons make errors. It is usual for them to, upon realising this, alert all people concerned, not leave it till cross-examination. Professor Haynes stated in the course of his cross-examination that he was “presuming it would come up, and I was presuming I would tell you”. This is quite remarkable and does not demonstrate an appreciation of an expert’s obligations under the Tribunal’s Persons Giving Expert and Opinion Evidence Guideline 2015 and in particular his obligation to assist the Tribunal as a responsible expert should.
  41. Some further comments should be made about the contents of the joint experts’ report. The Respondent contends that for the activities for which there is agreement between the experts in the joint experts’ report that they do not meet all of the statutory requirements to be core R & D activities, the Tribunal should find accordingly.[105]
  42. The Tribunal accepts the submissions of the Applicant concerning this report and that it has its limitations. It should be considered in the light of what the decision-maker’s statutory task is. The Applicant contends, and the Tribunal agrees, that the language of section 355-25(1) allows for a set of related experimental activities to be registered and evaluated against the statutory criteria. Therefore, a consideration of a single outcome arising from a set of related experimental activities is permitted.
  43. The statutory criteria should be applied having regard to the activities as a related set of activities directed at testing the viability of creating a commercial scale plant that could convert MSW into electricity, fuel and char, which had not previously been successfully achieved.
  44. Two passages from Mr Bland’s evidence reinforce this conclusion. In one passage he held that the Applicant had undertaken the work at a high level, developed concepts, and then evaluated those concepts using established principles of engineering to see whether they will work in a technical or commercial sense, and then to see whether there is a better way of doing the same thing to get a better outcome.[106]
  45. In another passage he describes how one can compare it with the development of an aero engine. The Applicant has had an idea to develop the business and examine what has already been done in that space. They found examples of some relevance which they have then tested to determine whether such examples can be used. Where they have been found lacking, the Applicant has sought to address those deficiencies and to improve the process. Whilst they may not have developed a process that meets their commercial goals with sufficient technical reliability it shows several improvements over what has been done before.[107]
  46. The Tribunal also considers that the joint report has limited utility because Mr Bland and Professor Haynes were not requested to address, as contended by the Applicant, any relevant question. That question they were asked to address was whether the activities could “reasonably be expected” to generate new knowledge. Correctly, the Applicant contends that the question posed by the statute is whether the Applicant had the necessary purpose of generating new knowledge, not whether the relevant activities could reasonably be expected to generate new knowledge.
  47. There was another ground that the Applicant relied upon to justify its contention that such report had its limitations. This contention centred around the fact that many of the “activities” addressed were not in fact activities as such. By way of example, the Applicant contended, and the Tribunal agrees, that the inclusion of documents in the preliminary Project Scope Report were not an “activity” in any relevant sense. By way of contrast, referring to the production of the Technical Summary and Final Design Overview Report, a single activity tends to overlook the collective activities undertaken that resulted in the production of the report concerned.

CONSIDERATION OF SPECIFIC ISSUES RAISED BY THE RESPONDENT

  1. The Respondent identified several specific issues upon which it relied in support of a contention that the reviewable decision should be affirmed.[108] These issues were derived from the contents of Professor Haynes’ first report. It is appropriate to address them in these reasons.

Absence of documentary records

  1. The first specific issue raised by the Respondent was that there was no reference in the “output documents” to a hypothesis or activities being directed towards testing a hypothesis. It should be recalled that the Applicant’s hypothesis was that the proposed MSW treatment process would enable capability to produce both electricity and fuel. As the Applicant contends, if one examines the consultancy agreement between the Applicant and PLPD dated 15 August 2014, the fundamentals of the hypothesis are recorded in “Schedule 1 - Particulars”, under the sub-heading “1. Services”. Whilst the Tribunal observes that it is most likely not an “output document” as contemplated by Professor Haynes, it does demonstrate the existence of the hypothesis and the planned progression of the work to be undertaken. It is a contemporaneous document given the date it bears. The fundamental elements of the hypothesis are identified in that part of the consultancy agreement, being that the proposed MSW treatment plant would enable capability to produce both electricity and fuel.
  2. The fundamentals of the hypothesis also appear in several of the other documents in evidence before the Tribunal apart from the terms of the consultancy agreement itself. For instance, the relevant spreadsheets contained in exhibits “MA-15”, “MA-16” and “MA-17” to the second witness statement of Mr A contain the elements, or integers, of the hypothesis proposed by the Applicant. The contents of those spreadsheets are referred to for their full force and effect. However, by way of example, they identify the processes, heat calculations, the mass balance at a pyrolysis kiln identifying the material introduced to the kiln and the products leaving the kiln such as gas, which of course is fuel for the purposes of the hypothesis.

Fundamental errors in the work performed

  1. The next complaint of the Respondent, relying on the first report of Professor Haynes, is that there were fundamental errors in the work performed. Professor Haynes described the primary output of the conceptual process design as the PFD. This diagram was for the pyrolysis plant. He described from his observation three features[109] which appeared to him to be grossly inaccurate or un-physical.[110] Mr Bland disagreed and considered that, notwithstanding any errors that may have been identified by Professor Haynes, there was still a systematic progression of work based upon established principles of science.[111]
  2. Mr Bland also approached it from the perspective that the Applicant had found some examples of relevance then sought to test whether those examples can be used. Where such examples have been found to be lacking, they sought to address those deficiencies and improve the process. This may well take place where there are errors in the process. It still shows applications of principles of established science in this case chemical engineering.[112]
  3. Another important point of distinction between Mr Bland and Professor Haynes arises from the opinion expressed by Mr Bland that in the case of pyrolysis of MSW, the overall reactions are more complex due to lower temperatures (400 to 500°C) together with the complexity of the MSW. The result of these considerations is that more reactions occur during the course of the pyrolysis which has the effect of producing a wider range of chemical products which have a number of possible characteristics on any particular day, including but not limited to sticky, corrosive, reactive, capacity to act as a solvent and so on. Those characteristics can cause significant operating issues such as, but not limited to, blockages, corrosion, environmental issues and failure of bearings and seals.
  4. Professor Haynes did concede in cross-examination, when pressed, that an experiment might be conducted badly. However, it does not follow that, because it was done badly at the outset, the outcome was something that could be known or determined in advance. He did steadfastly maintain that because of the errors the whole process was invalid. This concession made by him is consistent with the observations of Mr Bland both in his reports and from the witness box. In particular, this concession sits consistently with the observations made in the previous paragraph concerning the opinions expressed by Mr Bland.
  5. The Tribunal considers that Mr Bland’s assessment is to be preferred. The fact that there are errors in the work product, however they may be described, whether fundamental or otherwise, does not necessarily mean that the work was not carried out in a manner consistent with a systematic progression of work based on established principles of science. There may well be errors made for a variety of reasons, but it does not exclude the possibility nonetheless of the conclusion being reached, as has occurred in this matter.
  6. The Applicant submits that, in any event, when one considers the construction and meaning of section 355-25 of the ITA Act, it is not concerned with the quality of the work carried out in any given step. The Tribunal agrees with this contention. Whilst of course it must naturally be preferred that errors are avoided, presumably Parliament would have, when drafting this section, included a proviso, or prerequisite that the experimental activities within the contemplation of it be error-free, or adopted some other language that would have left a party seeking the benefit of such legislation in no doubt as to a requirement of accuracy in the relevant sense. This should also be looked at in light of the objects of the ITA Act which are designed to encourage research and development. It is timely in this context to recall that section 355-5 of the ITA Act states that the object of the division is to encourage industry to conduct research and development activities that might otherwise not be conducted because of an uncertain return from such activities, in cases where the knowledge gained is likely to benefit the wider Australian community. It should be emphasised that the objects contemplate research being undertaken by private industry not institutions such as universities or other research organisations with which we are familiar, such as the CSIRO or perhaps the Walter and Eliza Hall Institute. It is, as contended by the Applicant, beneficial legislation which should be construed in a manner consistent with its text and objects.
  7. Also in the context of errors, it should not be lost sight of that Mr Morey in his evidence observed that Lycopodium focused on taking the design that PLPD had provided, peer reviewing it to determine if it would work, and modifying it as necessary so that it could be developed for the next phase of the project. He also stated, in his first witness statement, that one of the first tasks undertaken by Lycopodium when it was retained was to review the design, provide input into it and carry out a sanity check to determine whether the technology would really work for MSW. The engineering drawings furnished by PLPD to Lycopodium were used to construct a 3D model of the plant. As Mr Morey said, such 3D model continued to develop throughout the progression of the project. The Tribunal accepts this evidence from Mr Morey.[113]
  8. Mr Morey, in paragraph 12 of his second witness statement, exhibited a copy of a M & E Balance Review completed by Mr Brian Zugna, the Principal Process Consultant of Lycopodium.[114] Mr Morey readily acknowledged that the review identified some errors in the M & E Balance at the stage the document was produced. He was probed on this in cross-examination and consistently maintained that, whilst there were errors in some of the calculations and assumptions, he did not believe the process itself was flawed. He emphasised that Lycopodium was highlighting to the client that there were some areas that needed closer investigation in the next phase of the work. It should be noted that following an exchange of correspondence it was agreed between the parties that it would not be put that Mr Zugna did not review the mass balance calculation. The review was apparently undertaken in approximately April 2015.
  9. The Applicant contends and the Tribunal agrees that, even if there were still errors in it, the process of undertaking a mass and energy balance and having it reviewed by Lycopodium shows an application of principles of established science, as it contends, that of chemical engineering.
  10. Presumably, errors (however they may be described), including as identified by Professor Haynes, could be, and would be, addressed by all relevant parties having an input to the process from time to time, as required. It would also, presumably, have the features of a process of refinement. In this consideration, one must also recall that the project was not solely undertaken during the one financial year with which the Tribunal is concerned in this application. On this topic, Mr A during his cross-examination, was questioned about, amongst other things, the whole project being the development and construction of a new commercial scale MSW plant. His response was that when he referred to the “whole project” he meant the Applicant’s entire project which he said could span over 5 to 10 years.[115] This evidence is understandable and consistent with Mr Morey’s evidence about the ongoing and dynamic nature of the project. Mr A was not pressed on this response and it is accepted by the Tribunal. As was apparent from the material, in a subsequent financial year Lycopodium further refined the design of the MSW treatment facility. Indeed, Mr Morey, in exhibit “PM-4” of his first witness statement, exhibited an updated Project Scope Report issued in or about July 2016. Presumably, in the refinement process, further errors could have, and would have, been addressed.
  11. Another aspect of Mr Morey’s evidence concerning errors should be referred to. In the witness box he was asked whether it is good practice to keep a PFD up to date. In response he stated that in the majority of projects once the development proceeds to the stage of piping and instrumentation diagram (P & ID), the PFD’s are not up to date. One of the principal reasons for this is that most clients do not wish to pay for it. The Tribunal finds this understandable. He further observed that his company “would love” its clients to pay to keep the PFD as a live and up-to-date document, even when it moves to the P & ID.[116] Another observation he made along these lines was that as the information contained in the PFD and the P & ID is retained in the computer database or control system, this then means that the computer and its contents (or data base) as he put it really became a live document for most clients, not the PFD. He also put it another way as being drawings on the computer screen identifying all the key equipment required for a project. Whilst an up-to-date PFD might well be, as he put it, traditionally good practice, it does not accord with the commercial reality. Most clients are ultimately interested in the core process information that is stored in the computer. The Tribunal accepts Mr Morey’s evidence on this topic. In a range of commercial endeavours, clients more than ever tend to be cost conscious, and if money can be saved by omission or limitation of a step and the ultimate result still achieved, most will adopt that course of conduct.
  12. Mr Morey’s evidence on this topic in his second witness statement was also in a similar vein. He observed (at paragraph 45) during Lycopodium’s review of the M & E Balance, certain errors in respect of the data were noted and adjusted as the design developed into the more detailed P & IDs. Due to Lycopodium’s previous experience with the soil treatment facility he said there was more focus on the progression of the P & ID. He observed that the PFD’s are useful in the earlier stages of the design phase, but it is not a commercial practice to always keep this document revised and up to date. He reiterated this evidence in the witness box.[117] He stated that in the majority of his projects, once Lycopodium “get into the P & ID’s the PFD is not kept up-to-date”. Once again to his credit, he readily acknowledged that whilst it is good practice and he would love his clients to pay to keep the PFD as a live up-to-date document, generally that does not occur. He also made the relevant comment in response to a question from the Tribunal that no one has built a plant from a PFD. The Tribunal accepts this evidence. This contention by Mr Morey was not challenged or contradicted by Professor Haynes.
  13. Further, during Mr Morey’s evidence as to errors in the M & E Balance calculations, particularly in cross-examination, he was taken to spreadsheets forming part of exhibit “PM-6” to his first witness statement. M & E Balance calculations had been provided by PLPD to Lycopodium on or about 20 March 2015. These calculations were assessed by Lycopodium’s principal process consultant, Mr Brian Zugna. His assessment of those calculations was contained in several of the spreadsheets comprising exhibit “PM-6”.[118] Mr Zugna found errors in those spreadsheets. He provided comments in the appropriate column of the spreadsheet concerning those errors. Mr Morey readily acknowledged the errors concerned and candidly stated that they represented work that still needed to be resolved. The Tribunal accepts this evidence and further considers the response to be understandable. It is consistent with his evidence that the project was ongoing and subject to further refinement. In undertaking that refinement, he readily acknowledged that there were errors in some of the calculations and assumptions but did not agree that the process itself was flawed. He considered that the errors identified by Mr Zugna (amongst others) were highlighted and would require closer investigation, and presumably rectification, in the next phase of the work.
  14. The evidence of Mr Morey on this topic was largely corroborated by the evidence of Professor Haynes from the witness box during which he explained that in process engineering there is a hierarchy of process engineering development. The hierarchy normally commences with what he described as a scheme diagram, a block diagram, moving to a PFD and, from that, the P & ID. He described this as following a natural course.[119] Presumably, the document that sits at the top of the hierarchy is, more likely than not, to assume primacy for a commercial operation such as that undertaken by the Applicant, and also in a process engineering sense.
  15. During his evidence from the witness box, Mr Bland was probed about errors, particularly concerning the work undertaken by PLPD identified in subparagraphs 8(a), (b) and (c) of the joint experts’ report. He responded that there was some considerable checking of such work by Lycopodium in the course of its retainer. The Project Scope Report prepared by it in May 2015 in his opinion corrected quite a few of those errors. He described this process of correction as part of a systematic approach to scientific work.[120] This is also consistent with and corroborative of Mr Morey’s evidence on this topic, which the Tribunal accepts. The overall impact of errors must also be considered in light of the fact that this project was an ongoing one. Mr Morey observed in his evidence that subsequent work was carried out on the project after provision of the initial Project Scope Report issued on 18 May 2015.[121] This further work, as he put it, refined the design of the facility. The refined design was contained in what he described as an “updated Project Scope Report” issued in July 2016,[122] which included a Project Execution Plan for the construction of the facility on the site alongside the existing soil treatment facility. Once again, presumably in carrying out the further work which led to the refining of the design for such facility, any further errors in the work could and most likely would have been corrected. It is considered that this would limit the impact of such errors and serves as further evidence of the systematic approach to scientific work suggested by Mr Bland.
  16. Mr Morey was probed in cross-examination quite searchingly about several of the errors.[123] To his credit, he readily acknowledged those errors, as noted above. He reiterated that he did not see there being fundamental flaws with the overall process. He agreed that there were errors in some of the calculations and assumptions which have been previously identified. However, he reiterated that the process itself was not flawed and that those areas where errors had been identified would receive closer investigation in the next phase of the work. The Tribunal accepts this evidence from Mr Morey. It is also consistent with the fact that the project itself would take more than one year from commencement to completion.
  17. This evidence concerning errors must be viewed with the realities of developing a project of this magnitude in mind. Whilst registration under the IR & D Act occurs yearly, such a project as the MSW treatment plant proposed by the Applicant, almost invariably will take several years from commencement to completion. There is no requirement that the Core R & D and Supporting R & D activities be wholly completed within the course of one year. Errors of whatever type would almost invariably be corrected from time to time as the project evolves.
  18. Mr A also touched on the question of errors in his evidence. He said that to progress from one step or phase of the process to the next in the development of a facility using pyrolysis technology for the treatment of MSW, it was necessary to complete the previous step. He stated that the completed step did not need to be achieved with 100 per cent accuracy. However, it needed to be developed sufficiently to deliver outcomes that would enable commencement of the next phase with confidence. Additionally, in this context, he observed that, when dealing with a facility such as this on a commercial scale, it is a large project and involves several elements including the market, finance, and logistics. The technological outcomes are only one part of the entire package of commercial considerations that affect a project of this type. Presumably, the tolerance of errors in a commercial setting may be different to that which experts in a non-commercial environment would accept. This evidence, which the Tribunal accepts, does to some considerable extent corroborate Mr Morey’s evidence concerning errors. It is also a basis for accepting the Applicant’s contentions concerning the hypothesis, and the purpose of the activities concerned.

Lack of a logical process

  1. A criticism of the Applicant’s work by the Respondent was said to be the lack of a logical process.[124] Professor Haynes described it in his first report as being the case that some of the activities claimed by the Applicant were out of order. He identified three matters which he considered supported a conclusion that the activities were out of order from the point of view of normal project engineering progression. Firstly, the PFD was developed without closing the knowledge gaps around process fundamentals identified in the original R & D application. Secondly, the production of detailed mechanical designs by PLPD prior to finalisation of the PFD, when any changes in the PFD would very likely necessitate redesign. Thirdly, any changes to the materials used for construction would very likely necessitate redesign.
  2. Mr Bland, by way of response, amongst other things considered that the explanation of the three-step process, given by Mr A in paragraphs 17 to 43 of his second witness statement, did identify a logical progression of the work. It seems to the Tribunal that when one examines those paragraphs of the second witness statement of Mr A, there is a logical process engaged. Whilst there might well be a question of sequencing, as Professor Haynes identified by reference to the three activities or issues, it does not deprive that three-step process of any logic whatsoever. The steps on their face each have the indicia of being both logical and systematic. It may well be, as Professor Haynes opines, that this could likely necessitate redesign. However, that is a matter that can only be determined by future application and use of the work product derived from the activities embodied in the three-step process which was described in Mr A’s second witness statement amongst other things. The totality of the evidence adduced before the Tribunal of the hearing of this application must also be considered and is relevant to this conclusion. It need not be reproduced further for the purpose of these reasons.
  3. Reference should be made also to Appendix E referred to in paragraph 5 of Mr Bland’s first report. That paragraph recorded six steps followed by the Applicant to achieve the outcome of the activities concerned. He expressed the opinion that the six steps undertaken by the Applicant followed the path visually depicted in the colour green in that appendix. The Tribunal observed it to be a logical process.
  4. There was much reference in the course of the hearing to spreadsheets that had been prepared by the Applicant and received in evidence. The Tribunal considers that the spreadsheets themselves, and the various evidence from several witnesses who were taken to their contents, also reveal the existence of a logical process and a systematic progression of work. The Applicant pointed out, in the course of both evidence and submissions, that it could not have been known or determined in advance how the particular configuration of the pyrolysis plant to treat MSW would be configured; that was with two trains instead of one, with a pre-dryer (which the Applicant submitted and Professor Haynes conceded was unexpected), with the use of steam to heat the dryer, and the use of the recycling and re-burning of the flue gas prior to its entry into the chimney. This configuration significantly differentiates the Applicant’s proposed plant from that situated in Burgau.
  5. The Applicant in its submissions included an “Appendix A, Evidence of activities”. This appendix helpfully identified each activity in the category of “Core activity 1.1” and “Supporting Activity 1.1.1 – Project Evaluation and Documentation.” Appendix A was tabulated with each activity identified and reference made to the evidence before the Tribunal at the hearing of this application, which was the witness statements of the several witnesses who gave evidence, and exhibits thereto, together with transcript references of their viva voce evidence. The analysis contained in Appendix A demonstrates a logical process. The Tribunal considers this logical process to be undertaken within the meaning of both sections 355-25 and 355-30 of the ITA Act.
  6. It is worthwhile identifying the activities referred to by the Applicant in Appendix A. The specific references to the evidence relied upon in support of each of the processes need not be repeated in these reasons. However, those references are referred to and relied upon in reaching the conclusion that there was indeed a logical process engaged in by the Applicant.
  7. “Core activity 1.1 – Process design” activities identified are:

Process design involving:

  1. Calculating the required equipment sizing, and amending sizing based on the equipment operating efficiency;
  2. Determining how effectively the equipment transfer heat and testing equipment reaction times processing the inputs;
  1. Detailed and mechanical design of the thermal units, including pyrolysis kiln, drum dryer, burning chamber and steam generation boiler;
  1. Experimenting with different volumes of MSW feeds to determine the volume that can be processed by the treatment facility, an analysis of how the inputs chemically react and transfer through the process.

Modelling involving:

  1. Process simulation of inputs to develop a detailed process design. There wasn’t a guarantee of consistent inputs. As a result, the simulation involves various types of MSW compositions to ensure there was a flexibility of inputs;
  2. Process simulation involving experimentation with alternative combinations of construction materials to mitigate heat loss.

Mechanical design involving:

  1. Designing the treatment facility, which required trialling various equipment sizing, and equipment support structures to allow equipment to expand during heat up to reaction at 600° Celsius;
  2. Determining the ideal operating conditions to mitigate the impacts of equipment distortions while enabling ongoing equipment operation for the core pyrolysis section;
  1. Designing general plant input and output section.

    1. Verification across the engineering calculations with respect to design inputs and outputs;
    2. Preparation of specifications for equipment that can be procured “off-the-shelf” to support the proprietary equipment;
    1. Development of drawings for equipment, including the dimensions of equipment and inputs, and support structures;
    1. Determination of whether the specifications would also comply with the relevant Australian Standards;
    2. Preparation of the report, including process description, equipment and instrument lists etc.

    1. Project management/scheduling;
    2. Meetings with design team to evaluate progress and design changes;
    1. Analysis to determine unknowns;
    1. Meetings with suppliers;
    2. Procurement of materials.

The identification of a hypothesis

  1. The next specific issue raised by the Respondent concerning the Applicant’s case was said to be the fact that there was very little, if any, identification of a contemporaneous record of a hypothesis or objective.
  2. This has largely been canvassed earlier in these reasons.
  3. The Applicant, as noted earlier, pointed to the hypothesis being included in the consultancy agreement with PLPD dated 15 August 2014.
  4. There was some considerable debate in the submissions between the Applicant and the Respondent about the necessity for an applicant to produce evidence by way of contemporaneous documentation. Understandably, the Respondent places considerable emphasis on what it describes as a paucity of a contemporaneous record of a hypothesis or objective. It also contends, in accordance with established authority, that the purpose of claimed research and development activity is not to be determined by means of a retrospective reconstruction, inference or attribution of purpose after the activity has been carried out. Rather, it directs attention as to the aims and objectives of the activity before and during the carrying out of the research activity.
  5. The Applicant does not quarrel with this contention. However, it does contend, and the Tribunal agrees with such contention, that the purpose of such research and development activity can be established by viva voce evidence.[125]
  6. There is the evidence of such witnesses as Mr A who described a hypothesis in terms of whether or not it was possible to construct a large-scale MSW treatment facility that could produce both electricity, fuel and char.[126] In the course of his evidence he also explained the hypothesis or purpose in these terms, or terms such as “a commercial scale pyrolysis plant” or “a commercial scale facility”. Evidence was also given by him that the overall project of developing a commercial scale pyrolysis facility to process MSW involved a series of sequential steps. To progress from one step or phase to the next it was necessary to complete the previous step. He stated that the completed step did not need to be achieved with 100 per cent accuracy. However, it needed to be developed sufficiently to deliver outcomes that would enable commencement of the next of those steps with confidence. This explanation was consistent with his evidence concerning his investigations about the possible application of pyrolysis technology to process MSW. The evidence given by him concerning these investigations are considerably corroborated by reason of his evidence concerning the development of the soil treatment facility. The Applicant engaged PLPD to design and construct the soil treatment facility which was completed and operated under a commissioning approval issued by the Victorian Environmental Protection Authority. This facility uses pyrolysis to treat contaminated soil. It should be noted that both the Applicant and PLPD each successfully applied to have the R & D activities concerned with the design and construction of that soil treatment facility registered. If pyrolysis technology could be applied to treatment of contaminated soils, presumably it could be applied to the processing of MSW. The Tribunal accepts Mr A’s evidence concerning his formulation of the hypothesis, and his investigations leading to it.
  7. Mr A also gave evidence concerning the existence of the hypothesis which was briefly touched on in a consideration of his evidence above. In cross-examination it was put to him that the purpose of the activities undertaken by the Applicant, including the design, it’s refinement at various stages, change of materials and detailed specifications (including the various elements), was for the development and construction of a commercial scale MSW treatment plant rather than the testing of a hypothesis or the development of new knowledge. He disputed this and stated that it certainly was, and further, that the work carried out in the initial phase certainly was for such purposes, otherwise, there would be no progress to the next phase. There is much logic to such a response, it was consistent with his earlier evidence and the Tribunal accepts this evidence from Mr A.
  8. Mr G gave evidence of the existence of the hypothesis. Particularly, this was in relation to the Respondent’s contention that there was a lack of contemporaneous records identifying what the hypothesis was. He also stated that he had seen the consultancy agreement between the Applicant and PLPD which contained the deliverables listed within it, which PLPD was responsible for undertaking. The Tribunal accepts this evidence. His evidence was also consistent with the “top-down” approach adopted by Mr Bland which was to look at the end product of the activities undertaken. When probed as to whether there was a documented plan, to his credit he readily conceded that it was quite informal and that the documentation was largely produced for the purposes of the end product or end of the project as he put it. This is also consistent with the evidence, not only of Mr Bland but also of Mr Morey, that, in a commercial setting, the level of record-keeping would not be as detailed or comprehensive as might be found in another institution or a university research setting. It is also relevant in terms of the industry-based objects of the IR & D Act which direct the decision-maker towards industry and venture capital considerations.
  9. The Respondent placed much emphasis on a contention that any hypothesis that included commercial production of liquid fuels was disproved in approximately September 2014. This was indeed conceded by Mr A in cross-examination. As the Respondent further correctly contends, there was no evidence, certainly in a documentary form, that the hypothesis had been revised after the realisation that production of liquid fuel was not feasible, or, put another way, the hypothesis had been disproven.[127] The experts were probed during the course of their evidence from the witness box concerning the issue of liquid fuel generation. Mr Bland and Professor Haynes confirmed that neither of them knew of a study that addressed the production of liquid fuels from MSW. It was suggested the outcome could reasonably be expected by way of analogy of the production of liquid fuels by pyrolysed biomass. Mr Bland stated that liquid fuels had been produced from biomass, which is a plant material, previously. He further stated that refining of such liquid fuel would be costly and expensive. He was aware of the embryonic stage research in the United States and Europe concerning these processes. He did not think anyone had progressed to a pilot plant as yet.
  10. Mr A gave evidence that the knowledge concerning the production of liquid fuels did follow on from some of the work that had been undertaken, including the production of the mass and energy balance in the discussions of various professionals who had been engaged about the overall outcome of the project. He gave evidence that it would not have been possible to have achieved the outcome (which was the conclusion that it was impossible to generate liquid fuels by the pyrolysis of MSW) unless a process had been undertaken.
  11. The fact that the production of liquid fuels had also been disproved by September 2014 does not mean that the hypothesis postulated by the Applicant no longer applied or was in some other way invalid, void, or of no force or effect. One limb of the hypothesis was disproved. The remainder continued and was addressed.
  12. The Respondent places considerable emphasis, not only with respect to the hypothesis issue, on the contention that the activities as such were not research but a commercial venture involving the design and construction of a plant. This in turn suggests that the purpose was not the generation of new knowledge. Whilst this is understandable, and the issue of purpose will be touched on later in these reasons, the Tribunal does not accept this contention. As noted earlier, the Tribunal accepts the evidence of the lay witnesses called by the Applicant. Without the existence of a hypothesis which the Tribunal considers has been established, with subsequent testing there would not be a commercial venture involving the design and construction of the plant.

Assumptions as to MSW composition

  1. Another issue raised by the Respondent concerns assumptions as to MSW composition. MSW feedstock varies in its composition. Accordingly, the composition of gas produced as a result of the pyrolysis process varies according to such composition of the MSW feedstock. The Respondent contended that there was no evidence as to how the assumptions of MSW composition were arrived at. Further, it was contended that the assumptions relied upon by the Applicant “were fundamentally inaccurate in terms of moisture content and heating value of different particle sizes.” Professor Haynes expressed several opinions concerning the assumptions relied upon by the Applicant regarding the composition of MSW. Those opinions were expressed in various ways. He stated that the PFD had an invalid set of compositions in terms of the breakdown between moisture, heating value and particle size. He also expressed the opinion that the composition of the MSW assumed in the flowchart was not represented in typical MSW.
  2. Mr Morey, in his second witness statement, together with evidence from the witness box, explained that the data concerned represented extreme values so that the design could accommodate variation in feedstock properties. As he noted in his evidence, the plant does not control the MSW feed. In such circumstances, it would not be possible to fix the source of the waste stream into fines, high calorific, low calorific, high moisture and low moisture categories. It does recognise and accommodate variability in feed properties or composition of the MSW feedstock. Indeed, Mr Bland described it as modelling with unusual characteristics representing the extreme properties of such feedstock. Mr Morey further stated that the adoption of values that represented extremes in the composition of MSW went to the flexibility that the Applicant looked for in their proposed plant. Once again, it was an example of adoption of extremes so that the design could, as he saw it, accommodate variances in the make-up and allocation of the MSW feedstock. Additionally, he also stated, and the Tribunal accepts, that Professor Haynes’ assumption that the PFD was the only iteration of how the plant would run was fundamentally flawed.
  3. The Tribunal accepts the contentions of Mr Morey and Mr Bland concerning this issue. Given that the evidence before the Tribunal is that the composition of MSW feedstock varies in terms of moisture, heating value and particle size, to adopt laymans’ terms, there cannot be a “one size fits all” approach. Mr Morey’s evidence is consistent with the evidence of Mr Bland that, given the variables in its composition, the design intent would be to ensure flexibility and extreme values would be adopted to accommodate the variation in feedstock properties noted earlier. Also, it was contended by the Applicant that any assumption in the PFD as to fine fractions and heat value was not regarded as significant at that stage of the process due to such design flexibility as outlined.

WERE THE ACTIVITIES EXPERIMENTAL?

  1. The Respondent has accepted that the relevant activities comprising Activity 1.1 are “activities” within the meaning of section 355-25(1) of the ITA Act.[133] However, it contends that they are not experimental activities of the type required under section 355-25.[134]
  2. The term “experimental” is not defined in the ITA Act. The parties agree that it should be given its ordinary meaning. Such meaning includes a test or trial, or an act or operation for the purpose of discovering something unknown or testing a principle of supposition.[135]
  3. Having found that the activities, or work, were carried out as referred to earlier in these reasons, the Tribunal also finds that the activities were experimental within the meaning of the section 355-25(1) of the ITA Act.
  4. It is appropriate when considering whether the activities undertaken in this application were experimental within the meaning of section 355-25(1) of the ITA Act to recall some of the observations made by the Full Court of the Federal Court in Moreton Resources Ltd v Innovation and Science Australia.[136] The Full Court noted that the words “experimental activities” in the opening line of section 355-25(1) have very little, if any, work to do beyond reflecting the type of activities described in subparagraphs (a) and (b) of this subsection. The Full Court held that, given the detail and content of the description in subparagraphs (a) and (b), it is difficult to envisage activities that would meet the description in paragraphs (a) and (b) but would not be considered experimental activities. Experimental activities can apply to activities that have the purpose of generating new knowledge with respect to the application of an existing technology at a new site.
  5. Both experts, in the course of their evidence, were questioned about their understanding of the word “experimental” for the purposes of this application. This was by reference to part the description of the three-step process identified in Table 1 “Responses to Questions” in the joint experts’ report of 24 April 2018. They were both questioned about the entries in items 8(a) “A process description, a Mass & Energy balance and PFD”, (b) “A scheme diagram of the system”, and (c) “Piping & Instrumentation diagrams”.[137]
  6. Mr Bland considered the term “experimental” to require evaluating a concept and then assessing the success of the experiment and, where improvements can be identified, going back and developing a new experiment and repeating the process. In this context he identified in his evidence that the experiment undertaken by the Applicant was whether liquid fuels could be produced. He described the activities as development of the PFD, a concept on how such production might be achieved and undertaking various tests involving spreadsheets to determine technical and commercial feasibility. When the Applicant determined that such experiment would not work, a new PFD was developed to look at an alternative, primarily, to produce electricity. In the course of doing so, the process was an improvement over what was already known, particularly when compared with the plant at Burgau.[138]
  7. Professor Haynes described the term “experimental” as a “puristic” approach, where one makes an assumption and then progressively refines the assumptions on the basis of the results which one obtains.[139]
  8. Both experts were then asked whether the diagrams referred to in those sections of item 8 of Table 1 were experimental because they depicted something that hadn’t been done before, or were they referring to the activities involved in the production of the drawings. Both of them stated the “activities” involved in the production of the drawings (sometimes referred to as “diagrams”) themselves were experimental.
  9. One is minded to pose the question, given these two responses by the experts, whether indeed a “trial and error” approach would be captured by the definition. Is not the making of an assumption which is progressively refined or improved a form of trial and error? The Tribunal considers that it probably is in this sense. The evidence of Mr G referred to earlier in these reasons, where he referred to the process of looking at variables, making calculations, reworking the variables and inputs to achieve new calculations involving, to a significant level, trial and error for the purposes of achieving targeted optimal designs, does accord with the definition or concept of experimental in this context and within the meaning of section 355-1(a) the ITA Act.
  10. This conclusion is to some extent also supported by the response of Professor Haynes in cross-examination. He was probed in cross-examination about the differences between the Applicant’s designs and previous designs concerning the dryer and the drying process adopted. Several differences were identified which he conceded. The importance, or perhaps the simplicity or lack thereof, of these differences was the subject of some contention on his part. He further conceded that, before establishing the design of the dryer, a number of calculations needed to be made. He readily conceded that it was a complicated area concerning the process specification. His evidence was that the process specification comes out of understanding how much energy, for example, needed to be transferred. He then critically stated that the design response to that question is, typically and often by trial and error, to identify one approach and then having identified such an approach, learning from it so as to try another one. He described that approach as an experimental design approach proceeding by trial and error[140]. This observation by Professor Haynes is consistent with the steps taken by the Applicant, which the Tribunal has found occurred. It is, to some extent, also an explanation for why errors may be encountered, such errors only being capable of resolution by further refinement in the design and other activities that might be then or subsequently undertaken. His categorisation of this process development as an experimental design approach is consistent with a finding that the activities were experimental within the meaning of the section.
  11. Under the heading “Agreed assumptions regarding design process” in Table 1 of the joint experts’ report, there is identified the three-step process. They are described in items number 23 to 37.[141] The first column “(a) Experimental?” is referred to. The response to most of the questions by Mr Bland and Professor Haynes is to agree that they were experimental.
  12. Both the experts mostly agreed in the third column, “Outcome only determinable by applying a systematic progression of work that (i) is based on principles of established science, (ii) proceeds from hypothesis...to logical conclusions”, that many of those activities were of the kind for which the outcome was only determinable by applying a systematic progression of work of the relevant kind.
  13. In their viva voce evidence both experts considered the activities were experimental. This was particularly with respect to the development of the PFD, which assumed a considerable amount of attention in the course of the evidence and the hearing. This included but was not limited to, the process description, mass and energy balance and PFD; the scheme diagram of the system together with the piping and instrumentation diagrams. The experiments undertaken by the Applicant it should be recalled included whether liquid fuels could be produced by using the pyrolysis process to treat MSW, and further whether the same process could be used to produce electricity. This did also include developing what was already known by reference to the MSW plant at Burgau.
  14. Mr Bland in paragraph 4.1 of his First Report identified the “Experiment” as constituting three steps being:
    1. process design (requiring four components or steps);
    2. modelling (requiring two components or steps); and
    1. mechanical design (requiring three components steps).

The description adopted by Mr Bland of the experiment is referred to. It includes calculations, such things as testing equipment reaction times and processing inputs, experimenting with different volumes of MSW feeds to determine what volumes can be processed, trialling various equipment sizing and designing general plant input and output section. These tasks or activities are experimental according to the ordinary meaning of the term. They surely are based upon untested ideas or techniques which had not yet been established or finalised. As contended for by the Applicant there is an element of scientific procedure involved in these tasks for the purposes of making a discovery, testing a hypothesis, or demonstrating facts without certainty as to outcome.

  1. Another matter referred to in Mr Bland’s first report, which reinforces a finding that the activities undertaken by or on behalf of the Applicant were experimental, arises from the contents of Appendix E to the report.[142] It is headed “Systematic Progression of Work to Determine Outcome of Activities.” This appendix is a flowchart or process diagram and is a visual representation of the evaluation of the hypothesis or, perhaps more accurately, methodologies adopted by the Applicant in undertaking the activities concerned. It identifies each step undertaken in a logical and rational way. Mr Bland stated that the Applicant, as he put it, followed the path delineated in that diagram shown in the colour green. Each task or activity is identified, and each step required to be taken as a result of a finding in the affirmative or the negative after such step is undertaken and recorded. Mr Bland also opined that he knew of no sensible way to conduct the activities, other than by the method described in the flowchart, being Appendix E. This process, when applied to the hypothesis that the proposed MSW treatment process for a hybrid facility would enable capability to produce both electricity and fuel, can only be concluded to be experimental within the meaning of section 355-25(1) of the ITA Act.
  2. Additionally, on the question of whether the activities were experimental, reference should be made to the contents of Mr Bland’s third report of 1 November 2018 in which he made reference to the concept design work undertaken by PLPD in relation to the design of the feed dryer of the proposed MSW pyrolysis plant. He observed that PLPD’s arrangement of the tubes in the kiln was different. He opined that he had never seen such an arrangement before, nor had he been able to find any evidence that such arrangement had been used elsewhere. His conclusion was that, in the circumstances, PLPD’s concept design work on the dryer was experimental. Mr Bland’s opinion on this topic was not the subject of any real challenge from Professor Haynes or in cross-examination. The Tribunal accepts this evidence of Mr Bland. This is a further factor that gives rise to a conclusion that the activities were experimental in the relevant sense.
  3. By reason of the foregoing matters, the Tribunal concludes that the relevant activities were experimental within the meaning of section 355-25(1) of the ITA Act.

WERE THE CORE R & D ACTIVITIES EXPERIMENTAL ACTIVITIES WHOSE OUTCOME COULD NOT BE KNOWN OR DETERMINED IN ADVANCE ON THE BASIS OF CURRENT KNOWLEDGE, INFORMATION OR EXPERIENCE?

  1. In addressing this question, the decision-maker must apply an objective test which is not determined by reference to the knowledge or experience of those undertaking the activities which are the subject matter of the application.[143]
  2. The question for the decision-maker to address is whether the outcome can be known or determined in advance, rather than the probability of achieving such an outcome.
  3. The Tribunal considers that the Core R & D activities were experimental activities whose outcome could not be known or determined in advance on the basis of current knowledge, information or experience.
  4. The starting point for reaching this conclusion is, as contended for by Mr Bland, that such outcomes could not have been determined in advance because there is no available existing knowledge, information or experience about comparable plants that operate satisfactorily. That is, similarly sized plants using a pyrolysis process for the treatment or, as he put it, “destruction” of MSW.[144] When probed on this in the witness box, he stated that there were some similar plants, but none sufficiently comparable for the Applicant’s purpose.[145] Professor Haynes, in response to a similar question in the witness box, stated that he believed that there are comparable plants. He did not identify such plants. Mr Bland then also stated in response that, relating specifically to MSW pyrolysis, technology was far less developed than other pyrolysis processes. Professor Haynes agreed with him.
  5. Mr Bland then contended that the differences between what the Applicant was aiming to achieve when compared with the plant at Burgau were significantly different. Therefore, the result or outcome sought by it required a significant amount of work. Professor Haynes disagreed, contending that he considered what the Applicant achieved was an adaptation within the scope of what would be expected from a competent chemical engineer, without what he would identify as innovation.
  6. The Tribunal prefers the opinion expressed by Mr Bland. There are several reasons why it does so.
  7. Early in his evidence, Professor Haynes acknowledged that the process of treating MSW by the pyrolysis method was not well-established. He readily conceded there were not many plants, or many people who had tried to do it. He conceded that what the Applicant had tried to do was something new. In doing so, he acknowledged a real difficulty for the Applicant, in that it was trying to turn rubbish into something of value. It is, as he saw it, a very difficult thing to do.[146] As noted earlier, Professor Haynes did not identify any comparable plants. One would have expected an expert witness in his position, assuming he was aware of their existence, to have identified them and explained why they were comparable. The Tribunal infers that he did not do so because they do not exist. The Tribunal accepts Mr Bland’s version, discussed earlier, that there is no existing knowledge, information or experience about comparable plants that operate satisfactorily. Indeed, there was no evidence before the Tribunal of an existing comparable plant capable of processing the large volumes of MSW in the way identified in the documentation prepared by the Applicant.
  8. PLPD’s “star-shaped” design of the heating pipe arrangement is different to traditional designs or arrangements of the pipes. There was no evidence to indicate that the arrangement of tubes in a star shaped configuration had been adopted in any other plant or proposed plant. Certainly, there was no evidence that it had been adopted in a proposed plant using the pyrolysis method for the treatment of MSW.
  9. The Burgau plant for treatment of MSW differs from the proposed plant of the Applicant depicted in the material before the Tribunal. Much has already been said about the differences between them in these reasons at paragraphs 210 to 220 and 286 to 288, in the section above addressing the “Specific Issues Arising” identified by the Respondent in its closing submissions under the sub-heading “The similarities and differences between the project design and other projects”.[147] Those paragraphs and their contents are referred to and repeated.
  10. The Burgau plant did not have a separate drying step (or perhaps one should describe it as a separate dryer). Mr Bland noted in his first report that the MSW used in Burgau was shredded and not dried before entering the only kiln in that plant. He further expressed the opinion that separate drying and pyrolysis is a major variation from the process adopted by Burgau, which had integrated drying and pyrolysis. His conclusion was that data on the latter approach could not reliably be used for the development of the former without additional work such as that carried out by the Applicant. This new work is presumably generating new knowledge within the meaning of the section. This opinion is supported by the material and it is considered by the Tribunal to be a logical conclusion given the significant differences between the design of the plant at Burgau and that of the Applicant.
  11. Other differences were identified by Mr Bland between the Burgau plant design and that of the Applicant. He observed that the flowsheet for Burgau did not reveal any preheating of the combustion air for the “Burning chamber”. This is different from the process designed by the Applicant which used hot gases extracted from the pyrolysis kiln heating side to pre-heat the combustion air. It was the adaptation, or creation, of a unique form of heat exchanger in that setting. This, the Tribunal finds, was another example of the generation of new knowledge as a result of the core activities undertaken by the Applicant as described in evidence before the Tribunal.
  12. The Applicant’s process incorporated a filter into the vapour outlets of the drying and the pyrolysis kilns. Burgau used a different process by way of a cyclone for cleaning of the pyrolysis vapours leaving the pyrolysis kiln before their introduction to the “Burning chamber”.
  13. The Applicant’s plant capacity of 140,000 t/y introduced via means of two kiln lines,[148] was significantly larger than that of Burgau with 38,000 t/y via one kiln line.
  14. There are no known examples of a plant undertaking the treatment of MSW by pyrolysis on a commercial scale where condensation of the vapours leaving the pyrolysis kiln occurred.
  15. The PLPD design recirculates dirty flue gas, whereas the Burgau plant recirculates clean flue gas.
  16. Burgau uses “gas from landfill” as a supplementary fuel unlike the Applicant’s design. [149]
  17. The Applicant contends, and the Tribunal agrees, that when one considers the language adopted in section 335-25(1) of the ITA Act, the outcome could not be known or determined in advance on the basis of the then current knowledge. The activities did lead to the generation of new knowledge in the design of the proposed treatment plant on a commercial scale. Indeed, Professor Haynes did concede that the facility designed by the Applicant is unlike any existing MSW pyrolysis facility,[150] and, assuming it works, it would represent an improvement to existing or known processes for the treatment of MSW.[151] These concessions by Professor Haynes do largely accord with the opinions expressed by Mr Bland which have been referred to above.
  18. For these reasons, the Tribunal concludes that the Core R & D activities were experimental activity since the outcome could not be made or determined in advance on the basis of current knowledge, information or experience.

COULD THE OUTCOME ONLY HAVE BEEN DETERMINED BY APPLYING A SYSTEMATIC PROGRESSION OF WORK THAT PROCEEDED FROM HYPOTHESIS TO EXPERIMENT, OBSERVATION AND EVALUATION, LEADING TO LOGICAL CONCLUSIONS WITHIN THE MEANING OF SECTION 355-25 OF THE ITA ACT?

  1. The Tribunal considers that at all material times the Applicant had established a hypothesis, which was whether a proposed MSW treatment plant could be designed using pyrolysis to enable the production of electricity, liquid fuel and char.
  2. The Applicant identifies several sources in support of its contention that the hypothesis was applied by way of a systematic progression of work that proceeded to experiment, observation and evaluation, leading to logical conclusions.
  3. Mr Bland, at Part 5 in his first report, identified six steps which characterise the activities undertaken by the Applicant. They need not be repeated for the purposes of these reasons. Helpfully, in his report at Appendix E (referred to earlier in these reasons) he provides a pictorial representation of the systematic progression of, as he put it, working through such six steps.[152] When Appendix E is examined it does support the contention made by Mr Bland that there is really no sensible way to conduct the activities, other than as described therein. He concludes that it constitutes a systematic progression of work based on established science and proceeds from hypothesis to experiment, observation and evaluation leading to logical conclusions. It should be noted that in his analysis of the contents of Appendix E, Mr Bland, with respect to what he describes as “Step 1”, records three matters of significant relevance. Firstly, there are no facilities that he was aware of where the vapours in the gaseous stream are used to produce liquid fuels. Secondly, similar facilities exist, but they differ in important areas including feedstock composition and size. Thirdly, the majority of facilities appear to have been either unrelated to, or unable to achieve, consistent commercial operations. The authenticity of Appendix E was not really challenged by either cross-examination of Mr Bland or in the evidence of Professor Haynes.
  4. Mr Bland, in his report of 3 November 2017, identified three separate instances when in his opinion the Applicant tested the hypothesis. The hypothesis being that the proposed MSW treatment plant would enable the production of both electricity and fuel. The contents of paragraphs 5.2.1, 5.2.2 and 5.2.3 of that report are referred to. Concerning the first test of the hypothesis, Mr Bland notes that the work concerned was described in more detail in the second witness statement of Mr A, which, as noted earlier, together with his viva voce evidence, is accepted by the Tribunal. That witness statement, in his view, detailed a very logical three step sequence of investigations. Additionally, he observed that there was a record of the results of the activities undertaken in the Technical Summary and Final Design Overview Report prepared by PLPD on 11 November 2014, and also the Project Scope Report prepared by Lycopodium, both of which were referred to earlier in these reasons.[153] An examination of those reports certainly, on their face, confirms the opinion expressed by Mr Bland. They are lengthy documents which condescend to considerable detail. This was described by Mr Bland as detailing a very logical sequence of investigations in three steps encompassing a series of plant configurations being tested until a satisfactory one was found. He opines, and the Tribunal accepts, that each new configuration was a logical improvement on, and a response to, the shortcomings of its predecessor.
  5. The second test of the hypothesis by the Applicant identified by Mr Bland in his 3 November 2017 report occurred when it undertook modelling involving process simulation of inputs to develop a detailed process design. This resulted in an improved understanding of reaction pathways, kinetics and parameters for the pyrolysis of MSW. He relied upon the contents of the first witness statements of Mr A and Mr Morey to this effect. Their evidence was that new knowledge concerning those matters was obtained using modelling software. This evidence is accepted by the Tribunal. Mr Bland agreed with the evidence Mr Morey stated that such knowledge generated would have been useful, if not essential, for developing the mass and energy balances used for the plant design, and in selecting and designing equipment for emissions control.
  6. The third test of the hypothesis by the Applicant identified by Mr Bland in his 3 November 2017 report was, he considered, undertaken during the mechanical design phase of the activities. He referred to the description of the mechanical design activities contained on page 9 of the application made to Innovation and Science Australia.[154] He referred to the contents of Exhibit “MA-13” of Mr A’s first witness statement as being the predominant source of the results of these activities which he said constituted a test of the hypothesis.
  7. When one examines these sources of evidence for Mr Bland’s conclusion concerning these three instances of testing of the hypothesis, they do possess the indicia of logical testing. Each of the three activities identified were undertaken for the purpose of testing the hypothesis and thus generating new knowledge, including new knowledge in the form of new or improved processes in the sphere of treatment of MSW to generate electricity, fuel (and also presumably char.) The Tribunal accepts this contention.
  8. Further, with respect to this consideration, Mr Bland, in his report of 1 November 2018, made reference to the Applicant’s concept design work on the dryer.[155] This was touched on earlier in these reasons, but it is useful to reiterate this aspect of them. He noted that the Applicant’s arrangement of tubes in the dryer design was clearly different from traditional designs. He had never seen such an arrangement as that adopted by the Applicant before, nor was he able to find any evidence that such an arrangement had been used elsewhere. The adoption of such an arrangement was primarily to improve the flow of MSW through the dryer. Following these observations, by reason of the differences and that such a design had not been used before, he considered that the Applicant’s concept design with respect to the dryer:

(a) was experimental;

(b) did have an outcome that could not have been determined in advance;

(c) did have an outcome determinable only by application of a systematic progression of work based on the principles of established science, and which proceeded from hypothesis to logical conclusions; and

(d) did generate new knowledge.

  1. These observations by Mr Bland were not completely dispelled by Professor Haynes. Nor was Mr Bland in any way shaken in cross-examination on this topic whilst he was in the witness box. The Tribunal accepts this evidence from him.
  2. The Applicant also contended that the planned progression of work was recorded contemporaneously in the consultancy agreement with PLPD on 15 August 2014 which has been referred to earlier in these reasons. The contents of the document need not be referred to again, however it did identify several processes and phases of work. Several witnesses, as also noted in these reasons, gave evidence which has been accepted by the Tribunal of the services and work that was performed.
  3. The Applicant also justified its contention that there was a systematic progression of work in the relevant sense required by section 355-25 of the ITA Act by reference to the spreadsheets that were in evidence. Similarly, the drawings and similar documentation which included the dual train layout are further evidence of the systematic progression of the work.
  4. The Tribunal accepts this contention on the part of the Applicant. By way of example, if one looks at the spreadsheets themselves, they reveal a systematic progression of work, or, perhaps more accurately, an analysis of the process concerned. They are broken down into several parts, for instance Annexure “MA-15” to Mr A’s second witness statement commences with “Assumptions and Calculations”.[156] Each part of the process is broken down such that the reader (including a non-technical or technically trained reader) can logically follow what has been undertaken. Detailed calculations together with the integers and formula applied by the spreadsheets’ creator are recorded. They have the hallmarks of a document that has been constructed with considerable thought and detailed attention. There may well be errors, as contended by the Respondent by reason of the evidence of Professor Haynes. However, this does not seem to the Tribunal to deprive such documents, or the work that they represent, of their essential characteristics for the purposes of determining whether or not there had been a systematic progression of work that proceeded from hypothesis to experiment, observation and evaluation, leading to logical conclusions. The Applicant submitted that, when one examines the language used in section 355-25(1) of the ITA Act, there is no proviso or requirement concerning the quality of the work carried out at any given step. There is no qualification that the application of a systematic progression of work comprising the experimental activities be error-free. The Tribunal agrees with this contention. Properly construing the words used in section 355-25(1) of the ITA Act does not impose an error-free requirement on the work product concerned.
  5. The Tribunal was also persuaded by the Applicant’s contention that the development of a plant for the treatment of MSW by using the process of pyrolysis comprising the activities concerned would take several years to complete. By reason of this fact it is, as was noted by Lindgren J in Industry Research and Development Board v Coal & Allied Operations Pty Ltd,[157] relevant to the consideration of a claim for the development of particular technology to have regard to the whole project, including the subject activities, before and after the year being considered. Errors in the work and the work product represented by relevant drawings, diagrams and spreadsheets would no doubt be refined and corrected as required in subsequent years. Indeed, this was the case, as verified by the Project Scope Report prepared by Lycopodium in July 2016 which is outside the relevant period. It does show a logical progression of work on the project that continued to occur after the June 2015 financial year.
  6. Further, by way of example, reference should be made to the spreadsheets contained in Annexure “MA-17” to Mr A’s second witness statement.[158] These spreadsheets were referred to by him in his evidence and demonstrate the development of the dual train system with two dryers and two kilns. Once again, this documentation demonstrates the application of a systematic progression of work that proceeded from hypothesis to experiment, observation and evaluation, leading to logical conclusions.
  7. The question of errors in the Applicant’s work product was also pursued by the Respondent during the course of the evidence given by Mr Bland and Professor Haynes concerning the steps identified in item 8 of “Table 1: Responses to Questions” of their joint report.[159] Professor Haynes contended that the existence of the errors that he identified were not consistent with a systematic progression of work, based on established principles of science.
  8. Mr Bland adopted a different point of view. He firstly gave evidence that part of the systematic approach to scientific work was the considerable checking of the work undertaken by Lycopodium. These steps were identified in Lycopodium’s May 2015 report referred to earlier in which Mr Bland stated several errors were corrected. Mr Bland also suggested in his evidence that the production of the documents concerned demonstrated that some work had been done. He quite properly and fairly stated that it was difficult to identify the iterative process adopted by the Applicant because there was no recording of the intermediate steps undertaken.
  9. The Tribunal prefers the approach adopted by Mr Bland. It is consistent with the evidence referred to earlier, particularly that of Mr Morey, that in a commercial environment not every step (presumably what was referred to by Mr Bland as “intermediate steps” or the “iterative process”) will be recorded. Similarly, Mr Morey, in paragraph 10 of his second witness statement at annexure “PM-5”, produced a USB stick said to contain the current 3D model of the plant. He stated that because the development of the 3D model is an ongoing and dynamic process it is not possible to produce a version of the model from an earlier time. This is understandable and an explanation that the Tribunal accepts. It is also consistent with the evidence of the other witnesses called by the Applicant referred to earlier, namely Mr A and Mr G, about the work that was actually undertaken which is recorded earlier and the Tribunal accepts. Mr G also in his witness statement recorded that adjustments were made to the design and were never frozen. It was an ongoing process. Once again, this is understandable, and the Tribunal accepts this evidence.
  10. For these reasons, the Tribunal concludes that the outcome of the activities could only have been determined by applying a systematic progression of work that proceeded from hypothesis to experiment, observation and evaluation, leading to logical conclusions within the meaning of section 355-25 of the ITA Act.

PURPOSE OF GENERATING NEW KNOWLEDGE

  1. It should be observed, as contended by the Applicant, that the words “new knowledge” in section 355-25(1)(b) of the ITA Act are in effect of broad compass; it is specifically expressed to include new knowledge in the form of improved materials products, devices processes or services. The Tribunal considers that this is a comparatively low statutory threshold for an applicant to cross. Also, in this context it is worthwhile repeating the observations made concerning the decision of the Full Court of the Federal Court of Australia in Moreton Resources that it can capture technology that does something that has been done before in a different way.
  2. The Applicant contends that Core Activity 1.1 was carried out for the purpose of generating new knowledge (including new knowledge in the form of new or improved materials, products, devices, processes or services).
  3. There was evidence before the Tribunal which supports this contention.
  4. Mr A, both in his two witness statements, and in his viva voce evidence, asserted that this was the case. His evidence concerning this topic has already been canvassed extensively earlier in these reasons in the section specifically providing details of the same.[160] The thrust of his evidence was that the purpose of such activities that he referred to in the course of his evidence was to test whether a commercial scale waste treatment facility to treat MSW could be designed which would produce fuel, electricity and char.[161] In cross-examination, when pressed by counsel for the Respondent that the purpose of the core activities was not the testing of a hypothesis, or the development of new knowledge, he responded that it certainly was. He also responded that certainly the work was carried out, and so carried out in the initial phase, otherwise there would not have been progress to later phases as described. The Tribunal accepts this evidence.
  5. The terms of the Consultancy Agreement between the Applicant and PLPD entered into on 15 August 2014 were also relied upon by the Applicant in support of this contention. The terms of the Consultancy Agreement have been referred to above. An examination of the terms of that agreement reveal that this very purpose is almost precisely what PLPD was required to develop. It accords with Mr A’s evidence.
  6. The Tribunal also accepts a contention advanced by the Applicant that the purpose can be inferred from what was done, and the circumstances in which it was done. counsel referred the Tribunal to the decision of Raymor Contractors Pty Ltd v FCT.[162] In that case it was held that generally speaking a person will be said to intend the natural and probable consequences of their acts, and likewise their purpose may be inferred from them. If it is necessary to draw the inference contended by the Applicant, which it probably is not given the Tribunal’s acceptance of the evidence of amongst others, Mr A and Mr Morey, together with a combination of the contents of the Consultancy Agreement, the plans, drawings and spreadsheets that are in evidence together with the scoping reports, as well as the viva voce evidence of the several witnesses referred to, that purpose can be inferred from those facts, circumstances and matters. What was sought to be done by the Applicant in this instance and what was in fact done had not successfully been done before. Therefore, one should draw the inference that the purpose was to develop new or improved processes within the meaning of the applicable section of the ITA Act. As previously mentioned, in determining the purpose of the activities concerned when applying section 355-25 of the ITA Act the Tribunal prefers the approach of Deputy President Forgie in Moreton Resources v Innovation and Science Australia[163] and considers that ultimately, whether experimental activities are conducted for “the purpose” of generating new knowledge is a matter to be decided on the evidence in a particular case and not according to a characterisation of the purpose as substantial, or more than insubstantial. This leads the Tribunal to the conclusion that the Core Activities 1.1 and the Supporting Activities 1.1.2 referred to in its application to the Respondent were conducted by the Applicant for the purpose of generating new knowledge, including the developing of new or improved processes, being the designing of a commercial scale waste treatment facility to treat MSW and produce fuel, electricity and char. It should also be observed that the Tribunal finds, on the preponderance of the evidence before it, that such a commercial scale MSW waste treatment facility as proposed by the Applicant had not been developed previously.
  7. It should also be repeated that the purpose of generating new knowledge within the meaning of section 355-25(1)(b) of the ITA Act is capable of coexisting with other purposes. Insofar as the Applicant had a purpose of carrying out the activities to develop and construct a new commercial scale plant processing MSW, it can coexist, and almost certainly will as a matter of course, with the purpose of producing electricity, fuel and char in accordance with the hypothesis developed.
  8. As identified in several earlier parts of these reasons, new knowledge was indeed generated.
  9. There was the modelling undertaken by PLPD which established that the additional energy required to condense the syngas and convert it into a saleable consistent fuel product was unworkable. The Tribunal considers that the definition of “new knowledge” in section 355-25(1)(b) of the ITA Act cast a sufficiently wide dragnet so as to capture new knowledge in the form of something that cannot be achieved as much as what can be. The outcome of the modelling undertaken by PLPD, as described by Mr A in his evidence, must be new knowledge in the relevant sense as it resolves the question of what can and cannot be done when attempts are made to process syngas in this way.

WERE THE ACTIVITIES COMPRISING SUPPORTING ACTIVITIES 1.1.1 AND 1.1.2 “SUPPORTING R & D ACTIVITIES” WITHIN THE MEANING OF SECTION 355-30 OF THE ITA ACT?

  1. The Applicant contends that once it is accepted that activities comprising Activity 1.1 were Core R & D activities, it can be seen that those comprising Activities 1.1.1 and 1.1.2 were directly related to the core activities. In Appendix A to its final submissions, it identifies the relevant components of any supporting activity and the evidence it relies upon in support of the contention that they were directly related to the core activities.
  2. The Respondent, on the other hand, relies upon Professor Haynes’ evidence which in summary concludes that the supporting R & D activities for the most part appear to be either indistinguishable from the core R & D activities, lacking evidence that they were actually undertaken or lacking evidence as to whether they were actually undertaking in the relevant period. Also, as with other considerations concerning the core activities, he opined that he could not confirm whether the supporting activities were carried out solely in the period between 1 July 2014 and 30 June 2015.
  3. The Tribunal accepts the evidence relied upon by the Applicant, particularly that contained in Appendix A to its submissions. Such evidence need not be repeated. Much of it relies upon acceptance of the evidence of the lay witnesses called by the Applicant during this hearing of the application. That evidence as noted earlier has been accepted by the Tribunal.
  4. The other matter that is relevant to the Tribunal accepting the submission of the Applicant concerning supporting activities arises from the joint experts’ report. It will be recalled that earlier in these reasons reference was made to Table 3 of the joint experts’ report prepared on 24 April 2018. Part 4 of that report addressed supporting activities. Table 3 provided an agreed response which overall concluded that in most cases the work was covered by agreed assumptions given to them and that the activities supported all the agreed activities and assumptions. There was not in the course of that report any real disagreement between the experts concerning the connection between the claimed supporting activities and the core activities concerned. Given this, the Tribunal considers it provides a further reason for concluding, as it does, that the Supporting Activities 1.1.2 were directly related to the Core Activities in the relevant sense.

CONCLUSION

  1. The Tribunal sets aside the reviewable decision. In substitution, the Tribunal decides that, pursuant to section 27B of the Industry Research and Development Act 1986, the correct and preferable decision is that:
    1. the activities comprising Core Activity 1.1 were “core R & D activities” within the meaning of section 355-25 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 conducted during the income year ended 30 June 2015; and
    2. the activities comprising Supporting Activities 1.1.1 and 1.1.2 were “supporting

      R & D activities” within the meaning of section 355-30 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 conducted during the income year ended 30 June 2015 in relation to the activities comprising Core Activity 1.1.
