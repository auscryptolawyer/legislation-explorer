Re Terence Michael Dwyer and Department of Finance; Superannuation Fund Investment Trust and Australian Government Senior Executives Association (Victorian Branch) [1985] AATA 312 (11 November 1985)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TERENCE MICHAEL DWYER

And: DEPARTMENT OF FINANCE; SUPERANNUATION FUND INVESTMENT TRUST and

AUSTRALIAN GOVERNMENT SENIOR EXECUTIVES ASSOCIATION (VICTORIAN BRANCH)

No. A.84/163

Freedom of Information

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Justice J.D. Davies (President)

Mrs R.A. Balmford (Senior Member)

D.R.S. Craik (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Freedom of Information - request for access - legal opinions by counsel and government lawyers on status of Superannuation Fund Investment Trust - Trust exempt from provisions of Act - copies held by Department of Finance and others - whether documents protected by legal professional privilege - whether waiver - role of FOI Act in enforcing private obligations

Freedom of Information Act 1982 (Cth) - ss.7, 9, 11, 42

Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) - s.163(2)

Attorney-General for the Northern Territory of Australia v Kearney, unreported (High Court of Australia), delivered 25 September 1985

Baker v Campbell [1983] HCA 39; (1983) 153 CLR 52

Waterford v Department of the Treasury, unreported (Federal Court of Australia), delivered 15 February 1985

Re Witheford and Department of Foreign Affairs (1983) 5 ALD 534

Interfirm Comparison (Australia) Pty Limited v Law

Society of New South Wales (1952) 2 NSWLR 104

Re Londonderry's Settlement (1964) Ch 594

HEARING

CANBERRA

11:11:1985

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

This is an application for review of a decision under the Freedom of Information Act 1982 (Cth) ("the FOI Act") refusing access to documents. The applicant initially directed his request for access to Mr J.V. Monaghan, who is a Commissioner of the Public Service Board and that request was, pursuant to s.16 of the FOI Act, transferred by the Public Service Board to the Department of Finance ("the Department").

2. At the commencement of the hearing, the Superannuation Fund Investment Trust ("the Trust") and the Australian Government Senior Executives Association (Victorian Branch) made application, pursuant to s.30(1A) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth) ("the AAT Act"), to be joined as parties to the proceedings and they were so joined.

3. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged by the respondent, pursuant to s.37 of the AAT Act, and other material produced at the hearing by the respondent, to some of which it will be necessary to refer. Evidence was given at the hearing by Mr C.R. Orr, Assistant Secretary, Commonwealth Superannuation Branch, Department of Finance, and by Mr K.A. Searson, Acting Chief Investment Manager, Office of the Trust.

4. The Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme was established by the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Superannuation Act"). By virtue of s.17 of that Act, the Commissioner for Superannuation has the general administration of the Act, except for Part III. Part III establishes the Trust and the Superannuation Fund ("the Fund"), which is administered by the Trust. The Trust consists of three members. It has no power to appoint its own staff and staff are provided for it by the Department. The costs of the management of the Fund, including the salaries of those staff, are met by the Fund. Ministerial responsibility for the Superannuation Act rests with the Minister for Finance ("the Minister") and the Department is therefore directly concerned with the Scheme because of its role in the giving of policy advice to the Minister.

5. In January 1984, Mr Monaghan was appointed by the Government to undertake an investigation into the management and operations of the Trust. He presented a report to the Minister on 30 March 1984. Paragraph 2.3 of that report ("the Monaghan Report") reads :

"2.3 The Trust has obtained extensive legal advice

as to its legal status and statutory

responsibilities. Advisings by Senior Counsel and

the Attorney-General's Department indicate that

the Trust and its members are not 'trustees' in

terms of the general law, a consequence being that

action for breach of trust could not be taken

against them under the general law by members of

the Scheme. The Department however considers that

the Trust and its members are trustees of a trust

for statutory purposes; the full consequences

flowing from that status - eg as to what would

constitute a breach of trust - are said to be

difficult to state, although there is an

implication that contributors would have

enforceable rights in relation to the Fund. That

advising also proposes that, although moneys in

the Fund are public moneys within the Audit Act

definition, it does not necessarily follow that

the legal ownership of the assets of the Fund is

necessarily vested in the Commonwealth and,

indeed, there is some indication that the Fund is

vested in the Trust."

6. Dr Dwyer's request for access reads as follows :

"Dear Mr. Monaghan,

Superannuation Trust

I have read your report on the above with close

interest. I am particularly concerned, as a

contributor, about my legal rights to trust moneys

ostensibly held on my behalf, especially in the

light of the High Court's decision in Fund's South

Australian stamp duties case.

I would therefore be most grateful if you could

send me copies of the legal advisings referred to

in paragraph 2.3 of your report relating to the

contributors' beneficial interests in the fund and

rights of action against the Trust (if any).

I would expect this information to be given

without reservation of any sort to a contributor

but, if it is necessary to do so, I request you

treat this letter as a request under the Freedom

of Information Act."

7. Mr Orr described five documents as coming within Dr Dwyer's request for access. Mr Monaghan had advised him that, to the best of his recollection, he had regard to all five documents in preparing paragraph 2.3 of his Report. Those documents, numbered for ease of reference, are :

Document 1

Memorandum of advice to the Trust by Mr R. Searby,

QC, dated 17 December 1981;

Document 2

Opinion provided to the Trust by the Attorney-

General's Department, dated 20 December 1978;

Document 3

Opinion provided to the Trust by the Attorney-

General's Department, dated 3 June 1982;

Document 4

Opinion provided to the Trust by the Attorney-

General's Department, dated 6 August 1982;

Document 5

Opinion provided to the Trust by the Attorney-

General's Department, dated 26 November 1982.

Copies of the documents were made available to the Tribunal, subject to an order pursuant to s.35 of the AAT Act that they not be disclosed to any persons other than the members of the Tribunal or the staff of the members of the Tribunal until further order.

8. The relevant history of the several documents, so far as appears from the evidence, is :

(a) Document 1 was prepared by Mr Searby, QC,

who was briefed to advise by the Crown

Solicitor. When his advice was received,

copies were forwarded from the Melbourne

sub-office of the Crown Solicitor to the

Chairman of the Trust and also to the

Department and to two other offices of the

Crown Solicitor.

Copies of Documents 2, 3 and 4 were

forwarded to the Department by senior

officers of the Trust, in each case within a

few months of their receipt by the Trust

from the Attorney-General's Department.

A copy of Document 5 was forwarded directly

to the Department by the Attorney-General's

Department at the time of its preparation

and a further copy supplied to the

Department some months later by the Trust.

(b) All five documents were supplied by the

Trust to Mr Monaghan for the purposes of his

inquiry; Document 2 was also supplied to

him by the Department. After the completion

of his report, Mr Monaghan forwarded the

papers accumulated in the course of his

inquiry, including the five documents, to

the Department for retention.

(c) A Joint Working Party, of which Mr Orr was

chairman, was established by the Minister to

examine and consider certain matters arising

out of the Monaghan Report. The undated

copy of the Report of the Joint Working

Party which was put before the Tribunal

refers to certain of the issues mentioned in

paragraph 2.3 of the Monaghan Report.

However, Mr Orr indicated that so far as he

could recall, the documents were not made

available to that Joint Working Party.

(d) It would appear that copies of document 5,

and probably also of documents 1, 2, 3 and

4, were obtained by the Auditor-General in

the course of his ordinary operations.

(e) Brief references to the content of the

documents were made before the Senate Select

Committee on Finance and Government

Operations on 12 June 1984 and by a former

member of the Trust, Mr J.R. Smith, in a

letter to the Minister on 15 May 1984.

9. Mr Orr's evidence was that documents are supplied by the Trust to the Department because, by virtue of its responsibility to provide policy advice to the Minister, the Department is seen as having "a need to know", as regards material which may require or relate to the giving of such advice. He said in evidence :

"It is difficult to speak authoritatively on how

things have occurred since 1976, particularly as

many of the people involved are no longer with the

branch. There has been a close co-operation

between the Department and the Trust, or with

officers of the Trust. The Department obviously

needs to be aware of emerging problems that may

arise in respect of the Trust's operations. The

Trust was set up in 1976. It has been feeling its

way and I think you can perhaps regard the

Monaghan exercise and the Joint Working Party's

efforts last year as part of the process of

evolving a more appropriate mechanism under which

the Trust can operate. Clearly, the Minister for

Finance, as the person ultimately responsible to

the Parliament for the operation of the Trust and

for the operation of the Commonwealth

Superannuation Scheme as a whole, must be aware of

the problems that the Trust may be experiencing.

.....

I think it would be necessary to make it clear

that the Trust did not ordinarily provide us with

all of the legal opinions that it obtained, but

when it did come up against a particular problem,

it alerted the Department to them and we in due

course alerted the Minister to any particular

problems that may be of significance to him."

There is a general awareness of the existence of s.163(2) of the Superannuation Act which reads :

"163.(2) The Trust shall furnish to the Minister

such information relating to the management of the

Fund by the Trust as the Minister may from time to

time require."

In this context, Mr Orr said :

"... if we are to use this sort of large stick to

obtain information from the Trust, then we have to

have some awareness of particular developments and

the existing (sic) of opinions or other material

before we actually go ahead and demand them. I

think it would be a most unfortunate thing if the

Department had to resort to this type of

approach."

10. Mr Searson said in his affidavit :

"The Trust provides documents to the Department on

the implied, if not expressed, understanding that

they are supplied in confidence ...".

In evidence, he said :

"... it has been a long standing practice, as far

as I am aware, both from my time in the Department

and my time now in the Trust, that these documents

would be provided on a confidential basis, because

it is the nub on which mutual confidence between

the two organisations is based."

Asked by the Tribunal how this differed from the ordinary duty of a public servant as to confidence with respect to what occurs in the Department, he said :

"I suppose documents of this nature would not

normally be divulged, say, by the Trust to other

departments, notwithstanding they contain, they

have public servants working in those departments,

and the Trust would be making documents available

to the department because of the Department of

Finance's particular need to know them; and as I

say, they are nothing more than just the general

understanding that any of these documents that do

exchange between officers where there is that need

to know are done on that commercial ((sic) read

'confidential') basis; but we would not expect

having done that that the Department of Finance

would violate that confidence by giving it to

somebody else who did not have the need to know,

whether he was a public servant or not."

He agreed that it would be a breach of the duty of the officers of the Department to disclose information of this nature other than to people who need to know it.

11. Mr Searson said that his understanding was that, at the time of the inquiry, it was seen as important for the Trust to co-operate with Mr Monaghan and give him every assistance. Mr Searson agreed that the Trust had no control over what use Mr Monaghan made of the documents, over whether he included them or extracts from them in his report, or over whether the report was published, this last being a matter for the Minister. Similarly, the Auditor-General could make such use of these, or any other documents, as he thought fit.

12. Section 7(1) and Part I of Schedule 2 of the FOI Act, when read with the definitions of "agency", "document of an agency" and "prescribed authority" in s.4(1), effectively remove documents in the possession of the Trust from the operation of the Act. However, those copies of the documents which are in the possession of the Department are subject to the Act by virtue of the inclusion of "a copy" in the definition of "document" in s.4(1).

13. Section 11 of the FOI Act reads :

"11. Subject to this Act, every person has a

legally enforceable right to obtain access in

accordance with this Act to -

(a) a document of an agency, other than an

exempt document; or

(b) an official document of a Minister, other

than an exempt document."

The respondent submitted that the five documents were exempt documents by virtue of s.42(1) of the FOI Act, which reads :

"42.(1) A document is an exempt document if it is

of such a nature that it would be privileged from

production in legal proceedings on the ground of

legal professional privilege."

14. Document 1 was prepared by Mr Searby, QC, on the instructions of the Crown Solicitor; the remaining documents were prepared by officers of the Attorney-General's Department. In the context of legal professional privilege, the Attorney-General's Department, vis a vis another Commonwealth Department, is in the same position as a private legal practitioner vis a vis a private client. See the remarks of Gibbs CJ, Wilson and Dawson JJ in Attorney-General for the Northern Territory of Australia v Kearney (unreported, delivered 25 September 1985). As appears from the extract from the Monaghan Report above, the documents contain legal advice as to the legal status of the Trust and its statutory obligations and, thereby, material of a kind to be dealt with on a confidential basis.

15. The rationale behind the doctrine of legal professional privilege was examined by Dawson J in Baker v Campbell [1983] HCA 39; (1983) 153 CLR 52. His Honour said, at p.127 :

"... The modern theory that the doctrine is

necessary to promote freedom of consultation of

legal advisers by clients did not clearly emerge

until the nineteenth century. ... But it became

firmly established in the modern form in which it

is expressed not merely as a rule of evidence, but

as a matter of public policy with a natural

application wherever compulsory disclosure of

evidence is involved, whether in judicial

proceedings or not.

The law came to recognize that for its better

functioning it was necessary that there should be

freedom of communication between a lawyer and his

client for the purpose of giving and receiving

legal advice and for the purpose of litigation and

that this entailed immunity from disclosure of

such communications between them."

And later, at p.128 :

"... The cover of legal professional privilege was

extended from communications relating to actual

litigation to communications in anticipation of

litigation and it is now 'sufficient if they pass

as professional communications in a professional

capacity'. See Lawrence v. Campbell (1859) 4 Drew

485 at p 490 [1859] EngR 385; (62 ER 186 at p 188); Minet v.

Morgan (1873) 8 ChApp, (361) at p 368."

The principle of public policy enunciated by his Honour was recognised by Parliament in the enactment of s.42(1). It has been held that s.42(1) is not to be read so as to import into the provision any additional notion of public interest such as qualifies other exemptions in the FOI Act - see Waterford v Department of the Treasury, unreported, delivered 15 February 1985 in which Fisher, Gallop and Neaves JJ said :

"... Reading the Act as a whole we can discern no

reason for reading down the plain language of

sub-section 42(1). That sub-section recognises

that there will be circumstances in which the law

gives protection from production on grounds of

legal professional privilege and it leaves the

scope of that protection to be determined by the

application of the relevant principles of the

general law. We see no reason derived from a

consideration of the other provisions of the Act

to limit the exemption for which sub-section 42(1)

provides."

16. Each of the documents with which we are concerned is a copy of a professional communication between legal adviser and client and, as such, is prima facie "of such a nature that it would be privileged from production in legal proceedings on the ground of legal professional privilege".

17. In the general law, the privilege, despite the title by which it is known, is the privilege of the client, not of the lawyer. Murphy J in Baker v Campbell, cited above, at pp.84-86, chose for this reason to describe it as "client's legal privilege". Dr Dwyer argued that the client for which the documents were prepared was the Trust and thus only the Trust could claim the privilege. He submitted that the copies of the documents in the possession of the Department, a body other than the Trust, were not privileged and were therefore not exempt under s.42(1). He submitted also that these proceedings under the FOI Act be considered as if they were legal proceedings in which the Trust had claimed the privilege. In our view, whatever the position would be as to production of the documents in legal proceedings, a matter with which we are not here concerned, that argument does not take into account the emphasis in s.42(1) on the nature of the document. A document so defined is exempt, not "if it would be privileged", but "if it is of such a nature that it would be privileged". A document which would, of its nature, be privileged, retains that nature whether proceedings are or are not on foot in which that privilege may be claimed.

18. However, it is necessary to consider whether, by virtue of the disclosure of the documents to Mr Monaghan, to the Department and to the Auditor-General and otherwise in the manner described above, the documents have, for the purposes of the FOI Act, lost their privileged nature.

19. In the context of actual litigation, the question as to whether, and, if so, in what circumstances, documents coming into the hands of a third party lose their privileged status, may not be an easy one. At pp.67-68 of Baker v Campbell, cited above, Gibbs CJ described the position in the following words :

"... One cannot, however, ignore the rather

remarkable rule that if the privileged document,

or a copy of it, has been obtained by the opposing

party, by accident, trickery, or even by theft, it

may be given in evidence although the party

entitled to the privilege or his solicitor could

not have been compelled to produce it : see Lloyd

v. Mostyn [1842] EngR 973; (1842) 10 M & W 478, at p 482 (152

ER 558 at p 560); Calcraft v. Guest (1898) 1

QB 759; Bell v. David Jones Ltd. (1948) 49

SR(NSW) 223, at p 227; Kuruma v. The Queen

(1955) AC 197, at pp 203-4; Butler v. Board of

Trade (1971) Ch. 680 at p 690; Reg. v. Tompkins

(1977) 67 Cr.App.R 181, at p. 184; Waugh v.

British Railways Board (1980) AC, at p 536.

This rule has recently been recognized in Canada

(see Descoteaux v. Mierzwinski (1982) 141 DLR

(3D), AT PP. 601-602) but rejected by the Court of

Appeal in New Zealand. In Rex v. Uljee (1982) 1

NZLR 561 it was held that a constable who

happened to overhear a confidential discussion

between an accused person and his solicitor which

was taking place for the purpose of obtaining

legal advice should not be allowed to give

evidence of the conversation unless the accused

waived the privilege. The court declined to

follow the English authorities, which it regarded

as 'of limited cogency in New Zealand in 1982'

(see per Cooke J. at pp.566-567), and derived some

support for its conclusion from the provisions of

a New Zealand statute which revealed a legislative

policy that evidence of communications which would

otherwise have been privileged should not be given

by a person who had lawfully intercepted them (see

at pp.569, 571). There are some qualifications to

the rule stated in the English cases and

recognized in Australia in Bell v. David Jones

Ltd., (supra) : the owner of the document which

has been improperly obtained may secure an

injunction prohibiting the use of the document,

including its use in evidence, provided that he

does so in separate proceedings and before the

document has been put in evidence : Ashburton v.

Pape (1913) 2 Ch. 469; and in some circumstances

the document may not be admitted because it was

obtained by means which amount to a contempt of

court (e.g. by stealth or trickery within the

precincts of the court) : I.TC Ltd. v. Video

Exchange Ltd. (1982) Ch. 431."

Mason J at page 80 referred to certain of the specific difficulties posed by the authorities, but expressly leaves their resolution on one side as not being necessary in the case before him. Deane and Dawson JJ each (at pages 112 and 129 respectively) referred to the difficulties without finding it necessary to reach a firm conclusion. Dawson J said, by way of enunciation of the question (at p.129) :

"... there is authority for the proposition that

the privilege may be lost if a document to which

it attaches comes into the hands of someone other

than the legal adviser or his client, even

dishonestly, so that secondary evidence of it may

be given."

Brennan J, at page 110, after considering the authorities, concluded :

"In my opinion, Calcraft v. Guest should not be

followed in this country so far as it requires the

admission of a document or of a copy of a document

which is privileged because it has come into

existence 'merely as the materials for the brief'.

It is unnecessary to consider, in the context of a

valid s 10(b) warrant, whether Calcraft v. Guest

would require a court to ignore any illegality in

the means by which a document tendered in evidence

has been obtained. If that question were to

arise, it would be necessary to consider the

judgments of this Court in Cleland v.The Queen

[1982] HCA 67; (1982) 151 CLR 1; and Bunning v. Cross (1978)

[1978] HCA 22; 141 CLR 54."

Wilson and Murphy JJ made no reference to the question.

20. There is no suggestion in the matter before us that the documents were obtained improperly by any person : although it will be noted that the proposition as formulated by Dawson J extends its application beyond circumstances of that kind. It appears to us, from the evidence, that the documents moved between the legal advisers, the Trust and the Department on the basis described in Re Witheford and Department of Foreign Affairs (1983) 5 ALD 534 at p 542, where the Tribunal said, in the context of whether the disclosure of certain documents would constitute a breach of confidence in terms of s.45 of the FOI Act :

"... we consider that there is a category of

confidence that ought to be respected but which,

without seeking to rule upon the point, may not

exist in respect of documents coming into

existence after the commencement of the Act. This

category covers documents disclosure of which

would constitute a breach of confidence where that

confidence was reposed and received not on the

faith of a legal duty to respect that confidence

but on the faith of a voluntary undertaking or

understanding to respect it either in a specific

case or as part of a tradition of dealing, and

where it was so reposed and received at a time

when there was an expectation that having once

been committed to writing it would not be

subjected to disclosure to the public or to a

specific person." (emphasis added).

21. We have no direct evidence as to Mr Monaghan's understanding of the terms on which he received the documents. However, we are satisfied that he would have been aware of their privileged nature, and would, in his dealings with them, have respected their confidence. The inquiry was not a public inquiry. The disclosure of the documents to Mr Monaghan arose in a very specific and limited situation and, in our view, did not detract from the confidential nature of the documents. Similarly, the disclosure to the Auditor-General or the Department could not seriously be regarded as having had that effect. There is nothing in the substance of the documents, or in the history of their movements, to suggest any change in the initial understanding that they would be dealt with in confidence. We note the decision of Bowen CJ in Eq (as he then was) in Interfirm Comparison (Australia) Pty Limited v Law Society of New South Wales (1975) 2 NSWLR 104, in which his Honour held that the distribution, in certain specific circumstances, to twenty-seven firms of solicitors, of the material in respect of which confidentiality was claimed, did not destroy that confidentiality.

22. The nature of the documents, the status of their authors, the information that they contain, the relationship between the Trust and the Department and the circumstances in which disclosure of the documents to the Department took place in our view show that it was intended that their confidence be maintained. It is true that none of the documents was marked "Confidential" but that factor cannot be decisive of itself. A relationship of confidence need not be expressed. Indeed, in the most confidential of circumstances, an unspoken assumption may be expected.

23. In our opinion, the evidence establishes that there has not been a general waiver of privilege by the Trust. The limited waivers in favour of the Department, Mr Monaghan and the Auditor-General did not destroy the legal professional privilege attaching to the original documents and to the copies thereof.

24. Nor did the brief reference to the documents made to the Senate Select Committee on Finance and Government Operations on 12 June 1984 and by Mr J.R. Smith in his letter of 15 May 1984 destroy the confidentiality attaching to the documents. The substantial contents of the documents are not public knowledge but remain confidential to the Trust and to those other persons who properly have had access to them.

25. Accordingly, we do not consider that the subject documents have lost their privileged nature.

26. Dr Dwyer based further submissions on principles of equity. He submitted that the Trust was a trust within the generally accepted meaning of that term and that the applicant as a beneficiary of that trust could not be withheld documents on the basis of legal professional privilege, or in the alternative, he had a right to inspect the documents in dispute. Dr Dwyer submitted that the Trust had a fiduciary duty to grant him access to the documents. See Re Londonderry's Settlement (1964) Ch 594. These submissions were predicated on the assumption that disclosure under the FOI Act is personal disclosure. But the FOI Act evinces an intention to the contrary. The object of the FOI Act as set out in s.3(1)(a) is to create a "general right of access to information in documentary form". A document released under the FOI Act is made available to the public at large. The only exception is in relation to personal records. See Re Witheford and Department of Foreign Affairs (1983) 5 ALD 534. Thus, the Tribunal, if it finds a document to be exempt under s.42, cannot order its disclosure to particular persons or a particular class of persons.

27. If Dr Dwyer is correct in his submission that he has an equitable right of access to the documents in dispute, that right may be enforced in the general courts of the land. The FOI Act is not the appropriate vehicle by which to enforce private rights or compel the performance of private obligations. The statutory nature of the Trust provides no basis for distinction from this principle.

28. Dr Dwyer submitted further that the respondent was required to publish the documents in dispute pursuant to s.9 of the FOI Act and, therefore, that the documents were excluded from the operation of s.42(1) by the provisions of s.42(2) which read:

"42.(2) A document of the kind referred to in

sub-section 9(1) is not an exempt document by

virtue of sub-section (1) of this section by

reason only of the inclusion in the document of

matter that is used or to be used for the purpose

of the making of decisions or recommendations

referred to in sub-section 9(1)."

However, the subject documents do not meet the description in s.9(1) of documents "used by, the agency or its officers in making decisions or recommendations, under or for the purposes of an enactment or scheme administered by the agency ...". The administration of any enactment or scheme relevant to the present proceedings is undertaken by the Trust, not by the Department, and the Trust is not an agency. It is unnecessary to consider whether, if the Trust were an agency, the documents would otherwise meet the criteria of s.9(1).

29. Mr L.W. Maher, who appeared for the Australian Government Senior Executives Association (Victorian Branch), put submissions to the same general effect as those put by Dr Dwyer. We need not deal separately with them.

30. For the above reasons, therefore, all five documents are, in our opinion, exempt documents by virtue of the operation of s.42(1) of the FOI Act. It is not necessary for us to discuss the other grounds of exemption relied upon by the respondent, though we should state that the evidence given in relation to those grounds and the submissions put in relation to them have assisted the Tribunal to be satisfied that legal professional privilege and confidence still attaches to the subject documents. The decision under review should be affirmed.
