Last Updated: 4 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION - ASSESSABLE INCOME - company's profits not distributed within prescribed period under Division 7 - whether undistributed amount assessable income of company - whether shares or dividends were held by company on trust - whether express, implied, constructive or secret trust existed.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss. 103, 104(1), 105A, 105B

Allen v Snyder (1977) 2 NSW LR 685

McCormick v Grogan [1869] UKHL 1; (1869) LR 4 HL 82

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

REASONS FOR DECISION

7 February 1989 Deputy President R.A. Layton

Dr D.B. Williams (Member)

Mr D.J. Trowse (Member)

  1. This reference, which relates to the 1983 year of income, concerns an assessment issued pursuant to Division 7 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Assessment Act"). At that time, private companies not distributing a required percentage of their profits within a prescribed period were liable to pay "undistributed profits tax". That liability was calculated at the rate of 50 cents in the dollar. Section 105B of the Assessment Act, as it then applied, provided that companies answering the description of private were, for the purpose of calculating that impost, permitted certain retentions based on the various categories of assessable income and yet, in the circumstances of this matter, it is notable that dividends received from other private companies, carried no such entitlement.
  2. The issue which this reference raises is whether or not a sum of $54,646 was assessable income in the hands of the applicant company, it being submitted by the applicant company that the said amount was received by it in the capacity of a trustee. If the applicant company is correct, then there would be no additional tax payable under Division 7, as such a receipt would play no part in the determination of its after-tax profit.
  3. The respondent on the other hand contends that the said money was not received by the applicant company in any capacity as trustee, rather it was received in its own right, and therefore must be brought to account in calculating "the undistributed amount" (see s.103 of the Assessment Act).
  4. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by an agent and the respondent by senior counsel. Supplementary documents were received as part of the information before the Tribunal, and further documentary evidence was tendered in the course of the hearing. Additional to that documentary evidence we heard evidence from Mr and Mrs G who were directors and shareholders in the applicant company.
  5. We make the following findings of fact.
  6. In the late 1920's Mrs G's father set up an engineering company called 'P. Pty Ltd'. By the late 1970's, the shareholders of the company were Mrs G's father and mother and her two brothers. Mrs G did not have any shares in that company. Subsequently, 'P. Investments Pty Ltd', a holding company, took up all the shares in P. Pty Ltd. Later, a further company was formed, namely 'L. Pty Ltd', which in turn took up shares in P. Investments Pty Ltd. The shareholders of that company included Mrs G, her sister and two brothers.

7. It was decided in about June 1977 that as a result of the gradual increase in the families and members with children and grandchildren, various trusts should be formed in order to provide for the families of Mrs G, her sister and two brothers, and that these trusts would acquire various investments from and in the P. group of companies. Four nominee companies were incorporated to act as trustees of these trusts and in respect of Mrs G, ABC Nominees Pty Ltd ("the Trust Company") was the company formed for the purpose of acting as trustee of the trust settled for the benefit of her and her immediate family. The choice of the name was unfortunate in that it was very similar to the applicant company, namely ABC Investments Pty Ltd, ("the Investment Company") which had been incorporated in 1956 as an investment company, primarily for Mr G who controlled the company in his capacity as governing director. ABC Investments Pty Ltd, certainly as at July 19B1, had as its shareholders, Mr and Mrs G and their three daughters, J.T., P.H. and D.A. There were also two further shareholders, A.E. and B.M. whose specific relationship was not identified, but were, by name at least, related to the other shareholders.

8. The evidence of both Mr and Mrs G was that the Trust was primarily (as with the other family trusts) set up to receive the benefits from shares taken up with L. Pty Ltd and to distribute those benefits to members of their family. Their evidence also was that the Trust was perceived by them as being the receptacle for Mrs G in that the benefits would be flowing from the group of companies set up by her father.

9. Under the trust deed, the beneficiaries are as follows:

"(i) Mr G.

(ii) The spouse of the said Mr G.

(iii) Any child or children of the said Mr G.

(iv) The spouse of any child or children of the said Mr G.

(v) Any grandchild or grandchildren of the said Mr G.

(vi) The spouse of any grandchild or grandchildren of the said Mr G.

(vii) Any charitable institution person or persons body corporate or incorporate or howsoever constituted whom the Trustee in the Trustee's absolute discretion considers worthy of receipt of funds either for charitable or educational purposes or for the relief of poverty or for religious scientific or public educational purposes in Australia including a public hospital or any hospital which is carried on by a society or association otherwise than for the purposes of profit or gain to the individual members of that society or association."

(Exhibit Al, p.196)

  1. The listing priority afforded Mr G appears at odds with the overall stated desire to benefit Mrs G, and yet perhaps that position is explained by the fact that Mr G's brother was the settlor of the Trust.
  2. Following the incorporation of the Trust (apart from the initial settlement figure of $100), no further funds were advanced to the Trust until the latter part of the 1983 financial year. As a consequence, Mr G, through the Investment Company, paid various accounting fees for the preparation of income tax returns for the Trust Company and the Trust. He gave evidence, which we accept, that the Investment Company would not have paid those fees if those other entities had had sufficient funds to do so. It seems that the purpose of that evidence was to establish that it was not uncommon for the applicant company to pay outgoings on behalf of the Trust.
  3. On 20 July 1981, it appears from documentation that a meeting of directors of the Trust Company was held at which Mr and Mrs G are recorded as being present, and at which the following resolution was passed:

"Mr G advised the meeting that he had received an offer for the company to take up 100 "U" Class Shares of $1.00 each in the capital of L. Pty. Ltd. It was resolved that an application for allotment of shares be completed by the company as trustee of the Family Trust and forwarded to the accountants for L. Pty. Ltd. [name of accounting firm]"

(Exhibit A2, p.64)

There was also a resolution passed as to the use of the common seal and it was recorded in the minutes of the meeting that:

"Authority was given for affixing the common seal of the company to the application for shares."

That minute was purported to be signed by Mr G on the same day, namely 20 July 19B1.

  1. Also on that day, Mr G signed an application for allotment of shares in L. Pty Ltd (Exhibit A2, p.4). There was considerable evidence given by him as to the circumstances of the signing of that application. Our findings on the evidence are that the form for the application for allotment of shares was typed in the offices of the accountant for L. Pty Ltd but the handwriting and the stamped endorsements were performed by Mr G. Furthermore, the deletion of the name of Mr G appearing on that document and the substitution of the applicant company as the prospective applicant was also performed by Mr G. Therefore the document, on the face of it, records that the Investment Company was applying for an allotment of 100 Class "U" shares in L. Pty Ltd. Mr G gave conflicting evidence as to how it was that the application was made in the name of the Investment Company and not in the name of the Trust Company when a decision had been purportedly made on the same day that such shares be taken up by the Trust Company on behalf of the Family Trust.
  2. In any event, the form, having been executed under the common seal of the Investment Company, together with a cheque for $100 drawn on the bank account of that same company, were forwarded to the accountants acting for L. Pty Ltd. The cheque butt for this payment stated that it represented "Application 100 Class "U" shares at $1". It made no reference to any trust arrangement, nor did it indicate that it was a payment on behalf of some other person or entity. The Investment Company was entered on the share register of L. Pty Ltd as the holder of the 100 shares and presumably the share scrip to evidence that entry was issued.
  3. In one portion of his evidence, Mr G said that he knew that it was the Investment Company which was taking up those shares in its own right, although he believed any benefits or dividends to come from those shares would be payable to his wife and three children (transcript pp.46, 47, 52).
  4. In other portions of his evidence Mr G suggested that there was "confusion" between the name of the Investment Company and the name of the Trust Company and that the wrong company had applied for the allotment of shares - it should have been the Trust Company and not the Investment Company (transcript p.47).
  5. There is still further confusion with Mr G's evidence when we look at the objection lodged in relation to this reference which includes the following statement (supplementary documents p.17):

"(4) At a subsequent meeting (which the G. family-adviser did not attend) it was decided that an allotment of shares by L. Pty. Ltd. should be made to the various trustee companies, including the trustee company for the G. family.

At this point is (sic) is contended that confusion arose and shares were wrongly issued to the Investment Company rather than the Trust Company. Shares were correctly issued to each of the other trustee companies."

This objection suggests that in some way L. Pty Ltd was responsible for the wrong allotment of shares to the Investment Company.

  1. A further variation occurs in the statutory declaration attached to the objection, recorded at p.20 of the supplementary documents, in which Mr G declares:

"The application for shares in L. Pty. Ltd. was made on behalf of the Trustee for the Family Trust however, due to confusion regarding which company held this position shares were incorrectly issued to the Investment Company."

This statutory declaration conflicted with Mr G's evidence in that the statutory declaration specifically refers to confusion regarding which company held the position as trustee for the Family Trust, namely whether it was the Investment Company or the Trust Company. However, according to the evidence given by Mr G at the hearing, he was not confused as to which company was trustee for the Family Trust, that it could only be the Trust Company.

  1. We will deal with this conflicting evidence later in these Reasons for Decision, but for the moment will continue with the chronological history.
  2. Following the allotment of shares, there was no income derived from the L. Pty Ltd shares until 30 July 1982 when a cheque drawn on a joint account in the names of P. Investments Pty Ltd and L. Pty Ltd for the sum of $54,646.25 and made payable to the Investment Company was received by Mr and Mrs G.
  3. Mrs G gave evidence, which we accept, that upon seeing that cheque, she was amazed that the cheque was made payable to the Investment Company and not the Trust Company. She expected that any moneys coming from L. Pty Ltd would be made payable to the Trust Company which was essentially her family trust company. We infer from Mrs G's evidence that she was not aware that the application for shares in L. Pty Ltd was made in the name of the Investment Company. Mrs G then rang her accountant to find out what should be done in relation to what she saw as a mistake in the name of the payee. Mrs G. stated that her accountant advised her that the matter could be resolved by the cheque being paid into the account of the Investment Company and then immediately drawn upon by issuing cheques made payable to selected beneficiaries of the Family Trust. Acting on his advice the cheque was deposited in the account of the Investment Company and the same amount immediately withdrawn in separate cheques and distributed as follows - $18,646.25 to Mrs G, $12,000 each to J.T., P.H. and D.A., her three daughters.
  4. On 12 August 1982 it was recorded that a meeting of directors of the Investment Company was held, at which Mr and Mrs G were present, and the following resolution passed:

"IT WAS RESOLVED to pay a dividend out of the profits for the year ended 30th June, 1983 amounting to $54646.25.

The amount to be allocated as follows:

Mrs G $18646.25

J.T. $12000.00

P.H. $12000.00

D.A. $12000.00

Payment to be satisfied by crediting the relevant amount to the shareholders current accounts. This amount to be a debt payable on demand to the shareholder unless otherwise agreed."

It is noted that the resolution makes reference to a "dividend" and "profits" with the payment to be satisfied by "crediting the relevant amount to the shareholder's current accounts". Yet in fact there was no crediting of the relevant amount to the shareholders; the individuals were specifically paid by cheque.

  1. Mr G stated in his evidence that his usual method of dealing with dividend declarations ex profits resulting from dividends received on shares of the Investment Company was to make entries in loan accounts of the shareholders and then to reinvest the profits. This procedure was not adopted in relation to the $54,646.25. Mr G highlighted this difference in support of his contention that the Investment Company, in dealing with this money in a different way from its usual proceeds, indicated that the dividend proceeds were held in trust and paid out to the beneficiaries for whom it was always intended.
  2. We will return to discuss these matters and their implications later in our Reasons for Decision. We note that the applicant Investment Company in its income tax return for the year ending 30 June 1982 declared as its assets 100 shares in L. Pty Ltd at a cost of $100. This same information is repeated in the income tax year in question (1983), as well as in the year ending 30 June 1984. The tax return of L. Pty Ltd for the year ending 30 June 1982 recorded the Investment Company as the owner of 100 "U" Class shares and the same information is repeated in the return for the year 1983, but in the year 1984 it is recorded that 5,000 "U" Class shares are held by the Trust Company and none are recorded for the Investment Company.
  3. As to these 5,000 shares, there is a directors' report which indicates that a further 4,900 "U" Class shares should be issued which would certainly account for the Trust Company becoming holders of 4,900 shares but there is no record nor is there any evidence as to the source of the other 100 shares. There is no record of any transfer of shares from the Investment Company to the Trust Company although the probable inference is that the 100 shares needed to make up the 5,000 were the same shares that were previously held by the Investment Company. It may well be that there was an alteration made to the share register but there is no share transfer document to support that possibility.
  4. The tax return for the Investment Company in the 1983 year, as previously mentioned, records 100 shares in L. Pty Ltd and also records a dividend of $54,646 (supplementary documents p.8) and the dividends are further accounted for in the profit and loss statement (supplementary documents p.10). There is no indication on the face of that income tax return that the said amount of $54,646 was received by the Investment Company in a capacity of trustee for any other person or body and there is nothing on the face of any other documentation which would indicate that any such dividend from L. Pty Ltd should be payable to any person or body other than the Investment Company.
  5. Following receipt of the applicant Investment Company's tax return for the 1983 year, the Australian Taxation Office issued a statement recording the prescribed period and the amount of sufficient distribution based on the return as lodged (T12). The prescribed period for the distribution of those dividends was between 1 May 1983 and 30 April 1984. However the declaration of dividend and distribution of the sum of $54,646 to Mrs G and her three children occurred in or about August 1982 which was outside the prescribed period. The applicant was therefore prima facie caught by the provisions of sub-ss.104 (1) and 105A of the Assessment Act.
  6. Having completed the chronology of events, we will now turn to consider the further matters upon which we must make findings. The first relates to the actions of Mr G on 21 July 1981 in putting the seal and stamp of ABC Investments Pty Ltd on the application for allotment of shares. We are not satisfied that there was confusion in the mind of Mr G as to which company and which seal should be applied in relation to that application for shares. We do not consider that Mr G mistakenly put the name of the Investment Company and the seal of that company on the document instead of the Trust Company. He well knew the difference between those two companies notwithstanding their similar names, and we think it more likely that, as Mr G stated in his evidence at p.50 of the transcript, he did not think the net result would be any different because he would still be able to distribute dividends to the persons who would otherwise be the recipients under the Family Trust by using his Investment Company. Such a position would have been achieved had the dividend declaration in favour of Mrs G and her daughters occurred within the prescribed period.
  7. There is no evidence of any discussion which occurred between Mr G and Mrs G which would suggest that she was even consulted or made aware of, let alone authorised, the application for allotment of shares to be made in the name of the Investment Company, and her amazed reaction to the cheque being made out to the Investment Company supports this conclusion. Therefore on the evidence before us, whatever may have been in the mind of Mr G at the time of the application for the share allotment, his intention was not conveyed to either Mrs G or any of the other beneficiaries of the Family Trust.
  8. The other matter we address is the effect of the meeting held on 12 August 1982 (Exhibit A2, p.37). This documentation does not rest easily with the premise that the Investment Company was holding the proceeds of $54,646 in trust for any person or body. The directors of the Investment Company purport to deal with those proceeds as though they were income of the company and as though they were payable as dividends to the individuals named - although, there is an inaccuracy in the documentation insofar as no current accounts were credited but instead the persons named were paid by cheque. We also note that the persons to whom the distribution was made were both shareholders of the Investment Company as well as beneficiaries under the Family Trust.
  9. Before proceeding further, it is appropriate that we offer a comment on the evidence that the function of the Family Trust was to hold shares in companies under the control of Mrs G's family and to distribute dividends from these holdings to her and her children. The Family Trust balance sheet, prepared as at 30 June 1983 and which formed part of the supplementary documents before the Tribunal, discloses that the Family Trust held fixed interest deposits with various lending institutions to the extent of $532,500 and that those investments had been acquired either from P. Pty Ltd and P. Investments Pty Ltd or from funds made available by them. Recognising the effect of the law as it then existed and more specifically the minimal rate of retention allowance applicable to income from property, the tax advantage of having interest received at the bottom rather than the top of the chain soon becomes apparent. In our view, it seems highly probable that the tax benefit resulting from the shifting of funds down the ladder of entities became obvious and that this desirable rearrangement of investments played more than a casual role in the formation of the Family Trust.
  10. We now turn to the legal aspects. In setting out the law we have had regard generally to Jacobs Law of Trusts in Australia, 5th edition, Principles of the Law of Trusts, Ford and Lee and Halsbury's Laws of England, 4th edition Volume 48.
  11. There are several forms of trust which must be considered to ascertain whether the above findings would lead to our being satisfied that the dividend of $54,646.25 was either received or held in trust by the applicant company for another person or body. The forms of the trust are: an express trust, an implied trust, a constructive trust and a secret trust. We eliminate from the beginning a resultant trust as there were no facts which suggested that such a trust may be applicable.
  12. An express trust by its very description means a trust which is created expressly either in writing or orally and there is certainty as to the essential elements of the trust, namely the trustees, the trust property and the beneficiaries. Whilst it is not necessary for the establishment of an express trust that the word "trust" be used either in a document or orally, there must, however, be sufficient evidence that such a trust (where the beneficial interest in property is held for another) was intended. There is no evidence which satisfies us that Mr G, acting on behalf of the Investment Company, applied for those shares with the intention of that Company holding them as trustee for any other body as beneficiary and certainly no such intention was conveyed to any other person or body. In the alternative, we are not satisfied that Mr G, acting on behalf of the Investment Company, intended that any dividend derived from those shares should be held by that Company on trust for any other person or body. Insofar as he may have applied his mind at all to how any dividends would be dealt with, his view was that he could achieve the same effect through the Investment Company as would be achieved through the Trust Company and the Family Trust, the major relevant shareholders and beneficiaries being the same. We also think there is insufficient certainty as to who the beneficiaries would have been even if we had been satisfied that the requisite intention to create a trust had been satisfied. Whilst Mr G mentioned the beneficiaries as being "his wife and three children", we note that there is a much broader range of beneficiaries nominated in the Trust Deed and which includes other members of the family as well as charitable organisations. For these reasons we are not satisfied that an express trust was created or existed.
  13. As to whether an implied trust exists, such a trust arises where it is intended that a trust be created but there is a lack of certainty in the expression on the part of the person to create such a trust or, alternatively, where the parties have so conducted themselves that, notwithstanding the lack of express intention, a trust can be inferred. An implied trust therefore arises by operation of law. On the facts in this case, however, we cannot be satisfied that it was intended by Mr G on behalf of the Investment Company that a trust be created. The problem with an express trust is the need to be satisfied that there was an intention to form a trust which is also true of an implied trust. For that reason alone we cannot be satisfied that an implied trust exists.
  14. A constructive trust also arises by operation of law and without regard to, and frequently regardless of, the intention and desires of the parties. The question is whether the circumstances under which the property has been acquired are such that it would be unconscionable for the holder of the legal interest to retain the beneficial interest or that it would be a fraud for a party to deny the trust (see generally Jacobs Law of Trust in Australia (supra) p.284 and following). The circumstances in which courts have imposed a constructive trust are limited and confusing, particularly in relation to what is often called the "new model" constructive trust. Justice Samuels in the NSW Appellate Court in the decision of Allen v Snyder (1977) 2 NSW LR 685 at 701 observed that at the best the "new model" constructive trust was "a mutant from which further breeding should be discouraged".
  15. The question of any unconscionable holding by the Investment Company is difficult to maintain when in fact the money was distributed to persons who could claim to have an interest in those moneys both as shareholders and also as the beneficiaries of the discretionary Family Trust. There is no basis on which a court of equity could intervene in such a circumstance. The situation may well have been different if Mr G as governing director, instead of distributing the moneys in the manner in which it was done, chose to distribute the whole of the dividend to himself with no moneys being given to either Mrs G or the children. That hypothetical situation has not arisen and therefore it is not a matter which we have to consider. There is therefore nothing unconscionable about the Investment Company dealing with the dividend in the manner which it did and according to the documents distributing by way of dividends to the shareholders. Those circumstances do not provide the basis upon which a court of equity would impose a constructive trust in the circumstances.
  16. Finally, as to a secret trust. The principles of secret trusts remain a subject of considerable legal uncertainty (see Jacobs Law of Trusts in Australia (supra) para.735). A secret trust exists where property is given to a person or body either absolutely or upon an indefinite trust, but where there has been an undertaking or understanding between the trustee and the donor that the property is to be applied for the benefit of another person or object (see McCormick v Grogan [1869] UKHL 1; (1869) LR 4 HL 82 at pp.88 and 89, Jacobs (supra) paras. 711 ff, Halsbury's Laws of England (supra) paras 570 ff). There is also a distinction to be drawn between a fully secret trust and a half secret trust. In this case the facts could potentially support only a fully secret trust as there is no evidence suggestive of a semi-secret trust. There is still some confusion as to whether a fully secret trust is a form of express trust or a constructive trust, however whichever it be, in view of our findings as above, even though Mr G may have been silent as to the acquisition of the shares and what he may have intended as to their dividend proceeds, Mr G purported to be acting on behalf of the Investment Company and not in his own right as an individual. The other director of the company, Mrs G, was not consulted. Therefore his own personal intentions could not be implied as the Investment Company's intention. That problem aside, the secret trust like the express trust, must fail because of our lack of satisfaction as to intention to create a trust. Alternatively if a secret trust be a trust which is something treated as a constructive trust, it must also fail for the same reasons as a constructive trust.
  17. For the above reasons, therefore, we consider that the applicant company must fail on the review sought. We are of the view that at the time of the acquisition of shares there was no trust formed whereby the Investment Company held those shares on trust for any other body or person, nor that any dividends received in relation to those shares would be held for or on behalf of any person. Therefore, the amount of $54,646 received by the Investment Company represented assessable income in its hands, and as such, must be included in the Division 7 calculations. The dividend declared on 12 August 1982, falls outside the prescribed period and is therefore excluded from those same calculations. For these reasons the Commissioner's decision is upheld.
