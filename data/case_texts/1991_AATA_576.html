TT90/11 and Commissioner of Taxation [1991] AATA 576; (1991) 22 ATR 3257; 91 ATC 322 (19 June 1991)

Last Updated: 27 May 2009



INCOME TAX - Partnership - Interest on bank loan used to reduce capital - Whether deductible under positive limbs of section 51(1) - Whether private, domestic or capital exclusions in Section 51(1) applied - Whether characterisation of outgoing altered by changes in the partnership's membership since the loan was obtained.



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1988, secs 51(1), 90, 92(1)



FC of T v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153

Tikva Investments Pty Ltd v FC of T [1972] HCA 68; (1972) 128 CLR 158

Ure v FC of T 81 ATC 4100

REASONS FOR DECISION



Before: Dr P Gerber (Deputy President)

Date: 19 June 1991



In brief outline, a notice of amended assessment was issued on 14 November 1989 in respect of the applicant's assessment for the 1988 year of income. That amended assessment increased the applicant's taxable income by $3,089, being her proportionate share of the deduction claimed by the partnership of Jarndyce & Jarndyce, solicitors of Burnie, Tasmania, on a loan of $125,000 obtained by the partnership from the National Australia Bank Limited ("NAB"). The applicant objected to the amended assessment, the respondent disallowed the objection, hence this application.



THE FACTS



2. At all material times the legal business conducted by the partnership required substantial capital by way of equity contributed by the partners and funds borrowed from outside sources.



3. In June 1984, the partnership agreed to admit Sandy Bay as a new partner. In the past, the practice of the partnership had been to enable in-coming partners to buy into the partnership on a "drip-feed" basis whereby the existing partners lent the incoming partner the amount necessary to buy into the partnership and that amount was repaid over a period of time. However, the then existing five partners decided that, commencing with Mr Bay, all future partners would have to provide their own funds in order to buy into the partnership.



4. Mr Bay, however, was not in a position to provide from his own funds the $54,000.00 demanded by the existing five partners as the price for his share. His evidence was, having moved his family to Tasmania in approximately September 1983 to take up his position as an employee of the firm, at the end of that year he had purchased a new home described as a "renovator's delight" for approximately $100,000.00. He had brought with him approximately $80,000.00, the net proceeds of the sale of his house in Perth, and borrowed approximately $65,000.00 from a bank (secured by mortgage) to cover the balance of the purchase price and the cost of the renovations. Having committed all the capital he had towards the purchase of his new home, he stated in evidence that he would have had to borrow the entire $54,000 required to buy into the partnership.



5. Given Mr Bay's financial plight, the then existing partners decided to reduce their capital input by $25,000 each, thereby allowing the new partner (and future partners) to buy into the partnership at a reduced cost. To give effect to that intention, the partnership borrowed the aforementioned $125,000 from NAB, drew five cheques each for $25,000 and handed one to each of the five partners. By this device it was sought to reduce the cost price payable by solicitors who were to be introduced into the partnership after June 1984 by an amount which bore to the principal of the NAB loan the same proportion as the proportion of the equity of the partnership purchased by the new partner.



6. Each of the five partners duly banked their cheque for $25,000 in the last week of the 1984 financial year. The current senior partner, Mr Devonport, who was also one of the then existing five partners, readily conceded in cross-examination that he and his other partners regarded the receipt of the $25,000 as purely their own private business. It was not suggested that any of the money found its way back into the business.



7. Beginning with Mr Bay, it was and has remained a condition and consequence of entry into membership of the partnership that any partner, upon admission as such becomes and remains personally liable to an unlimited extent for the debts, liabilities and obligations of the partnership, including repayment of the principal and payment of the interest upon the NAB loan, subject to the right of such partner to contribution from each other partner to the extent that the debts, liabilities and obligations are paid or satisfied equally between the partners inter se.



8. Mr Bay became a partner on 1 August 1984. Due to the existence of the NAB loan, the amount he was required to pay (and therefore borrow) in order to buy into the partnership was $34,434 rather than $54,000.



9. On 30 April 1985, one of the "original" partners retired from the partnership and on 1 November 1985, a new (sixth) partner was introduced.



10. On 30 April 1987, another one of the "original" partners retired from the partnership.



11. On 1 May 1987, the applicant, Miss Collinsvale, became the seventh partner. Her evidence was to the effect that, like Mr Bay, she needed to borrow the funds necessary to buy into the partnership. In her case, the amount required was $35,072.



12. Like Mr Bay, the applicant did not and will not receive any part of the proceeds of the NAB loan.



13. In discharge of the personal liability of the applicant, the interest on the NAB loan was paid during the subject year of income from partnership funds. As at the end of the subject year of income, none of the NAB loan principal had yet been repaid.



THE LAW



14. The issues to be decided are:-



(a) whether the interest on the NAB loan is an allowable deduction to the partnership pursuant to section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") and



(b) whether the characterisation of the interest is affected by the changes in constitution of the partnership that occurred between the obtaining of the NAB loan and the admission of the applicant as a partner.



15. Dealing with (a) above, pursuant to section 92(1) of the Act:-



"The assessable income of a partner in a partnership shall include-



(a) so much of the individual interest of the partner in the net income of the partnership of the year of income as is attributable to a period when the partner was a resident; and



(b) so much of the individual interest of the partner in the net income of the partnership of the year of income as is attributable to a period when the partner was not a resident and is also attributable to sources in Australia."



16. Section 90 of the Act defines "net income" as:-



"the assessable income of the partnership, calculated as if the partnership were a taxpayer who was a resident, less all allowable deductions except deductions allowable under section 79E, 80, 80AA or 82 AAT;"



17. The definition of "net income" in section 90 provides the basis for deciding the issue of deductibility of the interest on the NAB loan as if the partnership was a separate legal entity from its partners.



18. Section 51(1) of the Act provides:-



"All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."



19. To quote Brennan J in Ure v FC of T 81 ATC 4100 at 4104:



"Section 51 requires that a deductible expenditure be incurred 'in' gaining assessable income, that is to say, it must be incidental and relevant to the gaining of that income (Ronpibon Tin NL & Tongkah Compound NL v FC of T [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 C.L.R. 47 at p. 56). An outgoing of interest may be incidental and relevant to the gaining of assessable income where the borrowed money is laid out for the purpose of gaining that income (FC of T v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 C.L.R. 153 at pp. 170, 171, 197; Texas Co (Australasia) Ltd v FC of T [1940] HCA 9; (1940) 63 CLR 382 at p. 468). The laying out of the borrowed money for the purpose of gaining assessable income furnishes the required connection between the interest paid upon it by the taxpayer and the income derived by him from its use."





20. Deane and Sheppard JJ similarly held (at pp 4109-4110):



"In a case such as the present where the outgoing claimed as a deduction is interest paid on borrowed money, one cannot ordinarily look to the direct object or advantage which the outgoing was intended to achieve for the reason that that will ordinarily be the receipt of the borrowed money which is likely to be neutral in character. One must, of necessity, look more to the objects or advantages which the application and use of the borrowed money were intended to gain (FC of T v Munro (supra at p. 197). Where there is a difference between intended and actual use of the borrowed money or a change in use, difficulties may arise in determining what are the relevant objects or advantages which the payment of interest was calculated to procure."



21. Mr Abbott, of learned counsel for the applicant submitted that



"when the loan is taken out, the advantage gained for the partnership is to reduce the capital utilised within the business and to replace that capital by the principal sum of the loan. It is that which gives the interest payable upon that loan the character of expenditure incurred in the course of a business for the derivation of assessable income or incurred in gaining assessable income itself."



22. With respect, this assumes that the proceeds of the loan became part of the business of the partnership. True it is that the loan of $125,000 was credited to the partnership bank account, nevertheless I am satisfied that that was merely a shorthand way by which the then five partners each obtained $25,000. However, that shortcut is fiscally no different from, say, drawing five $25,000 cheques and handing one to each of the partners. Had the latter procedure been adopted, it could hardly be maintained that the money thus withdrawn was used for purposes of the partnership business. In slow motion, what occurred here was a borrowing of money by the partnership from NAB. So far so good. The next question to be asked is: what were the borrowed funds used for? Here, the answer is clear: it was handed to the five existing partners to do with as they pleased; indeed, it was conceded by Mr Devonport in cross-examination that each partner regarded his use of his share of the $125,000 as a private matter.



23. At the risk of going out on a limb, it seems to me that purpose must be subservient to use. In Ilbery v FC of T 81 ATC 4661 at 4667, Toohey J noted:



"As Brennan J. pointed out (in the Magna Alloys case 80 ATC 4542 at p. 4547):



Though purpose is not the test of deductibility nor even a conception relevant to a loss involuntarily incurred, in cases where a connection between an outgoing and the taxpayer's undertaking or business is affected by the voluntary act of the taxpayer, the purpose of incurring that expenditure may constitute an element of its essential character, stamping it as expenditure of a business or income-earning kind.



Conversely, I would add, purpose may stamp the outgoing as one having no relevant connection with the gaining or producing of assessable income."



24. I am satisfied that the interest paid on that loan was not an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income of the partnership; nor was it necessarily incurred in carrying on the business of the partnership. If the use of the money cannot be shown to have an intimate connection with the derivation of the income of the business, the fact that the borrowing was intended to facilitate, say, attracting impecunious solicitors into the partnership is too remote from the business of the firm to make the interest on the borrowed funds deductible pursuant to either limb of sec 51(1). This proposition is surely one of the fundamental verities of the Tax Act (if there is such a thing) going back to Munro's case (supra), which is authority for the proposition that merely borrowing money on the security of rent-producing property which is used for a purpose whereby no income is produced, does not make the interest paid thereon an allowable deduction pursuant to what is now sec 51(1).



25. On the whole of the evidence, I am satisfied that the interest payment by the partnership not only fails the two positive limbs of sec 51(1), but, in any event, is of a "private or domestic nature".



26. Mr Pagone, of learned counsel for the Crown, argued in the alternative that the transaction was an affair of capital.



27. Having concluded that the use of the borrowed funds were "private or domestic", it is strictly unnecessary to deal with this argument. However, in deference to counsel, and for the sake of completeness, it suffices if I content myself with the observation that where a partner withdraws part of the capital of the business, it is classically an affair of capital, and it is no less capital if the mechanics involve a loan secured against the equity of the business.



28. It was submitted by Mr Abbott that even if I found that the interest payments were not allowable deductions of the partnership as constituted at the time the loan was taken out, those payments became an allowable deduction of the reconstituted partnership which included the applicant. It was submitted that the applicant simply bought her share of the partnership with the NAB loan already in place, and as such the interest payments paid by the partnership of which she was a member were capable of a characterisation different from that which they had when paid by the partnership as constituted at the time of the commencement of the NAB loan.



29. Mr Pagone's response to this submission was two-fold. He submitted:-



(a) the partnership as constituted at the time of the commencement of the loan had not, as a matter of law, been dissolved and as such the characterisation of the interest payments remained the same; and



(b) in any event, the loan had been a separate borrowing and had remained as such in the partnership's books of account. Hence, there was no question of its having lost its identity.



30. Regarding the first submission, I am satisfied that for tax purposes, the effect of a change in the membership of a partnership is that the old partnership is dissolved. I can do no better than quote Stephen J in Tikva Investments Pty Ltd v FC of T [1972] HCA 68; (1972) 128 CLR 158 where his Honour held:

"When sec 92 refers to "the partnership", it necessarily refers to the "association of persons" described in the definition of "partnership" in sec 6(1); with every alteration in the identity of the persons together, whether because of the substitution of a new partner for an old or the introduction of additional partners, a new association of persons comes into existence, the old association of persons being replaced by the new. ..."



31. It follows that the instant partnership was, for tax purposes, dissolved each time there was an admission or retirement of a partner.



32. However, that is not necessarily the end of the matter. The fact that the loan was made by a separate borrowing, and kept as such in the partnership's accounts leads me to uphold the respondent's submission that the loan has kept its identity sufficient to maintain the characterisation of the interest payments as non-deductible by the partnership as subsequently constituted. I am therefore satisfied that the "net income" of the partnership for the 1988 year of income should be calculated on the basis that the interest incurred on the NAB loan in that year of income was not an allowable deduction of the partnership. The parties have agreed that the $3,089 increase in the applicant's taxable income affected by the amended assessment, represents the proportion of the interest incurred during the abovementioned year of income on the NAB loan which is equal to her partnership interest. It is therefore unnecessary for me to do those calculations.



33. For the above reasons, the objection decision is affirmed.
