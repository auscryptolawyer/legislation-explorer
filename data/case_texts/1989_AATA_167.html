ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Allowable deductions - Living-away-from-home allowance - Taxpayer transferring with family to remote location - Whether "living-away-from-home".

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s.51A, s.79A, s.221A

R. v Hammond [1852] EngR 108; (1852) 117 ER 1477

REASONS FOR DECISION

14 July 1989 Mr Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. In 1980 the Applicant, a hydrographic engineer and surveyor, was residing in the Australian Capital Territory. At that time he accepted an appointment to the service of an organisation carrying on business in Western Australia. In consequence he transferred his home from Canberra to Perth, Western Australia. During 1984 he was called on by his employer to work at and from Port Hedland in Western Australia - a township some 2,000 kilometres to the north of Perth. In the months preceding August 1984 he continued to maintain his home in Perth and travelled to Port Hedland as required. He had several trips in that period, the longest period he would have been at Port Hedland and away from his home in Perth would have been some two months.

2. In August 1984 his wife and young son moved with him to Port Hedland. A second child was born there some months later. From that point until late 1987, when the family returned to Perth, they resided in rented accommodation in Port Hedland. The cost of that accommodation was subsidised by the organisation for which the Applicant's employer was working.

  1. I accept that living conditions in Port Hedland were of such a character as to be considered to qualify some of the persons living there for the benefits of "zone allowances" for the purposes of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). The entitlement to the allowance was confined to persons who satisfied the concept of being a "resident." within the zone as provided for by S.79A (3B) of the Act. The Applicant claimed and was granted such benefits.
  2. I accept that in some respects the cost of living for families residing in Port Hedland was greater than for those residing in Perth. I accept that some items were dearer, but I also find that there was less need or occasion to incur some expenses than would be commonly experienced in Perth. As to some other items the evidence was not sufficient to enable me to say one way or the other. I instance the matter of the costs of accommodation. Whether the cost to the Applicant of his subsidised rented accommodation was greater or less than he had incurred in Perth by way of mortgage interest and other charges is unknown. While residing in Port Hedland the duties of his employment from time to time took him away from his home. Sometimes he would be on a drilling rig located off the coast; on other occasions he would travel to other places in continental Australia. When engaged in that way in the course of his employment, his employer made provision for the extra expenses occasioned by such travel.

5. I accept that the fact of his move to Port Hedland was occasioned by the request if his employees that he should move from his then place of abode, Perth, to take up duties at Port Hedland. I also accept that in consequence or the move he was paid what was described by his employer in his group certificate as "LAH" and I accept that that was intended to refer to what the Applicant described in his return of income as assessable income received by way of "living-away-from-home allowance".

6. During the year of income ended 30 June 1986 the Applicant so received $16,640. In the same year of income he claimed a deduction in the sum of $16,536 pursuant to Section 51A of the Act - a section repealed by Act No 41 of 1986 with effect from and for the year of income commencing 1 July 1986. Section 51A so far as is material read:

"(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in Section 51, where the assessable income of a taxpayer who is an employee includes the amount of any living-away-from-home allowance, an amount ascertained in accordance with this section shall be an allowable deduction.

(2) The amount of the deduction allowable under this section shall be ...

(3) For the purposes of this section -

'employee' has the same meaning as that expression has in Section 221A; and

'living-away-from-home allowance' means so much of any allowance or benefit paid or granted in money or otherwise as the Commissioner is satisfied is in the nature of compensation to the employee for the additional expenses (not being expenses which are allowable as a reduction under section 51) incurred by him, or which would be incurred by him if the allowance or benefit were not received, through having to live away from his usual place of abode in order to perform his duties as an employee,"

7. In my view the claim must fail. Indeed, but for two circumstances I would not have reserved my decision in the matter. Those circumstances were that the Applicant was not able to attend the hearing (his evidence was taken by telephone) and that his representative understandably placed strong reliance upon the decision of the Taxation Board of Review No 1 reported as Case R81 (1966 16 TBRD 392).

8. In that case an airline pilot whose home base was in an Australian capital city was posted to an overseas station for a maximum period of two years. Whilst overseas he was paid (inter alia) a "station allowance". The Board held that the allowance was a "living-away-from-home allowance" and allowed a deduction under Section 51A of the Act. It was an unanimous decision. In my view the conclusion reached rested very much upon a finding of fact that "on these facts we think it is true to say that, even though he took his family with him, the taxpayer was required in terms of the definition "to live away from his usual place of abode in order to perform his duties as an employee". So viewed it is not necessary or appropriate that one should comment upon the finding, or speculate as to whether one would have reached the same conclusion upon the evidence before that Board.

9. If that is not the true basis for the decision, I express the view that the decision was incorrect. In my view the sense of the concept of a "living-away-from-home allowance" is best understood by reference to the concept of residence expressed by Lord Campbell in R. v Hammond [(1852) 17 Q.B.D. 772; (1852 117 E.R. 14771 when his Lordship said:

"A man's residence, where he lives with his family and sleeps at night, is always his place of abode in the full sense of that expression."

  1. That dictum has long been respected and has been applied in many cases (Case N31, 81 ATC 167 provides an example). Difficulties in its application have been experienced from time to time, particularly in application to the claims of young persons who have moved out from the residence of their parents to commence employment beyond daily commuting distance from the family home. In some circumstances and for some taxpayers it has been held that the parental home has remained the home of the taxpayer (Case L64 (1960) 11 T.B.R.D. 395; Case R99 34 ATC 650); for others the finding has been that the young person has established a new place of ordinary abode independently of the family residence (Case H3 76 ATC 9).
  2. But no such difficulty exists in this case. It is true that the Applicant had not transferred to Port Hedland with any intention of permanently residing there. But nonetheless in moving there as he did he not only changed his place of work but he also changed his place of residence. In my view for the duration of his stay his ordinary place of abode was Port Hedland. I accept that the perquisites of his employment were increased as an inducement to him to accept the appointment, and with it, residence in Port Hedland. But those benefits were not compensations to make good the additional expenses of living away from his home: if anything they were moneys paid to him as compensation for maintaining his home in a place at which the expenses of maintaining his household were increased.

12. I adopt what was said by Board of Review No. 2 in Case C55 (71 ATC 242 at 245) when it said:-

"The statutory provision appears to us to be here clearly dealing with a situation wherein a man has a home and lives away from it for the purposes of his work, thus having to maintain a home in one place while partly, or maybe in some cases wholly, having to maintain himself in another place."

Put another way, the allowance is to provide a measure of relief for those who incur additional expense by reason of living away from home. It is not to provide relief for those who incur additional expense by reason of moving their home to either the city or to remote country areas.

13. In my view the determination of the Commissioner upon the objection under review must be upheld.
