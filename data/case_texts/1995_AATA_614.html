NT95/144-147 and Commissioner of Taxation [1995] AATA 614; (1995) 31 ATR 1259 (27 September 1995)

Last Updated: 13 February 2009



Administrative Appeals Tribunal





DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE - taxation - whether applicant competent to object after bankruptcy - whether right to have objection determined by Tribunal “property” under Bankruptcy Act 1966



Taxation Administration Act 1953 ss 14ZU, 14ZZA

Bankruptcy Act 1966 ss 5(1), 58(1)(b), 60, 116



Szajntop v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 93 ATC 4307

Case 2/94 94 ATC 106

Case X/18 90 ATC 219

Fuller v Beach Petroleum NL [1993] FCA 453; 117 ALR 235

AAT No 10445



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) NO s NT95/144-147

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF

TAXATION



Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal : Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)



Date : 27 September 1995



Place : Sydney



Decision : The Tribunal decided that —



1 (a) the applicant is incompetent to seek a review of the original objection decision;



(b) the applicant is incompetent to apply to have the objection amended or substituted;



(c) the applicant is incompetent to apply for an extension of time.



2 The application is therefore dismissed.

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

NOs NT95/144—147

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re :

Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION



27 September 1995 Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)



  1. Following an audit of the taxation affairs of the applicant, a letter was written to him by an officer of the respondent on 20 July 1992. Consisting of 7 pages of analysis of various transactions, the officer indicated that in his opinion there had been a significant understatement of income in the applicant’s personal income tax returns for the reasons set out in the letter. The applicant’s comments were then invited in relation to proposed adjustments and additional tax set out in the last page of the letter.
  2. On 19 August 1992 the applicant’s solicitors wrote a brief reply under 7 headings, some of which could conceivably be regarded as reasoned responses and others of which might be described simply as denials.
  3. There is no evidence whether the letter of 19 August 1992 had an effect on the decision-making process. It was followed by amended assessments issued on 7 April 1993.
  4. On 4 June 1993 the same solicitors wrote to the respondent in these terms —

“We have been instructed to object on behalf of our client [the applicant] against the Notices of Assessment for the years ended 30th June 1988, 30th June 1987, 30th June 1986 and 30th June 1985 on the ground that the income which these assessments are based is overstated and incorrect.

Our client also objects to any penalties included in the assessments.

Please forward all communications to our client in care of this office.

We have also been instructed to accept service of any process against our client.”

  1. This letter was apparently intended to constitute an objection against the assessments. Clearly, however, taken in isolation the letter does not comply with s 14ZU of the Taxation Administration Act in that it does not state “fully and in detail” the grounds that the applicant relied upon. It was to be argued before me on a later occasion that the letter of 4 June 1993 could be looked at in conjunction with the 2 earlier documents of 20 June 1992 and 19 August 1992, even though those earlier documents came into existence prior to the issue of the assessments and that looked at in their totality, the 3 documents could amount to an objection which complied with the statutory requirements. The applicant sought support for this proposition in a judgment of a Full Court of the Federal Court in Szajntop v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 93 ATC 4307. For reasons to which I will presently come, it will not be necessary to decide at this stage whether the 3 documents constituted a valid objection.
  2. On 13 December 1994, a sequestration order was made in bankruptcy against the estate of the applicant. In the meantime, between December 1994 and March 1995, there was extensive correspondence between the respondent and the applicant’s solicitors in an attempt to identify the grounds upon which the applicant intended to rely. There is also some reference in the correspondence to High Court litigation between the applicant and the respondent which may have delayed a final decision on the objection.
  3. Ultimately, this was given on 15 March 1995. The relevant parts of the letter conveying the objection decision were —

“Your objections dated 4 June 1993 against your amended assessments that issued on 4 July 1993 [sic] for the income years ended 30 June 1985 to 1988 inclusive has been considered and has been disallowed for the following reasons.

In respect to each year, the failure to provide grounds for objection, or any material to be considered.”

  1. On 19 May 1995 the applicant applied to the Federal Court for an order annulling the sequestration order. The solicitor appearing in the proceedings before me is not the same solicitor that has the conduct of the annulment proceedings. I am not satisfied that I have received accurate and up to date information concerning the progress of these proceedings. Certainly at the time of the hearing before me those proceedings had not been heard by a judge nor had they been concluded.
  2. On 9 May 1995, the applicant applied to this Tribunal to have the objection decision of 15 March 1995 reviewed pursuant to s 14ZZA of the Taxation Administration Act. Following correspondence with the respondent and a preliminary directions hearing, a formal application was made by solicitors for the applicant to “lodge amended grounds of objection on behalf of the above taxpayer”. The application was accompanied by a document which is not so much an amendment, as a completely new formal objection which appears to raise fully and in detail the grounds relied upon. The solicitors also applied on behalf of their client for an appropriate extension of time. The principal reasons for refusal of the respondent’s consent to both applications was that the applicant is an undischarged bankrupt and was therefore not legally competent to lodge an extension of time request, or any further objection, or any amended objection.
  3. If the respondent is correct, then not only will the interlocutory applications fail, but the whole application to the Tribunal will fail for lack of competency.
  4. Some time was spent debating before me the meaning and effect of parts of s 60 of the Bankruptcy Act. Sub-section 60(2) provides that an action commenced by a person who subsequently becomes a bankrupt is, upon his becoming a bankrupt, stayed until the trustee makes election , in writing, to prosecute or discontinue the action. Sub-section 60(5) defines “action” to mean “any civil proceeding, whether at law or in equity”. A letter was tendered from the Official Receiver indicating that he was unable to advise if he would be seeking a review of the objection decision. This would depend on whether or not the Court granted the annulment which the applicant sought.
  5. In my view, the terms of s 60 are irrelevant in the present circumstances. The sequestration order was made on 13 December 1994. The proceedings in this Tribunal were commenced on 9 May 1995. There is therefore no question of a stay of action coming into effect. It is therefore not necessary to examine the reasoning of the Tribunal in Case 2/94, reported at 94 ATC 106, in so far as it dealt with the stay aspect. It is also not necessary to indicate my view whether the approach in Case X18, reported at 90 ATC 219, is to be preferred. It was also recognised in Case 2/94 that in the circumstances of that case, s 60(2) had no effect. All the Tribunal’s observations on this aspect of the case, therefore, were obiter. In my view it would not be helpful to engage in another analysis of the meaning of s 60 and its effect on Tribunal proceedings when it is clearly unnecessary to do so having regard to the facts of this case.
  6. This is so whether the letter of 4 June 1993 in conjunction with the earlier correspondence constituted a valid assessment, or whether the document now propounded by the applicant is to be regarded as a fresh objection or an amendment of the earlier objection. The fact is that the applicant’s bankruptcy preceded the initiation of proceedings in this Tribunal. These proceedings therefore are not susceptible of being stayed upon the occasion of the making of a sequestration order.
  7. The real point to be determined in the present proceedings is whether the applicant is competent to conduct these proceedings. Section 58(1)(b) of the Bankruptcy Act provides that after acquired property of the bankrupt vests, as soon as it is acquired by, or devolves on, the bankrupt, in the official trustee (relevantly). Property of the bankrupt is defined in s 5(1) to mean (relevantly) the property divisible among the bankrupt’s creditors and any rights and powers in relation to that property that would have been exercisable by the bankrupt if he or she had not become a bankrupt. Property is defined in the same section to mean real or personal property of every description, whether situated in Australia or elsewhere, and to include any estate, interest or profit, whether present or future, vested or contingent, arising out of or incident to any such real or personal property. Section 116(1) defines property divisible among creditors as including (relevantly) all property acquired by the bankrupt after the commencement of the bankruptcy and the capacity to exercise, and to take proceedings for exercising all such powers in, over or in respect of property as might have been exercised by the bankrupt for his own benefit at the commencement of the bankruptcy or at any time after the commencement of the bankruptcy and before his discharge. Excepted from these general words in paragraph 116(2)(g) is any right of the bankrupt to recover damages or compensation for personal injury or wrong done to the bankrupt, the spouse of the bankrupt or a member of the family of the bankrupt.
  8. The question to be asked is whether the right to have the objection decision of 15 March 1995 reviewed by this Tribunal is a right which can properly fall within the wide definition of property. In my opinion, it can and does. Such a right in the present circumstances is after acquired property and vests in the Official Receiver as the applicant’s trustee in bankruptcy immediately upon the right of review coming into existence, namely upon the making of the objection decision. Since 15 March 1995, the Official Receiver is the only person capable of exercising that right.
  9. This was not the view taken by the Tribunal in Case 2/94. Regrettably, I find myself unable to agree with the reasoning of the Tribunal in that case. This aspect was dealt with in paragraphs 20 to 32. The Tribunal considered a decision of a Master of the Supreme Court of Western Australia, involving the same parties that were before it in the application to review an objection decision. For reasons that are set out in the Tribunal’s decision, the Master took the view that a right to apply to set aside a monetary judgment fell within the definition of property in s 5 of the Bankruptcy Act and held that such a matter impacted upon the estate of the bankrupt and was not merely something relating to the person or feelings of the bankrupt. The Tribunal sought to distinguish the facts of the matter before it from those before the Master on the basis that the Supreme Court was dealing with a point of Civil Law, whereas the Tribunal was dealing with a point of Administrative Law. Having established this distinction to its satisfaction, the Tribunal then went on to say without further explanation that a right of a taxpayer to apply pursuant to s 14ZZA could therefore not be regarded as property as defined in s 5 of the Bankruptcy Act. I am unable to agree that the stated conclusion followed from what went before.
  10. It was apparent from the text of the decision that the observations of the majority of a Full Court of the Federal Court in Fuller v Beach Petroleum NL [1993] FCA 453; 117 ALR 235 was not considered. The Tribunal decision was given on 17 December 1993. The Federal Court decision had been given 3 months prior to that, but possibly was not published by the time the Tribunal decision was arrived at. It seems unlikely in the circumstances that the Tribunal was referred to the decision. Many of the submissions made by counsel for the applicant and summarised in paragraph 24 of the decision are clearly inconsistent with dicta of Gummow and Whitlam JJ in Fuller.
  11. It was submitted by counsel for the applicant, for example, that to eliminate a liability is not to increase the property available to creditors but merely to reduce the amount of debts owed and increase the prospect of repayment to creditors. It followed, so the submission went, that a challenge to an income tax liability did not directly affect the property of the bankrupt. The Court in Fuller, referring to the width of the definition of property, came to an opposite conclusion. At page 242, for example, their Honours said —

“In our view, the statutory right of appeal given by s 24 of the Federal Court Act to, for example, a party against whom judgment has been entered falls within the meaning of the expression “personal property of every description” in the definition of “property” in s 5(1) of the Act. This, in our view, is so even though the object of the appeal is the removal of the burdens of a judgment debt and costs orders placed upon the appellant, as an unsuccessful defendant, by the orders the object of the appeal, rather than, for example, the seeking on appeal of orders for payment of money which were unsuccessfully sought below by the appellant, as plaintiff.”

  1. A letter from the Official Receiver tendered in the present proceedings indicates that a proof of debt has been lodged by the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation in an amount of $1,383,809.55. If the application to review the objection decision is successful, then the property divisible among the applicant’s creditors would be significantly increased. The fact that the application is to deal with the lifting of a burden of a debt rather than the positive acquisition of property is therefore irrelevant. As their Honours observed (at 241) —

“The term “property” when used in modern statutes may, in its context, include rights and interests which are created by statute and which would not be identified as proprietary by traditional conveyancing law.”

  1. Their Honours concluded that the statutory right of appeal given by s 24 of the Federal Court Act to a party against whom judgment has been entered, falls within the meaning of the expression “personal property of every description” within the definition of “property” in s 5(1). They concluded that this was so, even though the object of the appeal was the removal of the burden of a judgment debt and costs orders placed upon the appellant, as an unsuccessful defendant, rather than the seeking on appeal of orders for payment of money unsuccessfully sought below by the appellant, as plaintiff. They held, therefore, that the appellant’s right to appeal vested in the trustee of the estate of the appellant and that the appeal should be dismissed as incompetent.
  2. I am unable to see any difference between a right of appeal in those circumstances and a right to have an adverse objection decision reviewed by this Tribunal. A right of review can lead to a redefinition of the rights and obligations of the parties to an objection decision in no lesser way than that which can follow from a curial right of appeal. I am also unable to see the significance of the distinction sought to be drawn in Case 2/94 between proceedings under Civil Law and proceedings under Administrative law. Having regard to the reasoning of the majority in Fuller, it seems to me that there is no such distinction which would impact upon the understanding of the broad definition of property.
  3. That being so, I have concluded that the applicant is incompetent to seek a review of the original objection decision and is equally incompetent to apply to have the objection amended, or substituted, or to apply for an extension of time for such purpose. Accordingly the application will be dismissed.
  4. I have deliberately not dealt with the argument concerning the validity of the original objection. If, in due course, the applicant’s bankruptcy is annulled, then with suitable extensions of time, he may be in a position to recommence proceedings in this Tribunal. It is therefore not appropriate to prejudge the merits either of the assertions made in the so called objection or the validity of the objection documents themselves. In view of the unsatisfactory information concerning the annulment proceedings, it is inappropriate to postpone this decision until after they have been resolved.
