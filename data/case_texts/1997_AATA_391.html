Russell Eryl Glenn and Rex William Over and Commissioner of Taxation [1997] AATA 391 (17 October 1997)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

RUSSELL ERYL GLENN and REX WILLIAM OVER v. COMMISSIONER OF

TAXATION

Nos. VS97/10 and 11

AAT No. 12309

Number of pages -

6

Income Tax



COURT

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

(SITTING AS SMALL TAXATION CLAIMS TRIBUNAL)

B.H. PASCOE (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Life assurance policy surrendered - amount received as or by way of bonus - agreement with employer for split of interests and premiums - whether unreasonable to include full value of reversionary bonus in assessable income - whether premium paid by employer to be taken into account

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s. 26AH

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 23 September 1997 (hearing), 17 October 1997 (decision)

17:10:1997

For the Applicant: Mr H Koustas, Tax Agent

For the Respondent: A Departmental Officer

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decisions under review.

DECISION

B.H. PASCOE

These are applications to review the decisions of the respondent to disallow objections lodged by the applicants, Mr Glenn and Mr Over, against amended assessments of income tax based on their income for the year ended 30 June 1992. Both amended assessments were issued on 27 June 1995 to include as assessable income an amount received as or by way of bonus under a policy of life assurance. The bonus was said to be assessable under section 26AH of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). The facts relating to the matter were not in dispute. What was disputed was the way in which this section of the Act should be applied to those facts. By consent both applications were heard together.

Each applicant is a director and employee of Rydell Holdings Pty Ltd ("the company") which acts as a trustee for the Rydell Unit Trust and operates a belting manufacturing business. On or about 15 December 1988 the company took out policies on the lives of the applicants. The policies were in the name of the company, the premiums were paid by the company and it would appear that the company received the proceeds of the surrender of the policies on 20 February 1992. However, the arrangements were subject to an agreement entered into by each of the applicants with the company and dated 15 December 1988. The recital to the agreement stated that the company wished to provide for the loss it may suffer in the event of the death of the employee/applicant and that the employee wished to provide for the welfare of himself and dependants. The agreement then provided for the company to effect a life policy on the life of the employee and to hold it upon trust for the employee under the terms of the agreement. The company was to be liable for that portion of the premium which would be payable under a separate policy of term insurance providing identical cover equal to the sum assured on the life of the employee to his normal retirement date. The employee was liable to reimburse the company for the balance of the premium. If the employee died before the normal retirement date, the company was to be entitled to an amount equal to the sum assured and the employee's estate entitled to the balance of the policy proceeds. The policy was to vest solely in the employee on the normal retirement date or on earlier termination of the agreement by consent, termination of employment or failure of either party to make payments required by the agreement.

The facts in relation to each applicant were the same other than the amounts involved. For convenience, it is appropriate to deal with the policy on the life of Mr Glenn and apply the findings to the similar situation of Mr Over. The sum assured was $3 million and four annual premiums were paid totalling $315,120. Of the total premiums the company paid $42,540 and Mr Glenn $272,580. The policy was surrendered on 20 February 1992 and advice from the insurance company showed the following:

Surrender value of sum assured $113,187.02

Surrender value of reversionary bonus 183,283.98

Gross surrender value $296,471.00

Following an audit, the respondent issued an amended assessment to include an amount of $23,891 in assessable income. This amount was arrived at by deducting the premiums of $272,580 paid by Mr Glenn from the gross surrender value or proceeds of the policy of $296,471. A rebate of income tax applicable to the amount so included was allowed pursuant to section 160AAB of the Act. It was accepted that the total proceeds were received by the company in trust for Mr Glenn.

The provisions of section 26AH which are relevant to this matter are:

"26AH(1) In this section, unless the contrary intention appears:

'eligible period', in relation to an eligible policy, means the period of 10 years commencing on the date of commencement of risk of the policy;

'eligible policy' means a policy of life assurance in relation to which the date of commencement of risk is after 27 August 1982;

26AH(2) ...

26AH(3) This section applies to any amount received after 27 August 1982 under an eligible policy.

26AH(4) For the purposes of this section, but subject to sub-section (5), a taxpayer shall be taken to have received an amount under or in relation to an eligible policy although the amount is not actually paid to the taxpayer but is re-invested or otherwise dealt with on his behalf or as he directs.

26AH(5) Sub-section (4) does not apply in relation to an amount in relation to an eligible policy if the amount is re-invested or otherwise dealt with on behalf of the taxpayer or as the taxpayer directs so as to increase the amount that might reasonably be expected to be received under the eligible policy on a surrender or maturity of the eligible policy.

26AH(6) Where, during the eligible period in relation to an eligible policy, a taxpayer receives an amount (in this sub-section referred to as the "relevant amount") under the policy as or by way of a bonus, being an amount that, but for this section, would not be included in the assessable income of the taxpayer of any year of income, the assessable income of the taxpayer of the year of income in which the relevant amount is received shall include-

(a) if the relevant amount is received during the first 8 years of the eligible period - an amount equal to the relevant amount;

(b) if the relevant amount is received during the ninth year of the eligible period - an amount equal to two-thirds of the relevant amount; or

(c) if the relevant amount is received during the tenth year of the eligible period - an amount equal to one-third of the relevant amount.

26AH(7) ...

26AH(8) Where-

(a) sub-section (6) would, but for this sub-section, apply to an amount (in this sub-section referred to as the "relevant amount") received by a taxpayer by reason of the forfeiture, surrender or other termination of the whole or a part of an eligible policy; and

(b) the Commissioner, having regard to-

(i) the total amount of premiums paid under the eligible policy;

(ii) the total amounts received by the taxpayer or by any other person under the eligible policy and the total amounts of bonuses included in the amounts so received;

(iii) the amount of the surrender value of the eligible policy at the time when the forfeiture, surrender or other termination occurred; and

(iv) such other matters as the Commissioner considers relevant,

is of the opinion that it would be unreasonable for sub-section (6) to apply to the relevant amount or to a part of the relevant amount,

sub-section (6) does not apply to the relevant amount, or to that part of the relevant amount, as the case may be.

26AH(9) Where-

(a) otherwise than as or by way of a bonus, a taxpayer receives an amount (in this sub-section referred to as the 'relevant amount') under an eligible policy; and

(b) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the relevant amount or a part of the relevant amount represents the whole or part of-

(i) a bonus that has accrued or has been declared in respect of the policy; or

(ii) a bonus that can reasonably be expected to accrue in respect of the policy,

the relevant amount or the part of the relevant amount, as the case may be, shall, for the purposes of sub-section (6), be deemed to have been received by the taxpayer under the policy as or by way of a bonus."

The respondent had taken the view that the bonus received by Mr Glenn was $183,283.98 as advised by the insurance company. However, it was seen as appropriate that the provisions of subsection (8) should apply to reduce the amount to be included as assessable income under subsection (6) to the difference between the total surrender value of the policy and the premiums paid by Mr Glenn. It was submitted that it was inappropriate for the premiums paid by the company to be taken into account as these were not paid by the taxpayer, Mr Glenn, and represented the cost to the company of the term life cover security which it was seeking. It was said that that the policy was taken out under a "split dollar" arrangement. This was described as involving a policy in which there were two components, a risk component and the investment savings component, and where two parties agree to share the benefits provided by the policy and the premiums payable. It was said that the agreement, such as that entered into between the company and Mr Glenn, was necessary to overcome the fact that the Life Insurance Act does not recognise a partial or limited assignment of a life policy. It was argued that the proportion of premiums paid by the company related solely to its interest in the policy to which no bonus or surrender value applied. Consequently, it was said, the surrender value, including bonuses, related to the investment component applicable to the premiums paid by Mr Glenn.

For the applicant, it was said that the insurance policy in question was one policy only and any split of premiums was an artificial split. It was submitted that the provisions of section 26AH(8) required the total premiums paid, $315,120, to be deducted from the surrender value of the policy so that there was no surplus to be included in assessable income. It was argued that intention of the section was to ensure that no amount described as a bonus would be included in assessable income unless the total proceeds of a policy exceeded total premiums paid. In a statement filed prior to the hearing, it was contended that only the excess of the surrender value of the reversionary bonus $183,283.98, over the amount of investment component premiums paid on the policy should be included as assessable income. As this latter amount of $272,580 exceeded such surrender value, no such excess existed. This alternative contention was not maintained at the hearing.

Unfortunately, the word "bonus" for the purposes of section 26AH is not defined. Subsection (6) refers to a bonus, being an amount that, but for the section, would not be included in the assessable income of the taxpayer. Section 26(i) includes in assessable income "any amount received as or by way of bonus other than a reversionary bonus on a policy of life assurance". Consequently it is assumed that it is a "reversionary bonus on a policy of life assurance" that is the bonus referred to in section 26AH(6). It would appear correct, therefore to say that it is the amount of $183,283.98 which would be included in Mr Glenn's assessable income of the year ended 30 June 1992. However, this is subject to subsection (8) and consideration of whether, having regard to the four matters referred to, it would be unreasonable for the whole of that amount to be so included. The section was inserted by Act No. 14 of 1984 and was so enacted to ensure that profits derived by taxpayers from investment in short term life assurance policies, that is, less than ten years, were to be subject to tax. The assessability was linked to section 160AAB to allow a rebate of the tax paid by the insurer on the investment profits added to the policy. The definition of reversionary bonus according to The Macquarie Dictionary, 2nd Edition, is "a periodic free addition to the sum insured under a life assurance policy, made from surplus earned on the insurer's investments".

In this case, the respondent accepted that it was unreasonable for subsection (6) to apply to the whole of the amount described as surrender value of reversionary bonus. I have no doubt that this was a correct view. The primary argument was whether, in determining the part of the amount to be excluded, the total premiums of $315,120 should be allowed for or only the premiums paid by Mr Glenn, $272,580. It should be borne in mind that subsection (8) applies only where the whole or a part of policy is forfeited, surrendered or otherwise terminated. It would not appear to apply where proceeds of a policy are received at its maturity date, either at the date specified in the policy or the earlier death of the life assured. In such cases the sum assured will be that set out in the policy and any excess over such sum assured would represent the reversionary bonus. The subsection is clearly there to deal with cases such as these where the description of reversionary bonus is misleading given the relatively small value attributed by the insurance company to the surrender value of the sum assured.

It was accepted by Mr Glenn that the result of the surrender of the policy was that he received $296,471 and had paid $272,580 in premiums, producing a profit in his hands of $23,891. He argued, however, that the additional cost of $42,540 incurred by the company should be taken into account. Whilst it is correct to say that subparagraph 26AH(8)(b)(i) requires "the total amount of premiums paid under the eligible policy" to be considered, subparagraph 26AH(8)(b)(iv) also requires consideration of "such other matters as the Commissioner considers relevant". In a case such as this it is relevant to consider the fact that the taxpayer, Mr Glenn, made a gain from the policy and the terms of the agreement between him and the company demonstrated that the company's interest was solely in the term life cover with any investment surplus to be the property of Mr Glenn. Consequently and taking into account the four matters to which paragraph 26AH(8)(b) refers, which, in my view, allow the whole arrangement to be considered, the exclusion of all but $23,891 from the surrender value of the reversionary bonus is reasonable. The simple facts were that Mr Glenn invested $272,580 in an insurance policy and received proceeds of $296,471. The fact that another, albeit related, entity incurred costs to provide a form of security it desired should not result in a reduction of that bonus, or investment profit, in his hands. The fact that Mr Glenn was an employee of the company and the arrangement enabled him to receive the full benefit of the surrender values of the policy in his capacity as an employee is a relevant matter.

As indicated earlier, the only differences in facts in relation to the two applicants were the amounts involved which arose because of a difference in age. For Mr Over the relevant amounts were:

Premium Paid

Company $ 46,350

Mr Over 281,010

$327,360

Surrender value

Sum Assured $118,987.77

Reversionary bonus 189,622.33

$308,610.10

Included in Assessable Income

Gross Surrender Value $308,610

Premium paid by Mr Over 281,010

$ 27,600

For the foregoing reasons, the decisions under review should be affirmed.
