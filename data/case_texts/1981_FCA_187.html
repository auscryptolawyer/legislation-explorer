Re Commissioner of Taxation v Gary Francis Mullins [1981] FCA 187; (1981) 56 FLR 256 (25 November 1981)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

And: GARY FRANCIS MULLINS [1981] FCA 187; (1981) 56 FLR 256

No. G7 of 1981

Income Tax

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

QUEENSLAND DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Deane(1), McGregor(2) and Lockhart(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - short term property sale - assessable income under s.26AAA(2) - whether s.26AAA(5) applies to exclude s.26AAA(2) - whether the property was included in "the assets of a business" carried on by the taxpayer - whether "as a result of the sale" an amount will be included in the assessable income of the taxpayer of the tax year under some provision other than s.26AAA - whether s.54 or s.59 applied in relation to "the property".

Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 - 1976, ss. 26AAA, 36, 54, 59

Income Tax - Profit on sale within twelve months of acquisition - Land included in business sold - Fences part of land - Fences depreciated - Balancing charge therefore included in income when business sold - Whether all property within exception to s. 26AAA - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), ss. 26AAA, 54, 55, 59, 62. The respondent taxpayer carried on the business of pig farming. He sold the land on which he was farming within twelve months of acquiring it. Included in the sale were fences which the respondent had depreciated for income tax purposes as part of his farming business. The Commissioner assessed the respondent on his profit on the sale of the land under s. 26AAA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. The respondent argued that the fences were part of the property sold. The whole of the property sold, including the land, was therefore within the exceptions to s. 26AAA. Therefore no part of the profit was assessable. The taxpayer objected to the Commissioner's assessment, the Commissioner disallowed the objection, the taxpayer successfully appealed to a Board of Review, the Commissioner unsuccessfully appealed to the Supreme Court of Queensland, and then appealed to the Federal Court.

Held, per majority, McGregor J. dissenting, appeal dismissed, that the land was included in the assets of a business carried on then sold by the respondent. An amount would be included in the income of the taxpayer as a balancing charge for the fences he depreciated. The exception under s. 26AAA (5) (a) therefore applied.

Per Deane and McGregor JJ., Lockhart J. expressing no opinion, that another exception under s. 26AAA (5) (b) relating to s. 54 and depreciated property, however, did not apply.

ORDER

DECISION

For some months prior to 23 April, 1976, the taxpayer and his wife carried on, in partnership, a business of pig farming on an area of approximately eight acres of land which they owned at Brassal near Ipswich. On that day, they entered into a contract to sell the land. The land had been purchased by them pursuant to a contract dated 26 June, 1975, that is, less than twelve months before they contracted to sell it. In the result, the taxpayer's share of the profit on disposal of his estate in the land constituted assessable income pursuant to the provisions of s.26AAA(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 unless, as the taxpayer contends, the circumstances are such as to result in the application of the provisions of s.26AAA(2) being excluded by one or both of clauses (a) and (b) of s.26AAA(5).

The detailed facts and the relevant statutory provisions are set out in the judgments of Lockhart J. and McGregor J. which I have had the benefit of reading in draft form. It is unnecessary that I repeat them other than to note that it is common ground that, following the sale of the land, a small amount was included in the assessable income of the taxpayer pursuant to s.59(2) of the Act representing recoupment, upon the sale, of the amount of depreciation allowable in respect of fences erected on the subject land. Clause (a) of s.26AAA(5) will exclude the application of s.26AAA(2) in relation to the sale of the property if "the property" sold "was included in the assets of a business carried on by the taxpayer and, as a result of the sale, an amount will be included in the assessable income of the taxpayer" of the tax year under some provision other than s.26AAA. Clause (b) of s.26AAA(5) will exclude the application of s.26AAA(2) in relation to the sale if "s.54 applied in relation to the property" and, as a result of the sale, "s.59 applies in relation to the property".

As at present advised, I agree, with McGregor J. that clause (b) does not, in the circumstances, exclude the application of s.26AAA(2) in relation to the sale for the reason that it cannot properly be said that either s.54 or s.59 of the Act applies in relation to the "property" the subject of the relevant sale regardless of whether that "property" be the taxpayer's estate in the relevant land or the land itself. With due respect to those who see the matter differently, it seems to me that s.54 and s.59, in their application to an item of plant, structure or structural improvement, apply to, or in relation to, the item, structure or structural improvement itself. They do not apply to, or in relation to, the whole area of land, however vast or small, to which that item of plant, structure or structural improvement may happen to be affixed or on which it may happen to be found. I am, in indicating this tentative view, conscious of the difficulty posed by structural improvements which consist of the shaping of the land itself, such as dams (see, s.54(2)(b)). It seems to me, however, that s.54 and s.59, in their application, effect, even in such cases, a notional isolation of the relevant item of plant, structure or structural improvement with the result that it cannot properly be said that either s.54 or s.59 applies in relation to the overall area of land. It is, however, unnecessary that I form or express any concluded view on that question since I have come to the view that the application of the provisions of s.26AAA(2) is excluded by reason of the operation of the provisions of clause (a) of s.26AAA.

Two questions in relation to the applicability of clause (a) have caused me more than ordinary difficulty. The first, which is of interest only for the purposes of the present appeal, is whether the property sold was an asset of the pig farming business carried on by the taxpayer and his wife. The second, which is of more general importance, is that raised by McGregor J., namely, whether the small amount representing recoupment of allowable depreciation in respect of fences on the land which the Commissioner concedes should be included in the taxpayer's assessable income pursuant to s.59(2) of the Act, can properly be regarded, for the purposes of clause (a), as being so included "as a result" of the sale by the taxpayer and his wife of their estate in the land. I turn to the second and more important of these two questions.

As has been indicated, the Commissioner expressly conceded that, following the sale, an amount representing recoupment of allowable depreciation in respect of fences was assessable income of the taxpayer pursuant to s.59(2). This concession, no doubt, accorded with the Commissioner's established administration of the Act. With some hesitation, I am of the view that the Court should, for the purposes of this appeal, accept the concession as properly made. The consequence of the concession is that, by notional apportionment of the proceeds of sale, $400 of the sale price is seen as attributable to the fences upon the land. This notional apportionment was for the purposes of s.59 in the sense that, without some such apportionment, it would be impossible to determine whether the property in respect of which depreciation was allowed or was allowable (i.e. the fences) had been disposed of for a consideration that exceeded its depreciated value. The question which arises is whether this notional apportionment should be treated as converting, for the purposes of s.26AAA(5)(a), the one sale of an estate in land on which fences were erected into two sales, namely, a notionally distinct sale of the fences and a sale of the balance of the land and improvements. If it should, the consequence would be that the inclusion, in the taxpayer's assessable income, of the amount representing recoupment of allowable depreciation would be seen as the result of the notionally distinct sale of the fences and not as the result of the sale of the estate in the land. The provisions of Clause (a) would not exclude the application of s.26AAA(2) to the sale of the estate in the land.

If there had, in truth, been a combined sale of two distinct pieces of property, it would be possible to divide the sale for the purposes of s.26AAA notwithstanding the fact that such division would involve a notional apportionment of the proceeds of sale. The plain fact of the matter in the present case is, however, that the taxpayer sold but one "property". That "property" was his estate or interest in the land on which were situated improvements and fixed structures (including fences) (see s.26AAA(1)(a): "a reference to property . . . includes a reference to an estate or interest in property . . ."). That was the "property" which he sold for the purposes of s.26AAA(2) of the Act. It is to the "sale" of that "property" to which both the introductory words of s.26AAA(5) and the words of clause (a) refer. (Note, in this regard, the reference, in s.26AAA(5)(c)(i), to the "property" consisting of "an estate or interest in land on which is situated a dwelling".) The case is not one where, because of lack of identity between what was purchased and what was sold, the application of s.26AAA necessarily involves some notional division of either the purchase or the sale. The fact that some notional apportionment may be accepted for the purposes of s.59 does not alter the fact that there was, for the purposes of s.26AAA, no other distinct sale by the taxpayer. It is not, in my view, permissible to use the notional apportionment of the proceeds of sale for the purposes of s.59 as a basis for constructing a hypothetical sub-sale of fences for the purposes of s.26AAA(5)(a) with the result that the amount included in the assessable income of the taxpayer pursuant to s.59 was so included as a result of a sale of the "fences" and not as a result of the sale of the estate in the land. In my view, the amount included in the assessable income of the taxpayer pursuant to s.59 was so included as the result of one sale of property for the purposes of s.26AAA, namely, the sale of the estate or interest in the land on which the fences were erected.

I turn to the question whether the property sold was, for the purposes of clause (a), included in the assets of a business carried on by the taxpayer. I agree, for the reasons which he gives, with the conclusion of Lockhart J. that it was. At one stage, I was inclined to think that the pig farming business should properly be seen as being carried on on the relevant land and that it was more accurate to see the land as containing the pig farming business than to see it as being included in the assets of that business. I have, however, come to the view that, in all the circumstances, the interest or estate of the taxpayer and his wife in the land constituted, an asset of that partnership business. I also agree, for the reasons which he gives, with Lockhart J's rejection of the submission that clause (b) of s.26AAA(5) exclusively covered the field of exclusion from s.26AAA(2) by reason of the application of s.54 or s.59.

The appeal should be dismissed. In view of the conditions upon which leave to appeal was granted, the Commissioner must pay the taxpayer's costs of the application for leave to appeal and the taxpayer's reasonable solicitor and client costs of the appeal.

THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION (Commissioner) appeals pursuant to leave granted on 1 May 1981 as permitted by the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (the Act) s.196(5)(a) against a decision of the Supreme Court of Queensland which followed a hearing on 17 and 18 February 1981. Judgment was delivered on 24 March 1981 in favour of GARY FRANCIS MULLINS (Respondent) who had been a successful appellant in a hearing before the Taxation Board of Review.

By Contract dated 26 June 1975 the respondent and his wife purchased 8 acres 1 rood 14 perches of land at Brassal near Ipswich, Queensland for $6,500.00. Certain fencing was then on the land. It appears from the judgment to which reference has been made that after the purchase the said fencing was the subject of some repairs. Thereafter the respondent entered upon certain activities of a farming nature on the land. The learned Judge of the Supreme Court found that the respondent at Brassal had carried on the business of pig farming and the fencing on the land was being used for pastoral purposes. Apparently the Board of Review had so found and it is said and not denied that the Commissioner has accepted this contention.

Reference has been made in the judgment the subject of the appeal to the evidence upon which the learned Judge relied (as he was entitled so to do) for his conclusion that the respondent had carried on the business of a pig farmer.

It is not necessary to repeat it in detail for the reason that this Court varied the basis upon which the leave to appeal was granted, so that the Commissioner was not entitled to argue against the finding of fact made by the learned Judge of the Supreme Court.

By contract dated 23 April 1976, the respondent and his wife sold the property for $11,300.00. Having regard to s.26AAA(1)(g) of the Act, the dates quoted are significant.

In his Income Tax return dated 23 February 1977 for the year ended 30 June 1976, the respondent disclosed a capital gain of $4,046.80 related to the sale, perhaps overlooking in that return the half interest of his wife. There had been no dissection of the consideration prices in the contracts. In the Depreciation Schedule accompanying the return a value of $200.00 was attributed to the fencing on the property at acquisition in respect of which $7.00 depreciation was mentioned. The method of this calculation is referred to in the evidence before the learned Supreme Court Judge and the figure has not been questioned. In the same Schedule, the value attributed to the fencing on disposal was $400.00. The farming venture made a loss. However, the use made of the depreciation figure is of importance in the events with which this Court is concerned.

Section 26AAA reads -

"(1) For the purposes of this section -

(a) a reference to property generally or to a particular kind of property includes a reference to an estate or interest in property or in that kind of property, as the case may be;

(b) a reference to an agreement or option includes a reference to an agreement or option that is not enforceable by legal proceedings whether or not it was intended to be so enforceable;

(c) an arrangement or understanding, whether formal or informal and whether express or implied, shall be deemed to be an agreement;

(d) the acquisition of a share by way of subscription of capital, with or without the payment of any other consideration, shall be deemed to be the purchase of that share;

(e) the issue by a company to a person of shares in the company as, or as part of, the consideration for the sale of property by the person to the company shall be deemed to constitute the purchase by the person of the shares so issued;

(f) if property is transferred from a person or persons to another person or other persons in exchange for other property or without consideration, the transfer shall be deemed to constitute the sale of the property by the first-mentioned person or persons and the purchase of the property by the second-mentioned person or persons; and

(g) if land is sold to or purchased by a person in pursuance of a contract, the date on which the contract was made shall be deemed to have been the date on which the land was so sold or purchased.

(2) Where -

(a) a taxpayer has purchased property after 21 August 1973 and before the commencement of this section or purchases property after the commencement of this section; and

(b) the taxpayer has, whether before or after the commencement of this section, sold the property or an interest in the property before the expiration of the period of 12 months from the date on which he purchased the property,

then, subject to this section, the assessable income of the taxpayer includes any profit arising from the sale of the property or interest."

The respondent seeks to avoid the incidence of sub-section (2) by reliance upon sub-section (5), viz -

"(5) Sub-section (2) does not apply in relation to a sale by a taxpayer of property if -

(a) the property was included in the assets of a business carried on by the taxpayer and, as a result of the sale, an amount will be included in the assessable income of the taxpayer of the year of income under a provision of this Act other than this section;

(b) section 54 applied in relation to the property and, as a result of the sale, section 59 applies in relation to the property; or

(c) the property consists of -

(i) an estate or interest in land on which is situated a dwelling, or in a flat or home unit, used by the taxpayer after the date on which he purchased the property as his sole or principal residence; or

(ii) a share in a company that entitles the holder of the share to a right of occupancy in relation to a flat or home unit used by the taxpayer after that date as his sole or principal residence,

and the sale took place as a result of a change in the place of employment or place of business of the taxpayer."

In the Act Division 3 of Part 111 (s.48-s.82KG) s.54 reads -

"(1) Depreciation during the year of income of any property, being plant or articles owned by a taxpayer and used by him during that year for the purpose of producing assessable income, and of any property being plant or articles owned by the taxpayer which has been installed ready for use for that purpose and is during that year held in reserve by him shall subject to this Act, be an allowable deduction.

(2) In this section, "plant" includes -

(a) animals used as beasts of burden or working beasts in a business other than a business of primary production, and machinery, implements, utensils and rolling stock;

(b) fences, dams and other structural improvements on land which is used for the purposes of agricultural or pastoral pursuits, structural improvements (not including an improvement that is an access road as defined by section 124E) completed after the year of income that ended on 30 June, 1963, on land that is used for the purposes of forest operations and structural improvements completed after 30th June, 1958, which are used wholly and exclusively for the purposes of pearling operations and are situated at or in the vicinity of a port or harbour from which those operations are conducted, other than -

(i) structural improvements used for domestic or residential purposes are provided for the accommodation of employees, tenants or share farmers engaged in or in connexion with those pursuits or operations, as the case may be; or

(ii) structural improvements, bores, wells or pipes expenditure on which has been allowed, or is or has been allowable, as a deduction under paragraph (g), (ga), (gb), (h), (i), (j) or (1) of sub-section (1) of section 75, or section 76, from the assessable income, of any year of income, of the taxpayer or of any other person; and

(c) plumbing fixtures and fittings, including wall and floor tiling, in premises acquired after 30th June, 1938, or installed in premises after that date, by a person carrying on a business for the purpose of producing assessable income, where those fixtures and fittings are provided principally for the use, for personal purposes, of persons employed by him in that business or for the care of children of those persons.

(3) Depreciation of any property that is a leisure facility for the purposes of section 51AB is not an allowable deduction.

(4) Sub-section (3) does not prevent depreciation of any property from being an allowable deduction to the extent, if any, to which the depreciation took place during a part of the year of income referred to in paragraph (a) of sub-section (5) of section 51AB."

Section 55 reads -

"(1) In the first calculation of the depreciation to be allowed in respect of any unit of property, an estimate shall be made by the Commissioner of the effective life of the unit assuming that it is maintained in reasonably good order and condition, and the annual depreciation per centum shall be fixed accordingly."

Section 59 reads -

"(1) Where any property of a taxpayer, in respect of which depreciation has been allowed or is allowable under this or the previous Act, is disposed of, lost or destroyed at any time in the year of income, the depreciated value of the property at that time, less the amount of any consideration receivable in respect of the disposal, loss or destruction, shall be an allowable deduction.

(2) If that consideration exceeds that depreciated value, the excess, to the extent of the sum of the amounts allowed and allowable in assessments for income tax under this Act and any previous law of the Commonwealth in respect of depreciation, shall, subject to the succeeding provisions of this section, be included in his assessable income of that year.

(2A) Where an amount, being the whole or a part of the consideration receivable in respect of the disposal, loss or destruction of a unit of property in the year of income (in this sub-section referred to as "the balancing charge") would, but for this sub-section, be included in the assessable income of the taxpayer under the last preceding sub-section, the Commissioner shall, if the taxpayer so requests in writing when lodging the return of income of the year of income, or within such further time as the Commissioner allows, in lieu of including the balancing charge in the assessable income, successively reduce -

(a) the cost, for the purpose of calculating depreciation allowable under this Act, of any unit of property acquired by the taxpayer during the year of income to replace the unit of property so disposed of, lost or destroyed;

(b) the cost, for the purpose of calculating depreciation allowable under this Act, of any other unit of property acquired by the taxpayer during the year of income; and

(c) the depreciated values, at the beginning of the year of income, of other units of property,

by such amounts as do not exceed, in the aggregate, the balancing charge.

(2B) The cost or depreciated value of a unit of property shall not be reduced under the last preceding sub-section unless -

(a) at the end of the year of income the unit is used wholly for the purpose of producing assessable income or has been installed ready for use wholly for that purpose and is held in reserve; and

(b) depreciation under this Act is allowable to the taxpayer in respect of the unit.

(2C) Where an amount which would, but for sub-section (2A), be included in the assessable income of the taxpayer of the year of income under sub-section (2) exceeds the sum of reductions made under sub-section (2A), the amount of that excess shall be included in his assessable income of the year of income.

(2D) Where, during a year of income not later than the second year of income after the year of income in which a unit of property is disposed of, lost or destroyed, a taxpayer acquires, to replace that unit, a unit of property which, at the end of the year of income, is used wholly for the purpose of producing assessable income, or has been installed ready for use wholly for that purpose and is held in reserve, and the taxpayer has not made a request under sub-section (2A) in relation to that disposal, loss or destruction, the Commissioner shall, if the taxpayer so requests in writing not later than the date of lodgment of the return of income of the first-mentioned year or within such further time as the Commissioner allows -

(a) exclude from the assessable income of the year of income in which the property was disposed of, lost or destroyed so much of the amount that would otherwise be included in that assessable income under sub-section (2) by reason of the disposal, loss or destruction as does not exceed the cost of the unit of property so acquired; and

(b) reduce by an amount equal to the amount so excluded the cost, for the purpose of calculating depreciation allowable under this Act, of the unit of property so acquired.

(2E) An amount by which the cost or depreciated value of a unit of property has been reduced in pursuance of sub-section (2A) or the last preceding sub-section shall, for all purposes of this Act, be deemed to be depreciation which has been allowed in respect of that unit in the assessment in which the reduction was made.

(3) The consideration receivable in respect of the disposal, loss or destruction means -

(a) in the case of a sale of the property - the sale price less the expenses of the sale of the property;

(b) in the case of loss or destruction of the property - the amount or value received or receivable under a policy of insurance or otherwise in respect of the loss or destruction;

(c) in the case where the property is sold with other assets and no separate value is allocated to the property - the amount determined by the Commissioner;

(d) in the case where property is disposed of otherwise than by sale - the value, if any, of the property at the date of disposal."

Section 62 which is part of the same Division in the Act as the sections other than s.26AAA quoted, reads -

"(1) In this Division, "depreciated value" of any unit of property at any time means the cost of the unit to the person who owns or owned the property at that time less the total amount of depreciation (if any) allowed or allowable in respect of that unit in assessments of the income of that person, for any period prior to that time, under this Act or any previous laws of the Commonwealth.

(2) For the purposes of the last preceding sub-section, in any case in which section 60, or the corresponding provision of the previous Act, applied or applies in relation to any unit of property, the person who acquired or acquires the unit shall be deemed to have acquired or to acquire it at a cost equal to the depreciated value of the unit immediately prior to the time of the acquisition, or, if the case is one in which the proviso to sub-section (1) of that section applied or applies, the sum of that depreciated value and the amount required to be added to that depreciated value for the purposes of that proviso."

In the Supreme Court, the learned Judge found, for reasons which he has set out, that there was a sale of but one property part of which was the fencing; that s.54 could therefore be said to apply in that the fencing was "plant" as defined in s.54(2) in respect of which, according to that section, depreciation was an allowable deduction. Further, that there had been a disposal of the property in respect of which depreciation had been allowed and that the excess to the extent of the sum of the amounts allowed and allowable in the assessment for tax were included in the assessable income for the relevant year, since the consideration exceeded, in respect of the fence, the depreciated value. Accordingly, he found that s.59 also was applicable in relation to the property in terms of s.26AAA(5)(b). Since, on that finding sub-section (5) applied to the respondent's case then sub-section (2) did not apply in the relation to the sale by the taxpayer and, accordingly, the respondent was not liable to tax in respect of the profit. He made no finding as to the applicability of sub-section (5)(a) other than to say that in respect of it he did not express disagreement as to the finding of the Board of Review.

Relying on sub-section (2), the Commissioner contends that assessable income of the respondent includes the profit on the sale, which, pursuant to an amendment of the assessment presented before the Board of Review, is claimed to be $1,924.00, i.e. the respondent's share of the profit on the sale. The Commissioner's contention is that the reference to "property" in sub-section (2) is to be read in the light of or as bearing the meaning set out in sub-section (1)(a), i.e. that what was bought and sold was an estate in fee simple; that thus read the profit is assessable income of the taxpayer arising from the sale of that estate. As to the subject of a contract for the sale of land being prima facie a contract for the sale of the fee simple, senior Counsel referred to Cane v. Sinclair 10 V.L.R. 60 at pp.64 and 65 (Cane) where the judgments relied on Hughes v. Parker (151) E.R. 1028) [1841] EngR 563; 8 M. & W. 244. It is possible that in that decision Lord Abinger or Baron Alderson at pp.247 and 248 respectively (ibid) were not stating a rule in such general terms as set out in Cane. See also the note on Hughes v. Parker in "The Law of Vendor and Purchaser" Stonham at p.125 ("a case related to pleadings"). In "Sugden on Vendors & Purchasers" (13th Edit.) p.247 it is cited for the proposition that "A GENERAL agreement to sell a property means a sale in fee simple, and the Court will not infer that a term of years only is sold on account of the smallness of the price."

The respondent's senior Counsel argued that what was sold was a pig farm (as the property had become) which was included in the assets of a business, i.e. of pig farming carried on by the respondent; that, as a result of the sale, an amount of $7.00 became, and was, included in the assessable income of the respondent under a provision of the Act other than s.26AAA viz. under s.59(2); that thus the exception to sub-section (2) available in sub-section (5)(a) was attracted.

Further, as to sub-section (5)(b), he submitted that s.54 applied to the fences and since they were part of the land, s.54 could there be said to apply "in relation to the (pig farm) property"; that as a result of the sale s.59 also applied in relation to the property. Thus the sub-section (5)(b) exception is fulfilled also.

It has not been argued by either party that what was sold was different from that which was purchased. It is inappropriate to categorise the nature of the "property" with which sub-section (2) of s.26AAA treats without reference to sub-section (1). In doing so I assume that the object of the Act in s.26AAA was to bring to tax, profits made by sale within twelve months of purchase of the property then sold, relieving the revenue authority from having to prove the existence, e.g. of a business-like operation (of buying and selling) or a "purpose" as in s.26(a) of the Act yet without depriving the taxpayer of his statutory right to claim depreciation otherwise.

It is, of course, fundamental to the interpretation of s.26AAA(2) to decide what is meant by "property", accepting that the meaning must accommodate to the transaction, i.e. to decide what property has been purchased and later sold; and presumably the property must be the same, at least for the reason that there being a profit can only eventuate if there is an "identity" between what is purchased and what is sold. (cf. Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. McClelland [1969] HCA 72; (1967) 118 C.L.R. 353 at p.359: A.L. Hamblin Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1974] HCA 44; (1974) 131 C.L.R. 570 at p.575 per Barwick C.J., at p.581 per Mason J.)

That which was purchased and sold, in my view, was an estate in the Brassal land (sub-para. (a) of sub-section (1) of s.26AAA) (in fee simple); or possibly, the Brassal "land" itself, (sub-section (1)(g)). It is not suggested that any business was sold. If it is of assistance to say so I suggest a layman would say of the transaction that the respondent had sold his land; or even, if given to grandiloquence, had disposed of his estate; but he would hardly say that the respondent had sold his land and fences; or that he had sold his fences. They are not commonly regarded as separate items of property. No part of s.26AAA indicates that the legislature was envisaging a sale of fences. It is inapposite, I suggest, to refer to fences (physical objects) as being part of an estate, a legal term meaning -

". . . . . varieties or modes of ownership, especially in land"

See "A Dictionary of English Law" (Jowitt) at p.735.

For the purpose of the Act, the fences and other plant appearing on the Depreciation Schedule are to be considered as separate from the estate or the land. That Schedule treats the various units of property as other than the estate or land. The feasibility of such a separation is envisaged in the view of Menzies J. (dissenting) in Ferling v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1966] HCA 85; (1965) 115 C.L.R. 603 (Ferling) at pp.635-636. In the same case, Taylor J. (pp.630-631) thought there was much to be said for the view that where in an agreement there was no part of consideration separately receivable for improvements which could not be and therefore were not made, independently, the subject of a sale, s.59 did not apply. He seems to have been influenced to that point of view by the words "in this section" in sub-section (2) of s.54 and the significance to be attributed to "property" in s.59. See also per Owen J. (ibid) at p.649. In that regard respondent's senior Counsel referred to the decision in Henty House Pty. Ltd. (in voluntary liquidation) v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1953] HCA 54; (1953) 88 C.L.R. 141 where he said it had been accepted that depreciation provisions apply even where there is no separate sale. Accepting that as correct, the statement does not assist to identify the "property" for s.26AAA purposes. However, both decisions were before the introduction of s.26AAA and when there was no definition of "property" in the Act. In one sense it might be said to be "highly artificial" (see per Windeyer J. in Ferling p.608) to attribute a value to "plant" when that plant is a fixture on land or may be said to have become part of it.

But s.26AAA(5) while envisaging that s.59 may apply, does not, I suggest, thereby widen "property" so that it has a meaning beyond that which may have been attributed to it following an evaluation of the transaction when considering the applicability of sub-section (2) of s.26AAA. Nor is there to be allowed any depreciation of or in relation to an estate in land. Whether because of its intrinsic character, so it was argued, it is incapable of depreciation, in at least an accountancy sense, does not matter; it is not "plant or articles" within the meaning of s.54 though there is mention there of improvements on land. Further, land would hardly be susceptible to the estimate referred to in s.55. For the purposes of this discussion one assumes that the estate or land was included in the assets of a business; cf. "Lindley on Partnership" 14th Edit. p.455 sub.nom. "Land Acquired for Purpose of Trade". I proceed to consider the possible applicability of the paragraphs of sub-section (5) of s.26AAA.

S.26AAA(5)(a):

The sale referred to here is the sale of the same property as is (said to be) included in the assets of a business. I do not agree that as a result of the sale of the estate or land there will be an amount included in the assessable income of the respondent, i.e. other than under s.26AAA. When para.(a) of sub-section (5) refers to "the sale" it is of "the property" (estate in or land); it was not as a result of that sale that an amount could be included in the assessable income; but by reason of the disposal of fences and the attribution in the Income Tax return of a higher value to them.

In my opinion the exception to sub-section (2) of s.26AAA provided by paragraph (a) of sub-section (5) does not apply here.

S.26AAA (5)(b):

Senior Counsel for the Commissioner also contended that paragraph (b) of sub-section (5) of s.26AAA did not so apply as to avoid the operation of sub-section (2). He submitted, inter alia, that for sub-section (5)(b) to apply, both ss.54 and 59 of the Act would have to apply "in relation to the property" and not only to what he said had become part of the property, i.e. the fences. Further, he argued, paragraph (b) of sub-section (5) could not apply to a sale of an estate or land because s.54 did not apply to these concepts; that the "property" referred to in sub-section (5) was the same as is mentioned in sub-section (2). Accordingly, he said that the property being an estate or land could never be the subject of any depreciation; so s.54 could not apply and it mattered not then that s.59 did or did not apply.

In my opinion, s.54 does not apply in relation to the estate or land though it does apply in relation to fences. It cannot be said, I consider, that if s.54 applies to a depreciable asset (fences) on land it applies thereby "in relation to the land". Indeed, s.54(1) of the Act, if extended by including "fences" as the relevant "plant" would read -

"Depreciation during the year . . . . of any fences . . . . on land which is used . . . ."

i.e. specifically distinguishing fences from land. Their depreciation is unaffected by the quality, the situation or the area of the land. Section 59 is designed to protect the revenue as well as the taxpayer on disposal of depreciable assets and does not, I consider, apply in relation to the disposal of an estate or land merely because on it are to be found depreciable assets. This would appear to be in harmony with the references made by Taylor J. referred to earlier.

I consider that paragraph (b) of sub-section (5) of s.26AAA does not apply here.

In respect of both paragraphs (a) and (b) of sub-section (2) of s.26AAA the interpretation contended for by the appellant would, in my view, avoid an "improbability of result" which is referred to in Cooper Brookes (Wollongong) Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 26; 35 A.L.R. 151 at p.169. Such an improbability would, in my opinion, confirm the interpretation of the provisions which I have accepted.

Accordingly, I consider s.26AAA(2) applies to the sale and that there is not the exception thereto referred to in sub-section (5) of s.26AAA. I would uphold the appeal. Having regard to the decision of the majority, it is not necessary to set out the terms of the Order I would propose.

This appeal from the Supreme Court of Queensland concerns s.26AAA (5) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act").

By agreement dated 26 June 1975 Gary Francis Mullins ("the taxpayer") and his wife agreed to purchase about eight acres of land five miles from Ipswich, at Brassall, for $6,500.00. They sold it by agreement dated 23 April 1976 for $11,300.00. The Commissioner, relying upon s.26AAA (2), assessed the taxpayer for the year ended 30 June 1976 on his share of the profit arising from the sale of the property namely, $1,924.00.

The taxpayer appealed to a Board of Review. His appeal was heard by Taxation Board of Review No. 1 which allowed it. The Commissioner appealed to the Supreme Court which dismissed the appeal. The Commissioner appeals by leave to this Court from the judgment of the Supreme Court.

The taxpayer contended both before the Board of Review and the Supreme Court that sub-s. (5) of s. 26 AAA applies so that his share of the profit arising from the sale of the property is not brought to tax.

Section 26 AAA (2) and (5) provides so far as material as follows:-

"(2) Where -

(a) a taxpayer . . . purchases property after the commencement of this section; and

(b) the taxpayer has, . . . sold the property or an interest in the property before the expiration of the period of 12 months from the date on which he purchased the property,

then, subject to this section, the assessable income of the taxpayer includes any profit arising from the sale of the property or interest.

. . .

(5) Sub-section (2) does not apply in relation to a sale by a taxpayer of property if -

(a) the property was included in the assets of a business carried on by the taxpayer and, as a result of the sale, an amount will be included in the assessable income of the taxpayer of the year of income under a provision of this Act other than this section;

(b) section 54 applied in relation to the property and, as a result of the sale, section 59 applies in relation to the property; or

(c) . . ."

Section 54 (1) provides so far as relevant:-

"54 (1) Depreciation during the year of income of any property, being plant, or articles owned by a taxpayer and used by him during that year for the purpose of producing assessable income, and of any property being plant or articles owned by the taxpayer which has been installed ready for use for that purpose and is during that year held in reserve by him shall, subject to this Act, be an allowable deduction.

(2) In this section, 'plant' includes -

. . .

(b) fences . . . on land which is used for the purpose of agricultural or pastoral pursuits . . ."

Section 59 provides so far as relevant:-

"(1) Where any property of a taxpayer, in respect of which depreciation has been allowed or is allowable under this or the previous Act, is disposed of, lost or destroyed at any time in the year of income, the depreciated value of the property at that time, less the amount of any consideration receivable in respect of the disposal, loss or destruction, shall be an allowable deduction.

(2) If that consideration exceeds that depreciated value, the excess, to the extent of the sum of the amounts allowed and allowable in assessments for income tax under this Act and any previous law of the Commonwealth in respect of depreciation, shall, subject to the succeeding provisions of this section, be included in his assessable income of that year."

Each member of the Board of Review concluded that paragraph (a) of s. 26AAA (5) applied. Two members of of the Board (Mr. Hogan and Dr. Gerber) concluded that paragraph (b) applied also. The Supreme Court held that paragraph (b) applied; but did not decide whether paragraph (a) applied.

Before this Court the taxpayer relied on both paragraphs (a) and (b).

The Board of Review and the Supreme Court held that at all material times the taxpayer carried on the business of pig farming on the land in question. Leave to appeal to this Court was granted subject to certain conditions one of which, imposed during the course of the hearing of the appeal, was that this finding remains undisturbed.

The facts bearing on the questions for our decision are not in dispute and may be briefly stated. The taxpayer and his wife purchased the land with the intention of running pigs and growing small crops. The taxpayer ploughed six acres of land with the intention of planting it to oats to produce food for the pigs. There was no house on the land; but the taxpayer intended to build a house on it. The taxpayer and his wife lived in Ipswich where he was an employee of the Ipswich City Council. At the time of the purchase of the land there was some fencing on it; but it had been badly damaged by floods in January 1974. When the land was purchased the taxpayer estimated the value of the fencing on the basis of forty chains at $5.00 per chain namely $200.00. He repaired the fences. He erected a small pig sty which consisted of a concrete slab of cement surrounded by weld mesh. He purchased an irrigation pump and ancillary equipment with the intention of irrigating for oats. He also intended to grow vegetables. He purchased a small second-hand tractor. He acquired three sows and produced a total of nineteen piglets, of which six died and four were sold.

In his return of income for the year ended 30 June 1976 the taxpayer apportioned the purchase price of the land as to $6,300.00 for the unimproved value of the land and $200.00 for fencing. He also apportioned the sale price of the land as to $10,900.00 for the unimproved value of the land and $400.00 for fencing. The Commissioner accepted this apportionment.

It is common ground that the fences on the land answer the description, for the purposes of s. 54, of property being plant owned by the taxpayer and his wife and used by them during the year of income ended 30 June 1976 for the purpose of producing assessable income and that the depreciation of the fences is an allowable deduction. It is also common ground, for the purposes of s. 59, that the fences were the property of the taxpayer and his wife in respect of which depreciation had been allowed or was allowable under the Act; that they were disposed of during the year of income ended 30 June 1976; and that the consideration receivable in respect of the disposal exceeded the depreciated value of the fences in that the excess, to the extent of the sum of the amounts allowed and allowable in assessments of income tax under the Act, were included in the assessable income of the taxpayer that year.

I turn first to the question whether paragraph (a) of sub-s. (5) of s. 26 AAA applies.

The taxpayer submitted that "the property" was the land (or the taxpayer's interest therein namely, an undivided one-half share for an estate in fee simple); that it was included in the assets of a business carried on by him and his wife namely, the business of pig farming; and that as a result of the sale of the land, which necessarily included the fences as fixtures, an amount was included in his assessable income for the relevant year of income under a provision of the Act other than s. 26 AAA namely, the amount included in respect of the fences under s. 59 (2).

The Commission submitted that paragraph (a) did not apply to this case for two reasons: first, because the land was not included in the assets of the business of pig farming carried on by the taxpayer and his wife; and second, because paragraph (a) should be construed as referring to non-depreciable property as paragraph (b) expressly relates to depreciable property.

The Commissioner does not dispute that if he fails in both these submissions paragraph (a) must apply.

I turn first to the question whether the land was included in the assets of the pig farming business carried on by the taxpayer and his wife.

The phrase "assets of a business" appears also in s. 36 (1) (b) which includes in the assessable income of a taxpayer the value of property being trading stock which the taxpayer disposes of and which "constitutes or constituted the whole or part of the assets of a business which is or was carried on by the taxpayer: . . .".

In C. of T. v. St. Hubert's Island Pty. Limited (In Liquidation) (1978) 138 C.L.R. 210 which concerned s. 36 (1), Jacobs J. said at p. 234:-

"I cannot separate the question whether the land was trading stock of the taxpayer from the question whether it was an asset of a business carried on by the taxpayer, as I shall attempt to explain.

The description 'assets of a business' is not satisfied simply by examining as disparate questions whether the taxpayer carries on any kind of business and then whether the property is to be regarded as an asset of that business carried on by the taxpayer whatever it may be. In order to satisfy the requirement of s. 36 (1) (b) the property must bear the quality of its description in s. 36 (1) (a) in the business which must be found to be or to have been carried on by the taxpayer. For instance, a man may be a shopkeeper and out of the accumulated profits retained in the business he may purchase an area of land with the purpose of selling it at a profit at some time in the future. In one sense the land is an asset of the business, but not, I think, in the sense of s. 36 (1). There must be a relationship between the property and the business whereby it can be said that the property bears the description of one or another of the kinds enumerated, not in a general sense, but in specific relation to the business which was or is carried on. Thus property, being trading stock, must be an asset of a business of trading in that stock."

See also F.C.T. v. Westraders Pty. Limited [1979] FCA 13; (1979) 38 F.L.R. 306; a decision of the Full Court of this Court; affirmed by the High Court [1980] HCA 24; (1980) 54 A.L.J.R. 460.

Although the words "included in" the assets of a business in one sense suggest some act of appropriation or annexation of property to a business carried on by the taxpayer; I think they have substantially the same meaning as "comprised in" or "contained in" or "part of".

To determine whether "the property was included in the assets of a business carried on by the taxpayer" it is necessary to look at the business in the sense of the undertaking or enterprise and see what assets are in fact employed therein. The assets must be inseparably connected with the business; or specifically related to the business which is carried on - see Jacobs J. in St. Hubert's Island (supra).

There are reported cases which examine the meaning of "business" but in different contexts. The question what is the content of the gift by will of a testator's business has been considered in some cases. They show, for instance, that in more recent years, due doubtless to the increasing complexity and sophistication of business, courts are inclined to take a much broader view than was formerly taken of what is comprised in the gift by will of a testator's business and in some circumstances to include, in the gift of a business, the business premises themselves: see In re Rhagg (dec'd); Easten v. Boyd 1938 1 Ch. 828; In Re White (deceased); McCann v. Hull 1958 1 Ch. 762; and re Price (deceased); Union Trustee Co. of Australia Ltd. v. McCorrie, Whitpaine and Salvado (No. 1), 1941 Q.S.R. 192.

In A Home Away Pty. Limited v. Commissioner for Corporate Affairs (1980) C.L.C. 34,444, a decision of the Full Court of the Supreme Court of Victoria, Jenkinson J., with whom McInerney J. agreed, held that a broad view should be taken of what is meant by the expression "assets . . . of any . . . business undertaking or scheme" appearing in the definition of "interest" in s. 76 (1) of the Companies Act 1961 (Vic.).

Although these decisions concern different questions to those involved in this case I have found them helpful in resolving the questions here.

There is, of course, a real distinction between the assets of a business carried on by a man and assets on which he carries on a business. The distinction is well illustrated in the case of land. Two people may carry on a farming business on land owned by only one of them or by a third person, and leased to them. The land is not an asset of the business (although in some cases the lease may be); but it is used in the business. Where, as here, the same people both own the land and carry on business upon it, it is easier to conclude that the land is included in the assets of their business. Yet the distinction must not be blurred. A man may own land and use it to carry on his business; but it does not necessarily follow that the land is an asset of his business. In many cases it will be, especially if the land is used by him solely for the purpose of carrying on the business. But what if only part of the land is used in the business? Take the case of a man who owns one hundred acres of land only ten of which are used to carry on the business of extracting sand and gravel, the balance being a natural wilderness which he visits periodically for pleasure. If the land is bought and sold in one undivided parcel, there may be difficulties in bring it, or the ten acre part thereof, within the framework of sub-s. (5). This is but one example which illustrates the difficulty that arises in applying the sub-section. The difficulties must be resolved by practical considerations in each particular case as it arises.

In my opinion, the land was not only used by the taxpayer and his wife to carry on their business of pig farming; but was also included in the assets of that business. It was put to no use except pig farming except that the taxpayer dumped fill on the land and levelled part of it as a site for a house which he intended to build and occupy. That does not deprive the land of its character as an asset of the business of pig farming.

In the taxpayer's income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1976, which was prepared by his accountant and signed by the taxpayer on 23 February 1977, there appears a page titled:-

"GARY F. AND SUSAN J. MULLINS

STATEMENT OF JOINT VENTURE INCOME FOR YEAR ENDED 30TH JUNE, 1976"

It shows as an expense of the joint venture:

"Rates - City of Ipswich $111

Legal Expenses re purchase 50".

It is not disputed that the legal expenses concern the purchase of the

subject land. This provides some indication that the taxpayer regarded the land as part of the joint venture with his wife and tends to confirm that the land was an asset of the business.

I turn to the next question that arises under paragraph (a) namely, whether because s. 26 AAA (5) (b) expressly relates to depreciated property, paragraph (a) should be construed so as to refer to the non-depreciable assets of a business.

The Commissioner submitted that, if paragraph (b) had not been enacted, the whole of its subject matter namely, depreciable property, would fall within paragraph (a); so that paragraph (b) must be read as an exhaustive statement in relation to depreciable property. Otherwise it has no work to do - it is an unnecessary provision.

I do not accept this submission. Assuming for the moment the accuracy of the Commissioner's base premise (that without paragraph (b) all its subject matter would be included in paragraph (a) paragraph (b) might equally be said to have been inserted for more abundant caution and to allay the fears of taxpayers who are apprehensive that profit arising from the sale of depreciated property may be included in their assessable income under s. 26 AAA (2). See Commissioners for Special Purposes of Income Tax v. Pemsel 1981 A. C. 531 especially per Lord Herschell at p. 574.

But the base premise is erroneous. Section 54 provides that depreciation in respect of plant or articles owned by a taxpayer and used by him during the year of income for the purpose of producing assessable income or installed ready for use for that purpose and held in reserve shall be an allowable deduction. Sub-section (2) of s. 54 gives an extended meaning to the word "plant" so as to include, for example, fences.

Section 59 contains provisions for the balancing adjustment to be made when property in respect of which depreciation has been allowed or is allowable is disposed of lost or destroyed. Where the consideration receivable is less than the depreciated value of the property, the deficiency is an allowable deduction. Where the consideration receivable exceeds the depreciated value of the property, the excess is assessable to the extent of the amount of depreciation allowed or allowable.

In other words, where on the disposal, loss or destruction of depreciable property the depreciation allowed is inadequate, a further deduction is allowable; but where the amount of the consideration receivable shows that the depreciation allowed is greater than the depreciation in fact incurred, the amount of the excess must be accounted for by inclusion in the assessable income.

Thus s. 59 may apply either to allow a further deduction (s. 59 (1)) or to include an amount in the taxpayer's assessable income (s. 59 (2)). The latter would fall within s. 26 AAA (5) (a), the former would not. Hence s. 26 AAA (5) (b) does have an additional operation to paragraph (a).

I have had the advantage of reading the reasons for judgment of Deane J. who discusses the question whether the small amount representing recoupment of allowable depreciation in respect of fences on the land which the Commissioner concedes should be included in the taxpayer's assessable income pursuant to s. 59 (2), can properly be regarded, for the purposes of paragraph (a), as being so included "as a result" of the sale by the taxpayer and his wife of their estate in the land. I agree with what Deane J. says about this question.

I therefore reject the Commissioner's submissions.

Paragraph (a) applies to this case so that the taxpayer's share of the profit arising from the sale of the land should not be included in his assessable income.

It is therefore unnecessary for me to consider the submissions of the parties relating to paragraph (b) itself.

I would dismiss the appeal.
