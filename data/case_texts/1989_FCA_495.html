Re Graeme Oriel Morris v Commissioner of Taxation [1989] FCA 495; 89 Atc 5303 25 FCR 556 (6 December 1989)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: GRAEME ORIEL MORRIS

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Nos. G116-119 of 1988

FED No. 742

Income Tax

89 ATC 5303

25 FCR 556

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

QUEENSLAND DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Hartigan J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - income of honorary consul paid during term of appointment - Consul citizen of Australia - Whether honorary consul's income exempt from taxation.

Consular Privileges And Immunities Act 1972-1973 (C'wth):- s.5

The Vienna Convention On Consular Relations:- Articles 1, 66 and 71

The Queen ex parte Saunders v. The Postmaster General (1878) 3 QBD 428

Shipping Corporation of India Ltd. v. Gambers Chemical Co. A/Asia Ltd. [1980] HCA 51; (1980) 147 CLR 142

Daniel v. Daniel [1906] HCA 74; (1906) 4 CLR 563

Ford v. Ford [1947] HCA 7; (1947) 73 CLR 524

Viveash v. Becker (1814) 2 Mes 285[1814] EngR 715; , 105 ER 619

HEARING

BRISBANE

6:12:1989

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr. J. Sheahan

Solicitors for the Applicant: Messrs. Feez Ruthning

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr. J. McGill

Solicitors for the Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

1. The appeal be dismissed.

2. The applicant pay the respondent's costs of this

appeal including all reserved costs.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

The applicant appeals against the decision of the respondent disallowing the applicant's objections to assessments of income tax. The appeals relate to each of the years of income ended 30 June 1977, 30 June 1978, 30 June 1979 and 30 June 1980. The issue in each appeal is the same. The issue is whether the applicant is by virtue of Article 66 of the Vienna Convention of Consular Relations (the Convention) exempt from taxation in relation to part of his income. Certain articles of the Convention are given the effect of law in Australia by section 5 of the Consular Privileges and Immunities Act 1972.

2. The applicant in this matter is the Honorary Consul of the Netherlands at Brisbane appointed by royal decree of Her Majesty the Queen of the Netherlands dated 17th August, 1979. The applicant's appointment as Honorary Consul is recognised by the Commonwealth of Australia by means of an exequatur issued by His Excellency The Governor General of the Commonwealth of Australia.

3. By a letter dated September 30, 1976 (exhibit 2) The Consulate General of the Netherlands by its then Consul-General, Mr. C.S. van Straten, informed the applicant that, inter alia:

"The Chancery-fees charged are to be kept by

you from the date of the Royal Decree by which

you were appointed i.e. 17th August 1976, to the

legal maximum of Hfl.24,000.= per calendar-year

(equivalent of approximately Au$ 7,000.=)."

The letter went on to request that each quarter the applicant submit to the Consul-General a "simplified account" of receipts of Chancery fees and expenses. Receipts and expenses were to be entered in a "simple Register".

4. According to the evidence of the applicant,which I accept,the Consulate at Brisbane was staffed usually by persons engaged in the consular service of the Department of Foreign Affairs of the Netherlands or a locally engaged clerical assistant of Netherlands citizenship. The former are referred to as "career staff".

5. These career and clerical consulate staff performed duties on behalf of the Consulate which necessitated the collection of fees for the services performed, known as the "chancery-fees" referred to in the letter exhibit 2. It was the applicant's evidence that the Consulate staff accumulated the fees and at the end of each month, or thereabouts, would deliver the accumulated amount to the applicant. The applicant tendered a bundle of receipts, admitted as exhibit 1, evidencing these payments to himself.

6. It was then a matter for the applicant as to what he did with these amounts delivered to him. There is no evidence that the sums received by the applicant in the income years in question exceeded the Australian dollar equivalent of the maximum amount allowed per annum to the applicant as specified in the letter exhibit 2. However, I presume that any sums which would have taken the total amount above the equivalent of 24,000 gilders would have been refused or at least replaced in the Consulate's coffers and therefore would not form any part of the applicants income for that year.

7. Whilst the terms of the applicant's appointment as Consul provided for these payments continuing during his period of appointment, the appointment carried with it no specific obligations in return for the reward of up to 24000 gilders per annum. It is the applicant's evidence that he performed any duties as Consul as were required of him from time to time by his superiors. The applicant formally represented the government of the Netherlands in Queensland, attended official functions as Consul and maintained the records of the Consul, amongst other things. Further the applicant provided the consulate with office space without asking for or receiving rent. It could not be said that the amount received by the applicant in a year in any way reflected the number of duties performed by him as consul, the continued appointment of the applicant as consul was sufficient to generate the continuing payments to the specified level.

8. As a consequence of the applicant's continued appointment as Consul he received $4,062.00 in the income year 1977, $4,309.00 in the income year 1978, $6,919.00 in the income year 1979 and $7,423.00 in the income year 1980.

9. In each of the years of income mentioned the respondent assessed the amounts as income and it is from these assessments that this appeal arises.

10. It is the applicant's ultimate submission that, by virtue of section 5 of the Consular Privileges and Immunities Act 1972-1973 ( "the Act" ) giving the force of law in Australia and its external territories to, inter alia, Article 66 of The Vienna Convention on Consular Relations ( "the Convention" ), those assessments are wrong and the amounts are exempt from taxation.

11. Section 5(1) of the Act provides that:

"Subject to this section, the provisions of

Articles 1,5,15 and 17, paragraphs 1,2, and 4

of Article 31, Articles 32,33,35 and 39,

paragraphs 1 and 2 of Article 41, Articles 43

to 45 (inclusive) and 48 to 54 (inclusive),

paragraphs 2 and 3 of Article 55, paragraph

2 of Article 57, paragraphs 1,2 and 3 of

Article 58, Articles 60 to 62 (inclusive), 66

and 67, paragraphs 1,2 and 4 of Article 70,

and Article 71 of the Convention have the

force of law in Australia and in every

external Territory."

12. Article 66 of The Convention provides that:

"An honorary consular officer shall be exempt

from all laws and taxes on the remuneration

and emoluments which he received from the

sending State in respect of the exercise of

consular functions."

13. Mr. Sheahan, for the applicant, submitted that whilst there was no definition of the term "honorary consular officer" in the Convention nor, I shall interpolate, is one to be found in the Act, it was not in dispute that the applicant fell within this category of persons. Mr. McGill, for the respondent, did not dispute the applicant's description of himself as an honorary consular officer. The author of Lewis, State and Diplomatic Immunity, Second Edition, 1985 at p 197, suggests that" (as) an honorary consular officer may be paid for his work (this is clear from Article 66) the test is probably whether he is full-time employed or not and whether it is a post normally held for the purpose of prestige." I find that extract helpful. I find that the applicant is a "consular officer" for the purposes of the Convention as defined in sub-paragraph 1(b) of Article 1 of the Convention. I accept that the applicant falls within the definition of honorary consular officer for the purposes of the Act and Convention.

14. I accept the evidence of the applicant.

15. The amounts which the applicant received in the income years in question were chancery fees received at the Netherlands Consulate at Brisbane for consular services at that Consulate.I also accept his evidence that the accounts were handed over to him pursuant to the term of his appointment relating to that. I have already referred to that term. I accept the evidence of the applicant that chancery fees were usually paid in cash or by money order for services rendered at the Consulate by the applicant or by members of the career staff or by the locally engaged clerical assistant.

16. I accept the applicant's evidence that the fees were paid in relation to such matters as the issuance of visas, new passports, certificates of continued existence for pension purposes, preparing or noting powers of attorney and various other matters appropriate to the activities of a consular post. The amounts received were accumulated each month and delivered by the career staff to the applicant. The applicant signed and delivered to the career staff receipts for the amounts received, exhibit 1. The receipts show amounts received from the Netherlands Consulate.

17. I find on the evidence before me that the amounts in question in each of the income years under consideration were amounts which meet the requirements of Article 66 of the Convention. The amounts are, at the least, emoluments. (See The Queen ex parte Saunders v. The Postermaster-General (1878) 3 QBD 428 at 430 and 431). I also find on the evidence before me that, for the purposes of the Convention, the amounts were received by the applicant for carrying out the duties of his consular office. They were charges for services rendered at the Consulate by various persons. They were not services performed in all areas by the applicant personally. I consider that on the material before me the applicant was entitled to keep, by way of remuneration or emolument, that part of the moneys received by the Cousulate in respect of consulate services, which did not exceed Hfl.24,000. The applicant was required to account for the charges in excess of Hfl.24,000 to the sending State. I consider that in retaining moneys received at the consulate and then handed over to him by the career officers the applicant received fees or emoluments from the sending State. I do not consider that the fee or emolument must have been sent by the Netherlands's government from the Netherelands. On the facts of this case I find that the funds were received at the Consulate on behalf of the Netherlands government and the applicant under the terms of his appointment was required to account to the Consul-General of the Netherlands for the amounts so received less a deduction for fees or emolument up to a maximum of Hfl.24,000 per annum.

18. Mr. McGill submitted that Article 1(3) of the Convention applied to honorary consular officers and therefore Article 71 is the provision dealing with the status of honorary consular officers who are nationals or permanent residents of the receiving State.

19. Article 1(3) provides that:

"The particular status of members of the

consular posts who are nationals or permanent

residents of the receiving state is governed

by Article 71 of the present Convention."

20. I find that the applicant does fall within the definition of a member of the consular post as provided for in Article 1(1g) having regard to the definition of consular officer in Article 1(1d). It was not disputed by the parties and, on the evidence before me, I find that the applicant is a national of the receiving State for the purposes of the Convention, he being an Australian citizen (cf.subsection 5(2)(b) of the Act).

21. Article 71 of The Convention provides, in part:

"1. Except in so far as additional

facilities, privileges and immunities may be

granted by the receiving State, consular

officers who are nationals of or permanently

resident in the receiving State shall enjoy

only immunity from jurisdiction and personal

inviolability in respect of official acts

performed in the exercise of their functions,

and the privileges provided in paragraph 3 of

Article 44. So far as these consular officers

are concerned, the receiving State shall

likewise be bound by the obligation laid down

in Article 42 ..."

22. Mr. Sheahan submitted that the words of Article 71 in their ordinary or natural meaning do not, as was Mr. McGill's submission, mean that consular officers who are nationals or permanent residents of the receiving State have no rights and immunities other than those specifically referred to in Article 71. It was Mr. Sheahan's submission that the effect of Article 71 is to limit the rights that would otherwise be conferred by Articles 41, 43 and 44 of The Convention. These Articles deal with personal inviolability of consular officers, immunity from jurisdiction and liability to give evidence.

23. In approaching the interpretation of this Convention, and, in particular, Article 71, I have adopted the reasoning of Mason and Wilson JJ. in Shipping Corporation of India Ltd. v.Gambers Chemical Co. A/Asia Pty.Ltd. [1980] HCA 51; (1980) 147 CLR 142 at 159 where Their Honours said:

"It has been recognized that a national court,

in the interests of uniformity, should

construe rules formulated by an international

convention, especially rules formulated for

the purpose of governing international

transactions such as the carriage of goods by

sea,"in a normal manner, appropriate for the

interpretation of an international convention,

unconstrained by technical rules of English

law, or by English legal precedent, but on

broad principles of general acceptation", to

repeat the words of Lord Wilberforce in James

Buchanan & Co. Ltd. v. Babco Forwarding &

Shipping (U.K.) Ltd. (90); see also Stag Line

Ltd. v. Foscolo, Mango & Co. Ltd. (91).

It is important that we should adhere to

this approach when we are interpreting rules

which have been formulated for the purpose of

regulating the rights and liabilities of

parties to international mercantile

transactions where great store is set upon

certainty and uniformity of application.

To say this is not to assert that we

should exclude from our consideration of the

rules settled by an international convention

the meaning which has been consistently

assigned by a national court to words and

expressions commonly used in the documentation

by which international trade is transacted,

when the convention, in seeking to regulate

the rights and liabilities of parties to

international trading transactions, uses those

words and expressions."

24. Whilst this is not a situation involving the interpretation of rules relating to international trade, this is the preferable approach to adopt. Just as there is a need for uniformity in the interpretation of such rules there exists also sound reasons to adopt uniform practices when construing rules relating to the consular corps. One device to aid in uniformity must be to act upon general principles. Accordingly, in adopting this approach, I make the following observations.

25. Article 71 cannot be read on its own but must be read in the context of the convention as a whole. Article 71 is, by virtue of paragraph 3 of Article 1, the governing provision in relation to the particular status of members of consular posts who are nationals or permanent residents of the receiving State. Article 71 goes on to provide for the conferral of immunity and privilege. It is on its face clearly and unambiguously worded such that consular officers who are Australian citizens or permanently resident in Australia may enjoy only (that is, they may enjoy no other) immunity and privilege other than those stated in that article or otherwise granted. In this regard Article 71 does operate as a code in respect of the particular status of those consular officers.

26. That Article 71 does operate as such a code flows from the words of paragraph 3 of Article 1 of the Convention and from the words of article 71. The opening words of paragraph 1 of Article 71 indicate to me that the paragraph is of more general application than would be the case if I accepted the submissions of Mr. Sheahan. The words to which I refer are "Except in so far as additional facilities privileges and immunities may be granted by the receiving State....".

27. The word 'status' in paragraph 3 Article 1 is not defined by the Convention or by the Act and I can see no reason why it should be accorded any other definition than would normally be placed upon the word in common parlance. As I have said, this is the preferable approach to adopt in order to aid uniformity in interpretation of provisions of this sort. The High Court has held in relation to the word 'status' as it appears in The Matrimonial Causes Act 1899 (repealed) that:

"Without pretending to give an exhaustive

definition, I apprehend that the term status

means of this sort: a condition attached by

law to a person which confers or affects or

limits a legal capacity of exercising some

power that under other circumstances he would

not or could not exercise without

restriction." Daniel v. Daniel (1906) 4 CLR

563 per Griffith C.J. at p 566, followed by

Ford v. Ford [1947] HCA 7; (1947) 73 CLR 524.

28. Bearing in mind that Griffith C.J. did not intend the definition to be exhaustive and in adopting it in these circumstances, paragraph 3 Article 1 would have the effect that the particular condition attached by law to members of consular posts who are nationals or permanent residents of the receiving state which confer, affect or limit their legal capacity of exercising their powers as members of consular posts is governed by Article 71.

29. The Shorter Oxford Dictionary defines status as:

"The legal standing or position of a person as

determined by his membership of some class of

persons legally enjoying certain rights

subject to certain limitations....."

30. Applying this definition to paragraph 3 of Article 1 results in precisely the same consequence as does Griffith C.J.'s interpretation. I adopt the former and see no reason why such a definition would not include the latter.

31. By placing the word 'particular' alongside 'status', it is clear that in so far as members of that class of persons is concerned, their status is peculiar to that class and consequently exclusivity of status for members of the class is obtained.

32. It must be that the effect of paragraph 3 of Article 1 of the Convention is such that the status of the applicant as a consular officer, except in so far as any facilities, privileges and immunities may be granted by the Commonwealth, can only be ascertained or defined by Article 71. Article 71 exclusively governs the status of thos consular officers referred to in paragraph 3 of Article 1.

33. I do not accept Mr. Sheahan's submission that the effect of Article 71 is to limit only the provisions of Articles 41, 43 and 44. To read down the provisions of Article 71 in this way is contrary to the clear wording of Article 71. The words "Except in so far as additional facilities, privileges and immunities may be granted...." clearly contemplate that the Article goes beyond merely limiting a few provisions. The effect of these words so far as the interpretation of the provision as a whole is concerned is to necessarily imply that the only facilities, privileges and immunities granted to such consular officers are those specified in Article 71 unless the receiving state (i.e. the Commonwealth of Australia) sees fit to grant additional facilities, privileges and immunities. I am of the opinion that the words "facilities, privileges and immunities" cover the field in so far as there may be any distinction drawn between the status of the person in the street and members of consular posts in the eyes of the law. An exemption that may be granted to consular staff in respect of income tax falls within the term "facilities, privileges and immunities". It is therefore not open to argue that the exemption contained in Article 66 is available to those who are caught by Articles 71 and 1(3) as being something other than a facility, privilege or immunity.

34. Another difficulty which arises out of the construction relied upon by the applicant is that the particular privileges or immunities actually conferred by paragraph 1 of Article 71 are not limitations of facilities privileges and immunities already conferred in a fuller way by Article 58 on honorary consular officers. Thus paragraph 3 of article 44 applied to honorary consular officers by virtue of paragraph 2 of Article 58. Unless Article 71(1) is starting afresh in relation to honorary consular officers who are nationals or permanent residents of the receiving State there was no need for the draftsman to confer on these officers the benefits of paragraphs 3 of Article 44. It was on the other approach already conferred under Article 58(2) in co-extensive terms and the provision in 71(1) is redundant.

35. Paragraph 2 of Article 71 deals with other members of the consular post who are nationals or permanent residents of the receiving State and members of such members's families as well as members of the families of consular officers dealt with under Article 71(1). Paragraph 2 of article 71 provides that all these persons enjoy facilities, privileges and immunities only in so far as these are granted by the receiving State. These provisions and the further provisions of Article 71(2) when read with Article 71(1) demonstrate to me that Article 71 was intended to be a code dealing with consular officers and other members of consular posts who in each case are nationals or permanent residents of the receiving State and members of the families of such consular officers and other members of consular staff so far as facilities privileges and immunities are concerned.

36. Mr. Sheahan submitted, inter alia, that to place such an interpretation as outlined above on Article 71 would be extraordinary in light of the provision of Chapter III of the Convention and would have the effect of nullifying those provisions.

37. Chapter III is headed "Regime Relating to Honorary Consular Officers and Consular Posts Headed by Such Officers." Of Chapter III only Articles 60, 61, 62, 66 and 67 have the force of law in Australia by virtue of s.5(1) of the Act.

38. Of these provisions only Articles 66 and 67 relate to honorary consular officers. The other provisions deal with consular premises and consular archives and documents.

39. It was the evidence of the applicant that to his knowledge all Honorary Consuls known to him were nationals or residents of their respective receiving States. This is not disputed and indeed it is a matter of common knowledge recognised by the commentators and the courts that honorary consuls are usually residents of the receiving State . See Viveash v. Becker (1814) 2 Mes. 285[1814] EngR 715; , 105 ER 619 at 623 per Lord Ellenborough C.J., International Law, D.P. O'Connell, 2nd Ed., Vol.2 and International Law, The Earl of Birkenhead, 6th Ed. As I have stated, I accept that for the purposes of the Act and the Convention that the applicant falls within the definition of "honorary consular officer." That is not to say however that I find that there can be no other honorary consular officer than a permanent resident or national of the particular receiving state. As it is the case that honorary consular officers may not be nationals or permanent residents of the receiving state, Mr. Sheahan could at best only say that they usually were, Article 66 is not deprived of any effect and Chapter III stands on its own. Whilst it may be that the scope of Articles 66 and 67 is limited by Article 71 those provisions are not nullified or rendered nonsensical.

40. That long established international customs and practices may be departed from as a consequence of placing such an interpretation upon Article 71 cannot dissuade me from adopting such an interpretation in the light of the clear and unambiguous wording of the provisions. I note, that so far as the law of this country is concerned, that prior to this Act coming into force, the effect of s.23AAA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1967 (No. 19 of 1967) was to subject honorary consular officers to taxation on income. Having passed the Act, the Parliament then passed the Income Tax Assessment Act (No.4) 1972 (No.65 1972). That Act was part of a package of legislation introduced to give effect to what the Parliament perceived to be the effect of the Convention.

41. Section 4 of the Income Tax Assessment Act No.4, 1972 repealed s.23AAA of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936. The principal Act was by s.3 of the Income Tax Assessment Act No.4, 1972 amended so that s.23(a)(11) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 exempts from income tax the "official salary or and the income derived from sources out of Australia by, any person being....(ii) a representative in Australia of the government of any country not being ... a person in relation to whom any of the provisions of the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations, as having the force of law by virtue of the Consular Privileges and Immunities Act 1972, apply) .... if the representative ... is not an Australian citizen and is not ordinarily resident in Australia..."

42. I was referred to the second reading speech of the Honourable the Attorney-General relating to the Income Tax Assessment Act No. 4 1972 (Act No.65 of 1972). From this it becomes clear, so Mr. McGill submitted, Parliament did not intend to alter the imposition of a liability to tax upon honorary consuls who are citizens of or permanent residents of this country. The Attorney expressly referred to s.23AAA which he described as authorising exemptions which were in line with those required by the Convention. Parliament, it was submitted as it appears from the terms of the second reading speech, did not intend by repealing s.23AAA to effect any change to what had been the law. This is said to throw light on what Parliament intended by its passage of the Consular Privileges and Immunities Act 1972.

43. The second reading speech however relates to the Income Tax Assessment (No.4), 1972. It does not refer to the second reading of the bill which subsequently became the Consular Privileges and Immunities Act 1972. That is the speech which in this context is picked up by para.15AB(2)(f)of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 . However, ss.15AB(2) of that Act is not to be read as limiting the generality of ss.15AB(1). That subsection allows reference to any material not forming part of the Act but which is capable of assisting in the ascertainment of the meaning of any provision in an Act. I consider that the Attorney's second reading speech in relation to the Income Tax Assessment Act (No. 4) 1972 answers that description and assists in ascertaining the meaning of the relevant provison of the Consular Privileges and Immunities Act 1972. Having considered that speech I am assisted in finding that the meaning of Article 71(1) forming part of the schedule to the Act is the ordinary meaning conveyed by the text of the provision taking into account its context in the Act and the purpose or object underlying the Act (s.15AB(i)(a)).

44. I find that the second reading speech of the Attorney referred to above confirms that Parliament enacted the Income Tax Assessment Act No.4 and repealed s.23AAA of the Income Tax Assessment Act on the basis that s.23AAA was no longer necessary as on the passing into law of the Consular Privileges and Immunities Act 1972 the provisions of that Act the immunities provided by Article 71(1)of the Convention and s.23AAA of the Income Tax Assessment Act coincided.

45. I find therefore for the various reasons set out above that Article 66 of the Convention does not have any application in respect of the applicant's income tax liability.

46. Accordingly, that the Income Tax Assessments under appeal in respect of income derived by the applicant during the years ended 30 June 1977, 30 June 1978, 30 June 1979 and 30 June 1980 are confirmed and the appeal is dismissed.

47. The applicant is to pay the respondent's costs of this appeal.
