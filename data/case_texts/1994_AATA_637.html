Last Updated: 18 May 2009

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

TAXATION - review - debenture - whether or not redeemed - whether separate loan account existed - capital gains tax - method of redemption - no need for formailties - relevance of registration with Australian Securities Commission - accounts prepared late - penalty tax - whether applicant was reckless or careless - aggravation - effect of delay



Income Tax Assessment Act ss 26AAA, 160M, 160ZZN



Corporations Law s 269



Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Steeves Agnew & Co (Vic) Pty Ltd [1951] HCA 26; (1951) 82 CLR 408



AAT Decision No 9768



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) NO NT93/131

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:



Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal : Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)



Date : 5 October 1994



Place : Sydney



  1. The objection decision in relation to primary taxation is affirmed.

  2. The objection decision in relation to additional tax by way of a per annum component is affirmed.

  3. The objection decision in relation to additional tax based upon a culpability factor is set aside and the matter is remitted to the respondent with directions that this segment of additional tax be recalculated at the rate of 22·5 per cent.

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No: NT93/131

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:



Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER OF

TAXATION



Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION



5 October 1994 Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)



1. On 4 July 1986, the applicant acquired certain shares and options in a public company of which he was chairman (“the asset”) at a cost of $345,520-80. Approximately a year later, an opportunity arose to sell the asset at a considerable profit. The applicant took advice from his solicitor and from counsel on the question of arranging the sale so as to avoid or minimise capital gains tax. It was decided to take advantage of s 160ZZN of the Income Tax Assessment Act by transferring the asset to a wholly owned company which would then on sell to an arm’s length third party.

2. On 2 July 1987 the applicant acquired a shelf company. Of the 2 issued shares, one was held in the applicant’s name and the other in the name of his wife who executed a declaration of trust in his favour. Both the applicant and his wife were directors of the company. On 17 August 1987 the applicant transferred the asset to the company for a total consideration of $2,542,968-60 pursuant to an agreement for sale. The agreement provided that the consideration was to be satisfied by the granting to the applicant of a debenture by the company to secure repayment of the full amount of the purchase price.

3. On 18 August 1987 the company sold the asset to an arm’s length third party for a total consideration of $2,580,264-25.

4. The advice sought by the applicant related simply to the possibility of an immediate liability to capital gains tax as a result of the ultimate sale. In its 1988 return, the company declared a profit of $21,749 on account of the sale transaction and made a request for a s 169A ruling that the profit was assessable under s 26AAA. Ultimately, this was agreed to and the company was assessed accordingly. For reasons to which I will later refer, the company’s return was not lodged until April 1990. Shortly afterwards, the respondent commenced an audit of returns lodged at the same time by the company and by the applicant. In the course of the audit, further facts emerged.

5. After the company had sold the asset, it placed the cash consideration on deposit with 2 institutions. From time to time thereafter, payments were made by the company to the applicant in a manner to which I will later refer. By 30 June 1988 it was contended by the respondent that all amounts secured by the debenture had been paid to the applicant, either in cash or by conversion of the balance of a relatively small amount to an unsecured loan. Accordingly the respondent considered that a disposal had taken place in accordance with the terms of s 160M(3)(c). That paragraph provides that in the case of an asset being a share in, or debenture of, a company the redemption in whole or in part, or the cancellation of the share or debenture, constitutes a change in the ownership of an asset. The respondent considered that the debenture had been redeemed through payment of the amounts secured to the applicant and that accordingly capital gains tax was payable on the disposal.

6. This was calculated at $1,078,305-86. An amended assessment was issued for this amount. Included in the assessment, was an additional amount of tax calculated with a per annum component in accordance with the usual practice and at the usual rates, a culpability component of 40 per cent, and an additional culpability component of 5 per cent for aggravation. The total additional tax was therefore $716,092-58. The difference between the amount payable under the applicant’s amended assessment for 1988 and the previous assessment which had issued some years earlier was $1,794,397-86. The applicant objected to this assessment and the objection was disallowed. This application is now brought to review that objection decision.

7. The applicant’s case, shortly, is that the moneys paid to him by the company between July 1987 and February 1988 were by way of loan and not by way of redemption. There were, he says, 2 accounts between him and the company. There was a loan account increased by advances from time to time from the company. There was also the amount owing under the debenture which was not reduced from time to time. Any attempt to set off the one account against the other, he submitted, was unauthorised and was ineffective. His case is that the accounts of the company now properly show those 2 parallel lines of financial relationships. Accordingly, it is submitted by the applicant that there has not been a redemption and that consequently there has been no disposal attracting the imposition of capital gains tax.

8. The applicant gave evidence by way of affidavit and orally. He said that prior to the disposal of the asset he had some discussions with a chartered accountant who was not then his personal accountant but who had, for some time, provided advice to public companies with which the applicant was associated. It was the chartered accountant who pointed out the possibility of rollover relief. No written advice was provided. The chartered accountant said in his evidence that he met with the applicant and the applicant’s solicitor in the chartered accountant’s office where these arrangements were discussed. The solicitor suggested that further advice be obtained from counsel and this was duly done. It was not clear what advice was given. No written advice was produced at the hearing (or to the respondent’s officers who had requested it). It seems unlikely that counsel would have been asked to advise on liability for capital gains tax beyond the immediate liability which might otherwise have arisen from a direct disposal of the asset by the applicant to the third party purchaser. It seems unlikely that attention was ever given to the consequences of redeeming the debenture. The solicitor was not called to give evidence. The chartered accountant had nothing further to do with the arrangements until 1990.

9. The solicitor prepared certain documents after the company had been acquired. The first document was a simple sale agreement between the applicant and the company in which the applicant agreed to sell and the purchaser agreed to purchase the asset for the sum I have mentioned. Settlement was to take place forthwith. Certain warranties as to ownership were given. The consideration was to be satisfied by the execution of a debenture in a form attached to the sale agreement.

10. The debenture recites that it was issued pursuant to a resolution of the directors passed on 11 August 1987. The day and month were written in by hand. The primary covenant from the company was to pay the principal sum to the applicant on 17 February 1988 “or on such earlier date as the principal moneys hereby secured become payable under the provisions hereof”. The month and year were typed into the document but the actual day was left blank and was filled in by hand, presumably at the time of execution which is stated to be 17 August 1987. The debenture therefore was a 6 months obligation.

11. Clause 2 of the debenture provided that the company would, until payment of the principal sum, pay to the debenture holder interest at the rate of 12 per cent per annum by equal quarterly payments on 17 November and 17 February, the first payment to be made on 17 November. To secure its obligations, the company charged all its undertaking and property both present and future.

12. Paragraph 5 is headed “right to redeem” and reads —

“The company may repay the whole or any part of the principal moneys due on this debenture at par on giving to the holder thereof 2 months notice in writing of its intention to do so. On any such repayment the holder shall surrender this debenture to the company for a note to be endorsed thereon of such repayment or where the whole of the moneys due thereon are repaid, for retention by the company.”

13. The debenture concluded with certain other standard clauses providing for immediate repayment liability in certain insolvency situations and providing for powers of a receiver and manager in the event of appointment of any such person.

14. The applicant had no recollection as to why the debenture was to have what he called an initial term of 6 months. There is no provision in the wording of the debenture contemplating an extension of the term. The applicant said that he did not at the time regard the initial term of the debenture as being a matter of great significance. I can accept this. There is, however, no evidence to suggest that any further assets would be transferred or that the terms of the debenture would be somehow altered as the applicant now suggests. Had there been a plan to transfer other assets to the company and to secure payment of the purchase price through the existence of the debenture, I would have expected to find some provision in the debenture anticipating such an event.

15. The resolution of directors was passed at a meeting said to have been held at Dallas, Texas, on 17 August 1987. The applicant and his wife had travelled to the United States a few days earlier. The resolution also authorised the solicitor to execute the sale agreement and the debenture. Another directors’ meeting had been held prior to their departure at which a resolution was passed that a general power of attorney from the company be given to the solicitor and that an employed accountant be appointed acting secretary of the company to facilitate execution of the documents. There was a recurring tendency in the applicant’s evidence to hold others responsible for the situation in which he now finds himself. I have no doubt, however, that he discussed the terms of the agreement and the debenture with the solicitor, that he saw the documents and approved of them before they were executed, and that he understood the nature of the documents. The applicant had practised as a solicitor for 10 years in his own practice before changing to a commercial career. Since then, he had been executive chairman of at least one public company and had had other substantial property and business interests. He was a person well versed in legal commercial matters.

16. In September 1987 the stock market crash occurred and the value of other shares that he held in the public company fell dramatically. He said in evidence that he therefore abandoned any plan to sell further assets to the shelf company. In April 1988 he left Australia to reside for a time in the United Kingdom. While there, and while in Australia, he caused sums of money to be transferred from the deposit accounts of the shelf company to his own use and to his private cheque account. Between 17 September 1987 and 12 August 1988 there were 19 such transfers, according to a schedule subsequently prepared in the chartered accountant’s office and attached to a letter of 20 September 1991. The total amounts transferred were $2,751,910-21. Most of this had simply been transferred to the applicant’s cheque account. Some amounts had been paid at the direction of the applicant to third parties for the purchase of private assets and for other purposes. According to another attachment to the same letter, subsequent disbursements from the cheque account were for a wide variety of private purposes.

17. The transfers were effected by the applicant giving instructions to personal assistants. An employed accountant who gave evidence, another person and the applicant were the authorised signatories on the deposit accounts. Most of the transfers were done by way of telephone. Apart from these records of transfers, no other books of account were kept for the shelf company during the whole of this period. The applicant was later to say that these 19 transfers were fresh borrowings by him from the shelf company. There are no contemporary records to substantiate that statement. The applicant said in his affidavit that it was his intention and understanding that these “borrowings” would be separate from the debenture transaction. He said that he did not intend that moneys which he drew from the company’s account would operate as a partial repayment by the company to him under the debenture. For reasons to which I will later refer I do not accept this evidence.

18. Following the applicant’s return from the United Kingdom, he became aware in early 1990 that he personally had not lodged an income tax return for the 1988 year, nor had accounts and an income tax return been prepared for the shelf company for that same year. He instructed the chartered accountant to attend to these.

19. Draft accounts and draft returns were prepared by an accountant employed by the applicant and were forwarded to the chartered accountant’s office. The employed accountant gave evidence.

20. She said that she had never been an employee of the company, except to act as company secretary for one day on 17 August 1987, for the purpose of facilitating the execution of a power of attorney in favour of the solicitor. She was also appointed public officer of the company. She had no part in the planning of the transfer of the asset to the company.

21. When she received instructions from the applicant to prepare accounts for approval by the chartered accountant, she had only documentation in relation to the deposit accounts, the sale agreement and the debenture and statutory records. There were no accounting records. The applicant gave no particular instructions to the employed accountant as to the way she was to proceed and left it to her to do what she thought was appropriate. She summarised all the banking transactions, prepared draft journal entries, prepared draft ledger entries and draft accounts, which included a profit and loss statement and balance sheet. After those steps were completed, she forwarded the draft material to the chartered accountant’s manager (who was also not called at the hearing). The draft material treated the payments from the company to the applicant in alternative ways. A handwritten draft ledger refers to the sums as “repayments” as at 17 February 1988, the date for repayment of the debenture. Any sums not then paid were transferred to an account called “unsecured loan”, so that in the draft ledger all moneys secured by the debenture were cleared out by the date proposed for redemption in the document.

22. Interest was calculated in accordance with the terms prescribed in the debenture on a diminishing balance reflecting the treatment of each of the transfers as a “repayment”. In the applicant’s own return, a figure was also claimed for interest at the same rate so that the 2 sums balanced out and no net income was derived by the applicant from interest. The applicant was later to say that the charging of interest against him was evidence of the existence of a loan account. However, the charging of interest does not appear to have taken place until well after the event. The draft journal reflects a similar treatment of the movement of moneys and classified the payments as repayments of the amounts owing under the debenture. In any event it should be noted that no interest is recorded as having been charged by the company or the applicant after 17 February 1988, the day appointed for redemption.

23. In her evidence, the employed accountant said that she had no clear recollection as to why the draft material was prepared in that form. She suggested that in the course of preparing it she may have raised the matter with the chartered accountant’s manager and that it was he who suggested that the transactions be dealt with in that way. As he did not give evidence, there was no way of testing that hypothesis.

24. The balance sheet in the company’s accounts was drawn on the basis that the debenture had been redeemed on 17 February 1988 and that it no longer existed as at 30 June 1988. This was subsequently repeated in the 1989 accounts. The original of the statutory accounts which would have been signed by the directors as a true and fair record of the company’s financial affairs could not be found. I have no doubt, however, that the applicant saw the draft balance sheet and profit and loss account attached to the company’s income tax return, even though it was signed by the employed accountant as public officer. It had been sent to the applicant from the chartered accountant’s office prior to it being signed. The applicant insisted that he could not tell from a mere perusal of the balance sheet that those preparing the accounts had treated the debenture as having been redeemed and as no longer existing at 30 June 1988. I do not accept this evidence. Such a principal feature in the fairly simple accounts of the shelf company, which had no other function than to facilitate the original transaction, would have been easily seen and understood by a person with the education and experience of the applicant. The truth probably is that the significance of redemption was not present in his consciousness, and he simply paid no attention to the existence or non existence of the secured debt in the company’s accounts.

25. I should say here that I found the applicant to be an unreliable witness. He paid scant attention to details and had a very poor recollection of events in some areas. Where his own interests are to be advanced, I would accept his evidence only if corroborated objectively. An example of the way he dealt with legal requirements is found in the minutes (exhibit G) of the annual general meeting of shareholders of the company which he signed as chairman on 22 December 1989 and in which it was resolved that the financial accounts for the period ended 30 June 1988 be received and adopted. At that time, the accounts had not even been prepared in draft. Accounts for 1991, 1992 and 1993 were later prepared and tendered (exhibit F). I give no weight to these accounts as they are based upon ex post facto instructions from the applicant as to his version of events in 1988. They show the debenture as still subsisting. I will later turn to what little corroboration can be relied upon in support of that view.

26. The basic proposition of the applicant rests on an assertion that the 19 transfers constituted advances and that together they formed a loan account. In my opinion this assertion can not succeed. It relies upon the evidence of the applicant which I am not prepared to accept without corroboration. That corroboration is to be found only in one or two incidental references in the draft accounts prepared by the employed accountant. Because there were no accounting records, and because there was no guidance from the applicant, it is understandable that there were some inconsistencies in the draft accounts. There were no inconsistencies, however, in the final accounts that were adopted for 1988.

27. Had the applicant intended to maintain what was essentially an artificial construction of a loan account side by side with an account of moneys owing under a security, then one would have expected from a man of his background that steps would have been taken to record this fact. In the absence of any such mechanism, one would normally expect that any payments made by a company to a secured creditor on a continuing basis would be in reduction of the secured debt. The construction of an elaborate hypothesis of a parallel loan account with no right of set off is based on no convincing evidence. The employed accountant took the payments as one would expect her to take them, and treated them as repayments of the indebtedness.

28. The applicant did not communicate the separate loan account idea to the solicitor, to the chartered account, or to his wife. Nor did he discuss with any of them, his then intention which he now asserts to extend the term of the debenture. He remembers that he had some discussion with the chartered accountant as to the interest rate to be charged on what he referred to as the loan account. The evidence leads me to believe, however, that this discussion did not occur until 1990 and was quite incidental. It is certainly not evidence of the existence of a loan account.

29. I am not satisfied that there were any repayments by the applicant to the company of any moneys which he “borrowed”. A bank statement showed a movement of a sum of $80,000 from his account to the company on 8 January 1988. It was suggested that this might have been some evidence of a repayment in part. However, the applicant was unable to recall the circumstances and could throw no light on why the money had been paid to the company. If it had been an indication of a liveliness of the loan account, I would have expected a better recollection from the applicant of the surrounding circumstances.

30. It was submitted on behalf of the applicant that the requirements for a set off did not exist and that there was no legal basis for extinguishing the loan account in the absence of evidence authorising such a movement. A number of authorities were quoted of which perhaps the most important was Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Steeves Agnew & Co (Vic) Pty Ltd [1951] HCA 26; (1951) 82 CLR 408 at 419 and 420. In my view, it is not necessary to consider whether any such set off was authorised. The fact is that there was no loan account. It is a recent invention. It came into contemplation as a reconstruction of events after the danger of redemption emerged. All contemporary evidence leads me to believe that the payments from the company to the applicant were in reduction of the secured debt. No question of set off therefore arises.

31. It was submitted that the debenture could not have been redeemed on 17 February 1988 as nothing occurred on that day. I do not accept this submission. The debenture itself provided for redemption on 17 February 1988. Prior to that time, considerable moneys had been paid to the applicant. By 30 June 1988 all secured moneys had been either paid to the applicant in cash or had been treated as an unsecured loan. Clause 5, which I have quoted above, deals only with the right of early redemption and is a standard clause to be found in many such security documents. It is not necessary for the procedure set out in clause 5 to be followed in order to redeem the debenture, if redemption takes place on the due date.

32. It was then submitted that the debenture could not have been redeemed because it was still registered with the Australian Securities Commission pursuant to the Companies Code and the Corporations Law. In my view, this does not reflect a correct understanding of the consequences of registration. The effect of non registration under the Corporations Law is to confirm merely a limited invalidity. The mortgage or charge is bad merely against the liquidator or administrator, unless a notice in respect of the charge was lodged with the Commission within the relevant period (s 266). This differs from the provision under the prior Uniform Companies legislation which made the unregistered charge void also against any creditor of the company, phraseology which would of course include a secured creditor. The previous legislation did provide for a limited system of priorities regulation.

33. So far as the present legislation is concerned, however, the failure to register leaves the security intact as a security inter partes and also as quite valid as against parties taking under other dispositions by the company, for example, by way of sale. Provision exists for the giving of a memorandum to the Commission acknowledging that the debt or other liability secured by the charge has been paid (Corporations Law s 269) and for the entry of the same by the Commission. The fact that no such notice has been given, however, does not mean that the charge continues to exist. The provisions under the Real Property Act, for example, are quite different in this respect. Once the debt which is secured by the charge has in fact been repaid, the company’s equity of redemption is enlivened and the debenture is redeemed, both in law and in equity. The fact that the document is still on the public register is no evidence that it has not been redeemed.

34. The applicant submitted that the accounts prepared on his instructions for 1988 should be disregarded as they were in error. Furthermore it was said that to the extent that the accounts could be said to contain any representation or admission that the debenture was no longer in existence, such representation was made only by the company and not by the applicant. It was submitted that the accounts could not constitute evidence against him as an admission to the effect that the debenture had been redeemed or discharged. The true facts of the case are not evidenced exclusively by accounting records, especially those that were prepared years after the event. On the whole of the evidence, I am satisfied that the applicant, as the only beneficial proprietor of the company and as its only active director, was aware of the construction of the financial records of the company reflected in its accounts, and was aware of the fact that the debenture had been, to use a neutral word, terminated. What he did not realise was the significance of such an event. It was since that has been pointed out to him that he has been at great pains to re-write history. It is not necessary to rely upon any so called admission by the company to reach this conclusion.

35. I do not accept the submissions that the accounts were prepared in error, that others were to blame and not the applicant, that the employed accountant did not sufficiently consult with the applicant, that the chartered accountant’s manager was mistaken, or that the chartered accountant did not pay sufficient attention to the accounts before he sent them to the applicant for his approval and signature. All these attempts at laying the blame at the feet of others, in my view, are futile. The applicant was the person most concerned. He was the activator of the preparation of the accounts and the lodging of the returns. He knew of, and approved of, the way in which the financial transactions were treated. It was only after the audit that he began to change his mind.

36. In the course of that audit, the chartered accountant’s firm wrote a letter to the respondent referring to amounts paid to the applicant “on account of debenture redemption”. The author of the letter was doing no more than conveying what was then the generally accepted view of the transactions among the applicant and his advisers. It is not an admission, as counsel submitted, but is further evidence of the general understanding of the nature of these transactions.

37. I have come to the conclusion that there was a redemption in whole of the debenture. There is no evidence to support the conclusion that any partial redemption occurred. The whole of the secured indebtedness was cleared out and the company’s entitlement to redemption was without impairment at 30 June 1988. The primary assessment of capital gains tax should therefore be affirmed. I will now turn to the question of penalties.

38. The applicant failed to state in his personal return that the debenture had been redeemed in the 1988 year. The respondent took the view that this was recklessness, as defined in paragraph 19 of Income Tax Ruling 2517. It was said that the omission was rashly made without any real basis of fact on which to base the omission and without regard to the consequences. The definition provides that a finding of dishonesty is unnecessary. As a result, the respondent assessed a culpability component of 40 per cent, being the top of the range for recklessness short of deliberate evasion. In my view, this is a rate that should be kept for only the worst cases. This is not one of them.

39. The applicant has been inattentive and thoughtless. His conduct fits more easily into the terms of the definition of carelessness, which could attract a culpability component of between 15 and 30 per cent. The fact that redemption had not been disclosed, ought to be viewed in the light of the fact that there was a real contest before me as to whether in fact redemption had taken place. It should also be looked at in the light of the fact that in 1988, the significance of redemption was unknown to the applicant. None of his advisers had been asked to consider what would happen if the moneys received by the shelf company were to be paid to the applicant. He was clearly of the view, in his evidence, that it was not intended he should be kept from the money for any significant period. The legal advice he received however, does not seem to have gone on to consider the consequences, no doubt because his legal advisers were not asked to do so. The significance did not emerge until after a meeting with the respondent’s officers in October 1991, when (for all practical purposes) for the first time the consequences of redemption, dawned on the applicant’s advisers. Prior to that it was thought that the audit was concerned (as it was) with compliance with s 160ZZN. Once the significance became known, however, the applicant sought to give a version of past events for the first time inconsistent with the respondent’s allegations.

40. The inattentiveness and thoughtlessness of the applicant lay in his failure to keep any financial records of the company, his failure to direct his advisers and employees in a manner which would have been consistent with his later assertions, his general inattentive and thoughtless attitude to the dealings he had with the Taxation Office and, in particular, in the preparation of the returns of himself and the company. There should, in my view, be some recognition of this inattentiveness and thoughtlessness. It is not, however, of such an extreme nature as to call for a culpability component at the top of the range. In my view a fair rating would be the middle of the range, namely 22­5 per cent.

41. A further 5 per cent was added by the respondent as an aggravation factor because of the alleged failure to cooperate reasonably in terms of paragraphs 47 to 49 of IT 2517. Certainly the audit took some time. Many of the delays, however, were attributable to the conduct of the respondent as well as to the applicant. At no stage did the respondent find it necessary to rely on statutory notices. Although there was some delay in arranging meetings on a voluntary basis, the applicant attended all of them and all information that was requested was ultimately provided. There was some delay in the provision of this information, but some of that delay was caused by the respondent’s officers’ reluctance to show their hands by providing written questions prior to a meeting. Each side indulged in what can only be regarded as tactics. However, in my view, they did not go beyond the bounds of reasonableness, both in the assertion of the respondent’s point of view and in the defence of the applicant’s own interests. I have considered the file notes prepared by the respondent’s officers and have considered the submissions in relation to those notes made by counsel for the applicant. In my view, any blame for delay ought to be shared equally and no allowance should be made for aggravation.

42. The objection decision in relation to primary taxation is therefore affirmed. The objection decision in relation to additional tax by way of a per annum component is affirmed. The objection decision in relation to additional tax based upon a culpability factor is set aside and the matter is remitted to the respondent with directions that this segment of additional tax be recalculated at the rate of 22·5 per cent.
