Re Kenneth Noel Whiteford and Commissioner of Superannuation [1987] AATA 35 (6 February 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: KENNETH NOEL WHITEFORD

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V86/34

AAT No. 3201

Superannuation - Administrative Appeals Tribunal

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

I.R. Thompson (Deputy President)

Dr H.W. Garlick (Member)

C.G. Woodward (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - forced retirement on invalidity grounds - benefit classification certificate in force at retirement - whether invalidity on retirement caused or substantially contributed to by conditions specified in certificate - role of stress in physical and mental conditions on retirement - anxiety/depression syndrome resulting in excessive alcohol and tobacco consumption - direct contribution of certificate conditions to retirement small - whether indirectly certificate condition of brochitis substantially contributed to retirement

Superannuation - extent of Commissioner's power to consider medical conditions on retirement - ability to go beyond Public Service Board report - Tribunal may decide that other factors contributed to the invalidity - Coutts and Re Smooker distinguished - Commissioner must form an opinion on all the conditions causing invalidity retirement

Superannuation - two principal causes of invalidity - whether either of those causes were connected with conditions specified in the benefit classification certificate - Miller considered - whether the certificate conditions 'substantially' contributed to retirement invalidity - Re Schlatter considered - 'substantially' contributed read in context of privative clause in otherwise beneficial legislation - unhelpful to express degree of contribution by reference to a percentage figure - contribution must be of such a significance as to warrant depriving the employee of a significant part of benefits available under the Act

Administrative Appeals Tribunal - recommendation sought for payment of applicant's costs under section 69 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act - not appropriate for AAT to make a recommendation as to costs - relevant consideration set out - applicant's success in the proceedings largely as a result of expert medical evidence - representation by Counsel justified

Words and Phrases: "substantially"

Superannuation Act 1976 ss. 184(3), 66(2), 16(2), (3), (4)

Commonwealth Employees (Redeployment and Retirement) Act 1979 ss. 7(1)(b)(ii), 9, 14(1), 15

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 s. 69

Re Thies and Department of Aviation (Decision No. 2693: 29 May 1986)

HEARING

CANBERRA

6:2:1987

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside and in substitution therefor it is decided that the applicant is entitled to invalidity benefit in accordance with the provisions of section 66(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

DECISION

The applicant, who is qualified to practise as a solicitor, was appointed in January 1971 to be a Legal Officer in the Attorney-General's Department in Melbourne. Before he was appointed he was medically examined. He had suffered from bronchitis since his youth. Consequently the Commonwealth Medical Officer who conducted the examination recommended that the decision whether or not he should be appointed a permanent officer and admitted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund or the Provident Fund established by the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act") should be deferred for six months. He recommended also that in the meantime the applicant should be regarded as fit for temporary employment.

2. In July 1971 the applicant was again medically examined. On that occasion the Commonwealth Medical Officer recommended that he be accepted as a contributor to the Provident Fund. He stated the reason for that recommendation, and implicitly the reason for not recommending that he be accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund, namely that he had varicose veins and a history of bronchitis. Thereupon the applicant was appointed as a permanent officer and accepted as a contributor to the Provident Fund.

3. In September 1982 the Permanent Head of the Attorney-General's Department made a redeployment declaration in respect of the applicant pursuant to section 9 of the Commonwealth Employees (Redeployment and Retirement) Act 1979, ("the R. and R. Act"). He made it for the reason that in consequence of physical or mental incapacity the applicant was incapable of performing his duties. In October 1982 a delegate of the Public Service Board issued a certificate under section 14 of that Act. In it he certified his satisfaction that, because the applicant was incapable of performing his duties in consequence of physical or mental incapacity, he should be retired from his employment. On receipt of that certificate the Permanent Head retired the applicant from the Australian Public Service with effect from 26 August 1982.

4. On 7 July 1982 a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation issued, pursuant to section 184(3) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act"), a benefit classification certificate in which there were specified "history of bronchitis" and "varicose veins". On 6 April 1983 another delegate of the Commissioner determined that the incapacity which was a ground of the applicant's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by the condition "history of bronchitis" specified in the benefit classification certificate, so that the provisions of section 66(2) (d) of the 1976 Act were applicable to him. A letter dated 5 May 1983 which the applicant sent to the Commissioner was accepted as a request, made pursuant to section 154(2) of the 1976 Act, for reconsideration of the delegate's decision. On 6 January 1986 the Commissioner confirmed the delegate's decision. The applicant then applied to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal, pursuant to section 154(6) of the 1976 Act, for review of the decision which had been confirmed.

5. At the hearing of the application the applicant was represented by Mr B. A. Keon-Cohen, of Counsel, and the respondent by a departmental officer, Mr R. C. Whithear. Among the documents received as evidence during the course of the hearing were a number of reports of medical examinations of the applicant. They included the reports of the two examinations in December 1970 and July 1971 referred to above, a report of the examination conducted in August 1982 by a Commonwealth Medical Officer to ascertain whether or not the applicant should be retired on invalidity grounds, a report by Dr Luke Murphy, a consultant physician, of an examination of the applicant which he had made on 28 July 1982 at the request of the Commonwealth Medical Officer, reports by two consultant psychiatrists, Dr D. Sime and Dr F. Hocking, of examinations made on 24 June 1986 and 28 November 1986 respectively, a radiologist's report prepared in April 1982 relating to the applicant's chest, three reports dated respectively 3 May 1982, 3 June 1985 and 2 July 1986 by Dr R. A. Poppenbeek, a general practitioner whom the applicant had consulted for advice and treatment, and a report by Dr L. Massage dated 1 December 1986 relating to the applicant's consultation with him as a general practitioner between July 1979 and June 1986.

6. Also tendered in evidence were a copy of the record of the applicant's absences on sick leave, a copy of the statement of his duties as a Legal Officer, copies of documents addressed to the applicant by senior officers of the Attorney-General's Department regarding unsatisfactory aspects of his conduct, and copies of various reports relating to other medical conditions from which he was suffering. Finally, there was a copy of a record of appointments of the applicant to perform higher duties during the period between January 1973 and July 1977. Oral evidence was given by the applicant, Mr J. G. Clark, who is now Assistant Director, Legal Services, in the Attorney-General's Department, Dr Sime, Dr Poppenbeek and Dr Murphy.

7. It is clear, from the medical reports and the evidence given by the medical practitioners at the hearing, that, when the applicant was retired from the Public Service, his incapacity for work was the result of a combination of conditions which were related, or contributed, to one another to varying extents.

8. Dr Hocking considered that the applicant had suffered "stress reaction of the 'burn-out' type due to the interaction of his age, the pressures of his employment and the physical and mental consequences of chronic alcoholism". Dr Sime expressed the view that he had a "brittle personality" which had rendered him susceptible to stress. He described this in terms of "executive stress", to which, he considered, inter-personal factors in the work place had contributed. It was established by Mr Clark's evidence that from 1975 onwards the workload of the applicant had increased steadily; after a change in management of the Department's Melbourne office in 1978 the complexity of his work, as well as the load, had increased markedly. His sick leave record shows a considerable number of absences from 1973 to 1975, a period when he was separating from, and then divorcing, his second wife, and again from 1979 to 1982. In the latter period the absences increased very sharply as he approached the time when he was retired. We are satisfied that stress played a major role in the development of a number of the mental and physical conditions which incapacitated him for work in 1982. In particular, on the basis of Dr Sime's evidence, we find that it caused an anxiety/depression syndrome, which in turn caused and fed upon his alcoholism and played a major part in causing him to neglect his health. Aspects of that neglect were his becoming obese and smoking cigarettes to excess.

9. There is no doubt that, when he began work in 1971, he was suffering from chronic bronchitis and that he continued to do so throughout the whole period of his employment in the Public Service. By the time of his retirement it had resulted in his suffering chronic obstructive airways disease and some emphysema. We accept that the bronchitis, chronic obstructive airways disease and the emphysema must have interacted with the anxiety/depression syndrome brought about by the factors described by Dr Hocking, and that his life-long smoking habit, which must also have interacted with that syndrome, adversely affected the bronchitis, chronic obstructive airways disease and emphysema. Nevertheless, again on the basis of Dr Sime's evidence, we find that the contribution which the bronchitis, chronic obstructive airways disease and the emphysema made to his anxiety/depression syndrome was very small in comparison with the contribution made to it by the stress of his work and by his alcoholism.

10. Dr Murphy and Dr Poppenbeek both expressed the opinion that, quite apart from any effect which the bronchitis, chronic obstructive airways disease and the emphysema had upon the anxiety/depression syndrome, they had a directly incapacitating effect in relation to the applicant's work. There is no doubt that they caused him to suffer a persistent cough, that as a result he experienced pains in his chest and that he suffered considerable discomfort if he was obliged to hurry or to walk up stairs. His work included attendance at court on some days; however, for the most part is was sedentary. While he was seated, the only effects of his bronchitis, chronic obstructive airways disease and emphysema which might have impaired his ability to do his work were the coughing and the pain in his chest; both might have reduced his powers of concentration. Dr Poppenbeek assessed the direct contribution of the bronchitis, chronic obstructive airways disease and emphysema to his incapacity to perform his work as 10%. Dr Murphy expressed the opinion that they contributed 25% to his incapacity to perform such part of his work as required physical exertion, but very little to that part of his work which required no physical exertion. Such percentages can be only approximate and lack precision; however, we accept that the direct contribution which the bronchitis, chronic obstructive airways disease and emphysema made to the applicant's incapacity to perform his work in 1982 was small.

11. Having made those broad findings of fact we turn now to consider a number of questions of law and details of fact. Before doing so, however, we think it helpful at this stage to note that a benefit classification certificate issued pursuant to section 184(3) of the 1976 Act is deemed to have been issued under section 16(4) and also for us to set out the provisions of sections 16(2), (3) and (4) and 66(2)(c) and (d) of that 1976 Act and of section 14(1) of the R. and R. Act. They are:-

"16. (2) The Commissioner may, for the purposes of

this section, require a person (other than a person

to whom section 184 applies) who proposes to become

or becomes an eligible employee to undergo, within

such period as the Commissioner specifies, such

medical examination or examinations by an approved

medical practitioner or practitioners as the

Commissioner determines.

(3) A report or reports of the result or

results of the medical examination or examinations

shall be furnished to the Commissioner.

(4) The Commissioner shall consider the report

or reports, and such other matters (if any) as the

Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of the

opinion that the person is not likely, by reason of

or for a reason connected with a physical or mental

condition or conditions referred to in the report or

reports, to continue to be an eligible employee until

the person attains his maximum retiring age, the

Commissioner shall issue a benefit classification

certificate to that effect, being a certificate in

which the relevant condition or conditions is or are

specified."

"66. (2) Subject to sub-sections (3), (3A) and (4)

of this section and section 79, where -

. . . . .

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the

incapacity which was the ground for his

retirement was caused, or was substantially

contributed to, by a physical or mental

condition or conditions specified in the

certificate or by a physical or mental

condition or conditions connected with such

a condition or such conditions,

the person is entitled -

(d) where the period of contributory service of

the person is not less than 8 years -

(i) if the person does not make an

election under section 71 or 72 - to

invalidity pension in accordance with

section 70 and, where the person has

paid supplementary contributions, a

lump sum benefit in accordance with

that section;

(ii) if the person makes an election under

section 71 - to invalidity pension,

and a lump sum benefit, in accordance

with that section; or

(iii) if the person is entitled to make an

election under section 72 and makes

such an election - to a lump sum

benefit in accordance with

sub-section 72(2) and, where the

person has paid supplementary

contributions, an additional lump sum

benefit in accordance with

sub-section 72(3);"

"14. (1) Where an employee who has been declared to

be eligible for redeployment in accordance with this

Part for the reason referred to in sub-paragraph

7(1)(b)(ii) has not been redeployed in accordance

with section 11, the Public Service Board may, if it

is satisfied that the employee should, in consequence

of his incapacity, be retired from his employment,

issue to the Permanent Head or prescribed

Commonwealth authority concerned a certificate, in

writing, certifying that it is satisfied that the

employee should, for the reason so referred to, be

retired from his employment."

We note that the reason referred to in section 7(1)(b)(ii) of the R and R. Act is that the employee "is, in consequence of physical or mental incapacity, incapable of performing his duties".

12. Mr Whithear submitted that, because section 66(2)(c) is concerned with "the incapacity which was the ground for (the employee's) retirement", neither the Commissioner nor the Tribunal standing in his shoes is entitled to have regard to incapacity resulting from any conditions other than those recorded as the basis for the Public Service Board's satisfaction that the employee was incapable of performing his duties in consequence of physical or mental incapacity. In the applicant's case the Commonwealth Medical Officer stated them as "personality aberration with alcohol abuse", "obesity", "chronic obstructive airways disease" and "spondylitis of the lumbar spine". Mr Whithear submitted that the Commissioner could not properly form an opinion that the incapacity was caused, or substantially contributed to, by any other physical or mental condition. He acknowledged that in a number of decisions the Tribunal had decided that a condition which had not formed the basis of the medical opinion as to incapacity which had led to retirement had been accepted as having caused or contributed to the invalidity. However, he referred to a statement by Brennan J. in Coutts v. Commonwealth of Australia [1985] HCA 40; (1984) 59 ALR 699, made in relation to the provisions of the Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Act 1973, that a member of the Defence Force who had been dismissed on the ground of invalidity was entitled to invalidity benefits to which he would not have been entitled if he had been dismissed on some other ground. He referred then to a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal in Re Smooker and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1977) 1 ALD 74, where the Tribunal held that, even at the request of the member, he could not be treated otherwise than as having been retired on the ground of invalidity. However, in our view, the questions dealt with by the High Court and the Tribunal in those two cases was entirely different from that which was the subject of Mr Whithear's submission.

13. As we observed at the hearing, the Commissioner's concern about the extent of his power, and his view as to its limit, can be readily appreciated when regard is had to provisions of section 15 of the R. and R. Act. That gives an employee in respect of whom a certificate has been issued under section 14(1) the right to appeal to a Commonwealth Employees Redeployment and Retirement Appeals Tribunal against its issue. Where a benefit classification certificate has been issued in respect of an employee, his decision whether to appeal against the issue of a certificate under section 14 may well depend on the information available to him as to the conditions regarded by the Public Service Board as having caused his incapacity and, in particular, whether they include a condition in respect of which a benefit classification certificate has been issued. Even though no time limit is expressly set by section 15 for exercise of the right of appeal, an employee might well be prejudiced if at a date long after the certificate was issued under section 14 the Commissioner formed the opinion that his incapacity was caused or substantially contributed to by a condition not specified in it but specified in the benefit classification certificate or connected with such a condition specified in the benefit classification certificate.

14. However, it appears to us to be clear that the provisions of section 66(2)(c) give to the Commissioner power to have regard to incapacity resulting from conditions other than those recorded as the basis of the Public Service Board's satisfaction as to incapacity. The provision in section 66(2)(c) that he may form an opinion that a condition substantially contributed to the incapacity might possibly, if standing alone, have been construed as relating only to the degree of contribution to the incapacity made by a condition regarded by the Public Service Board as having rendered the employee incapable of performing his duties. But section 66(2)(c) also empowers the Commissioner to form an opinion that a condition specified in the benefits classification certificate caused the incapacity. That provision would be purposeless if he were bound to have regard only to the conditions taken into account by the Board. We are satisfied that section 66(2)(c) empowers him to form an opinion that a condition not taken into account by the Board caused the incapacity, at least where such opinion is not totally irreconciliable with the view adopted by the Board. In the circumstances of the present case it is not necessary for us to decide whether or not his power is qualified in that manner; it is, we consider, more appropriate to leave that for decision in a case where the point has to be decided.

15. Mr Whithear conceded that, if the medical evidence available to the Commissioner, or to the Tribunal standing in his shoes, led him or it to the conclusion that the employee's invalidity was caused by a condition which was essentially the same as the condition taken into account by the Public Service Board but which had previously been misdescribed, he or it could properly form the opinion that that condition caused the incapacity. We have no doubt that that is so. He accepted that in the present case we might possibly find that the condition referred to by the Commonwealth Medical Officer as "personality aberration with alcohol abuse" was in fact a misdescription of the condition properly described by Dr Sime as an anxiety/depression syndrome. Although it is suggested in some of the reports tendered in evidence that the description of the applicant's condition in 1982 was a matter of semantics, the evidence of Dr Sime appeared to indicate that personality aberration or personality disorder is a condition distinct from an anxiety/depression syndrome. No evidence was adduced as to what the Commonwealth Medical Officer meant when he used the phrase. It is possible that he was describing the same condition as Dr Sime. However, in the absence of the evidence of that we are unable to accept it. Nevertheless, we are quite satisfied that the opinion that an anxiety/depression syndrome caused the incapacity is not inconsistent with the view expressed by the Commonwealth Medical Officer that personality aberration with alcohol abuse made a 40% contribution to the incapacity. We have recorded above our finding that the anxiety/depression syndrome caused and fed upon the alcoholism. In our view the anxiety/depression syndrome was not the sole cause of his incapacity; but, together with alcoholism, it was one of the two principal causes.

16. Being of that opinion, we have next to decide whether that condition, which was not specified in the benefit classification certificate, was connected with either of the conditions which were specified. There is no suggestion whatsoever that it was connected in any way with varicose veins; nor did Mr Whithear suggest that the condition "history of bronchitis" played any major role in causing the anxiety/depression syndrome or the alcoholism. What he suggested, however, was that there was a common factor which played a major contributory role in causing both the bronchitis and the anxiety/depression syndrome and alcoholism. That, he said, was the applicant's personality, which Dr Sime described as obsessive and brittle. In support of his argument he relied on statements by Davies J. and Pincus J. in Commissioner for Superannuation v. Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 63 ALR 237. At 239 Davies J. said:-

"In the context, I would read the words 'connected

with' in ss 16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) as dealing with

the circumstance where the immediate or proximate

cause of the incapacity leading to the retirement was

a medical condition other than the condition existing

at the time the employee became an eligible employee,

but where, nevertheless, there is a chain of

causation such that the medical condition existing at

the time when the employee became an eligible

employee can be said, through a chain of events, to

have caused or substantially contributed to the

incapacity causing the retirement."

Mr Whithear relied more particularly on the words underlined in the following passage from the judgment of Pincus J. which appears at 247:-

"The expression 'connected with' is not very precise.

It is my view that the sort of connection which is

meant is a causal one. For the purposes of ss

16(10)(b) and 66(2)(c) of the Superannuation Act

1976, two conditions are connected with one another

if there is a substantial causal link between the

two. That may be so because one is a cause of the

other, or because both have a common cause."

17. Having read carefully the judgments in Miller's case, we doubt whether those words are part of the ratio decidendi. The concept referred to in them is different from that usually encompassed by the phrase "chain of causation", used by Davies J. Further it appears to us that what Pincus J. may have had in mind was the situation where two conditions are, in effect, different manifestations of one underlying condition. Neither the bronchitis nor the anxiety/depression syndrome is a manifestation of the applicant's personality. Each is a separate condition caused by it, in the case of the bronchitis through the intermediate condition of addiction to smoking. We doubt, therefore, whether, even if the evidence established that the applicant's personality was causally connected with his history of bronchitis in 1970, the history of bronchitis could properly be said to be connected with the anxiety/depression syndrome via his personality. However, as Mr Keon-Cohen pointed out, the evidence is quite inadequate for us to make a finding that the history of bronchitis in 1970, which extended back to the age of 16, was causally connected with his personality. We find, therefore, that neither the anxiety/depression syndrome nor the alcoholism is connected with a condition specified in the benefit classification certificate.

18. One matter remains for consideration; that is whether the bronchitis, chronic obstructive airways disease and emphysema from which the applicant was suffering in 1982 at the time of his retirement and which were undoubtedly connected with the condition of history of bronchitis specified in the benefit classification certificate were conditions which together substantially contributed to the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement. We have already found that they made a small contribution to it, both directly and also indirectly through acting upon and reacting with the anxiety/depression syndrome. But did they "substantially" contribute to that incapacity?

19. The meaning of "substantial" and "substantially" was considered by the Tribunal at some length in Re Schlatter and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (Decision No. 2293: 19 August 1985). The Tribunal pointed out that the word might be used in a qualitative or a quantitative sense and in either sense with a number of different meanings. In Tillmanns Butcheries Pty. Ltd. v. Australasian Meat Industry Employees' Union and Others (1979) 27 ALR 367 at 382, Deane J. observed that "'substantial' is not only susceptible of ambiguity: it is a word calculated to conceal a lack of precision". What is clear from the cases cited by the Tribunal in Re Schlatter is that the meaning which "substantial" or "substantially" bears in any particular statutory provision is to be found by reference to the context of that provision. Depending on the context its meaning in a quantitative sense may range from "not trivial, minimal or nominal" (Re Schlatter) to "considerable, solid or big" (Palser v. Grinling (1948) AC 291). In a qualitative sense it has been held to mean "weighty" or "important" (Re Howard Smith Industries Pty. Ltd. (1976) 28 FLR 385) and "sufficiently serious or significant to cause concern to a properly informed reasonable person" (see Re Thies and Department of Aviation (Decision No. 2693: 29 May 1986).

20. Section 66(2)(c) is a privative provision in a generally beneficial statute. The Act makes provision for the payment of pensions, inter alia to persons who are retired on the ground of invalidity. Section 66(2) reduces the benefit paid to certain persons who are retired on that ground. The provisions for the issue of benefit classification certificates and the privative provisions of section 66(2) are clearly intended to reduce the liability of the Commonwealth to pay such benefits in circumstances in which it would be inconsistent with proper stewardship of public funds for it to do so. That is the context in which the phrase "substantially contributed to" is used. Mr Whithear conceded that, if the contribution which was made to invalidity by any condition was of the order of only 10%, it ought not to be regarded as having substantially contributed to it. We agree broadly with that view. However, we think it unhelpful to try to express the degree of contribution which is to be regarded as substantial by reference to any specific percentage. Opinion as to the percentages of contribution by each of a number of conditions, particularly where they cause and feed upon one another, cannot be other than imprecise; that being so, for us to attempt to give precision in the context of section 66(2)(c) to the imprecise expression "substantilly" would be to impose an apparent rigidity which, we are satisfied, the legislation was never intended to possess and of which indeed we think it incapable.

21. However, because of the wide range of meanings which the expression is capable of bearing, it is necessary for us to state where in that range its meaning in the present context lies. Essentially, we are satisfied, the legislation intended that, for a condition to be regarded as substantially contributing to incapacity for the purposes of section 66(2)(c), it must be of such significance in relation to that incapacity as to warrant depriving the employee of a significant part of the benefits which he would otherwise receive under the provisions of the Act. The meaning of "substantially" must, therefore, be considerably higher up the scale of substantiality than "not trivial, minimal or nominal". On the other hand, in some cases the incapacity may be caused by two or three conditions with each contributing equally to it. In such a case, we have no doubt, each of them must be regarded as substantially contributing to the incapacity. So the meaning of "substantially" in section 66(2)(c) is not as far up the scale as "in the main" or "in the greater part".

22. In the present case we are satisfied that the contribution made by the applicant's chest conditions in 1982 was small in relation to the contributions made by his anxiety/depression syndrome and his alcoholism. It was not, in our view, of significance to such a degree that it warranted depriving him of a significant part of the benefits to which he was otherwise entitled under the 1976 Act. Accordingly, the decision under review is set aside and in substitution for it we decide that the applicant is entitled to invalidity benefit in accordance with the provisions of section 66(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

23. Mr Keon-Cohen informed us that the applicant intended to apply to the Attorney-General pursuant to section 69 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 for the provision of financial assistance in relation to this proceeding. He asked us to make a recommendation that such assistance be granted. From comments he made during the hearing it appears that he may have regarded delay on the part of the Commissioner as possibly a relevant consideration. Nearly three years elapsed between the initial decision and the Commissioner's decision to confirm it. It is now more than four years since the applicant retired. However, that has not, as far as we have been able to ascertain, involved the applicant in any additional costs in presenting his case to the Tribunal. A matter which may be relevant is that he has succeeded in this proceeding largely as the result of his having obtained expert medical advice and his having adduced expert medical evidence. Also representation by Counsel was undoubtedly appropriate. However, we are not aware of the criteria by which the Attorney-General decides whether or not to grant applications for financial assistance in proceedings before the Administrative Appeals Tribunal; consequently we do not know whether the applicant is more deserving of a share of what are presumably limited funds than are others who have applied for financial assistance. We, therefore, consider that it would not be appropriate for us to make any recommendation that financial assistance be granted; instead we have simply noted the factors which we believe to be relevant to consideration by the Attorney-General of the applicant's application when it is made.
