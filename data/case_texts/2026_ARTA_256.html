Lewis and Commissioner of Taxation (Taxation) [2026] ARTA 256 (25 February 2026)

Applicant: Scott Lewis

Respondent: Commissioner of Taxation

Tribunal Number: 2025/3716

Tribunal: General Member M Stratos

Place: Brisbane

Date: 25 February 2026

Decision: The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

..............................[SGD]..........................................

General Member M Stratos

CATCHWORDS

TAXATION – capital gains tax – sale of shares by family trust – applicant beneficiary of trust – distribution of proceeds of sale of shares to applicant – capital gain – intention when disposing of assets – provisions to disregard capital gain – discretion of Tribunal – decision affirmed

LEGISLATION

Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) ss 100-25, 100-30, 100-33, 100-35, 100-40, 102-5, 102-3, 104-5, 104-10, 115-1 and Division 152



Tax Administration Act 1953 (Cth) s14ZZK, Division 340 of Schedule 1



Public Governance, Performance and Accountability Act 2013



CASES

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Montgomery (1999) 164 ALR 435



Roche Products Pty Ltd and Commissioner of Taxation [2008] AATA 639; (2008) 70 ATR 703

Statement of Reasons

  1. Lockdowns related to the COVID-19 pandemic affected people across the world physically, emotionally and financially. Mr Lewis (the applicant) ran a small business that was hit hard economically and, in an effort to keep his fitness franchise operational the Scott Lewis Family Trust (the Trust) of which he was a beneficiary sold shares and made a distribution to the applicant. That distribution was treated as a capital gain in his 2020 income tax assessment. This left the applicant with a substantial taxation liability which he seeks to have reduced on the basis of the extraordinary circumstances occurring at the time of the sale of the shares, noting he intended to use those proceeds to support his ailing business, rather than for his personal advantage.
  2. For the following reasons, the Administrative Review Tribunal (the Tribunal) affirms the objection decision the subject of this application.

BACKGROUND

  1. The background facts were agreed by parties and are not in dispute. These details are drawn from evidence given by the applicant at hearing and documents before the Tribunal, namely the Tribunal documents, the applicant’s Statement of Issues, Facts and Contentions (SFIC) and the respondent’s SFIC.
  2. The applicant is a beneficiary of the Trust. On 2 August 2019, the Trust commenced trading in shares. From that time shares were acquired but sold prior to the end of June 2020. In its 2020 trust tax return, the Trust reported a capital gain related to the sale of shares of $201,9766 and made a distribution to the applicant of that same amount in that year.
  3. On 1 May 2024, the applicant lodged his income tax return for the year ending June 2020. He reported taxable income of $296,800, with a net capital gain of $201,976 received from the Trust distribution.
  4. The respondent issued a notice of assessment on 8 May 2024 regarding the 2020 financial year. It assessed the applicant’s taxable income as $296,800. The total liability owed by the applicant pursuant to this assessment was $117,045.
  5. On 23 December 2024, the applicant lodged an objection to the notice of assessment. The applicant requested the respondent reconsider the capital gain assessed on the proceeds distributed by the Trust following the sale of shares on the ground that extraordinary circumstances of government mandated lockdowns necessitated the involuntary sale of shares to sustain his business and support employees. The applicant urged the respondent to consider the intent and circumstances of the taxpayer when applying relevant legislation.
  6. By decision dated 11 April 2025, the respondent disallowed the applicant’s objection to the assessment concerning the capital gain for the year ending June 2020 for the reason that there was no discretion available to exclude the capital gain made as a result of the sale of shares and distributed to him by the Trust.
  7. On 6 May 2025 the applicant lodged an application for review of the reviewable objection decision dated 11 April 2025 with the Tribunal. In his application for review, the applicant outlined his dissatisfaction with the objection decision for the following reasons:

I am applying for review because while the ATO's decision aligns with the technical application of tax law, it completely disregards the extraordinary and involuntary nature of the circumstances that led to the capital gains event.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, I lost everything — my business, my home, my livelihood. I was a contributing small business owner employing others, supporting my family, and actively participating in the economy. Being in the "multi-site gym business", government imposed lockdowns shut down my business operations virtually overnight. I had no income, no relief fast enough to respond, and no option but to sell personal assets just to meet my basic obligations — to pay rent, staff, escalating legal costs and debts.

The shares I sold were not part of any calculated investment strategy. They were a last resort. I accepted the disadvantage of selling them before the 12-month CGT discount period had passed because I didn’t have the luxury of time — I needed funds immediately. This decision was driven entirely by government-mandated economic shutdowns, not profit-making intent. The ATO’s approach applies legislation in a vacuum, ignoring the real-world trauma, coercion, and economic survival decisions that individuals were forced to make during an unprecedented national crisis. It’s a narrow reading of tax law that leads to an outcome clearly at odds with justice.

This is why I turn to the Tribunal. I am not asking for special treatment, but for human judgement. I am asking for recognition that, in this case, the mechanical application of tax law produces a fundamentally unfair result. I believe the Tribunal has a duty not only to interpret law, but to consider its effect on the people subject to it — especially when those people acted in good faith during one of the most disruptive periods in modern Australian history.

ISSUES

  1. The Tribunal is required to determine whether the applicant has discharged their burden of proving the assessment for the 2020 financial year was excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessment should have been.[1]
  2. There was no review or audit to determine whether the amount of $201,976 as the capital gain was correct, as the respondent relied upon the applicant’s self-assessment when making its decision. However, parties agreed for the purpose of these proceedings that the issue for consideration is whether the capital gain amount of $201,976 should be included in the applicant’s taxable income pursuant to s 102-5 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) (the ITAA).

LEGISLATION

  1. Part 3-1 of the ITAA outlines the regime for the taxation of capital gains. A taxpayer’s income tax liability is affected by capital gains tax (CGT) where their assessable income includes a net capital gain for that income year.[2]
  2. Shares acquired after 20 September 1985 are a CGT asset.[3] The sale of shares acquired after 20 September 1985 is considered to be a CGT event, namely CGT event A1.[4] A CGT event A1 happens if a taxpayer disposes of an asset, meaning a change of ownership occurs from the taxpayer to another entity whether because of some act or event by operation of law.[5] Those amounts received from the CGT event are known as capital proceeds.[6] When such a sale has occurred in an income year, unless any exemptions apply to reduce or disregard the proceeds, and where the capital exceeds the total cost base associated with the shares, the excess amount constitutes a capital gain.[7]
  3. Concessional rules can apply to an individual if, among other factors, they have a capital gain from a CGT asset acquired at least 12 months before the CGT event that caused the capital gain.[8] This is known as a discount capital gain. No discount percentage on the capital gain is available to the applicant in his circumstances as the shares were acquired from August 2019 and disposed of before the end of June 2020, such facts agreed by parties and accepted by the Tribunal. The shares were acquired less than 12 months before the CGT event, being the disposal of the shares.
  4. There are exemptions that may reduce a capital gain or loss or allow a taxpayer to disregard same. These categories include exempt assets, certain transactions, anti-overlap provisions and small business relief.[9] None of these exemptions apply to the applicant’s circumstances.
  5. Roll-overs allow a taxpayer to defer or disregard a capital gain or loss from a CGT event in specific situations. A capital gain or loss can be rolled over until a later CGT event occurs where a CGT asset is replaced with another such asset. A same asset roll over allows a taxpayer to disregard a capital gain or loss from a CGT when the same CGT asset is involved.[10] None of these examples of roll-overs apply to the applicant’s situation.
  6. Division 152 of the ITAA provides that capital gains can be reduced by various concessions to help small businesses if certain conditions are satisfied. The applicant in this case is an individual and does not qualify as a small business entity and as such these concessions are not applicable to the applicant’s circumstances.

EVIDENCE AND SUBMISSIONS

Applicant’s Evidence and Submissions

  1. In submissions to the respondent in support of his objection, the applicant outlined his position in an email dated 17 March 2025:

The sale of my shares was not a voluntary investment decision, it was a forced financial action directly caused by government-imposed COVID-19 restrictions that crippled my business, eliminated my income, and left me with no alternative but to sell assets to survive.

This is not a standard capital gains event, where an individual chooses to sell an asset to realise a profit. Instead, this was a last-resort measure, necessitated by unprecedented, government-mandated economic hardship. The same government that forced the economic conditions leading to my financial distress is now attempting to tax me on a decision they left me no choice but to make.

It is also very clear that at the time I was fully aware that I would not receive a CGT discount, as I had not held the shares for 12 months. However, I did not have the luxury of time to wait. I was left with no alternative but to sell the shares immediately, despite knowing the full tax implications. This fact alone reinforces that this was not an investment strategy, but an act of financial survival.

Had these government restrictions not been imposed, my business would have continued operating, my income would have remained stable, and I would not have needed to sell these shares at all. The ATO cannot fairly assess tax on a sale that was only necessary due to artificial economic conditions created by government intervention.

By treating this sale as a voluntary capital gain, the ATO is ignoring the true cause of the transaction and penalising me for a financial crisis I did not create. The principles of fair taxation demand that financial hardship caused by government mandates be considered in the assessment of CGT liabilities.

  1. The applicant explained at hearing that at the time of the COVID-19 mandated lockdowns in 2020 he was working as a personal trainer with the oversight of several group fitness studios. The business was reasonably new with cash flow constraints. As a result of lockdowns, group fitness activities were unable to operate and the business’ ability to produce any cash flow was lost. There were legal disputes with other franchisees and the company found themselves insolvent shortly thereafter. The applicant sold whatever assets they had at that time as quickly as possible, including shares, and hence could not capitalise on the CGT discount for assets acquired at least 12 months prior to any disposal. The applicant found himself personally in financial hardship.
  2. The applicant contends in 2020 he was forced to sell shares at a significant discount solely to meet financial and legal obligations related to his collapsing business. He argued he received no financial gain or personal benefit from these transactions as the funds were used solely to meet liabilities incurred as a result of lockdowns and government mandates. The business later folded and the applicant himself remains in financial hardship as a result. He submitted the CGT imposed is excessive as he received no profit or gain from the proceeds of the sale, as those proceeds that went into the business represented a loss.
  3. The basis of his argument was that the Tribunal should disregard the CGT assessment on the ground he did not make a profit out of the sale or the ensuing transactions in the business. He stated there was no gain from the distressed sale. He submitted there must be a discretion available for a decision maker in unprecedented circumstances or where it would be unfair to impose a taxation liability.
  4. The applicant stated he understands the law regarding the tax treatment of CGT assets and disposals, but he urged the Tribunal to consider a humanitarian side to taxation assessments to take into account unprecedented and challenging situations such as those he described. He felt the government devastated his businesses by mandating lockdowns and followed that by penalising the applicant with a large tax bill, yet the taxation liability only arose as a result of actions taken to help mitigate the impact to his business.
  5. In his submission to the respondent objecting to the original assessment, the applicant relied upon s 102-5 of the ITAA to suggest the legislation requires the intent and circumstances behind any sale to be considered when determining the capital gain to be applied. He also submitted that s 118-195 of the ITAA allowed special provisions to be applied where there are forced disposals of assets due to hardship.
  6. The applicant also sought to rely upon case law, namely Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Montgomery (‘Montgomery’)[11] as support for the requirement to consider the context and intent when determining tax obligations. He also raised the case of Roche Products Pty Ltd and Commissioner of Taxation (‘Roche’)[12] to support the requirement to recognize extraordinary circumstances impacting taxpayers when making decisions. The applicant claimed these decisions and provisions highlighted the principle that CGT assessments should consider the broader economic and financial conditions leading to an asset sale, particularly when that sale was forced by extraordinary circumstances beyond a person’s control.
  7. The applicant submitted the intent of the capital gains tax regime is to tax wealth derived from investment growth and not to penalise taxpayers who are forced to sell assets for survival. The imposition of capital gains tax on the proceeds of the sale of the shares violates this purpose. He did not make a gain in this situation, and he had no choice but to act as he did.
  8. The applicant’s SFIC also submitted public law principles had been violated as the respondent acted unreasonably when exercising their power. He considered the application of the ITAA must be reasonable, fair and exercised in accordance with administrative justice principles. The COVID-19 situation was an unprecedented public emergency and it was manifestly unfair to treat financial outcomes caused due to government intervention as capital gains. The respondent was said to have unreasonably exercised its discretion, amounting to legal overreach, especially when considered in light of other mechanisms to assist taxpayers, such as Department of Finance waiver provisions.
  9. The applicant also submitted the treatment as a capital gain in this instance is inhumane, contrary to expectations of fairness and a systemic failure to accommodate exceptional circumstances. Furthermore, he considered the respondent has acted in contradiction to the spirit of fairness and integrity outlined in the Model Litigant Obligations by pursuing his tax debt.
  10. The applicant seeks relief in the form of setting aside the objection decision or a recommendation or referral to the Department of Finance for discretionary financial relief.

Respondent’s Submissions

  1. The respondent submitted in the objection decision, its SFIC and at hearing the reasons for and circumstances surrounding the disposal of a CGT asset are not relevant to whether the CGT event occurred or how any gain is calculated. It was submitted any CGT event was not dependant on a profit making intention by the Trust and the reasoning behind the sale by the Trust does not prevent the CGT event from happening.
  2. The respondent disagreed with the applicant’s contention that s 102-5 of the ITAA emphasised the imposition of CGT must reflect the intent and circumstances of the taxpayer. The respondent submitted that section relates to the methodology to be used in determining a capital gain and reveals no general discretion to disregard a gain based on the reasons or intent behind the sale of the asset. Similarly, the respondent considered the cases raised by the applicant as supporting his contentions to be irrelevant to his circumstances.
  3. The respondent contended there were no exemptions or concessions that applied to the applicant to disregard or reduce the capital gain made as a result of the disposal of the shares. He was not entitled to the discount method under s 115-5 of the ITAA, nor the relief for small business under s 152.10. Nor was s 118-195 relevant to his circumstances to allow the capital gain to be disregarded as that section relates to dwellings disposed of in connection with deceased estates.
  4. Statutory discretions are available in certain situations and are outlined in the ITAA. The respondent submitted if it was the intention to provide a decision-maker with the discretion to disregard a capital gain in the circumstances described by the applicant, the legislation would have provided for such. The respondent contended no such discretion exists. It was submitted there is no power to allow the respondent to excise a capital gain from the applicant’s 2020 assessment.
  5. In relation to any breach of the Model Litigant Obligations, the respondent noted those obligations do not restrain the respondent from defending its position in a proceeding brought against the Commissioner. Furthermore, the applicant has not identified any specific conduct which would demonstrate a breach of the obligations, which it submitted had not occurred. In any event, if there was a breach, which was denied, it would not mean the assessment of the respondent was excessive.
  6. The respondent submitted there had been no violation of administrative or public law principles, as it was the statutory duty of the respondent to assess tax in accordance with the law. The respondent cannot remove tax properly payable from an assessment. Any waiver of the debt or act of grace payment sought by the applicant are discretions exercised by the Finance Minister, not the respondent or the Tribunal. The respondent considered any suggestion that the enforcement of CGT is inhumane, unfair or fails to account for exceptional circumstances amount to general commentary and do not contravene the respondent’s statutory duty. The applicant, the respondent submitted, has failed to establish why in accordance with the relevant laws the assessment the subject of the proceeding is excessive.

CONSIDERATION

  1. The Tribunal understood the applicant’s main arguments as to why he should not be liable to pay CGT on the net capital gain received in the 2020 financial year are twofold. Firstly, that the proceeds from the Trust should not be treated as a capital gain as it was the result of a forced sale and there was no profit or gain as the proceeds went to support an ailing business rather than to the applicant for his own personal use or advantage. The applicant’s second main argument revolves around discretionary relief and that the Tribunal ought to disregard the capital gain in his 2020 assessment because of the unprecedented circumstances of COVID-19 and the impact that had on his business and personal circumstances.
  2. The Tribunal accepts on the evidence of the applicant, despite not having been provided with any documentary or corroborative evidence to support this proposition, that he felt compelled to sell shares to help financially support his ailing business due to lockdowns and that in the absence of any evidence to the contrary the proceeds were used for this purpose. It is not in dispute the proceeds distributed to the applicant upon the sale of the shares was in the amount of $201,976.
  3. The applicant argued that he did not make a gain or a profit from the sale of the shares, as he used the funds to support his ailing business, and therefore the proceeds should not be taxed as a gain. In ordinary usage, ‘profit’ is generally defined as the returns, proceeds or revenue from investments or being the amount remaining after deducting relevant costs.[13]. A characterisation of an amount as profit does not rely upon the use thereafter of those proceeds or amounts in the ordinary definition of that term.
  4. The ITAA speaks to proceeds of a CGT event, being the amount received from the disposal of an asset. CGT does not look to whether a ‘profit’ was made, but rather whether there has been a ‘gain’. The ITAA makes clear that the sale of shares in this instance constitute a CGT event. A gain is made when the capital proceeds from the event exceed the cost base of the asset the subject of the disposal. In the applicant’s circumstances, the Tribunal finds the applicant received a gain, in that the proceeds of the sale exceeded the total cost base associated with the asset which he thereafter received. Furthermore, the Tribunal finds it was the applicant’s intention to make such a gain, so that he could use the proceeds of that gain to help with his financial difficulties. The applicant’s use of the proceeds does not change the fact he received a gain from the sale of the shares.
  5. There are exemptions available under the ITAA to reduce the amount of any CGT payable on a capital gain. Division 118 outlines general exemptions, none of which apply to the applicant’s circumstances. Those exemptions and roll-overs as described in this decision above do not apply to the applicant in this instance. Similarly, he is not entitled to the discount capital gain as a result of not having acquired the shares that were disposed of at least 12 months before the sale occurred, being the CGT event.
  6. The applicant raised in his objection to the respondent dated 23 December 2024 that s 118-195 of the ITAA outlined special provisions to be applied where there are forced disposals of assets due to hardship. That section provides for a capital gain from a CGT event that happens in relation to a dwelling acquired from a deceased estate. This section is not applicable to the applicant’s circumstances.
  7. The applicant raised s 102.5 of the ITAA as support for his proposition that the intent behind the CGT provisions is to tax wealth gained from investment growth and not to penalise taxpayers where they make sales due to financial hardship. There is no part of s102.5 of the ITAA that uses this language, but rather that section speaks to the method of calculating net income to include in one’s assessable income. There is no statement or section outlining that the intent, purpose or reason for the disposal of the CGT asset is to be taken into account in any way when considering the capital gain.
  8. The applicant referred to the case of Montgomery as support for the requirement to consider the context and intent when determining tax obligations. The question which that case raised was whether a share of an inducement payment is income for taxation purposes. The case was argued on the basis that the ultimate issue was whether the amounts received were to be treated as income or capital.[14] The Tribunal considers this case does not assist the applicant, as although the judges determined the context and intent behind transactions were relevant to determining a taxpayer’s obligations, the ultimate inquiry hinged on whether the share of an inducement payment was income or capital in nature, not whether the amount should be disregarded completely from tax obligations which the applicant seeks upon this review.
  9. He also raised the case of Roche to support the requirement to recognize extraordinary circumstances impacting taxpayers when making decisions. This case considered the circumstances in which transfer prices, paid for acquisition of property by subsidiaries of multinational corporations, can be adjusted for income tax purposes and required an assessment of the benchmark for arm’s length sales. This applicant’s circumstances are not analogous to the taxpayer company in that case and the Tribunal finds no suggestion therein that can be relied upon to support the proposition that extraordinary circumstances or hardship can be taken into account to remove a CGT liability.
  10. The Tribunal finds there are no discretionary provisions to allow the decision maker under the ITAA to take the intent, reason or purpose for the disposal of the CGT asset into account to thereafter disregard the capital gain on the basis of hardship, forced sales or the use to which the proceeds are put. There is nothing in the ITAA that requires an assessment of a capital gain to consider the context of the disposal or gain, the intention of the disposal or gain or what the gain was thereafter spent on. The ITAA is unambiguous that the sale of shares is a CGT event. It does not provide an ability to consider whether the gain was made as a result of a forced sale, what the gain was used for or whether a person’s circumstances should be considered when determining if a gain has occurred.
  11. The Tribunal furthermore finds there is nothing in the available case law to suggest a decision maker has the ability to consider the reasons behind the disposal of the CGT asset, or to disregard a capital gain because of any hardship or unprecedented circumstances having occurred. A taxpayer can seek a release from their liabilities due to hardship, but that only occurs once the taxation liability has crystallised, not as a means to disregard or amend the calculation of a taxpayer’s assessable income.[15]
  12. The applicant argued the application of the law must be reasonable and to treat the outcome of the sale forced by government intervention as a regular capital gain is unfair and an overreach. The Tribunal understands the applicant has been through difficult experiences in relation to his business due to COVID-19 related lockdowns and appreciates he continues to face financial challenges to this day. He has a substantial taxation liability in excess of $120,000 plus interest. The Tribunal understands the operation of taxation laws can present outcomes that may be considered unduly harsh or unreasonable to an individual. However, the Tribunal is required to apply the law to the facts before it.
  13. The Tribunal has no general powers or discretion to review any decision or to make any decision upon review. The Tribunal is required to apply the law correctly to the facts as found. Its jurisdiction to review decisions comes from the legislation conferring power upon it. In reviewing its decision, the Tribunal can exercise the powers and discretions conferred upon the original decision maker for the purpose of reviewing that decision.[16] It has no greater power than the decision maker, and it is subject to the same constraints. The Tribunal has found above that although there are provisions that allow for a capital gain to be disregarded, there are no legislative provisions within the ITAA that confer a discretion or power to disregard a capital gain in the circumstances argued by the applicant. As such, the Tribunal has no power or discretion in this instance to disregard the gain made by the applicant.
  14. Given the above, the Tribunal finds the sale of the shares, whether forced or not, constituted a CGT event. The proceeds of that sale minus the cost base therefore represented a capital gain, regardless of the subsequent use of those proceeds. The Tribunal finds there are no discounts or exemptions in the Act that apply to reduce the capital gain in the applicant’s circumstances, nor is there any discretion to reduce or waive the gain as a result of hardship, the intention behind the disposal leading to the gain, what the gain was used for or extenuating circumstances. The Tribunal finds the applicant made a capital gain in the 2020 income year of $201,976, such capital gain to be included in his assessable income for that year.
  15. The applicant has sought relief in the form of a setting aside of the assessment the subject of this application, or a referral to the Department of Finance for a waiver of the debt under discretionary powers. The Tribunal has no power to order a waiver of the debt under the Public Governance, Performance and Accountability Act 2013. That is a power exercised by the Minister of Finance. Neither can the Tribunal make a decision about the release of the applicant from his taxation liabilities in this instance under Division 340 of Schedule 1 to the Taxation Administration Act 1953. The decision under review relates to an assessment of what the applicant’s liabilities are, and as such is the only matter for the Tribunal to consider. The applicant has not made an application for a release of his liabilities and there is no such decision before the Tribunal.
  16. The applicant contended pursuing his ‘tax debt...while technically permissible, violates the ethical standards imposed on the Commissioner and offends the spirit of fairness...’ outlined in the Model Litigant Obligations. The only conduct claimed as offending these standards is stated to be the pursuing of the tax debt. The Tribunal is not satisfied on the assertion before it that any conduct has occurred that would be in breach of the Obligations. The Tribunal was given no evidence to suggest the respondent had pursued a debt in an inappropriate manner. The behaviour the applicant appears to take issue with is the imposition of tax upon the capital gain. The Tribunal has found above that the correct assessment of the applicant’s liabilities for the 2020 income year include that capital gain for the reasons provided. The respondent arguing before the Tribunal that their objection decision was the correct decision in these circumstances does not appear to breach any such Obligation. Furthermore, the Tribunal notes, as outlined by the respondent in their SFIC, had a breach occurred, it would not render the liability void or change the decision of the Tribunal, but would provide the applicant with an avenue of complaint external to the Tribunal.

CONCLUSION

  1. The applicant has not discharged his burden of proving the 2020 assessment was excessive or otherwise incorrect. As the Tribunal has found the applicant had a capital gain in the financial year ending 2020 and that there are no grounds upon which that capital gain can be disregarded or waived, the decision under review is affirmed.
