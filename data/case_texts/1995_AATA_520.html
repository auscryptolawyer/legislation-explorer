Administrative Appeals Tribunal



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

INCOME TAX - Profit on sale of land - whether purchased for resale at a profit - whether assessable income of partnership



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936



AAT Decision No 10192



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL)

) Nos VT93/562-565

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re

Applicants



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal Mr B.H. Pascoe, Senior Member

Date 18 May 1995

Place Melbourne

Decision 1. The Tribunal sets aside the decisions under review and remits the matter to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with the reasons for decision attached hereto.

2. Reserve liberty to either party to apply.



..............................................

Senior Member

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL)

) Nos VT93/562-565

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re

Applicants



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION

18 May 1995 Mr B.H. Pascoe, Senior Member

  1. These are applications by two applicant companies L Pty Ltd and Z Pty Ltd seeking review of decisions of the respondent Commissioner of Taxation to disallow objections against assessments of income tax for the years ended 30 June 1987, 1988 and 1989 in respect of L Pty Ltd and the year ended 30 June 1987 in respect of Z Pty Ltd. As the substantive matter was the same for both applicants the applications were heard together.
  2. The primary issue related to the profit on sale of land purchased by the two applicants as tenants in common in July 1979 and sold during the year ended 30 June 1987. The profit was disclosed as $210,081 and described as a capital profit in a partnership income tax return of L Pty Ltd and Z Pty Ltd for the year ended 30 June 1987. The respondent treated the profit as assessable income and included two-thirds in the assessable income of L Pty Ltd and one-third in the assessable income of Z Pty Ltd in that year. Z Pty Ltd was a trustee of the Z Family Trust and the income was ultimately assessed to the beneficiaries of the trust.
  3. The applicants were represented by Mr Power of counsel and the respondent by Mr Murphy of counsel. Evidence was given by an accountant who prepared and lodged the partnership income tax returns for the years ended 30 June 1980 and 1981, a bookkeeper who was employed by one of the shareholders in L Pty Ltd and who had some involvement in the preparation of the accounts in subsequent years and by the two directors of L Pty Ltd. Evidence of Mr Z, a director of Z Pty Ltd, was provided by way of affidavit as age and illness precluded his personal attendance at the hearing.
  4. The land in question consisted of 56 acres of vacant rural land some 10-12 kilometres from Melbourne. It was originally purchased in 1973 as tenants in common by L Pty Ltd as to one-third, Z Pty Ltd as to one-sixth, M Pty Ltd as to one-third and D Pty Ltd as to one-sixth interest. The evidence of F, one of the directors of L Pty Ltd, was that the land was purchased for $305,000. Z Pty Ltd provided $60,000, M Pty Ltd and D Pty Ltd between them provided $60,000 and $185,000 was obtained under a mortgage. In 1979, M Pty Ltd and D Pty Ltd refused to pay any share of interest on the mortgage and the mortgagee entered possession of the property and proposed to sell it by auction. Both F and G, the other director of L Pty Ltd, gave evidence that, as they had both provided personal guarantees to the mortgagee, it was decided to purchase the land from the mortgagee for an amount equal to the amount owing, $170,000. For reasons which were not made clear the land was purchased by L Holdings Pty Ltd on or before 8 May 1979. On 1 July 1979 a contract of sale for $270,000 was entered into between L Holdings Pty Ltd as vendor and L Pty Ltd and Z Pty Ltd as purchasers as tenants in common as to two-thirds and one-third interests respectively. The transfer was never registered. In December, 1986 the land was sold to J Pty Ltd for $450,000.
  5. Both F and G in their evidence stated that, at the time of the original purchase in 1973, they had been led to believe that the land would be rezoned as industrial land. Both directors were involved with other companies engaged in building and construction. They stated that their original intention in having L Pty Ltd acquire the land in conjunction with the three other companies was to acquire land for use as a storage yard for building materials. Whilst F stated that his share of the land would be 14 acres, G suggested that the share would be 9 acres. There appeared to be the not unusual confusion by these witnesses between themselves as individuals, a company which they might control individually and a company which they might control jointly. My assumption is that the background for the comments on respective ownership of a number of acres was that both witnesses, F in particular, clearly regarded Z Pty Ltd as a financier who had over many years supported them financially in several ventures and whose sole interest was to obtain a return on that financial support. As a result, it would appear that F regarded himself as being entitled to a one-quarter share of the land, presumably subject to satisfying Z Pty Ltd requirement for some financial return. How this was to be accomplished was not explained. The subsequent acquisition from the mortgagee was said to have been made to protect F and G from exposure to the personal guarantees and with the ongoing intention of acquiring appropriate land for use as a storage yard. Again, it was not made clear but it would appear that Z Pty Ltd provided the finance for the purchase in May 1979, subsequently replaced by a mortgage from third parties. The affidavit of Z referred to the purchase of the land as follows:

“3. It was always my intention as a Director of one partner in acquiring this vacant block of land of 56 acres in...to hold the property in the long term of my grandchildren who would benefit from any increase in its value in later years via the Z Family Trust. From my memory the property was a vacant block with little potential at the time.



  1. I also remember that L Holdings Pty Ltd purchased the whole of the property from the mortgagee,...in May 1979, and that the partnership purchased the property from L Holdings Pty Ltd on 1 July 1979 for $276,000.00.

  2. The applicant shared one third of the partnership interest while L Pty Ltd shared the other two thirds as per the partnership agreement between the parties.

  3. My intention in relation to the property did not change with this re-purchase of the property by the partnership and it remained to hold on this property for the later benefit of my grandchildren.

  4. I was always guided by F in relation to the partnership matters but to my knowledge it was always intended by the partnership that its purchase of the property was as a long term acquisition and not for resale in the immediate future.”
  1. The sale in December 1986 was said to have taken place because Z “wanted the money - his share of the money and he wanted to get out”. Again, the evidence was not clear, but it appears that the purchaser on this occasion, J Pty Ltd, was a company jointly controlled by F and G. The land in question was apparently never rezoned and the $450,000 was said to be the market value of the land in December 1986.
  2. Much of the respondents argument was based on the fact that the balance sheets included in the partnership income tax returns for the years ended 30 June 1980 and 1981 showed the land as included under the heading “Stock of Properties”. The return for the year ended 30 June 1979 was not produced in evidence nor were any earlier returns. In the return for the year ended 30 June 1982 and subsequent years the heading for this item was changed to “Investments - Properties”. The accountant who prepared the 1980 and 1981 returns stated that he believed that he had simply followed the terminology which had been adopted by the previous accountant and could not recall having received any instructions from his client. The 1982 and subsequent years returns were prepared by an accountant employed by companies controlled by G. He was not called to give evidence and there was no evidence provided on the reason for the change in the wording of the balance sheet.
  3. The whole question of accounting for the land was unsatisfactory. The bookkeeper who did give evidence had little involvement in the preparation of accounts. His primary role was the maintenance of cash books and other books of original entry. Whilst the contract of sale of the land in 1979 showed the consideration as $270,000, the amount appearing in the 1980 balance sheet was $276,000. This was added to an existing balance in the Stock of Properties account of $32,961. This balance was said to be land at R purchased some time previously. The 1981 accounts showed the subject land as having increased to $278,000 and included a further addition of $222,354 described as “additions - Richmond”. In the 1982 accounts the Richmond land had disappeared and this was explained as having been incorrectly included as an asset of the partnership and consequently transferred out of the books in the subsequent year. Prior to continuing the saga of inadequately explained accounting entries, it should be said that, commencing with the 1980 return, an amount of unrealised profit was shown in the accounts. This 1980 return showed an unrealised profit of $69,052 at 1 July, 1979 reduced by profit of $29,692 realised during the year and a balance of $39,360 at 30 June, 1980. This was said to be profit from the sale of land at S which was being accounted for, and included as assessable income, on a profit emerging basis as proceeds of sale were collected. No change to this account occurred in 1981 or 1982 but, at 30 June 1983, it had been reduced to $7,400. The explanation for this was that the then accountant had taken the view that the land at R had been part of the land at S and the relevant cost written off against the unrealised profit from the sale of S. The evidence given during the hearing clearly showed that this view had been incorrect as the land at R was distinct and continued to be owned by the partnership. The fact that the reduction in unrealised profit was $31,960 and the amount shown for the land at R was $32,961 was one of the minor unexplained differences. Consequently, the balance shown under the item “Investments - Properties” became $278,000, being the subject land only. In 1984 this figure rose to $281,833. The additional $3,833 turned out to be an amount written off and claimed as an expense in 1982 under the heading “General Expenses”. This amount was written off to correct several loan accounts which were not in balance when the new accountant took over in 1982. Why the amount was claimed as an allowable deduction and what relevance it had in 1984 to the cost of the subject land remains a mystery. In 1985 the cost of the land was shown as $270,890. Part of the reduction of $10,943 appears to be a write off of the remaining balance of $7,400 in the unrealised profit account, which also disappeared and the bookkeeper “thought that the adjustment to cost had something to do with unrealised profits from previous years”. In 1986 the land cost had increased to $271,480 by some unexplained additional expenditure of $590. Finally in 1987 the closing balance of cost of land was $32,961 which was the reinstatement of the land at R which had incorrectly disappeared out of the accounts in 1983. It is then assumed, because there was no satisfactory accounting evidence, that this adjustment reduced the cost of the subject land to $238,519. The sale price was $450,000 which, on the surface, indicates a profit of $211,481. The difference of $1,400 between this amount and the profit of $210,081 shown in the return lodged for 1987 as a “capital profit” may well have been the legal expenses on the sale. In any event it was that amount of $210,081 which was assessed by the respondent as to two-thirds to L Pty Ltd and one-third to Z Pty Ltd. Clearly this amount of profit is incorrect.
  4. In view of the lack of adequate accounting evidence and the obvious error in the accounts over the years the Tribunal requested the parties to attempt an agreement on the actual quantum of profit in dispute. On the morning of the second day of hearing counsel advised the Tribunal that the parties had agreed on a figure of $198,211 as the total profit on sale with the share of L Pty Ltd as $135,474 and of Z Pty Ltd as $62,737. There is an obvious arithmetic error of $5,000 involved in this figure because the share of Z Pty Ltd is meant to be one-third of the total and L Pty Ltd two-thirds. The way this figure was provided indicates that the total was calculated by adding the two shares together rather than the other way around. In fact, the figures supplied were identical, apart from $5,000 for Z Pty Ltd, to those shown in the partnership return lodged which were:-

Operating Loss Capital Profit Total

L Pty Ltd (4,580) 140,054 135,474

Z Pty Ltd (2,290) 70,027 67,737

(6870) 210,081 203,211

Whilst it is tempting to accept the agreed figures it is not appropriate to do so when they are clearly wrong. A simple calculation demonstrates this. The land was purchased for $270,000 and sold for $450,000. Without any allowance for costs of purchase and sale or any other costs, the maximum profit is $180,000. It may well be that allowance has been made for the $39,360 of unrealised profit in 1980 which was never brought to account as income but, somehow, written off against the cost of unrelated land, but no evidence or comment was provided in relation to this. In addition, it may well be appropriate to take into account in calculating an ultimate profit on sale of land the costs incurred by the applicants prior to 1979 when they repurchased it after previously owing a one-half interest from 1973. Unfortunately, no evidence of these costs was provided at the hearing.

  1. It is appropriate to comment on other land owned by the partnership. The land at R was purchased prior to 1979 and consisted of 124 acres of farming land. F gave the reason for purchase as, whilst it was farm land it was in a proposed expansion corridor for Melbourne. He said “There was a time that Melbourne was supposed to be expanded in the corridors, but nothing happened.” This land is apparently still owned by the same parties with the applicants having a fifty per cent interest. Land at S was purchased about 1974. Again this was farm land but with 80 acres zoned as residential next to an existing township. The reason for sale was said to be to provide funds to repurchase the subject land. Given the limited evidence on the financing of the purchase of the subject land it is not clear that funds were provided from the sale of S. Of relevance, however, was the fact that the partnership returned the profit on sale of S as assessable income. The only other land was that in Richmond on which F stated three factories were developed and sold. He could not explain why the land was accounted for originally in the partnership but then taken out of these accounts.
  2. Mr Power submitted that the applicants were not carrying on a business of dealing in land and the subject land was not acquired for resale at a profit. He also sought to argue that the applicants were not in partnership but solely co-owners of land. Whilst he lead the Tribunal through the various principles of partnership he overlooked the definition contained in the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”). Section 6 of the Act provides that “partnership” means an association of persons carrying on business as partners or in receipt of income jointly, but does not include a company”. According to the returns lodged the applicants received jointly various amounts of rent and interest in 1979, 1980, 1981, 1983, 1984 and 1985 and in the two earlier years included profit on sale of land as joint income. Certainly in each of those years the two applicants were in receipt of income jointly and satisfied the definition of partnership. In each return of income including that for the year ended 30 June, 1987 the words partners and partnership were used frequently. For example, the 1987 return stated:

“During the year the partnership sold a property situated at...which it had held for a number of years for rental purposes.”

Whilst the language used is not determinative of the question, I am satisfied that the applicants were in partnership for the purposes of the Act.

  1. Mr Murphy, for the respondent submitted that the profit was assessable as having been derived in the ordinary course of business of the partnership and, in any event, as profit on land purchased for resale at a profit. As stated previously, emphasis was given to the classification of the land in the accounts of the partnership at the time of purchase. It was said that the obvious errors in later accounts should lead the Tribunal to reject the later classification in favour of that used in the contemporaneous documents of 1980 and 1981. No witnesses were produced to explain the reason for change or that the description used in 1980 and 1981 was incorrect. It was further argued that the stated reasons for purchase given by F and G ought not to be accepted. The land was purchased in 1979. By which time, it was said, there must have been grave doubts on the ability to have the land rezoned for use for their alleged purpose. Whilst no oral evidence was provided by Mr Z and his affidavit described his intention in relation to the subject land as being to hold it long term for the benefit of his grandchildren, he was described by F and G as the financier, sleeping partner who did not take a direct involvement as long as he achieved a return on his money.
  2. In his evidence F, when asked about the land at S and what was proposed in relation to the 80 acres, stated “we were going to hold on to it until we could use it, like, for developing - for houses or something like this”. He agreed that it was a sort of land bank and, when asked whether that was the same with the land at R, replied, “that’s right”. Whilst it may be accepted that the parties adopted a long term view on the purchase of land, including the subject land, I am satisfied, on the evidence, that the primary expectation was to develop the land after rezoning and dispose of it at a profit. Even if I was to accept the evidence of F and G that they had an intention of using their respective shares of the subject land for use as a storage yards it is clear that L Pty Ltd was not the entity which would so use the land. It would have been necessary for the respective interests in the land to be transferred to some other separate entity more directly connected with each of their own building interests. In all likelihood, Z Pty Ltd would require this to be done at market value so as to derive his share of the proper profit on any increase in value of the land. The description of each of the properties, the evidence of F and G, the affidavit of Mr Z and the original accounting treatment all lead to the conclusion that the land was purchased for the purpose of profit making by sale or from the carrying out of a profit making undertaking or scheme so as to fall squarely within the provisions of s. 25A of the Act. The evidence showed that each land purchase by the parties had been land which was expected to be capable of development and the subject land was part of this pattern.
  3. It is no reflection on the submissions of both counsel that I do not canvas the authorities referred to by them. This is a simple case of the intention of the parties at the time of purchase of the subject land and is a determination of the result of the evidence and facts of this particular case.
  4. As indicated earlier I am unable to determine the actual quantum of assessable income derived by the partnership in the year ended 30 June, 1987 and therefore the correct taxable income of either party. In the case of L Pty Ltd there were claims made under s. 80 of the Act for losses of prior years in the return for the year ended 30 June 1987 and subsequent years. Consequently the matter must be remitted to the respondent to amend assessments in each of these years in accordance with these reasons for decision. Given the apparent lack of ability on both sides to date to arrive at an appropriate calculation of the profit, I give leave to the parties to provide submissions to the Tribunal for determination of quantum if they are unable to agree on the amounts of assessable or taxable income for either or both parties for the relevant years.
  5. The applicants also sought review of the additional tax imposed under s. 223 of the Act. The additional tax imposed was 15% of the tax assessed. In my opinion that amount of additional tax is appropriate in the circumstances of this case and was properly imposed as a result of a misleading statement in the relevant returns lodged. The history of the partnership, the somewhat careless approach to proper accounting and the lack of information provided leads to this view.

I certify that this and the 10 ten preceding pages are a true copy of the decision and reasons for decision herein of

Mr B.H. Pascoe, Senior Member
