Last Updated: 29 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - allowable deductions - what constitutes "thermal insulation materials".

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss. 82KM(1), 82KN(1), 82KN(7), 174;

Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986 - s.221.

Case U36 87 A.T.C. 266.

Applicant

COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

No. NT86/7820

AAT Decision No 4483

Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

8 July 1988

Sydney

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT86/7820

Applicant

Respondent

Tribunal : Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

Date : 8 July 1988

Decision : The Tribunal affirms the decision of the Commissioner upon the objection under review.

(Sgd.) PETER M. ROACH,

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT86/7820

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re :

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

8 July 1988 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. The Applicant in this reference is a married lady who, in the early 1980's, together with her husband, was engaged about the task of building their first home. As he had a background in engineering and she was a school teacher, it is not surprising that they applied themselves assiduously and carefully to the task. They built their home in the hinterland of Sydney in a new and undeveloped area. On one side of the house the walls at both levels were wholly glazed. It was quite different with the western wall. There a window was set at the upper level and, at ground level, a glass door was set in a single opening. It led into the laundry and from there to the living room adjoining. Windows to door height were set in the living room wall immediately adjoining the laundry with the upper two-thirds being hinged. The lack of development to the western side of the house, whether by way of structures or trees, was such that in summer the western wall was exposed to the sun in mid-summer from about 1 p.m. to sunset. Because it was commonplace in summer for external temperatures to reach 40 degrees Celsius and more, and because mid-week both husband and wife were absent from their home during the day, the home became grossly overheated and tended not to cool off until the early hours of the morning.

2. With a view to overcoming that problem they diligently investigated the many possibilities available which could have resulted in the provision of some relief. Ultimately they determined upon a product which was new to the Australian market. It comprised shutters set over the glazed wall spaces. They were capable of being raised and lowered in a controlled movement through vertical U-channels. Lowered they quite closely sealed off the aperture. When raised, the panels would be recessed in a metal cabinet mounted above the apertures. The shutters were capable of being operated from inside the house. The Applicants chose a style of shutter which was fabricated of coloured plastic. Each section of the shutter was characterized by a hollowed centre and was designed to interlock with adjoining sections. They chose an off-white colour for the shutter material on the basis that it would be more effectively heat-resistant than any other colour.

3. They might have chosen aluminium shutters in which the hollow-core comparable to that on the plastic sections would have been filled with insulating material and would be fitted at intervals with metal rods designed to provide additional strength in the assembly. I accept the evidence that, had they chosen the latter structure, it would have been a useful security device but that, by using the hollow plastic material without reinforcing, its security functions would be minimal. I also accept the evidence that no special security measures were taken with the far greater expanses of window space on other walls.

4. Having installed the shutters, they experienced the advantages which they had expected. In summer by closing the shutters by day they could reduce the internal house temperature and block out the glare from the western sun. I accept that ample light was available by day through the windows in other walls. In winter, by closing the shutters of a night, they were able to more effectively preserve such heat as was available and, by opening the shutters by day, maximize the heat input to the premises. They also accepted a disadvantage that they had expected, namely that the housing for the shutters, although neat, provided an unattractive protrusion from the side walls of the property. They remained in residence at the property until April 1987. At the time of the hearing, they were residing with the parents-in-law of the Applicant while their solar-designed home was under construction. I accept that they were and remain very much committed to the preservation of natural resources.

  1. When the installation was effected during the year of income ended 30 June 1983, the sum of $885 was paid by the Applicant and that sum was in due course claimed as a deduction in her return of income as Item 20 "Home Insulation Expenses".
  2. When her notice of assessment issued on 25 January 1984 the claim to that deduction was disallowed, the explanation being:- "disallowed as not being eligible insulation material". The Applicant on 27 February 1984 objected, stating as her grounds of objection:

"(1) It is contended that the 'shutters' satisfies the requirements of S.82KM, i.e. they do not perform a decorative function, they are used on the western side of the house only when the sun is out and they roll into a box above the window and are not visible when not in use.

(2) The 'shutters' were purchased solely for their insulating properties i.e. reflects up to 80% of the sun's rays in summer and retains heat for warmth in winter."

  1. On 2 May 1984 the Commissioner gave notice that the claim "has been fully considered and has been disallowed". That was followed by a request from the Applicant that the Commissioner's decision on the objection be referred for independent review. The letter requesting that reference was not dated. In due course the Commissioner transmitted the request for reference and the formal documents related to it to the Tribunal for determination.
  2. Not having been resolved at a Preliminary Conference, the matter in due course came on for hearing. When it did it appeared that:-
    • (a) the relevant provisions of the Act were only in force from 24 June, 1981 to 23 August, 1983 [s.82KN(7)];
    • (b) the provisions in question have not been the subject of any earlier consideration by any Court or tribunal; and

(c) that the Tribunal had no jurisdiction to review the Commissioner's determination upon the objection.

9. The last point presents an insuperable obstacle for the Applicant. Why the point was not raised prior to hearing was not explained. Nor was any explanation offered - or sought – as to why the request for reference for independent review was responded to when it clearly did not comply with the statutory prescriptions as to matters prerequisite to vesting authority to review in this Tribunal or its predecessor – the Boards of Review. That the narrow rigidity of the procedural requirements of those times was unjust is not disputed. As a result of the Passage of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986 such inflexibility can no longer occasion injustice, but the provisions of s. 221 of that Act expressly denied any authority to grant relief in relation to such omissions which took effect prior to 1 July 1986. Why the injustices of the past should have been continued in relation to applicants such as the present Applicant has never been explained. But the fact remains that, due to an oversight on the part of her accountant, the request for reference was not made in time. Therefore this Tribunal has no jurisdiction to effect a review of the determination of the Commissioner upon the objection.

10. Nor is that the only matter for concern. The question can reasonably be asked:

* why did a matter so old (the 1983 year);

* involving so small an amount in tax ($347.34);

* concerning a point in tax law which operated for only twenty-six months; and

* which possibly concerned very few taxpayers,

come to be submitted to independent review at all? The cost to the community through the office of the Commissioner and to the community through this Tribunal must have substantially exceeded the tax involved for this taxpayer.

  1. The answer to such questions lies with the Commissioner. If the Applicant is but one of many taxpayers pressing similar claims, it is obviously appropriate that one such case - but not this one - should have been presented. If the claim of the Applicant was so lacking in merit as to be "unarguable" in the civil procedure sense, it was clearly appropriate that the interests of the community be defended. Consistency in decision-making is an attribute of justice.
  2. If such considerations operated, the reference upon request of a suitable case would be appropriate. Such a reference would result in an independent ruling on a point of interpretation as to what the Parliament intended, previously only interpreted publicly by a Ruling from within the office of the Commissioner: a ruling is quite likely to express what the Commissioner intended the draft legislation to mean, as distinct from expressing the meaning to be discerned upon an objective consideration of the terms of the Act.
  3. But there is a special significance in raising this matter. The costs of providing for an independent review, although now substantially less than they were prior to 1 July 1986, have come to be such that persons such as the Applicant who now find themselves in contention with the Commissioner on any point are being required to put forward fees of $240 for each and every application for independent review - regardless of how small that matter may be in the affairs of the community and regardless of how large the burden of the fee and the tax may be in the affairs of the individual.
  4. Those difficulties do not face persons who are claimants upon - rather than contributors to - the public purse in many of the proceedings before this Tribunal. Had the Applicant been a claimant on the Social Services vote she would not only have been provided with an opportunity to have a decision under the Social Services Act considered independently before a Social Security Appeals Tribunal without having to pay any fee, but, if dissatisfied with any subsequent decision, she could insist upon a review before this Tribunal - again without fee and, certainly, without obligation to pay a fee of $240 for each year (if more than one) to which the dispute related.
  5. That circumstance casts a special responsibility on the Commissioner and his officers to most thoroughly review all aspects of objections before deciding to disallow them and in doing so to review such objections far more thoroughly and skillfully than in the past.

16. I also mention these matters for two other reasons related to the present circumstances of the Tribunal and the difficulties it faces in providing for the just determination of all matters which have been referred to it. My basic reason for doing so is that, with the limited resources available to this Tribunal, too much effort is being expended on matters of little or no importance to the community. The present case is an example. That it should never have been referred in the first place, is not a matter of any great consequence. At the worst it represents a mistake such as anyone might make - including any member or officer of this Tribunal. If anything, what is the greater cause for concern is the fact that, coming to light as late in the history of the matter as it did, that suggests that either the officer who discovered the matter did so in the course of re-working ground previously worked by others; or that no attention had previously been given to the matter.

17. I have said that this would seem to be a matter of little importance to the community. It seems unlikely that the issue - the interpretation of a fine and rare point in the Act, of no long-term significance - warranted putting the community to the expense it had to bear through the officers of the Commissioner and through this Tribunal. (However, I do not suggest that the matter is of such little importance to the Applicant. For her nearly $350 was at stake: $350 which, should her interpretation of the Act be correct, the Commonwealth was not entitled to claim from her. It was not momentous, but it was not trifling.) But for the Commonwealth and the community what was at stake was not a claim to $350, but rather whether it should spend possibly $2,000 or more of public money in an endeavour to justify its claim to the $350.

18. The second reason for raising the matter is occasioned by the current wave of enthusiasm for a recently announced "tax amnesty". That circumstance makes it opportune to repeat a proposal advanced in Case U36 a decision of this Tribunal delivered on 4 February 1987 and reported at 87 A.T.C. 266. It was then said (at page 290) in the context of penalties for incorrect return or non-return of income that:

"it might be an interesting Bi-centennial project to declare a period of tax amnesty in which persons would be offered the opportunity to make voluntary disclosures without risking liability for any culpability factor. Income, which might otherwise remain forever undetected, may come to light and be taxed".

19. The same principle as was there proposed might well be applied in relation to the determination of large numbers of undetermined objections involving relatively small sums and, in particular, sums which will be less than the cost to the community of procuring the determination of the issues; and which involve no great issue of principle.

20. I propose for the consideration of those with the power to implement the proposal that it might be in the best interests of the community and in the interests of advancing the prompt determination of the claims of other applicants still awaiting independent determination before this Tribunal to consider the implementation of a further "tax amnesty". It is proposed on the basis that:

(a) The Commissioner would have an unfettered discretion as to whether to extend the amnesty to any particular applicant. (Some matters involving small amounts may raise issues of "principle" or may be involved with other matters involving the same or related taxpayers so as to be fairly considered to be that part of a larger issue in dollar terms);

(b) The amnesty should be plainly declared to be a Bi-centennial project, only open for a limited period and then only in relation to disputes on foot in relation to years of assessment up to 30 June in a nominated year: probably 1987;

(c) The amnesty should be implemented on the basis of the Commissioner issuing amended assessments to allow in full objections under which the disputed amount of tax does not exceed a figure to be nominated: perhaps $500 or $1,000;

(d) In cases involving amounts not exceeding, say, $2,000 in tax, an offer could be made by the Commissioner to resolve matters on the basis that amended assessments will issue which will reduce the tax payable by the applicant by the amount selected for the purposes of the preceding clause plus one half of the tax in issue in excess of that figure. Applicants would be free to accept or reject the offer.
  1. I suspect that if the proposal were implemented not only would there be many citizens with something to celebrate, but in addition the entire taxpaying community would be advantaged. The public purse would be relieved of the burden of providing for the resolution of those disputes and the community would be assured that other pending disputes, and disputes yet to come, would be resolved more expeditiously, and, therefore more economically and satisfactorily than has been the case for many years.
  2. Nonetheless, as the Applicant has acted in good faith in responding to the assertions of authority and jurisdiction by this Tribunal - assertions made in ignorance of the fact that it had no jurisdiction, I determined to proceed to hear the brief evidence and arguments sought to be presented on behalf of the Applicant as to whether the requirements of the Section had been satisfied. I did so on the basis that, if I came to express a view on the substantive matter in dispute which favoured the Applicant, the Commissioner might exercise his discretionary power under s.174 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") to issue a reduced assessment.

23. The Commissioner's representative presented in evidence a copy of the Second Reading Speech by the Minister for Finance, Senator The Hon. Dame Margaret Guilfoyle, D.B.E. when presenting the relevant Bill in the Senate. The comments touching on the present issue were quite brief. The Senator said:-"HOME INSULATION COSTS

This concession will provide tax assistance to people who take steps that have an effect of reducing energy consumption."

Perusal of the Bill shows that it was not everyone who was to reduce energy consumption in the matter of home insulation who was to benefit. S.82KN(1) provides:-

"... This subsection applies to an amount paid by a taxpayer... in respect of thermal insulation material..."

S.82KM(1) defines such material in the following terms:-"In this subdivision, unless the contrary intention appears ... thermal insulation material, in relation to a dwelling, means any material or substance installed in, or to be installed in, the dwelling primarily and principally for the purpose of reducing the transfer of heat between the interior of the dwelling and exterior of the dwelling but does not include -

(a) any material or substance the whole or part of which performs, or is to perform a structural or decorative function in connection with the dwelling; or

(b) any material or substance that is used, or is to be used, in connection with a material or substance of the kind referred to in paragraph (a)."

The same subsection provides that the phrase "installed in" includes "affixed to".

  1. To satisfy the requirements of the Section, the Applicant must establish that what was affixed to the premises constituted "thermal insulation material" within the meaning of the positive element of the definition; and then establish that the exclusory provisions of the definition have no application.
  2. I am satisfied that:-
* the plastic which constituted the shutters fell within the description of any "material or substance" - no more or less so than materials and substances such as fibre-glass, whether loose or in batts;

* it was a material or substance "installed in", in the sense of being affixed to, a dwelling;

* it was installed for the purpose of reducing the transfer of heat between the interior of the dwelling and the exterior of the dwelling; and

* it was effective for that purpose, both in the sense that it reduced the build-up of heat within the dwelling from outside during summer and because, in winter, it reduced the rate of heat-loss from the interior to the exterior.

That being so I am satisfied that the positive elements of the test are satisfied. I therefore proceed to consider the negative tests.

26. The first point to be considered as to the negative tests is whether the shutter system had a "structural function in connection with the dwelling". In my view it did not. I think that the term "structural" is used in the sense of referring to parts of the essential framework of a building: something which has a function in holding the structure together, or giving to it stability or strength. The installation did not become so by reason only that it was affixed to a building.

  1. The second question to be considered is whether the installation performed "a ... decorative function in connection with the dwelling". I understand that terra to have the meaning of referring to additions or alterations to a structure designed to beautify it - whether successful or not. I find nothing in the type of installation in question which could be fairly said to beautify or enhance the appearance of the building.
  2. Accordingly, I am satisfied that, although the legislation is so drafted as to be anything but clear, on balance I am of the view that the Applicant has satisfied the criteria specified for "deductions for expenditure in respect of home insulation". Accordingly, had I the authority to do so, I would have directed that the determination of the Commissioner upon the objection under review be varied and that the objection be allowed.
  3. However, for reasons already stated, I have no such. authority. Accordingly, the determination of the Tribunal must be that the decision of the Commissioner upon the objection under review be affirmed.
