Re Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v Cyclone Scaffolding Pty Limited [1987] FCA 455 (11 December 1987)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

And: CYCLONE SCAFFOLDING PTY. LIMITED

No. G144 of 1987

Income Tax

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Bowen C.J.(1), Beaumont(1) and Wilcox(2) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - assessable income - tax accounting - trading stock - depreciated plant - business of hiring equipment - payments to owner in respect of damaged or lost equipment - whether payments assessable income as arising from sale of trading stock - whether income according to ordinary usage - whether special accounting treatment gave a substantially correct reflex of taxpayer's true income - whether other methods appropriate.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss.25, 54 et seq., 59.

HEARING

SYDNEY

11:12:1987

Counsel and Solicitors for Appellant: G.K. Downes Q.C. with C.L. Lonergan instructed by Australian Government Solicitor

Counsel and Solicitors for Respondent: A.M. Gleeson Q.C. with A.H. Slater instructed by Murphy & Moloney

ORDER

Appeal dismissed with costs.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

Cyclone Scaffolding Pty. Limited ("the taxpayer") appealed to the Supreme Court of New South Wales against its assessment of income for the year ended 30 June 1981 ("the assessment"). Hunt J. allowed the appeal and set aside the assessment (see (1987) 18 ATR 148); the Commissioner now appeals from these orders. The question in the appeal is whether the accounting procedures adopted by the taxpayer were, in the language of Sir Owen Dixon in Carden's Case (Commissioner of Taxes (S.A.) v. Executor Trustee and Agency Company of South Australia Limited [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108 at p 154), "calculated to give a substantially correct reflex of the taxpayer's true income." It is a case, as was Carden's Case, where "the admissibility of the method which in fact has been pursued must depend upon its actual appropriateness" (ibid). Hunt J. held that the taxpayer's method was appropriate but the Commissioner disputes this.

2. The Commissioner's assessment accepted the taxpayer's income as returned but added the following item which was in dispute in the Supreme Court:

"Profit on sale of general scaffolding,

timber, ply, and climbers not brought to

account . . . as assessable income 325,734"

3. The Commissioner assessed this amount pursuant to s.25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") as income according to the ordinary concepts and usages of mankind (see, for example, Commercial and General Acceptance Ltd. v. The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia (1977) 137 CLR 373 at p 383).

4. In order to understand the rival contentions of the parties, and, indeed, in order to understand why a particular accounting method was called for in order to give a reflex of the taxpayer's true income, some background facts should be mentioned. The taxpayer's equipment comprises tubes, fittings by which the tubes are joined together, modular scaffolding, frames and braces, timber planks, plywood sheeting and related gear; the life of equipment hired out is in the range of 10 to 15 years; the average duration of a hiring is three months. It is impracticable to trace every piece of equipment. Although the taxpayer does sell equipment, the major part of its business is the hiring out of equipment: hiring is the rule; sale is the exception. Because it is impracticable to trace every piece of its equipment with a view to determining the date of its purchase, the taxpayer has adopted a number of practices in its accounting procedures which, it claims, give a substantially correct reflection of its true income. Whatever the exact position of any particular sale, the taxpayer treats the proceeds of sales of equipment as relating to items acquired in that year of income, i.e. "last in - first out". Payments received for the replacement of equipment lost or destroyed are treated in the same way. Where there is an excess of such "sales" (including payments received for the replacement of equipment) over purchases in a particular year of income, the excess is treated as the sale of equipment recorded as being on hand in the immediately preceding year of income. Equipment purchased is regarded as trading stock up to the end of the year of income in which it is treated as having been purchased; thereafter, it is treated as plant and depreciated in accordance with ss.54 et seq. of the Act. Where equipment is sold, or notionally sold, in the year of income in which it is treated as having been purchased, the profit is returned as income, being trading stock. But sales, or notional sales, in subsequent years are returned as income only to the extent required by s.59.

5. For many years, the Commissioner has accepted the taxpayer's procedure as a valid approach in accounting terms; until recently, the Commissioner has also accepted that the taxpayer's accounting treatment gave a substantially correct reflex of the taxpayer's true income (and see the decision of the Board of Review in Case 69 CTBR (N.S.) Vol. 5 at p 429). It will be seen that there are advantages for both parties in the taxpayer's procedures: in the first year, the taxpayer is disadvantaged and the Commissioner is advantaged, by the taxpayer's loss of a claim for depreciation in respect of what are, in the main, capital assets. In subsequent years, the taxpayer is advantaged, and the Commissioner is disadvantaged, by the treatment of the equipment as fixed assets when a minor proportion of it may have been trading stock.

6. Whilst still acknowledging the validity of the taxpayer's accounting treatment, the Commissioner now seeks to dispute that the taxpayer's method throws up a substantially correct reflex of its true income, at least in one aspect: the Commissioner now seeks to say that the proceeds of sales, or of notional sales, should be returned as income not only in the first year (as is common ground) but in all subsequent years as well.

7. Hunt J. concluded from the primary facts that the taxpayer's dominant or substantial business was the hiring out of scaffolding equipment; and that, when it purchased equipment, the taxpayer did so "with no more than the contemplation of a mere possibility that it could be sold in the future if required." Since property could not, at one time, be both trading stock and plant, his Honour held that the equipment was plant, in which event the only tax payable by the taxpayer was by virtue of the operation of s.59. Hunt J. said (at pp 155-6):

"Very few procedures can be completely free from any

arbitrary approach. None could be devised in relation

to equipment of such a nature as that with which the

taxpayer here deals. It is clearly impossible to

devise a procedure in relation to the taxpayer's

business in dealing with equipment of this nature

which is both precisely accurate and practical. No

more appropriate procedure has been suggested by the

Commissioner either in cross-examination or by way of

address. I can think of none. By treating its

equipment as trading stock during the financial year

in which it was purchased, the taxpayer has, in my

view, adopted a procedure for taxation purposes which

is too favourable to the Commissioner; most equipment

actually sold is disposed of within that first year of

the date of its purchase, so that there is little if

any set-off in subsequent years in favour of the

taxpayer against the inclusion in favour of the

Commissioner of the damages or penalty as the sale of

trading stock in that first year. The Commissioner

cannot complain of that. I am satisfied that the

taxpayer's accounting procedures are calculated to

give a substantially correct reflex of its true income

for taxation purposes."

10. The Commissioner seeks first to challenge his Honour's finding that the taxpayer's substantial purpose in purchasing the equipment was to hire it out rather than to resell it. Particular reliance is placed by the Commissioner upon a display advertisement placed by the taxpayer in the "Yellow Pages" telephone directory, offering equipment for "sale or hire"; it is suggested that this indicates that the sale of equipment was regarded by the taxpayer as at least as important as its hire.

11. It could not seriously be disputed that the conclusion arrived at by the primary Judge was open to him. The taxpayer called evidence from a senior executive to the effect that the main orientation of its activities was towards the hiring, rather than the sale, of equipment. Hunt J. accepted this evidence. It is true that in determining whether a particular purpose is substantial, an element of judgment is called for. In our view, his Honour's conclusion was correct. An attempt was made before us to criticise the credit of one of the taxpayer's witnesses, but an apportionment of the taxpayer's gross receipts in the years 1975-1981 is perhaps the best indicator of where the real thrust of the taxpayer's operations lay:

12. The Commissioner next seeks to attack, for taxation purposes, the accounting practice of "capitalisation", that is to say, the taxpayer's treating as plant, and not as trading stock, equipment not sold by the end of the financial year. The Commissioner says that this is an artificial process which, for fiscal purposes, should be ignored.

13. It will be remembered that, because of difficulties in identifying particular items of equipment, the taxpayer adopted a "last in - first out" approach. Such a "by and large" treatment accorded with the reality of the situation: the life of equipment hired out was in the range of 10 to 15 years; equipment was leased out on a number of occasions for an average term of three months; and, usually upon receipt of an order for the purchase of new equipment, the taxpayer acquired that equipment specifically.

14. Whilst seeking to criticise the taxpayer's method as artificial, at least in part, the Commissioner is prepared to accept that it was appropriate for the taxpayer to treat the equipment as trading stock in the first instance. He is also prepared to accept that it was appropriate, in terms of accounting practice, for the taxpayer to treat as plant, and no longer as trading stock, equipment not sold at the end of the financial year. Again, this treatment accords with the reality of the situation. Recognising that there is a possibility of sale, the equipment is first looked at as stock; but if it is not sold and, instead, is devoted to the hiring side of the business, it is appropriate to regard it as plant, that is to say, as having ceased to be circulating capital and to have become fixed capital instead: it has become part of the taxpayer's profit-making apparatus as distinct from its stock in trade. There is nothing anomalous or inconsistent with such a treatment. On the contrary, this method recognises that although a market for new equipment exists, yet equipment acquired by the taxpayer which is not sold within a reasonable time of its acquisition may be anticipated to be devoted to the hiring side of its business. It is only reasonable to expect that, in the course of its life, the character of the equipment may change: initially, it may be treated as if it were circulating capital but, if it is not sold within a relatively short period, it should be regarded as having become part of fixed capital.

15. It is important to bear steadily in mind that it is not practicable to identify, and therefore to trace, the commercial history of each item of equipment. It follows that an exercise in estimation of income rather than its calculation, in the strict sense, is called for. There is nothing novel in this. A "substantial" or by and large approach is sometimes the best that can be done. As Barwick C.J. said in Henderson v. Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1969] HCA 14; (1970) 119 CLR 612 at p 647:

"The figure determined as that income may be the result

of estimation, as well as of calculation, and its

determination may involve the acceptance of opinions,

expert or otherwise. In the long run it may be the

outcome of an exercise of judgment."

16. As has been said, under the method adopted by the taxpayer and accepted by the Commissioner, the Commissioner obtains an advantage in the first year because property, a large percentage of which might no doubt be treated as plant, is treated as trading stock so the taxpayer derives no deduction for depreciation in respect of it in that year. On the other hand, in the second and succeeding years, the taxpayer derives an advantage because the equipment is then treated as plant so that sales of it, whether direct or as a result of loss or destruction of hired equipment, are treated as sales of plant, that is of capital items and as such non-taxable. We were not given figures to enable us to determine the advantage to the Commissioner flowing from the failure to claim depreciation on plant in the first year. However, the figures relating to the advantage gained by the taxpayer in respect of direct sales or amounts received in respect of lost or destroyed plant were before us and indicate that, because of the terms of the hiring agreements insisted upon by the taxpayer with its customers, it receives in respect of lost or destroyed property more than the price paid for it.

17. If the system of accounting, which until relatively recently has been accepted by the taxpayer and the Commissioner as giving a substantially true reflection of its income, does not give a true reflection, then the system requires alteration. But the Commissioner did not seek to suggest that an alternative accounting treatment was appropriate. On the contrary, he was content to accept the taxpayer's accounting method. His real contention is that all equipment acquired by the taxpayer should be treated, for all purposes, as if it were acquired for sale. He says that it is enough that the taxpayer was prepared to sell its equipment if asked. In so contending, the Commissioner is really saying that the whole of the equipment is trading stock at all times and that none of it is ever plant, that is, part of the taxpayer's profit-making apparatus.

18. We cannot accept the Commissioner's argument. In the first place, it is at odds with the finding of fact made by Hunt J. that although the activities of the taxpayer included both selling and hiring equipment, hiring was the rule and sale the exception. Moreover, the Commissioner's contentions are inconsistent with his acceptance of the taxpayer's accounting practice of treating as plant any equipment not sold by the end of the financial year. The Commissioner can hardly accept the taxpayer's treatment of the equipment as plant or fixed capital at the end of the first financial year and at the same time contend that it should then also be regarded as trading stock or circulating capital.

19. Once it is acknowledged, as it must be, that the taxpayer's income has to be estimated rather than calculated, the question becomes one as to the appropriate method of computation of its income (see Henderson's Case, supra, per Barwick C.J. at p 648). If a new system were to be devised, one which might be suggested is that the figures which showed the relationship of items for which money is received on disposal as against items used truly as plant in the hiring business could be quantified in some way as a percentage. For example, on a basis of estimation from the figures of past years, it might be said that X% of equipment purchased by the company from the beginning and thereafter should be treated as trading stock and Y% from the beginning and thereafter should be treated as plant. In the latter case, of course, depreciation would be allowed from the beginning. It would be a matter of some nicety to arrive at percentages which would give a true reflection of the taxpayer's income. Indeed, it might prove impracticable to arrive at appropriate figures. However, to alter the system in that way would eliminate the contradiction which is involved in the Commissioner's contention that the taxpayer's present method should be accepted as correct but that the taxpayer should be taxed on sales or disposal of plant as if on revenue account.

20. Another possible approach to the situation would be to treat the equipment as trading stock or circulating capital for a further period before it is regarded as plant or fixed capital.

21. Suffice it to say that no argument along any of these lines was submitted on behalf of the Commissioner before Hunt J. or before us. Rather, what the Commissioner sought to do was to accept the taxpayer's accounting for some purposes but to reject it for other purposes. In our opinion, this is not a permissible course. The question is one of determining whether a particular method is actually appropriate in the whole of the circumstances. As Dixon J. said in Carden's Case, supra, at p 154:

". . . the admissibility of the method which in fact has

been pursued must depend upon its actual

appropriateness. In other words, the inquiry should

be whether in the circumstances of the case it is

calculated to give a substantially correct reflex of

the taxpayer's true income."

22. This does not mean that the Commissioner could not contend that the taxpayer's suggested method of reflecting the income generated by its operations was deficient in a particular respect or respects. He could, for instance, accept the taxpayer's treatment in part and contend for a different approach in other areas. This is not what the Commissioner sought to do here. He was content to accept the taxpayer's accounting method of treatment of the equipment but at the same time to seek to invoke the independent principle that a profit or gain emerging on the sale of assets as a regular event in the ordinary course of business may represent income (see Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Myer Emporium Limited 87 ATC 4363; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Reynolds 81 ATC 4131; Jennings Industries Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1984] FCA 109; (1984) 2 FCR 273; J. & R. O'Kane & Co. v. The Commissioners of Inland Revenue (1922) 12 TC 303; London Australia Investment Company Limited v. The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1977] HCA 50; (1977) 138 CLR 106; The Gloucester Railway Carriage and Wagon Co. Ltd. v. The Commissioners of Inland Revenue (1925) 12 TC 720).

23. Such an approach must, in our opinion, be rejected. The Commissioner has to decide what attitude he will adopt to the taxpayer's treatment or method taken as a whole. If, as here, the Commissioner accepts that treatment, it is not open to him then to ignore it by removing the case from its context and claiming that certain transactions have generated income as ordinarily understood. The vice in this approach is that the transactions relied upon by the Commissioner cannot be looked at in isolation. They should be seen as part of the whole scheme of the taxpayer's treatment of its activities. So regarded, the sales in question are treated as the disposal of part of the taxpayer's profit-making apparatus and thus of fixed assets. Any profit thus accruing is on capital, not revenue account.

24. If it were permissible to isolate these transactions from their context within the general framework of the taxpayer's treatment of its activities overall, there would be much to be said for the Commissioner's contention that the profit arising on the sale of equipment held beyond the first year is income. But, unless the taxpayer's accounting treatment is undermined for some good reason (and none has been suggested), it is not permissible for the Commissioner to fasten on one aspect of the taxpayer's activities and, at the same time, ignore the rest. The Commissioner is really seeking to both approbate and reprobate. It is not open to the Commissioner to take such an inconsistent approach.

25. To determine whether the method adopted by the taxpayer is appropriate is essentially a practical question. Further, it arises in a special, and complex commercial context. In Carden's Case, Dixon J. observed (at p 157):

'Nor does the use of the word, "profit," "gain" and

"income" appear to me to throw any light upon it.

They do not decide the question. They in no way

remove the necessity of discovering whether, as a

matter of fact, the basis of the accounting is or

is not appropriate to reflect truly the

professional income of Dr. Carden. The

considerations which appear to me to affect any

such question are to be found in the nature of the

profession concerned and, indeed, the actual mode

in which it is practised in a given case.'

26. In the ultimate analysis, whether the taxpayer's method is apt to give a substantially correct reflex of its true income was primarily a matter of practical judgment for the trial judge. As we have said, other possible methods of treatment suggest themselves; but this is not to say that the taxpayer's long-standing procedures are inappropriate. On the contrary, we agree with Hunt J. that those procedures were apt to throw up a substantially correct reflex of the taxpayer's true income.

27. We would add that the recent decision in Memorex Pty. Ltd. v. The Commissioner of Taxation for the Commonwealth of Australia (Davies, Pincus and Einfeld JJ., unreported, 2 December 1987) can be distinguished for present purposes. It was there held that the goods supplied by that taxpayer were not part of its fixed assets at the relevant date (see per Davies and Einfeld JJ. at p 23). Here, the position is different. Under this taxpayer's accounting method, equipment is treated as if it were trading stock in the first instance and, after the first year, as plant or fixed assets. As has been said, the inquiry is one as to the appropriateness of the suggested method. We think that Hunt J. was entitled to conclude that the taxpayer's treatment was apt, given the special circumstances of the case. We do not, however, suggest that such a method would have been appropriate on the facts in Memorex. By the same token, the decision and reasoning in Memorex cannot be applied here.

28. We would propose that the appeal be dismissed with costs.

I see this case differently to the other members of the Court. The difference is fundamental, reflecting, as it does, a disagreement as to the significance of the fact that the relevant items were classified, for accounting purposes, as "plant". Whilst I accept that as a relevant fact, it is not in my opinion conclusive.

2. The only issue before this Court is whether certain moneys, recurrently received by the respondent, Cyclone Scaffolding Pty Limited and running at about half a million dollars per year, are comprised within the "gross income" of that company, within the meaning of that term as used in s.25(1)(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. The moneys fall into two distinct categories: receipts for sales of equipment, categorised as "plant" because the equipment was purchased during a previous taxation year, and compensation moneys paid by hirers by reason of the loss or destruction of "plant" hired by them. The items lost or destroyed are referred to in the evidence as "non returns", and the compensation moneys are called "indirect sales". For the sake of brevity, I shall use the term "sales", unless otherwise indicated, to include both sales in the true sense and indirect sales.

3. The present issue does not extend to the whole of the proceeds of "sales". It is common ground that such portion of the price or compensation moneys as represents the difference between the written down value of the particular items of equipment, as depreciated under s.54 of the Act, and their original cost is assessable income by virtue of s.59(2) of the Act. In substance, the question is whether the "profit" on sale, loss or destruction -- that is the amount by which the sum received exceeds the original cost to Cyclone -- is "income".

4. Numerous issues were litigated before the learned trial judge (Hunt J.) and findings were made by his Honour upon some matters which the respondent contends, but the appellant -- the Commissioner of Taxation -- denies, are determinative of the outcome of this appeal. They include the manner in which Cyclone, with the acquiescence of the Commissioner, has, over the years, dealt with scaffolding equipment in its accounts. The trial judge found, and this is not disputed, that, by reason of the nature and number of items of equipment, it is impracticable for Cyclone to trace each item of equipment. The result is that, when Cyclone meets an order for sale or receives compensation for equipment which has been lost or destroyed, it is generally impossible for it to say when the particular item or items was purchased. (The only exception to this position is that which arises when equipment is bought in by Cyclone expressly to meet a particular purchase order by a customer. In such cases the equipment never goes into the hiring pool.) Cyclone has met this difficulty by adopting a "last in, first out" policy. The Commissioner has not objected to that policy, the application of which would seem not to affect the taxation liability of the company.

5. However, there is a further dimension of the problem of tracing, which is more contentious. It arises from the circumstance that, in the case of scaffolding equipment, it does not matter to buyers whether equipment is new or used. Although some equipment is purchased by Cyclone expressly to meet purchase orders received by it, much equipment is purchased without there being any certainty as to its fate. The equipment is added to the hiring pool. Individual items may remain in the pool for years, being hired out on numerous occasions. Other items may be hired out upon a few occasions and then sold, or lost or destroyed. Items may be sold before the first hiring; or be lost or destroyed during the first hiring. Except in the case of equipment purchased against a particular order, it is not possible, at the time of purchase, for the company to predict what will happen to any particular item. The reality is that all equipment purchased by the company, other than to meet a particular purchase order, is purchased with the objective of making it available for both hire and sale, as may be required by its customers from time to time. In uncontested evidence, Mr James Bradley, a director of Cyclone said:

"When we buy equipment we have no idea really

whether it is going to be sold, we could be

purchasing it purely and simply for the hire

stock but we are continually buying equipment

that is available for both. If it is required

for sale it would be sold, but it could be

also required for hire."

Mr Bradley later agreed that, during the relevant period, both new and secondhand equipment was "available for sale".

6. The duality of purpose referred to by Mr Bradley creates the major conceptual problem in the case. Equipment acquired by a taxpayer for the purpose of being let out on hire is ordinarily regarded as being acquired on capital account. The purchase price is not a deductible expenditure but, by virtue of s.54 of the Income Tax Assessment Act, the taxpayer may claim a depreciation allowance in respect of the cost of purchase. In the event of the equipment being subsequently sold at a price higher than the then written down value, the excess -- up to the amount of the original cost of purchase -- is assessable under s.59(2). But, generally speaking, any profit on sale will be regarded as a capital profit; not providing "income" within s.25(1).

7. On the other hand, equipment acquired for the purpose of resale is ordinarily regarded as having been acquired on revenue account. It is "trading stock", the cost of purchase being deductible in the year of purchase and the proceeds of sale, including any profit, being "income" within s.25(1).

8. It is understandable that Cyclone has found difficulty in reconciling its practical situation with the conceptual dichotomy adopted in the Income Tax Assessment Act. It has attempted to meet the problem by adopting an arbitrary approach, which it no doubt thought to be fair but which is inconsistent with reality. For accounting purposes, the assumption is made that all equipment purchased is, until the succeeding 30 June, held for the purpose of sale, and is, therefore, "trading stock". Thereafter, it automatically becomes "plant"; the assumption, from that date onwards, being that it is held for hire. Both assumptions conflict with the true position, as explained by Mr Bradley, that, at all times, the equipment is held for both purposes.

9. The result of the accounting system is that particular items may be held for up to 12 months without any claim for depreciation being made by the company; notwithstanding that during that time they are included within the hiring pool, and may even be out on hire. Upon the opposite extreme, equipment purchased by Cyclone, say, in late June and resold a few days later in July is regarded, at the date of sale, as being "plant", so that the profit on sale is treated as a capital profit not providing assessable income. So far as appears, this rule is followed even in cases where the equipment has been purchased by Cyclone for the specific purpose of reselling to a particular customer.

10. Notwithstanding the artificialities underlying this accounting system, for many years the Commissioner accepted it as an appropriate method of computing Cyclone's taxation liability. His acceptance extended to the point that he did not seek to argue that profits realised on the sale of, or by receipt of compensation for, items which had, by this system, been designated as "plant" fell within Cyclone's gross income. Although this appeal does not call into question the manner of distinguishing between "trading stock" and "plant", the Commissioner is no longer prepared to abjure this latter claim; presumably because, as the evidence indicates, during recent years, the contribution made by "sales" to total revenue has increased.

11. The changed position of the Commissioner raises the issue now before this Court. But, before dealing directly with it, it is convenient to refer to certain findings of Hunt J. which were claimed by counsel for Cyclone to compel the dismissal of the Commissioner's appeal. The first of these matters was a finding "that the taxpayer's accounting procedures are calculated to give a substantially correct reflex of its true income for taxation purposes". The reference, of course, was to the words of Dixon J. in Commissioner of Taxes (South Australia) v. The Executor Trustee and Agency Company of South Australia Limited [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108 ("Carden's case") at p 154. The question in that case was whether it was permissible for a professional man to return his income upon a "cash received", rather than a "fees earned", basis. For reasons which he gave, at pp 157-158, Dixon J. was of the opinion that "according to ordinary conceptions the receipts basis forms a fair and appropriate foundation for estimating professional income". But the question whether a given system represents a "correct reflex" is one of fact. At p 157 Dixon J. pointed out that the use of words like "profit", "gain" and "income" do not decide the acceptability of a particular method of accounting: "They in no way remove the necessity of discovering whether, as a matter of fact, the basis of the accounting is or is not appropriate to reflect truly the professional income of Dr Carden".

12. The reasoning of Hunt J. on the matter of "correct reflex" is, with respect, not easy to follow. His Honour commented: "By treating its equipment as trading stock during the financial year in which it was purchased, the taxpayer has in my view adopted a procedure for taxation purposes which is too favourable to the Commissioner; most equipment actually sold is disposed of within that first year of the date of its purchase, so that there is little if any set-off in subsequent years in favour of the taxpayer against the inclusion in favour of the Commissioner of the damages or penalty as the sale of trading stock in that first year". It is true that in some years -- three out of the six years dealt with in the evidence -- a majority in value of "sales" related to equipment deemed, on the application of the "last in, first out" principle, to have been purchased by Cyclone in the same taxation year. But it was quite wrong to say that there is "little if any set off in subsequent years". As the company's figures make clear, the "sales" of "plant" amounted in each of the six years to a substantial proportion of all "sales". The relevant percentages were: 1976, 53.4%; 1977, 63.7%; 1978, 44.5%; 1979, 53.6%; 1980, 46.3%; 1981, 43.1%. But even if the proportions had been much lower, the reasoning of Hunt J. would remain open to the objection that it seems to reflect the view that it would have been an appropriate course for the company to treat all equipment as "plant" from the date of purchase, with the result not only that depreciation would be allowable from that date but that, the items being "plant", all profits on "sales" would be excluded from the computation of the company's income.

13. In the argument before us, counsel for the respondent did not defend the finding of "correct reflex" upon this basis. Rather, apart from denying the appellant's entitlement to challenge the finding at all, they sought to justify the finding by reference to a "swings and roundabouts" approach; the advantage to the company in not paying tax on the profits from sales of "plant" being offset by the disadvantage which it suffered in not claiming depreciation of equipment during its first year, whilst that equipment remained "trading stock". However, the difficulty about this approach is that it would be possible to say that the accounting system employed by the company was a "correct reflex" of its position only if it were clear that, over the years, the advantage and the disadvantage were substantially equal; so that the one cancelled out the other. There is no evidence from which this conclusion may be drawn. It is clear that the accounting method which has been adopted treats as sales of "plant" the sale of some items of equipment which were but recently acquired and which were never used for hire; perhaps never even intended to be used for hire. It is equally clear that the system treats as "trading stock" some items which have been committed to the hiring pool, and even used for hire. There is obviously a degree of off-set; but whether the end discrepancy is large or small, and whether it favours the taxpayer or the Commissioner, are matters left entirely to speculation.

14. In coming to the conclusion that there is no room in this case for the application of the "correct reflex" approach, I do not doubt that, upon occasions, the assessment of taxable income -- or of some other component of the tax assessment process -- must rest in estimation rather than in calculation. Nor do I doubt that there will be occasions upon which it is sensible for a taxpayer and the Commissioner to agree upon a method of estimation which is likely to produce a fair approximation of the relevant figure, and which is not unduly burdensome to one or both of them. But the "swings and roundabouts" approach, urged on behalf of Cyclone, is not a process of estimation at all. Rather it is the application of an assumption of mutual offset, the validity of which is neither conceded nor proved. Whatever short cuts may be taken when the parties are in accord, once the agreement breaks down the Court must determine the matter according to law.

15. As I have indicated, counsel for Cyclone disputed that it was open to the Commissioner to contest the finding of "correct reflex", the stated reason being that the filed Notice of Appeal did not put this matter in issue. However, that submission misconceives the position. As it happens, in his reasons Hunt J. dealt with the question in this case, assessability under s.25, and determined it adversely to the Commissioner before referring at all to the concept of "correct reflex". He then disposed of an alternative contention of the Commissioner, that the moneys were assessable under s.26(j) of the Act, before concluding: "It follows that the only tax payable in relation to what I have concluded were the sales of plant by the taxpayer was the amount fixed by virtue of s.59". Hunt J. then turned to what he called the "next" submission of the Commissioner, "that the accounting procedures adopted by the taxpayer are nevertheless not calculated to give a substantially correct reflex of its true income for taxation purposes", and made the finding referred to. I admit to difficulty in understanding how the question of "correct reflex" arose as an independent issue before Hunt J. but it is apparent that it was treated as such by the learned judge; so that it is hardly surprising that the Commissioner, being concerned to contest the issue earlier determined against him, did not put this matter specifically in issue in the Notice of Appeal.

16. Notwithstanding the fact that Hunt J. treated "correct reflex" as a separate issue, it is understandable that counsel for the respondent should have seized upon that finding as an answer to the appeal upon the s.25 point. By definition, an accounting system which correctly reflects Cyclone's tax liability brings into account all assessable income. But whether the system adopted in the present case does so is the question in dispute. To the extent that Hunt J. may have allowed his view on "correct reflex" to influence his conclusion upon the ultimate question, that view was simply a step in the reasoning process, against which it is neither necessary nor appropriate for a party to appeal: see Ah Toy v. Registrar of Companies for the Northern Territory (1985) 61 ALR 583 and the cases referred to therein. The Notice of Appeal did squarely put in issue Hunt J's ultimate decision, rejecting the Commissioner's claim that the proceeds of "sales" are assessable under s.25. It thereby raised for consideration in this Court all the steps taken by Hunt J. upon the road to that decision.

17. Two other preliminary matters. Firstly, it is not the case, as Hunt J. seems to suggest in the passage from his reasons quoted in the majority judgment, that the Commissioner may succeed only if he, or the Court, can devise a "more appropriate" accounting procedure. The Commissioner is entitled to have the particular issue raised by him examined upon its merits, that is for conformity with the Act. Secondly, there is no suggestion in the evidence of any previous agreement having been made by the Commissioner as to what constitutes an accounting treatment providing "a substantially correct reflex" of the company's income. All that may be said is that, over the years, Cyclone has not claimed depreciation on equipment during its first year and that, in relation to taxation years prior to 1975, the Commissioner has not sought to tax the proceeds of sale of equipment held beyond the first year. Only in that sense may it be said that the Commissioner has previously conceded anything about Cyclone's accounting treatment. However, each year each of the parties is free to consider whether to maintain the position which it or he had previously taken. At any time, it would be competent for Cyclone to claim first year depreciation and to have that claim dealt with upon its legal merits. In the same way, at any time, it is competent for the Commissioner to claim to tax the proceeds of sales and to have that claim dealt with upon its legal merits. Of course, it is possible that, if the Commissioner succeeds on that claim, Cyclone will reconsider the position which it has previously taken in relation to depreciation. It appears at least to be arguable that equipment which is not specifically designated for sale, and which is added to the hiring pool, is property "owned by the taxpayer which has been installed ready for use" for the purpose of producing assessable income -- see s.54(1) of the Act -- notwithstanding that it is also held available for sale and that, in the result and by reason of subsequent events, it may be sold before it actually produces income from hire. If, as the Commissioner contends, Cyclone conducts a single business consisting of the hire and sale of scaffolding equipment, an accounting system which claimed immediate depreciation upon all items received into the hiring pool, but which brought in as income all profits on "sales", would seem to reflect the true nature of the business and not to conflict with any provision of the Act. But this is simply a tentative view. I express no opinion as to whether a claim for immediate depreciation, if made, would succeed or, if it did, as to how the result would compare with that experienced under the system hitherto adopted. Those are not matters for consideration in this case. I refer to the subject merely to make the point that the fact, if it be the fact, that success by the Commissioner in this case may cause a reconsideration of other aspects of Cyclone's accounting system and taxation claims is of no present relevance.

18. A central aspect of the reasoning of Hunt J., in rejecting the Commissioner's argument that the profits on "sales" were taxable under s.25, was that all equipment was properly to be regarded as "plant" at the first 30 June following its acquisition. That finding, if it may properly be so called, lies at the core of the respondent's argument in this appeal. In their outline of submissions counsel for the respondent expressed the issue in the appeal as being "whether, accepting that what was lost or disposed of was plant, accepting that it was hired out by the respondent, accepting that it was properly depreciated, nevertheless the sums received in consequence of its loss or destruction were assessable as income according to the ordinary concepts and usages of mankind within s.25" (my emphasis). The burden of their submissions was that the subject moneys were received in consideration of the sale, or as compensation for the loss or destruction, of "plant" -- or, occasionally, "capital assets" -- so that the moneys must be regarded as having been received on capital account, and therefore as not being "income" within the meaning of s.25.

19. Counsel for the Commissioner did not dispute that, under the accounting system adopted by Cyclone, the relevant equipment was categorized as "plant", and depreciated as such. For the purposes of the appeal, they accepted Cyclone's method of dividing its equipment into "trading stock" and "plant". But they contended that the sale of the equipment categorized as plant was itself a regular and significant part of the operations conducted by Cyclone generating substantial profits which constituted what were properly to be described, in ordinary terminology, as part of the income of the business. Upon the view that the accounting system is conclusive, it is not necessary to address this argument. But, as it seems to me, the argument not only raises the critical issue in the case but is correct.

20. It is clear law that, in considering the assessability to tax of particular receipts, labels ought to be disregarded. In determining whether particular receipts constitute "income", the Court will be guided by commercial and accountancy practice but, as the Full High Court stated in Arthur Murray (NSW) Pty Limited v. Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 58; (1965) 114 CLR 314 at p 318: "The ultimate inquiry . . . must be whether that which has taken place, be it in the earning or the receipt, is enough by itself to satisfy the general understanding among practical business people of what constitutes a derivation of income". The fact that Cyclone, with the acquiescence of the Commissioner, treated the whole of its unsold equipment as "plant", as at the commencement of the relevant taxation year, so that the subject sales were all of "plant", is a matter relevant to the determination of the present issue; but, in my opinion, it cannot be determinative of that issue. The Court must decide whether, according to the ordinary usages and understanding of mankind, that which was received constituted part of the "income" of the business. For that question to be determined, one must look beyond the label and examine the whole of the facts.

21. In the advertisements which it has published in editions of the Yellow Pages of the Sydney telephone book since 1975, Cyclone has consistently described its business as "Cyclone Double Grip. Sale or hire". During the period 1975 to 1981 the amount received by the company under the heading "total hire" has ranged from a low point of $4,304,934 in 1977 to a high of $7,202,179 in 1981. Those figures included not only "equipment hire" -- which accounts for approximately 60% of "total hire" in each of these years -- but also "labour hire" and "cartage hire". The latter items are charges incurred by customers for the transportation, erection and dismantling of scaffolding; usually, but not necessarily, hired scaffolding. In those same years total scaffolding "sales", that is direct sales and compensation for non-returns has been as follows: 1976, $933,383; 1977, $939,156; 1978, $1,026,545; 1979, $1,119,114; 1980, $1,565,654; 1981, $1,246,765.

22. As these figures indicate, the revenue from sales is much lower than that from total hire and even from "equipment hire". Nonetheless, in each of those years, "sales" made a significant, recurrent contribution to the company's earnings; of the order of one million dollars a year. It was incorrect for Hunt J. to say, as he did in his reasons, that: "Neither the sales of depreciated plant nor the payment by way of damages (or penalty) for lost or destroyed equipment form a regular or systematic part of the taxpayer's business of hiring out its scaffolding business". The payments were regular. In the period 1975-1981 "sales", that is direct sales and non-returns, of "plant", that is equipment not deemed to be purchased in the relevant year of income were as follows:

1976 $493,355

1977 $587,907

1978 $450,178

1979 $589,982

1980 $715,571

1981 $527,855

The receipts were a systematic part of the business because they were the product of two policy positions adopted by Cyclone: that it would sell equipment upon demand at current list prices and that it would seek compensation, at current list prices, for lost or destroyed equipment. There is a high degree of consistency in the annual payments received, the amount of which is significant by any standards.

23. Although, as one might expect, there was some variation in the volume of "sales" from one year to another, earnings from this source rose steadily over the period. A businessman looking at the company's accounts, say with an eye to the purchase of the business, would, I believe, regard the revenue from "sales" as a predictable item which he ought to take into account in determining the value of the total business. Certainly, it might be expected that a vendor of the business would press upon the purchaser that he should do so. The parties would so act because, in the ordinary sense of that word, "sales" revenue constitutes part of the "income" of the company.

24. In Commissioner of Taxation v. Reynolds (1981) 81 ATC 4131 at pp 4141-4142 Neasey J. stated a practical test for determining whether particular receipts were "income" according to ordinary concepts:

"Whether it is to be characterised as income

for the purposes of the Act depends . . . upon

the character of the receipt and the

circumstances in which it came to the hands of

the taxpayer. In the case of a taxpayer

conducting a business, if the receipt is as a

matter of reality part of the proceeds of the

business or a product of or incidental to the

conduct of the business, it is usually

income."

The receipt of the subject moneys was, in my view, a product of the conduct of the respondent's business. The respondent "sold" from a hiring pool, the equipment in which was not segregated according to age or whether it was "trading stock" or "plant". It would not accord with reality, in my opinion, to break up the receipts for "sales" by saying, because only of the artificial distinction between "trading stock" and "plant" which I have described, that 57% of the receipts constituted income and the remainder a capital profit.

25. What I have said about "sales" as a whole applies equally to that portion of "sales" which constitutes compensation for lost or destroyed hire equipment. It is entirely predictable, given the scale of Cyclone's hiring operations and the nature of the building industry, that in each year substantial sums will be received by way of compensation for lost or destroyed equipment. The evidence confirms this prediction. During relevant years the yearly figures did not fluctuate very much: 1976, $382,688; 1977, $394,261; 1978, $359,525; 1979, $488,888; 1980, $544,029; 1981, $504,463. There is no evidence whether or not the company in fact included in its annual budgets items representing the cost of replacing non-returns, or receipts by way of compensation for non-returns. But there is a sufficient degree of constancy in the figures for this to have been done.

26. In their submissions to us counsel for the respondent likened the receipt by Cyclone of these compensation moneys to the receipt of moneys from an insurer following the loss or destruction of an insured article. I do not think that the analogy advances the argument. Insurance moneys are paid by way of indemnity only. They do not include any element of profit. At the most, an insured person would recover replacement cost; whereas the compensation moneys received by Cyclone were calculated at the company's current list price, as used for sales made by it. They included a component for profit, over and above the actual cost of replacing that item at that time. So the analogy immediately breaks down. But if, for the moment, one might suppose a company which receives each year payments from its insurers in satisfaction of numerous small claims, which payments involve a profit to the insured and which are sufficiently predictable and constant in amount as to be capable of being included in the company's annual budget, there would be no reason to deny to those payments the description of "income". The postulated situation, like the actual position of Cyclone, is quite dissimilar from the typical case, where a person receives compensation from an insurer for a particular item of equipment; where payment is made on the basis of either historic cost or replacement value, so that there is no profit element, and where the element of recurrence is lacking.

27. There was evidence before Hunt J. that, in adopting the policy of demanding compensation for non-returns at list prices rather than replacement cost, Cyclone was motivated by a desire to encourage hirers to care for, and to return, hired equipment. That evidence may readily be accepted but it does not affect the proper categorisation of the moneys received by way of compensation. The question is whether the compensation moneys were a product of the conduct of the business. They may answer this description notwithstanding that the maximisation of receipts was not the company's primary motivation in fixing the level of compensation.

28. The view that the moneys received by Cyclone for "sales", both true sales and compensation for non-returns, constituted part of the "income" of the company, according to the normal concepts and usages of mankind, is consistent with authority. In The Gloucester Railway Carriage and Wagon Co Limited v. Commissioners for Inland Revenue (1925) 12 TC 720 the House of Lords had to consider a case not unlike the present one. The appellant dealt in railway wagons, most of which it constructed in its own works. As part of its business it hired out wagons. In allocating wagons to the hiring section of its business, the company debited that section with the capital value of the wagons -- cost of manufacture plus an allowance for profit. Depreciation was calculated upon that value. During a period of scarcity of wagons, the opportunity arose to sell the wagons at prices above their then written down values. Notwithstanding the fact that the wagons constituted the "plant" of the hiring business, it was held that the profits were to revenue account. On behalf of the House, Lord Dunedin said, at p 748:

"The Appellants argue that this is really a

capital increment; and to say so they call

these wagons plant of the hiring business. I

am of opinion that in calling them plant they

really beg the whole question. The

Commissioners have found -- and I think it is

the fact -- that there was here one business.

A wagon is none the less sold as an incident

of the business of buying and selling because

in the meantime before sold it has been

utilised by being hired out. There is no

similarity whatever between these wagons and

plant in the proper sense, e.g., machinery, or

between them and investments, the sale of

which plant or investments at a price greater

than that at which they had been acquired

would be a capital increment and not an item

of income."

29. In London Australia Investment Company Limited v. Commissioner of Taxation [1977] HCA 50; (1977) 138 CLR 106 the issue was whether profits realised upon the sale by the appellant of certain shares constituted "income" within s.25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. The company invested in the shares with a view to the production of income from dividends, not with a view to making profits on sale. However, from time to time, shares were sold; generally when their dividend yield had become unacceptably low. By majority, Gibbs and Jacobs JJ., Barwick C.J. dissenting, the High Court held the profits on realization to be part of the company's assessable income. Gibbs J. referred to the words of the Lord Justice Clerk in Californian Copper Syndicate v. Harris (1904) 5 TC 159 at pp 165-166, which have often been applied to s.25 of the Australian Act:

"It is quite a well settled principle in

dealing with questions of assessment of income

tax, that where the owner of an ordinary

investment chooses to realize it, and obtains

a greater price for it than he originally

acquired it at, the enhanced price is not

profit in the sense of Schedule D of the

Income Tax Act of 1842 assessable to income

tax. But it is equally well established that

enhanced values obtained from realization or

conversion of securities may be so assessable,

where what is done is not merely a realization

or change of investment, but an act done in

what is truly the carrying on, or carrying

out, of a business."

His Honour also referred to Colonial Mutual Life Assurance Society Ltd v. Commissioner of Taxation [1946] HCA 60; (1946) 73 CLR 604, before saying, at p 116:

". . . it is in my opinion established by this

and the many other cases in which Californian

Copper Syndicate v. Harris has been applied

that if the sale in question is a business

operation, carried out in the course of the

business of profit-making, the profit arising

on the sale will be of an income character."

Gibbs J. regarded the fact that the appellant did not purchase the shares for the purpose of selling them at a profit as an "important circumstance". But he did not regard it as decisive. At p 117 he said:

"But the question whether the shares were

acquired on the capital account of the

taxpayer can only be answered by applying the

tests indicated by Californian Copper

Syndicate v. Harris . . . Helsham J. found that

during the three years in question it was an

integral part of the taxpayer's business to

deal in shares, in the sense that switching of

investments was desirable to produce the best

dividend returns and was indeed necessary if

the taxpayer's policy of investing in shares

with growth potential was to be adhered to.

In my opinion it is impossible to controvert

that finding; it was clearly right. Although

the company's business was to invest in shares

with the primary purpose of obtaining income

by way of dividends, the conduct of the

investment business required that the share

portfolio should be given regular

consideration, and that shares should

frequently be sold when the dividend yield

dropped, which for practical purposes usually

meant when the shares went up in value. The

taxpayer systematically sold its shares at a

profit for the purpose of increasing the

dividend yield of its investments. The sale

of the shares was a normal operation in the

course of carrying on the business of

investing for profit. It was not a mere

realization or change of investment."

30. I think that this approach may be applied to the present case. It may be accepted that the respondent's business was to invest in scaffolding equipment with the primary purpose, in money items, of obtaining income by way of hiring fees. The conduct of the hiring business necessarily involved losses, and destruction, of some of the hired equipment in respect of which the respondent, in the ordinary course of business, would seek compensation. Compensation rates were set at current list prices with a view to encouraging hirers to care for, and to return, the hired equipment; so profits on "non-returns" were a normal incident of Cyclone effecting that primary purpose. Those profits can truly be regarded as part of the profits attaching to the hiring operations.

31. The direct sale of scaffolding equipment to customers was not a necessary incident of the conduct of a hiring business. Presumably, Cyclone could have run a hiring business without direct sales. But, although -- in money terms -- sales were less important than hire, Cyclone chose to combine the two activities. At all material times, it stood ready to sell equipment at current list prices, that is at prices yielding profits to it. At least a secondary purpose of the acquisition by the company of the equipment was to provide itself with stock for sale upon demand. To use the words of Gibbs J., the sale of equipment from time to time was "a normal operation in the course of carrying on the business of investing for profit".

32. Reference should be made to the important principles recently enunciated by the Full High Court in Commissioner of Taxation v. Myer Emporium Limited [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 61 ALJR 270. The facts of that case are remote from those of the present case, not least because Myer concerned an isolated transaction entered into otherwise than in the ordinary course of the taxpayer's business. But, notwithstanding that circumstance, the High Court unanimously held that the proceeds of the transaction constituted "income" within s.25(1), by reason of the fact that the taxpayer entered into the transaction with the intention or purpose of making a profit or gain from it. At p 272 the Court said:

"Although it is well settled that a profit or

gain made in the ordinary course of carrying

on a business constitutes income, it does not

follow that a profit or gain made in a

transaction entered into otherwise than in the

ordinary course of carrying on the taxpayer's

business is not income. Because a business is

carried on with a view to profit, a gain made

in the ordinary course of carrying on the

business is invested with the profit-making

purpose, thereby stamping the profit with the

character of income. But a gain made

otherwise than in the ordinary course of

carrying on the business which nevertheless

arises from a transaction entered into by the

taxpayer with the intention or purpose of

making a profit or gain may well constitute

income. Whether it does depends very much on

the circumstances of the case. Generally

speaking, however, it may be said that if the

circumstances are such as to give rise to the

inference that the taxpayer's intention or

purpose in entering into the transaction was

to make a profit or gain, the profit or gain

will be income, notwithstanding that the

transaction was extraordinary judged by

reference to the ordinary course of the

taxpayer's business. Nor does the fact that a

profit or gain is made as the result of an

isolated venture or a 'one-off' transaction

preclude it from being properly characterised

as income . . . The authorities establish that

a profit or gain so made will constitute

income if the property generating the profit

or gain was acquired in a business operation

or commercial transaction for the purpose of

profit-making by the means giving rise to the

profit."

33. The present case is a stronger one, from the Commissioner's point of view, than was Myer. It does not involve a gain made otherwise than in the ordinary course of business; but, rather, gains made, regularly and systematically, in the conduct of that business. As I have pointed out, one of the purposes of the respondent, in acquiring the relevant equipment, was to make profits out of its hiring operations, an integral part of which was the receipt of compensation for non-returns. Another purpose was to make profits out of the resale of that equipment, in accordance with the demands of its customers.

34. Finally, it is desirable to comment upon the application to this case of the decision in Memorex Pty Limited v. Commissioner of Taxation (Davies, Pincus and Einfeld JJ., not reported, 2 December 1987). Bowen C.J. and Beaumont J. regard that case as distinguishable from the present one, but the suggested distinction depends entirely upon nomenclature. When one goes to the facts, the two cases are remarkably similar. In each case the taxpayer supplied equipment either by hire (lease) or sale, depending upon customer choice. During relevant years each taxpayer periodically sold equipment which had been previously hired (leased). In each case the profits on sales were a substantial, recurrent element in the income of the business. Although Davies and Einfeld JJ. said, at p 23, that there was no analogy between the case before them "and the case of plant or equipment that a taxpayer may have and may use as part of the structure of an enterprise", the Court was unanimous in rejecting the argument put by counsel for Memorex "that if the sales . . . were to be brought to account in a calculation of the applicant's taxable income, it could only be by treating the goods as trading stock". That argument is similar to that put by Cyclone, with the added rider in the present case that this course is impossible because the goods were treated in the accounting system as "plant". But the Court proceeded, as Davies and Einfeld JJ. put it at p 11, "upon the footing that the subject profits were assessable income if properly regarded as income under general principles and that the assessment was not precluded by the fact that, in the circumstances of the case, it is difficult to fit the goods neatly and conclusively at all times into the category of trading stock or of depreciable plant or articles". The application of that approach to the present case would require the allowance of the Commissioner's appeal.

35. The respondent put a second argument, submitting that, even if the profits received by it upon the "sale" of the equipment would constitute "income" within ordinary concepts, it was not assessable under s.25(1) of the Act because s.59(2) constitutes a complete statement of the circumstances under which the proceeds of depreciated plant are assessable to income tax. Reference was made to Reseck v. Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 38; (1975) 133 CLR 45, Commissioner of Taxes (Vict) v. Nicholas [1938] HCA 18; (1938) 59 CLR 230, Dickson v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1939] HCA 42; (1940) 62 CLR 687 and Commissioner of Taxation v. Belford [1952] HCA 73; (1952) 88 CLR 589.

36. This submission was rejected in Memorex, I think rightly. Section 59 is one of a series of provisions, commencing at s.54, dealing with depreciation. As might be expected, those provisions occur in Division 3 of the Act, which deals with deductions. Section 59(1) deals with the situation where a depreciated asset is sold at less than written down value, allowing a deduction to the extent of the difference. Section 59(2) covers the converse case. This provision was presumably thought to be necessary because in many, perhaps most, cases the proceeds of sale of such assets would not be "income" according to ordinary concepts; so that, in the absence of a special provision, the taxpayer would gain an advantage from the fact that too generous depreciation had been allowed. However, there is nothing to indicate that, in making this special provision, the legislature intended to interfere with the operation of any other provisions of the Act or to exempt from taxation the proceeds of sale of a depreciated item which, upon the facts, constituted "income" within the meaning of s.25. The situation is quite unlike Reseck, the point of which was that the inclusion in s.25(1) of the whole of lump sum severance payments would frustrate the operation of s.26(d) of the Act, which brings to taxation only five per cent of such payments. As to the other authorities referred to by counsel, far from assisting the argument they show that special provisions bringing to taxation amounts which might not fall within s.25 do not normally exclude the operation, in a proper case, of s.25: see particularly the approach of Dixon C.J. in Belford at pp 601-602.

37. In my opinion the learned trial judge should have held that the moneys received by Cyclone from "sales" constituted "income" within the meaning of s.25(1) of the Act. The appeal of the Commissioner ought to be allowed with costs. The order made by the Supreme Court of New South Wales ought to be set aside and, in lieu thereof, it ought to be ordered that the appeal to that Court be dismissed with costs.
