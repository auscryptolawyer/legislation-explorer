HEARING

PERTH

22:11:1991

ORDER

The decision on the applicant's objection to the refusal of a certificate of exemption is affirmed.

DECISION

On 27 December 1972 a medical practitioner practising in the south-west of Western Australia established a Foundation under the Associations Incorporations Act (WA) to provide financial support for the spread of Christian teaching and the carrying on of charitable works in Australia and overseas. In August 1973 the Foundation was advised by the Taxation Office that as a "religious institution" its income would be exempt from income tax under s 23(e) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. In 1982 the Bank Account Debits Tax Administration Act 1982 was passed. By virtue of later amendments it became the Debits Tax Administration Act 1982. It creates liability to pay tax on debits made to accounts held with banks and non-bank financial institutions. Accounts of religious institutions are exempt.

2. It was not until 1988 that the Foundation applied for a certificate of exemption under s.11 of that Act. On 29 March 1989 the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation refused the exemption although he appears to have dealt with the application on the basis that the Foundation was claiming to be a public benevolent institution. The decision was reviewed within the Australian Taxation Office and reaffirmed. An objection to the decision was lodged and on 22 May 1990 was disallowed. The Foundation then requested that the decision be referred to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal under s.23 of the Debits Tax Administration Act. The case turns on the question whether the Foundation is a religious institution for the purposes of the Act.

Factual Background

3. Doctor A. is a medical practitioner who graduated from Adelaide University in 1958. He and his wife are committed Christians with a particular interest in overseas mission work. That interest dates back to 1961 when they visited and worked at mission hospitals in India on their way to the United Kingdom for Dr A's postgraduate training. In 1964 on his return to Australia from England, Dr A. began work at Fremantle Hospital. He and his wife became foundation members of the Cockburn Baptist Church. At the end of 1966 they went to Nigeria as medical missionaries and remained there until 1969. After a further year at Fremantle Hospital, Dr A practised in a country town in the South West of Western Australia for seven years. In 1977 he returned with his family to Perth where he has carried on his practice since. During his time in the country, Dr A decided that he wanted to apply part of the income earned from his medical practice to the support of Christian missionary work. In 1972 he discussed ways of doing this with Mr B, then Principal of the Perth Bible College, which trains men and women for missionary service, pastoral ministry and other religious activities. He had known Mr B since his childhood days. In the early 1950's Mr B had given consideration to means of raising funds for Christian activities which would take advantage of the tax exemption provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. He knew of some suggestions which had been made in papers written by Sir John Nimmo. In 1961 he formed a body called "W.A. Church Funds Inc." under the Associations Incorporation Act. This body was intended to receive donations for the promotion of Christian evangelical activities and was evidently successful in that objective.

4. In their discussions Dr A and Mr B considered setting up a tax exempt organisation which, like W.A. Church Funds Inc., could receive donations and apply them to various Christian evangelical purposes. As a result of these conversations they formed the C Foundation under the Associations Incorporation Act (WA). They jointly drafted its constitution and rules. The Foundation was incorporated on 27 December 1972. The objects clause of its rules is in the following terms:

"The objects of the Association are religious and are the

encouragement and promotion of Christian Doctrines and Practices

in Australia and overseas without profit to any individuals and

in pursuance of these objects to accept donations of any real and

personal property and gifts by will or otherwise and to borrow or

lend money and to purchase or in any other manner acquire and

hold lands tenements and hereditaments goods chattels and effects

and all other real and personal estate for the purpose of the

Association and to let sell manage exchange hire or dispose of

all or any part of the Property or Assets Real or Personal of the

Association or otherwise deal with or authorise the Committee of

the Association to deal with in the same manner as fully and

effectually as an individual owner could do and for such purpose

to execute all such transfers deeds mortgages assurances

instruments writings and things as may be necessary or desirable

subject to clause 6 hereof."

The initial members of the Foundation were Dr A and his wife, Mr B and Miss s.(who later married Mr B).

5. The rules provide for a committee as the controlling body to consist of the officers of the Foundation and such other members not exceeding three as might be deemed necessary by the committee and elected by a majority vote at a general meeting of members. The officers of the Foundation are its Chairman and Secretary. They are to permanently occupy their positions until death or retirement or removal by a 3/4 majority vote at a general meeting of members. A quorum consists of one of the officers of the Foundation and one other member of the committee, while it does not exceed three and one of the officers of the Association and two other members of the committee if it exceeds three. Dr A has been chairman of the Foundation since its inception and his wife has been its secretary.

6. Rule 5 which governs membership of the Foundation, is in the following terms:

"Any person who has attained the age of 21 years may be

elected as an ordinary member provided he or she signs the

following statement: "I desire to join (C) Foundation and declare

that I am in agreement with the doctrinal statement of the

Scripture Union and agree to be bound by the Rules of the

Association." The election, removal, retirement and resignation

of members shall be at the absolute discretion of the Committee."

7. The doctrinal statement of the Scripture Union referred to in cl.5 is a statement of what are described as "...the historic truths of the Christian faith". They include a declaration of the existence of God as the creator of the human race in his own likeness, God the Father who sent his son, Jesus Christ, as the Saviour of Man from the consequences of sin, and the Holy Spirit who calls sinners to turn to Christ in repentance. They include also the assertion that the scriptures of the Old and New Testament are divinely inspired and that the Church is the body of Christ held together and growing up in him as global fellowship and local congregation. The commission of Christ to "proclaim the Good News to all people...." is also acknowledged.

8. At all times material for the purposes of this appeal, the members of the Foundation and its committee have consisted of Dr A, his wife, their four adult children and Mr and Mrs B. Dr A has never sought to expand the membership. It is apparent from the evidence that the primary source of funding for the Foundation comes from Dr A's income and that most of the decision-making as to the receipt, investment and disposition of that money is carried out by him and his wife. Significant changes in direction of Foundation investments would be discussed with the Bs. The administrative work of the Foundation, including the typing of correspondence, is done by Dr A's secretary or Mr B's secretary. Cheques to donees are usually signed by Dr A. or his wife. The Bs are signatories who sign cheques when Dr A and his wife are overseas. If Dr A only is overseas, then one of his sons acts as co-signatory with Mrs A. The Foundation has one formal meeting of its members annually, which is usually held in Mr B's office.

9. Since its formation the Foundation has built up a body of capital which it invests in various ways. In a letter to the Australian Taxation office of 15 November 1988, Dr A described its activities in the following terms:

"...to use the funds and deposits with the Foundation for

trading purposes and then distribute the profits to Christian and

Charitable works. The funds of the Association have been derived

from gifts and also by interest free deposits from interested

persons. Trading activities have primarily been on interest

bearing deposits, mortgages and rentals."

The evidence relating to the formation, operation and financial affairs of the Foundation and its relationship to the personal and business affairs of Dr A and his family was not without complexity. But the primary facts were not really in dispute and I have no hesitation in accepting Dr A and the other persons called to give evidence as credible and essentially reliable witnesses of the truth. No question was raised about the bona fides and depth of Dr A's christian commitment. And the evidence clearly supports the conclusion that the Foundation was formed and operated in order to provide financial support to advance the spread of Christian teachings and the carrying on by Christian people of charitable and missionary activities in various parts of the world. The debate in this case does not impinge upon those issues. Rather it centres on whether, in the statutory context, the Foundation is properly characterised as a religious institution. Before turning to the relevant principles, it is appropriate to lay out the statutory framework.

Statutory Framework

22. The taxing statute associated with the Debits Tax Administration Act is the Debits Tax Act 1982, which by s.3 provides that the Debits Tax Administration Act is to be incorporated and shall be read as one with it. Section 4 of the Debits Tax Act imposes tax in respect of:

(a) each taxable debit of not less than $1 made to a

taxable account;

(b) each eligible debit of not less than $1 made to an

exempt account.

Paragraph (c) relates to accounts kept outside Australia. The amount of tax imposed is provided for by s.5 read with the Schedule to the Act. The relevant definitions are to be found in s.3 of the Debits Tax Administration Act 1982. They include the following:

"taxable debit" means a debit (other than an exempt debit)

made to an account.

"taxable account" means an account (other than an exempt account)

kept in Australia.

"eligible debit" means a debit (other than an excluded

debit or an exempt debit) made to an account.

"excluded debit" means a debit -

(a) made to an account kept with a financial institution in

the name of -

.

.

.

(vi) any of the following:

A.a public benevolent or a religious institution;

.

.

.

being a debit made in relation to a transaction or

transactions carried out by or on behalf of the institution...

wholly and exclusively in furtherance of its objects.

The definition of "exempt debit" is not relevant for present purposes.

23. Section 8 of the Debits Tax Administration Act creates the liability to pay tax imposed in respect of taxable debits made to taxable accounts and eligible debits made to other accounts. The liability is imposed jointly and severally on the financial institution and the account holders.

24. Section 11 provides for exemption certificates in sub-s.(1):

"11(1) Where an account holder in respect of an account

kept in Australia applies to the Commissioner in accordance with

this section for the issue of a certificate of exemption in

relation to the account -

(a) if the Commissioner is satisfied that all debits made,

or to be made, to the account are, or are likely to be, either

excluded debits or exempt debits - he shall issue a certificate

of exemption in relation to the account; or

(b) if the Commissioner is not so satisfied - he shall

refuse the application and shall cause notice in writing

of his decision in relation to the application to be served, by

post or otherwise on the person who made the application."

An exempt account is defined by s.3 as "an account kept in Australia in respect of which a certificate of exemption is in force". It follows from the definition of "taxable account" that an account in respect of which a certificate of exemption is in force is not a taxable account. The right to object to and appeal against a decision refusing the grant of an exemption certificate arises from ss.22 and 23 of the Act.

Religious Institution

25. The exemption of religious institutions from the payment of tax dates back to pre-federation and early State taxing statutes. Section 17 of the Land and Income Tax Assessment Act 1895 (NSW) exempted the income and revenue of all "ecclesiastic, charitable and educational institutions of a public character". So too, did s.19 of the Land and Income Tax Assessment Act 1907 (WA). The Income Tax Assessment Act 1915 (Cwth) by s.11(1) exempted from income tax:

"(d) the income of a religious, scientific, charitable or

public institution."

The same statutory formula was used to create the exemption in s.14(1)(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922 and was duly replicated in s.23 of the 1936 Act.

26. The criteria of religion were identified by the High Court in the Scientology Case as belief in a supernatural Being, Thing or Principle and the acceptance of canons of conduct in order to give effect to that belief - Church of the New Faith v. Commissioner of Payroll Tax (Vic) [1983] HCA 40; (1983) 154 CLR 120 at 136 (Mason AC.J. and Brennan J.). The connotations of the word "religion" have been explained in many decisions in various contexts and it is not necessary to review them here. It has been said that there is a distinction between religion as concerned with God's relation to man and "mere" ethics as concerned with man's relation to man - Barralet v. Attorney General (1980) 3 All ER 918. That dichotomy may be right. But commitment to charitable works as an expression of the acceptance and pursuit of religious ideals is not "mere" ethical behaviour. It will fall within the category of religious action consistently with the approach enumerated in the Scientology case. It does not require a deep knowledge of Christian teaching to appreciate that it demands charitable activity as an integral part of the canons of Christian conduct. For "faith without works is dead". In the context of the religious values which are expressed in the objects of the Foundation, no relevant distinction can be drawn between the evangelistic and missionary activity it supports and the wider charitable objects.

27. The word "institution" was considered by Lord MacNaghten in Mayor of Manchester v. McAdam (1896) AC 500 when he said:

"It means, I suppose, an undertaking formed to promote some

defined purpose having in view generally the instruction or

education of the public. It is the body (so to speak) called

into existence to translate the purpose as conceived in the mind

of the founders into a living and active principle."

A mere trust established to accumulate funds to be paid to a local authority for the benefit of aged and deserving persons has been held not to be a charitable institution under the Canadian Income War Tax Act 1927 - Minister of National Revenue v. Trusts and Guarantee Fidelity Company Limited (1940) AC 138. The Privy Council said in that case at p 150 that:

"An ordinary trust for charity is, indeed, only a

charitable institution in the sense that a farm is an

agricultural institution. It is not in that sense that the word

"institution" is used in the sub-section."

What was said by Lord McNaghten and by the Privy Council in the two cases mentioned, was followed by Gibbs J., with whom Barwick C.J., Menzies and Walsh JJ. agreed, in Stratton v. Simpson (1970) 135 CLR 128 at 158. His Honour was there considering the effect of a clause in a will providing for payment of income from a residuary estate to "...institutions and bodies....exempt from duty under section 134 of the Administration Act 1903-1956..." The Act exempted inter alia "any publicly subscribed medical service or fund in the State which is assisted by any government grant or subsidy" and "any school... inspected and found efficient...". His Honour said that:

"A school could appropriately be called an institution

within the ordinary meaning of the word. Similarly the words

"medical service or fund", if construed to mean the body set up

to organise and control the service or administer the fund, could

also be regarded as describing an institution."

Windeyer J. at 145 could see no reason why, unrestrained by context, a fund raised by public contributions and administered by trustees could not be properly called an institution. The word "institution", he said, is a word of wide denotation. His Honour also referred to the judgment of the Privy Council and to the observations of Walsh JA. (with whom Asprey JA. agreed) in Christian Enterprises Ltd v. Commissioner of Land Tax (1968) 88 WN (Pt.2) (NSW) 112 at 119. Walsh JA. had there agreed with the observations of Nagle J. at first instance that wide and flexible as the word institution might be, it did not apply to a company incorporated to raise funds to promote specified religious purposes. While the company was therefore held not to be a charitable institution, it did answer the description of a "religious society" in s.10(1)(e) of the Land Tax Management Act 1956 (NSW).

28. Consideration was given to the "religious institution" exemption under s.11(1)(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1915 in The Young Men's Church Association of Melbourne v. The Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1926] HCA 2; (1926) 37 CLR 351. The Association was formed "to unite those young men who, regarding the Lord Jesus Christ as their God and Saviour according to the Holy Scripture desire to be His disciples in their doctrine and their life, and to associate their efforts for the extension of His kingdom amongst young men". The by-laws of the association also provided that one of its objects was "the improvement of the spiritual, intellectual, social and physical condition of young men". Holding that the association was a religious institution, Isaacs J. at 357 saw it as "an institution with a very broad religious programme carried out in an extremely practical manner". He entertained no doubt that it was entitled to exemption. Higgins J. at 360, referred to the definition in the Oxford Dictionary of "institution" as "an establishment, organisation or association, instituted for the promotion of some object esp. one of public or general utility, religious, charitable educational, etc. e.g. a church, school, college, hospital, asylum, reformatory, mission or the like; as a literary and philosophical institution, a deaf and dumb institution, the Royal National Lifeboat Institution". His Honour observed that it is true that the name is often popularly applied to the building appropriated to the work of a benevolent or educational institution but that this is rather a transferred sense. Gavan Duffy J. saw the association as "essentially an association of persons holding specific religious views and acting together for the purpose of comforting and encouraging one another, and of inducing others to adopt those religious views and to join that association". He concluded that it was therefore a "religious institution" within the meaning of the Act.

29. Significantly for present purposes, the New South Wales Court of Appeal has held that a body corporate controlled by the Church of England and managing properties vested in it to produce income for the purposes of the Church was not a religious institution for the purposes of the Pay-roll Tax Act 1971 (NSW). The case was Glebe Administration Board v. Commissioner of Pay-roll Tax (NSW) (1987) 19 ATR 297. Priestley JA., with whom McHugh JA. agreed, characterised the Board at 308 as a statutory corporation doing commercial work within limitations fixed by reference to religious principles. It held its capital for the benefit of a religious institution and large amounts of revenue were handed over to that institution. His Honour said:

"To my mind all these matters result in it being accurate

to describe the Board as a legal entity working in a commercial

area, guiding its commercial conduct by the principles of a

religious institution and, in ordinary language, working for that

religious institution. I do not think that this legal entity can

either by an ordinary or a technical use of language be

accurately called a religious institution."

30. More recently in Commissioner for ACT Revenue Collections v. Council of the Dominican Sisters of Australia 91 ATC 4602, a Full Court of the Federal Court considered whether a company limited by guarantee which operated a Catholic teachers' college for a religious order of nuns was itself a religious institution for the purposes of exemption from payroll tax. The Tribunal had held the company was a tax exempt religious institution. Their Honours saw as having considerable force a submission by the Commissioner that the company's object was predominantly educational rather than religious and that its principal activities were the retention of the legal title to the land upon which the college was located and the employment of teachers. Accepting that an institution which has a non-religious purpose incidental to its religious object would not lose its religious quality on that account alone, it was contended that "the means by which religion was pursued had become the principal object of the institution". The Court considered that the deep commitment to religion of the members of the Order and of the company might have led to an inadequate assessment of the matters which should have been taken into account in determining the company's primary object. In the result, it remitted the matter to the Tribunal to consider whether the primary and predominant object of the company was the promotion of religion.

31. The Foundation was established to further the religious purposes of its controllers, Dr and Mrs A, the members of their family and Mr and Mrs B. The ultimate objects it serves are religious and these are reflected in its constitution and rules including the adoption of the tenets of the Scripture Union to which reference was made earlier in these reasons. Notwithstanding this, it is my opinion that the principal function of the Foundation as revealed in its activities, is to hold, invest and disburse funds. The history of its formation indicates that it was established to do just that. It is closely integrated with the business affairs of Dr A and is the vehicle which he and his family use to pursue in a practical way their Christian purposes. But this does not make it a religious institution any more than the Glebe Administration Board was a religious institution. In coming to that conclusion I have regard to its predominate and immediate purposes in the sense contemplated by the Full Federal Court. Quite apart from that the usage of the term "religious institution" jars against the reality of an association which is substantially engaged in commercial activity albeit to serve the religious ends of its founders. I come to this conclusion with some regret and would wish to emphasise that it is a matter of legal characterisation. It does not reflect in any way upon the remarkable generosity, integrity and depth of commitment of Dr A and his family.

32. Counsel for the Foundation sought at the commencement of the hearing to introduce what amounted to a fresh and alternative basis for the appeal namely, that if the Foundation were held not to be a religious institution, then the Commissioner should, in any event, be estopped from denying the grant of a certificate of exemption. The estoppel was said to be based upon his prior conduct in conceding exemption under the Income Tax Assessment Act and the detriment suffered by the Foundation which had arranged its affairs on the assumption that it was exempt. I refused to allow that ground to be advanced. It was mentioned for the first time in written submissions filed on 26 March 1991, some nine days before the hearing. It potentially raised new factual issues. It had not been foreshadowed at the directions hearing in February. These factors were sufficient to justify the refusal to entertain it. In any event, the point was one which, in my opinion, had little, if any, prospect of success. And the Commissioner had indicated by counsel, that a decision adverse to the Foundation would not be implemented retrospectively in relation to the income tax exemption. For these reasons I refuse what would have amounted to an amendment to the grounds of objection.

33. In my opinion the decision under appeal must be affirmed.
