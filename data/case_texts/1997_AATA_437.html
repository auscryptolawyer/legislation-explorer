QT96/377 and Commissioner of Taxation [1997] AATA 437; 37 ATR 1134; 97 ATC 477 (30 October 1997)

Last Updated: 8 May 2009

SALES TAX - whether assessable dealings were taxable dealings - whether exemption applies - whether exemption Item 37 applies - whether quoted at or before time of sale - whether sales tax not legally payable - whether entitled to credit under CR1 or CR5 - whether Commissioner should have exercised his discretion to enter agreement that applicant entitled to credit - objection decision affirmed.

Sales Tax Assessment Bill 1992

Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications Act 1992 - Ss 4, 5, Items 37, 64, 126, 127,

Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935 - First Schedule Item 119

REASONS FOR DECISION



On 20 December, 1996, the applicant applied for review of an objection decision which had been made on 23 October, 1996 by a delegate of the respondent, the Commissioner of Taxation (“Commissioner”). The objection decision had been to allow, in part, the applicant’s objection against the Commissioner’s earlier decision which had been dated 8 February, 1996 and which had been to refuse a refund of sales tax amounting to $79,986.87. As a result of the objection decision, a refund of sales tax amounting to $3,196.65 was allowed under credit ground number 7 (“CR7”) for goods used in the manufacture of earthmoving machinery parts applied to the applicant’s own use in exempt circumstances.



2. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by Mr Schodel and the Commissioner was represented by Mr Coulsen. The documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“T documents”) were admitted in evidence together with a statement of agreed facts.



THE ISSUES



3. There were four issues in this case. The first was whether the dealings upon which the sales tax was paid were “taxable dealings” within the meaning of section 5 of the Sales Tax Assessment Act 1992 (“Assessment Act”). The second issue was whether the applicant is entitled to a refund under credit ground number 1 (“CR1”) of Table 3 of Schedule 1 of the Assessment Act because the sales tax was not legally payable. The third issue was whether the applicant had subsequent dealings with the goods which made the dealings AD3a or AD13a assessable dealings under Table 1 of Schedule 1 of the Assessment Act. If they were such dealings, they would give rise to valid claims for refund under credit ground number 5(“CR5”). The final issue was whether the Commissioner had valid grounds for exercising its discretion under section 59 of the Assessment Act.



THE BACKGROUND



4. After taking into account the statement of agreed facts and the documentary material, we have made several findings of fact which we will set out in this section.



5. The applicant is an earthmoving contractor and more than fifty per cent of its earthmoving business is undertaken pursuant to contracts with State or local government authorities. It applied to the Commissioner for registration under the Assessment Act and was notified of its registration in a letter dated 6 April, 1995 (T documents, pages 33-35).



6. On 23 June, 1995, the applicant lodged an application for a refund of sales tax amounting to $79,986.87. It made its claim in respect of heavy equipment parts purchased during the period 1 July, 1994 to 31 March, 1995. The claim was made on three bases: it was entitled to quote its registration number and did not, it applied the goods it had purchased to its own use in a way that satisfied an exemption item and the goods, or some part or element of them, became an integral part of new products. (T documents, pages 36-38).



CONSIDERATION



7. A person’s liability to pay sales tax is set out in Part 3 of the Assessment Act. Table 1 of Schedule 1 sets out all of the assessable dealings which can be subject to sales tax (sub-section 16(1) and section 5). The time of an assessable dealing is specified in Column 4 of the table. If, at the time of the assessable dealing no exemption applied under Division 2 of Part 3 of the Assessment Act, then it follows that the dealing is a taxable dealing, the person specified in Column 3 is the person liable to pay the tax, the tax becomes payable at the time of the assessable dealing and the tax is due for payment at the time that applies under Division 2 of Part 5 of the Assessment Act (sub-section 16(2) and see also definition of “taxable dealing” in section 5). The amount of the tax is calculated in accordance with sub-section 16(3).

9. Five exemptions are specified in Division 2 of Part 3. Mr Schodel raised two of them in support of his argument that the assessable dealings were not taxable. They were an exemption based on an exemption Item (Subdivision A) and an exemption based on quoting (Subdivision B).



10. Taking first the exemption based on an exemption Item, section 24 provides that:



“An assessable dealing is not taxable if:



(a) the goods are covered by an exemption Item that is in force at the time of the dealing; and



(b) all the requirements of that Item have been met at or before the time of the dealing.”



11. An “exemption Item” means an Item or subitem in Schedule 1 to the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1992 (“Exemptions and Classifications Act”) (Assessment Act, section 5). The Item upon which the applicant relies is Item 37 which provides:



“(1) Machinery, implements or apparatus (other than general-purpose road vehicles or parts for those vehicles) for use by a person mainly in earth-moving in the course of carrying out contracts for a body whose own use of the machinery or equipment would be covered by exemption Item 64, 126 or 127.



(2) In the item, “earth-moving” means the excavation or movement of earth, rock or natural deposits in the soil.



[Parts, accessories and attachments]



12. Item 64 applies to goods for use by a public transport authority (other than a Commonwealth controlled authority) for or in connection with the establishment, conduct or maintenance of transport services. In general terms, Items 126 and 127 apply to goods for use by the Australian government, by a municipal, shire or district council or by certain public authorities.



13. The effect of Schedule 1 in conjunction with the Assessment Act is explained in section 4 of the Exemptions and Classifications Act. The relevant provisions are:



“(2) In broad terms, Schedule 1 has the following effects (through the Assessment Act):



(a) if all the requirements of an exemption Item are satisfied at or before the time of an assessable dealing, the dealing is not taxable;



...

...



(d) an intention to satisfy the requirements of an exemption Item can be a ground for quoting;



...”



A “non-lease AOU” is “an application to own use that does not consist of the granting of a lease” (Assessment Act, section 5).

15. Mr Schodel submitted that Item 37 was satisfied at the time of the dealing. He referred to sub-section 5(2) of the Exemption and Classifications Act which provides that:



“In the case of parts, fittings, attachments or accessories for goods covered by a particular Item or subitem, the intended use of the parts, fittings, attachments or accessories does not have to be for the whole of the period specified in the Table.”



16. That provision, however, does not detract from the requirement in this case that the heavy equipment parts must be “for use by a person” for the purpose specified in Item 37 for at least part of the statutory period. This is apparent from the wording of Item 37 and sub-section 5(2) but it is also underlined by sub-section 5(3). That sub-section provides that the rules in the section have effect subject to any contrary intention in the exemption Item concerned. Nor does sub-section 5(2) detract from the requirements of sub-section 24(2) that the requirements of Item 37 as modified by sub-section 5(2) must be satisfied either at or before the time of the assessable dealing. It is the applicant which carries the burden of proof in establishing that it purchased the parts for that use for it has the burden of proving that the decision under review should have been made differently (Tax Administration Act, section 14ZZK).



17. What is meant by the words “for use by a person” which are used in Item 37? There would seem to be no direct authorities regarding their meaning in the context of either that item or of the Exemptions and Classifications Act. They were considered by French J in the context of Item 119 in the First Schedule to the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935. He did so in Inn Leisure Industries Pty Ltd (Provisional Liquidator appointed) v DF McCloy Pty Ltd and Another (1991) 100 ALR 447 where he reviewed the cases of Commissioner of Taxation (SA) v Ellis & Clark Ltd [1934] HCA 54; (1934) 52 CLR 85, Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Lincoln Industrial Cleaners Pty Ltd [1975] 2 NSWLR 499, Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Hamersley Iron Pty Ltd (1981) 37 ALR 595 and Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Stewart [1984] HCA 11; (1984) 154 CLR 385.



18. French J ultimately adopted the approach of Deane J in Stewart where his Honour had said:



“While the subjective intentions of manufacturer or purchaser are relevant and may well be conclusive, what is required is an objective characterisation of the goods themselves in the light of all the relevant circumstances. That characterisation must be made as at the time when liability for sales tax would otherwise attach. It will, in an appropriate case, be made with the benefit and in the context of knowledge of the actual use which was subsequently made of the goods.” (page 401)



19. After setting out this passage, French J continued:



“The dicta cited from these three cases do not yield any clear test for deciding whether goods are made `for use in’ a particular way. There are, as Sheppard J pointed out [in Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Lincoln Industrial Cleaners Pty Ltd], difficulties in using the intentions of the ultimate purchaser as the criteria. And there is an evident reluctance to tie the approach explicitly adopted by Deane J requires some `objective characterisation’ of the goods. I take this to mean that the characterisation if goods by reference to their purpose or intended use depends upon the view taken by an independent observer having regard to the intrinsic characteristics of the goods (which could be evidence, but not elements, of their purpose), the intention of the manufacturer, and that of the person, if any, for whom the goods are manufactured. This may reduce logically to the imputation of intention or purpose to the manufacturer, for neither can be divorced from some human agency. But that process does not require a judgement based solely on the manufacturer’s subjective intentions. These observations must be subject to the caveat that each item in the First Schedule is to be read according to its own language and that the application of purposive terms in different contexts may differ. But the general approach adopted by Deane J is, in my respectful opinion, the proper one to be taken to the application of the purposive words `to be used for’ in the disqualifying condition upon exemption in Item 119.” (pages 458-459)



20. In this case, there is no evidence that the heavy equipment parts were, at the time of the sales which were the assessable dealings, for use by the applicant for the purpose specified in Item 37 for either the whole, or part of, the statutory period. By virtue of their characteristics, the parts are fit for use by a person mainly in earthmoving for the purpose of carrying out work for State and local government authorities. Their characteristics are such that they are also fit for earthmoving for the purpose of carrying out work other than for State and local government authorities.



21. We have looked to the intentions of the applicant when it purchased the parts. There is none upon which we can make any finding of its intentions at that time. We have looked to any evidence as to the use which was actually made of the parts but again have been unable to find any. The fact that the applicant carries out more than fifty per cent of its earthmoving contracts with tax exempt local government authorities does not assist us. It does not follow from that finding that the particular heavy equipment parts sold in the assessable dealings were mainly for use for the purpose specified in Item 37. The particular parts may have been intended for use in equipment which was never, or only rarely, used in undertaking the applicant’s earthmoving contracts with State and local government authorities. Those parts may, at the time of the assessable dealings, have been for use as spares to be called upon as needed and with no thought as to whether they would be used, at any time during the statutory period, be for use mainly in earthmoving to undertake contracts for State or local government authorities.



22. In view of this, we are not satisfied that the requirements of Item 37 are met at or before the time of the assessable dealings in relation to the heavy equipment parts it purchased between 1 July, 1994 and 31 March, 1995.



23. The second exemption which is relevant in this case is found in sub-section 27(1) of the Assessment Act. That provides that:



“A sale is not taxable if the purchaser quotes for the sale at or before the time of the sale.”



24. To “quote” means to “... quote a registration number or exemption declaration” (Assessment Act, section 5). At the time of the assessable dealings, the applicant was not registered but, Mr Schodel submitted, was entitled to quote an exemption declaration. It was entitled, he said, in the circumstances set out in paragraph 83(1)(a). That provides that:



“An unregistered person (`the quoter’) may quote an exemption declaration for a dealing with goods if, at the time of quoting, the quoter has the intention of dealing with the goods in any of the following ways:



(a) using the goods so as to satisfy an exemption Item that is in force at the time of quoting;



...”



25. Mr Schodel argued that the applicant was entitled to quote at the time of the assessable dealing as it intended to use the heavy equipment parts mainly in earthmoving in the course of carrying out contracts for State and local government authorities. The Table in sub-section 5(1) makes it clear that the relevant intention is sufficient where the items set out in Schedule 1 of the Exemption and Classifications Act are a ground for quoting (see also sub-section 4(2)). It did not matter that the applicant did not quote its exemption declaration at the time of the assessable dealing as a “`taxable dealing’ means an assessable dealing ... for which no exemption is available under Division 2 of Part 3” (emphasis added). As the applicant could have used Item 37 as a ground for quoting, that exemption was available to the applicant and the assessable dealing was not a taxable dealing, Mr Schodel concluded.



26. We do not accept Mr Schodel’s submission for we are unable to overcome the clear words of sub-section 27(1) of the Assessment Act. According to those clear words, the applicant must quote for the sale at or before the time of the sale if the exemption is to apply. It does not give an exemption if the applicant could have quoted if it had chosen to do so. The exemption is only available to the applicant if it complies with sub-section 27(1). As it has not done so, the exemption does not apply.



27. As neither of the exemptions claimed by the applicant is available to it, the assessable dealings are taxable dealings.



28. Mr Schodel also sought to describe the assessable dealings as “tax-bearing dealings” rather than “taxable dealings”. The term “tax-bearing dealing” is defined in section 5 of the Assessment Act to mean



“... in relation to an amount of tax borne by a person, means the dealing through which, or because of which, the tax was borne”.



29. When viewed from the point of view of the applicant, the assessable dealing which we have found to be a taxable dealing is a dealing which is a tax-bearing dealing because it is the dealing through which it has borne tax. That would become relevant if the applicant were claiming a credit under credit grounds CR6, CR7 and CR12 (Assessment Act, section 52). It is not making such a claim and, furthermore, even if it were, the fact that an assessable dealing may be regarded as a tax-bearing dealing for some purposes does not detract from our conclusion that it is a taxable dealing for others.



30. The next issue to consider is whether the applicant is entitled to a refund of sales tax under CR1 because the tax was not legally payable. Part 4 of the Assessment Act is concerned with credits of sales tax. Table 3 in Schedule 1 of the Assessment Act sets out the situations in which a claimant is entitled to a credit. CR1, upon which the applicant relies in this argument and CR5 upon which it next relies, are:

31. As Mr Coulsen submitted, the claimant must first have paid an amount of tax before it can claim under CR1. In each of the assessable dealings which we have found to be taxable dealings, it is the seller and not the purchaser (the applicant) who is liable to pay the tax (Assessment Act, Table 1, Column 3 and paragraph 16(2)(b)) at the time of the sale (Assessment Act, Table 1, Column 4 and paragraph 16(2)(c)). It is, therefore, the seller who is liable to pay and who can be said to have paid an amount of tax and not the applicant. It is of no consequence that the applicant has paid a price that included tax and so has “borne tax” as that term is defined by sub-section 11(3) of the Assessment Act (section 5).



32. As the applicant has not paid the tax, it is not entitled to a credit under CR1. We note in passing that, even if it were irrelevant that the applicant had not paid the sales tax, the tax could not be described as “not legally payable”. There were assessable dealings which were taxable dealings as no exemption applied to them. Tax was payable in accordance with sub-section 16(2).



33. CR5 is also relied upon by the applicant (see paragraph 30 above). The applicant may be regarded as a “taxpayer” for it is the claimant for a credit (Assessment Act, section 5). It has also borne tax on the heavy equipment parts for it purchased the goods for a price that included tax (Assessment Act, sub-section 11(3)). Subject to certain exceptions which are not applicable in this case, CR5 only comes into play when there is a subsequent assessable dealing that is not taxable. In this case, there is no evidence of any subsequent assessable dealings by the applicant.



34. Mr Schodel also relied on sub-section 21(2) of the Assessment Act in pursuing his argument that credit ground CR5 is applicable. His argument was dependent upon our finding that the heavy equipment parts were not taxable because the exemption under section 27 was available to the applicant. As we have found that the exemption is not available, we have not set out his argument.



35. Mr Schodel’s final submission was that the Commissioner should have entered an agreement under section 59 of the Assessment Act. That section provides that:



“(1) The Commissioner may enter into an agreement with a person regarding the circumstances in which the person is to be entitled to credits and the manner of calculating and claiming such credits.



(2) So far as the agreement is inconsistent with this Act, the agreement prevails.”



36. Mr Coulsen submitted that the circumstances of the case do not warrant the exercise of the Commissioner’s discretion. He referred to various factors militating against the exercise of the discretion. The applicant had not, for instance, chosen to quote an exemption declaration. A considerable period of time had elapsed between the time of the first assessable dealing and the date of the applicant’s claim for a refund. If the applicant were to receive a credit of sales tax, it would be unjustly enriched at the expense of the exempt State and local government authorities for which it undertook contract work. The authorities paid a contract price which included a component for sales tax paid by the applicant.



37. Section 59 does not give any indication of the circumstances in which the Commissioner may choose to enter an agreement of the type to which it refers or the limitations upon his discretion. We may try to discern those circumstances and limitations by reference to the legislation itself as well as from extrinsic material such as the Minister’s Second Reading Speech and the Explanatory Memorandum. In doing so, we rely on the words of Woodward J when he said in Alexandra Private Geriatric Hospital Pty Ltd v Blewett and Anor (1984) 56 ALR 265:



“It is against this background of legislation, judicially construed, that I must consider the amendments to s40AA of the Act ... [regarding the calculation of fees].



In doing so I have been invited by the respondents to make use of the Minister’s Second Reading Speech to assist me in determining the effect of the amendments on the general scheme and purpose of the Act. It was argued that, whether or not the amendments to the Acts Interpretation Act 1901, concerning the use of extrinsic material in the interpretation of an Act, are in force by the time this judgment is delivered (as in fact they are), there is recent authority entitling me to do so, see TCN Channel Nine v AMP Society ([1982] FCA 169; (1982) 42 ALR 496 at 507-8, and cases there cited.



I prefer to rely on that authority rather than on the amending legislation, because the new s15AB of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 relates to `the interpretation of a provision of an Act’. What I am concerned with here is not exactly a question of interpreting a provision. It is rather the determination of the policy underlying the part of an Act which includes particular provisions granting powers, in order to determine what restrictions are implied on the exercise of those powers.” (pages 271-272)



38. The purpose of the Assessment Act is set out in its long title where it is said to be



“An Act relating to the imposition and collection of a tax on dealings with goods that have been manufactured in Australia or imported into Australia, and for related purposes.”



39. The Second Reading Speech delivered by the then Minister for Higher Education and Employment Services and Minister Assisting the Treasurer in introducing the Sales Tax Assessment Bill 1992 (“the Bill”) does not take the matter any further other than that the Bill part of a package of proposed legislation designed to replace and simplify the sales tax law then in place (Hansard, House of Representatives, 26 May, 1992, page 2769).



40. The explanatory memorandum to the Bill set out an overview of the legislation. It stated that



“The fundamental principle of both the existing and the new sales tax law is that tax should be imposed on the last wholesale sale of goods that have not been previously used in Australia. If the goods are not the subject of a wholesale sale (for example, because they are sold only by retail by their manufacturer), then the law seeks to impose tax on the appropriate retail sale or use of the goods. Tax is imposed on the wholesale selling price of goods. If goods are not sold by wholesale, alternative values are used. Tax is imposed on all goods at the general rate of 20%, unless the goods are specifically listed as being exempt, or taxable at a rate other than the general 20% rate. The sales tax law includes a large number of goods that are exempt from tax, either in their own right or when used in particular circumstances.” (page 9)



41. The explanatory memorandum goes on to deal with specific provisions of the Bill. Of clause 59, which was to become section 59, it is said:



“Agreements relating to calculation of credits: The Commissioner will be allowed to enter into agreements with any person regarding substitute methods of calculation credit entitlements Such agreements will override the legislation. [clause 59]” (page 190)



42. The explanatory memorandum would seem to deal with only one aspect of section 59 for, on its clear words, the Commissioner has a discretion to enter an agreement with respect to both the manner of calculating and claiming credits and the circumstances in which a person is entitled to claim credits.



43. As the matter was not fully argued by both parties in this case, we will not attempt to consider the principles defining the limits of the Commissioner’s powers. Whatever those limits may be, they must take account of the fundamental principle of the package of sales tax legislation that, subject only to defined exceptions, sales tax should be imposed upon the last wholesale sale of goods. Equally, those limits must take account of the clear provisions governing the situations in which a person is entitled to a credit and the manner in which that credit is calculated. The credit grounds fall into six broad categories as described in the explanatory memorandum: credits for overpaid tax, credits to avoid goods being taxed twice, export-related credits, import-related credits, credits where goods are leased and miscellaneous credits (relating generally to research and development and bad debts).



44. Putting aside the specific provisions relating to each of the credit grounds and looking only to their general categories, there are no grounds in this case for concluding that the applicant should be entitled to a credit. With regard to all but the first category, there is no suggestion in the evidence that there broad concepts are in any way applicable in this case. In relation to the first category, tax was not overpaid for the applicant, for whatever reason, did not choose to take advantage of sections 24 and section 27 which would have been available to it. In any event, in the absence of any evidence on the point, we are not in a position to find that the applicant has not passed on the amount of the sales tax to those with whom it contracts to undertake earthmoving work. Bearing in mind that a credit is only available to a claimant to the extent to which it has not passed on the sales tax and bearing in mind that the applicant did not attempt to take advantage of exemptions that were open to it, we do not consider that this is an appropriate case in which the Commissioner should have exercised his discretion under section 59 of the Assessment Act.



45. For the reasons we have given, we affirm the objection decision of the respondent dated 23 October, 1996.
