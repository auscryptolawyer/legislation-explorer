Welsh and Commissioner for Superannuation [1978] AATA 79 (8 December 1978)

Last Updated: 14 March 2007







Superannuation - Preservation of rights - Failure to make election - Extension of time - Special circumstances - Hardship - Superannuation Act 1922, s119U, s135.

Frederick Joseph Welsh

Commissioner for Superannuation

AAT Decision No 119

No: 78/2200

A.N. Hall (Senior Member), R.B. Hutchison (Member), W.J. Smith (Member)

8 December 1978

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

No. 78/2200

Re: FREDERICK JOSEPH WELSH

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

DECISION

The Tribunal decides to affirm the decision of the acting Commissioner for Superannuation made on 25 May, 1978, confirming the earlier decision of the Commissioner's delegate, refusing Mr Welsh's application for an extension of time in which to make an election under section 119U of the Superannuation Act 1922.





REASONS FOR DECISION

This is an application by Mr Frederick Joseph Welsh for review of the decision of the acting Commissioner for Superannuation made on 25 May, 1978, refusing to extend the period of time for the making "by Mr Welsh of an election under sub-section 119U(1) of the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the Act") so as to preserve his superannuation rights under Division 3 of Part XA of the Act. Mr Welsh had resigned from the Commonwealth Public Service and ceased to be a contributor to the Superannuation Fund on the 4 January, 1974. Sub-section 119U(1) of the Act allowed him 21 days after he ceased to be a contributor in which to make the necessary election for preservation of rights. He did not make an election within that period. Sub-section 119U(2) then provides that:-

"The Board may, if it is satisfied that there are special circumstances that justify it in so doing, extend the period for the making of an election under the last preceding sub-section."

By virtue of section 119ZEA of the Act, inserted by Act No. 32 of 1976, the reference in sub-section 119U(2) to "the Board" is to be read as including a reference to the Commissioner for Superannuation. The acting Commissioner, not being satisfied that there were special circumstances that justified him in so doing, refused to extend the period for making of the election. Hence, this application for review.

The arguments relied upon by the applicant in support of his application fell into two main categories:-

(a)arguments relating to the circumstances surrounding his resignation and his completion and signing of an application for refund of contributions on the 4 January, 1974; and
(b)arguments based on hardship.

The Evidence before the Tribunal

Mr Welsh, who is now 57 years of age, has had a varied career. Between 22 February, 1951, and the 28 August, 1953, he was employed in the Commonwealth Public Service. He then spent some 12 years working for the Sydney City Council from 31 August, 1953, to 31 August, 1965, followed by a short period from the 11 October, 1965, to the 5 November, 1965, with the Rockdale Municipal Council, after which, on 12 November, 1965, he re-entered the Commonwealth Public Service. He remained in the Service until the 4 January, 1974. On the 7 January, 1974, he commenced working for the Builders' Registration Board of Queensland. He remained in that employment until 4 March, 1977. He was then re-appointed to the Commonwealth Public Service on 7 March, 1977, and is still so employed.



It was not until 18 April, 1977, after he had rejoined the Commonwealth Public Service that Mr Welsh first raised with the Commissioner for Superannuation whether his earlier service from 12 November, 1965, to the 4 January, 1974, could be recognised for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 197.6 (as it then was). What he in fact asked was:-
"(1) Can I pay back the Superannuation paid to me when I resigned on the 4.1.74 to take up a position with the Builders Registration Board of Queensland. I received a refund of $3,694.91 your reference of 20/5/74 is 282284.

(2) Also what interest if any would be required to be paid. On the old super I intended to retire at 65 years.

(3) How much would I have to pay back if I was to retire at 60 years (which I doubt if I will).

(4) I have already requested that the Board take over my superannuation from the Builder's Registration Board of Qld. Could I also have your Board take over my original Superannuation paid by me to the N.S.W. Local Government Superannuation Fund and still being paid by me personally since I joined the Australian Public Service in 1965."

Having had no definite answer to his enquiry, Mr Welsh wrote again on 17 September, 1977, claiming recognition of his earlier service. In order to bring the matter to a head, the Commissioner decided to treat Mr Welsh's letter as, in effect, an application for extension of time within which to make an election under section 119U of the Act.

In his correspondence with the Commissioner and in his evidence before the Tribunal, Mr Welsh maintained that at no time was he advised that he could elect to preserve his superannuation rights. If he had been so advised, he would have made that election, in preference to accepting a refund of contributions, without interest. At the time, his understanding had been that a refund was all he was entitled to.

Nevertheless, one of the documents before us was a form SB.2A signed by Mr Welsh on 4 January, 1974, the date on which his resignation from the Public Service became effective. The form was headed:-

"Commonwealth of Australia SUPERANNUATION ACT APPLICATION FOR REFUND OF CONTRIBUTIONS

OR

ELECTION FOR PRESERVATION OF SUPERANNUATION RIGHTS".

Mr Welsh had originally believed that the details on the form (apart from his signature) had been completed by someone other than himself. Following his inspection of the original form during the hearing of the matter, however, he agreed that the handwriting was his and that the deletions made from the printed words on the form were also made by him. Mr Welsh's signature was witnessed by a Miss Swain, who was apparently a fellow employee of Mr Welsh in the Department of Housing and Construction in Brisbane. We were informed that Miss Swain (now Mrs Payne) had been contacted by the Commissioner but that she had no recollection of the circumstances in which she had come to witness Mr Welsh's signature.

The details on the form which were completed in Mr Welsh's own handwriting commenced with his full name and address and then followed the identification of the Department in which he was employed, namely the Department of Housing and Construction, the word "Queensland" and his designation as "Works Supervisor". The printed form then continued with three separate alternatives, namely:-

"I ... hereby* (apply for a refund of contributions

(apply for a refund of contributions on

( reserve units

(elect for preservation of superannuation rights

under the provisions of the Superannuation Act." The asterisk appearing after the word "hereby" led to a footnote stating "delete whichever inapplicable". The latter two alternatives were struck out so that the form requested a refund of contributions.

There then appeared in the centre of the form a space for insertion of the address to which a refund cheque was to be forwarded. This was completed by Mr Welsh by inserting his home address at Palm Beach, Queensland. Immediately underneath this space there appeared again in bold type the heading "OUTLINE OF CONDITIONS FOR PRESERVATION OF SUPERANNUATION RIGHTS". The bottom half of the page described the nature of the rights which could be preserved. This description included, in sharp relief, although not in quite such large type as the earlier headings, a summary in the margin of the entitlements which might flow from electing to preserve rights. These were:-

(i) a transfer value; or (ii) a deferred benefit; or (iii) a refund of contributions. There were thus two references in heavy bold type, at the top and in the centre of the page, to preservation of superannuation rights, whilst the lower part of the page was devoted entirely to an explanation of what those rights might be.

Mr Welsh claimed that he had no recollection of completing and signing the application for refund of contributions. He said that, at the time of his resignation, he was "as mad as a March hare" over the inefficiency of the War Service Homes Division in Brisbane, by whom he

was then employed, and over their lack of understanding of his problems. "Obviously", he said, he had not read the bottom part of the form at all. He conceded that he may have made a mistake or a "clerical error" but that, in making that mistake, there were special circumstances.

The "special circumstances" to which Mr Welsh referred may be briefly summarised as follows. In order to be closer to expert medical attention for his wife who was suffering from cancer, Mr Welsh had arranged a transfer from his position in the Commonwealth Public Service in Darwin to the War Service Homes Division in the Department of Housing and Construction at Brisbane in the middle of 1973. To obtain the transfer he accepted a lower classification as a Works Supervisor. He claimed that when he was interviewed for the 30b he had been "given to understand" that he would be assigned to work in the Gold Coast area "eventually". This did not eventuate and, in fact, the Department later denied any such commitment. As Mr Welsh had established his home on the Gold Coast, he thus was obliged to travel 67 miles from the Gold Coast to Brisbane and return each day. At the same time, however, the Department was sending an officer from Brisbane each week to the Gold Coast in order to do exactly the same work as Mr Welsh was doing in Brisbane. Mr Welsh considered that such a system was highly inefficient and, accordingly, on 10 December, 1973, he wrote to the Regional Director of War Service Homes requesting that he be transferred to the Gold Coast area. This request was refused by letter dated 11 December, 1973, on the grounds (inter alia) that it was not Departmental policy to leave a Works Supervisor in one area indefinitely. In the light of this advice, Mr Welsh calculated that he had the prospect of only one year working on the coast during the 13 years remaining until his retirement. He came to the conclusion that he would finish up a nervous wreck if he had to drive from the Gold Coast to Brisbane every day for 13 years.

He thought the Division's policy was "very poor" and that it demonstrated a lack of efficiency. In his "moment of flame and duress", he said that he "wasn't thinking very clearly" and decided to put in his resignation. This he did by letter dated 2 January, 1974, saying that he had considered the Director's reply to his request for a transfer and that he must resign "for health reasons". We were informed that the letter of resignation then went on:-

"I desire to go on leave from the finish of business on 4 January, 1974, for the period of leave owing to me, and my resignation to take effect from the completion of such leave."

On 4 January, 1974, the Department replied to Mr Welsh by letter stating:-

"Your resignation from the Public Service with effect from the close of business on the 4 January, 1974, has been accepted."

Digressing for a moment, it is perhaps worthy of comment that, according to Mr Welsh's evidence, there were other matters which had caused him some annoyance. Whereas other Works Supervisors were able to take their official vehicles home, he was not allowed to do so because he had to travel from the coast. There were also difficulties over his obtaining leave when he wanted it. He claimed that he was given the usual "new boy" treatment of last preference over leave periods. He was also having difficulty in ascertaining from the Department what his furlough entitlements might be, having written on the 5 November, 1973, for advice in that regard but having received no reply to his request.

Mr Welsh claimed that the acceptance of his resignation with such immediate effect was quite unexpected. He told us that he had submitted his resignation in anticipation of going on leave (i.e. furlough) for 3 months, and had not expected to be told he was finishing up on the Friday afternoon. As a consequence, he said that he had "no chance to rethink". Had he gone on 3 months furlough before resigning, Mr Welsh asserted that he might possibly have "woken up" to himself. He would have "cooled down", decided that he was being stupid and would have withdrawn his resignation.

As it was, there had been no opportunity for the Superannuation Board to be advised of his intending retirement, with the result that the Board had not advised him of his rights. Moreover, the Personnel Section of the Department had given him no assistance or explanation as to what his rights might be and what benefits may flow from electing to preserve his rights.

Mr Welsh referred to the instruction manual prepared by the Commissioner's office, which stressed the need for adequate counselling of persons who were resigning from the Service so that they might be fully aware of the options available to them under the Act. The speed with which his resignation had been accepted, his annoyance with the Department's inefficiency and lack of consideration, combined with continuing anxiety over his wife's health had all militated against him and had, he said, affected his judgment.

Before leaving this aspect of the matter, it is, we think, appropriate to quote three

paragraphs from a submission which Mr Welsh placed before the Commissioner in March, 1978. This submission was in support of Mr Welsh's request for reconsideration of the decision originally taken by a delegate of the Commissioner refusing to grant an extension of time:-

"6. I would have remained a member of the Fund had I known at the time this was possible. The Personnel Section never explained the details. I admit maybe I should have asked. However, I was quite mad with the Department at the time because of what I consider this inefficiency and lack of understanding of my problems.
My resignation was prompted because of my wife's health which was affected by cancer, a car accident which caused severe facial injuries and bone grafts also plastic surgery, followed by a further outbreak of cancer, which caused no end of concern when after the operation the P.M.G. lost the sample which was sent South for examination. This resulted in a further disfiguring operation of my wife's throat. So you can see many worries may have clouded my reasoning at the time.

I would ask you to consider would I have not stayed in the fund had I have known it was possible! Otherwise why would I have kept paying my local government superannuation all these years."

Finally, we were informed by Mr Welsh that having finished-up his employment with the Commonwealth quite unexpectedly on 4 January, 1974, he was nevertheless able to commence working the following Monday with the Builders' Registration Board of Queensland, where he was assigned to the Gold Coast area. When asked how this had occurred, Mr Welsh replied:-

"I finished up on the Friday and started on the Monday. I seen an ad and I went and asked them about the situation. The position was for someone under forty-five. I said, look, is that fair dinkum? I wrote my qualifications down and they said, well, under the circumstances we will look at it and I got the job. I think that was because I was an older fellow and I was put on the Gold Coast area. And I think I was put there because they reckoned an older fellow had to handle the type of people that resided in areas."

Following the completion of the hearing, the Tribunal requested further information from the parties as to the date on which Mr Welsh applied for the position advertised by the Builders' Registration Board and the date on which he was notified of acceptance of his application. Mr Welsh informed us that he applied for the job on 26 November, 1973, and that he was notified of his appointment by-letter from the Board dated 13 December, 1973, which he would have received on or about 17 December, 1973. The letter of appointment, a copy of which was forwarded to us by Mr Welsh, indicated that the commencement date of his appointment would be 7 January, 1974; that he would have to undergo training in Brisbane for a period of four to six weeks; and that he would begin operations in the area to which he was assigned on 18 February, 1974. The designated area of operations was from the N.S.W. border, north to Pimpana and west to the Albert Shire boundary. The arguments of hardship related to the delay of three months in making his refund of contributions and to the fact that by reason of his resignation being accepted on the 4 January, 1974, instead of at the end of his 3 months furlough, he had missed the opportunity to share in the distribution of surplus superannuation funds. In this regard, the Tribunal notes had Mr Welsh been a contributor (or had he been a person to whom deferred benefits were applicable) on 11 April, 1974, the date of commencement of the Superannuation (Distribution of Surplus) Act 1974, it appears that he would have been entitled to participate in the distribution. But his main ground of hardship related to the diminution in pension to which he would be entitled on ultimate retirement unless the period of time for making the election was extended. Under the 1922 Act, the consequence of an election, if made within time, would have been that, although Mr Welsh would not have been entitled to a transfer value under section 119V ("because the superannuation scheme operated by the Builders' Registration Board, of which he became a member, was not an "eligible" scheme), he would have been entitled to a deferred benefit under section 119W. This entitlement would have arisen because he became employed in "public employment" within the meaning of section 119Q within 3 months after ceasing to be a contributor to the Commonwealth Fund (see section 119N (l)(a)). It is unnecessary to explore in detail the precise circumstances in which the deferred benefit might have become payable, except to note that, subject to the quite complex provisions of section 119W, Mr Welsh could potentially have benefitted in the event of him becoming incapacitated for work by reason of invalidity or of physical or mental incapacity, in the event of his death, or in the event of his attaining the age of 65 years or, if he had so elected, an earlier age not being earlier than 60 years of age. Had he again become a contributor by rejoining the Commonwealth Public Service prior to 1 July, 1976, (the date of commencement of the Superannuation Act 1976) "the deferred benefits applicable in respect of him shall not become, or be, payable while he is such a contributor" (section 119ZB).

However, Mr Welsh in fact rejoined the Commonwealth Public Service after that date. He would thus have been subject to the more beneficial provisions of the 1976 Act. By virtue of section 114(1) of that Act, the deferred benefits would have ceased to be applicable by reason of Mr Welsh again becoming an eligible employee in 1977 before attaining the age of 60 years. By the operation of section 8(2) of the 1976 Act, there would have been added to the period of contributory service, which commenced when he rejoined the Commonwealth Public Service in 1977, his earlier period of contributory service from 12 November, 1965, to 4 January, 1974. As

the pension payable under the 1976 Act increases proportionally with the number of complete years of service (see section 56(3) and Table 2 in Schedule 1 to that Act), Mr Welsh would clearly have been better off had he elected to preserve his rights when he resigned on 4 January, 1974. Assuming he were to retire at 65, the difference in his annual rate of pension when expressed as a percentage of his final annual rate of salary would appear to be 24%, if his period of contributory service is only 12 years, from 7 March, 1977, to 5 November, 1985, (when he turns 65 years of age), as opposed to 40% if his earlier period of service (8 complete years) were available to be added to his current service.

The question which arises for our determination is whether we are satisfied that there are "special circumstances" that justify us in extending the period of 21 days within which Mr Welsh may make his election. Mr Pearson, on behalf of the Commissioner, submitted that, in the context of section 119U, special circumstances meant "something quite rare, quite unique". He pointed to the substantial benefits which an election to preserve rights may confer upon a person who has ceased to be a contributor and submitted that any period of time allowed beyond the day on which a person ceased to be a contributor conferred an opportunity for making an election with the benefit of hindsight. Thus, for Parliament to have allowed a period of 21 days after the date on which a person ceased to be a contributor for making an election, was itself a considerable concession. The longer the period is extended beyond 21 days, the greater the opportunity for persons, with the benefit of hindsight, to make elections which could be termed "selection against the Fund".



The Tribunal's Findings

The provisions of Part XA of the Act, with respect to preservation of rights, were inserted by Act No. 46 of 1971, which was assented to and came into operation on the 25 May, 1971. The entitlements conferred by that Part had thus been in existence for some 21/2 years before Mr Welsh resigned. Prior to the introduction of that amendment, it was, we believe, generally true that a Commonwealth employee resigning of his own volition before attaining retirement age to take up employment elsewhere, usually received only a refund of his contributions.

But, as our analysis of Mr Welsh's potential entitlements discloses, the 1971 amendment represented a very significant change of policy. Except in the case of a person who had

completed 20 years eligible employment at the time when he ceased to be a contributor to the Fund (in which case, by virtue of section 119W(l)(b), deferred benefits are applicable whatever the subsequent employment of the person may be), the key to the new rights was the entry into public employment within (in the majority of cases) 3 months of ceasing to be a contributor (see sections 119N(l)(a), 119V, 119W and 119Y(2)). If the person who had less than 20 years eligible service was not employed in public employment within that period, his election for preservation lapsed and his only entitlement was to receive a refund of contributions (section 119Y(1)).

If, as a consequence of making an election under section 119U, a transfer value to another eligible superannuation scheme becomes payable under section 119V of the Act, or, if a transfer value is not payable, a deferred benefit becomes applicable under section 119W of the Act, any benefit that would otherwise have been payable to the person by reason of his resignation is not payable (section 119X). In other words, the price of making the election is that the contributor must forego his entitlement under section 51 to a refund of his contributions. Furthermore, if after payment of the refund of contributions an election is validly made under section 119U of the Act, the election does not have any effect unless an amount equal to the amount of the refund of contributions is paid to the Fund within 7 days after the date of the election, or within such further period as the Board in special circumstances allows (section 119X(2)).

It is against this background that the discretionary power conferred upon the Commissioner to extend the period of time must be considered. The general rule established by Parliament itself is that an election must be made within 21 days. Nevertheless, the Commissioner is empowered to make an exception to that general rule if he is satisfied that there are special circumstances that justify him in so doing. Beyond indicating that the circumstances must be "special", Parliament gave no guidance as to the circumstances in which the discretion should be exercised. The very use of the word "special" is designed to make it possible for the Commissioner to consider each case individually in relation to its own particular circumstances; cf. Ex parte Bucknell [1936] HCA 67; (1956) 56 C.L.R. 221 at p. 224. Nevertheless, the discretion must be exercised with a view to achieving ends or objects which are conformable with the scope and object of the legislation: see Shrimpton v The Commonwealth [1945] HCA 4; (1945) 69 C.L.R. 613 at p. 620 per Latham C.J.

Turning then to the arguments advanced by Mr Welsh, we are of the opinion, firstly, that the form signed by Mr Welsh on the 4 January, 1974, was adequate to alert him to the existence of the alternative rights which he had under the Act. Having regard to the way in which the form has been drafted, it does call upon the person completing the form to make a choice between, on the one hand, acceptance of a refund of contributions which is the right of any contributor who resigns from the Service (section 51) and, on the other, making an election for preservation of superannuation rights.

Even accepting Mr Welsh's evidence that he may not have had the benefit of any personal explanation of the alternatives available to him under the Act, nevertheless, in our view, a man of Mr Welsh's age and experience could have been expected to ask for an explanation if, at the time of his resignation, he had been interested in preserving his rights as opposed to obtaining a refund of his contributions. He was, after all, in the Brisbane area at that time, where adequate advice should have been available, if sought.

Mr Welsh offered a variety of reasons why his act of completing and signing the Application for Refund (Form SB.2A) should not be seen as evidence, either that he was aware of the existence of the preservation rights or that he had made any deliberate decision to apply for a refund. He claimed to have been under "duress" and "mad" over his treatment by the Department. He suggested that his many worries over his wife's health may have "clouded his reasoning" at the time. He said that "obviously" he had not read the form.

From the evidence and the correspondence subsequently placed before us, we find that the major reason for Mr Welsh's resignation was his inability to persuade the War Service Homes Division to allow him to be stationed permanently on the Gold Coast. That resignation, far from being a precipitate and unexpected event appears to us to have been the predictable culmination of a simmering dissatisfaction which Mr Welsh had generated during his short period of employment with the War Service Homes Division. Whilst it is possible that, had he been successful in persuading the Department to appoint him permanently to the Gold Coast area, Mr Welsh may not have resigned, the probability that he would be unsuccessful was clearly so great in Mr Welsh's mind that he had taken the precaution to secure alternative employment with the Builders' Registration Board where he was expected to commence duty on 7 January, 1974. It is not without significance that his resignation was tendered almost

immediately after the Christmas/New Year holiday break, during which period he presumably had ample time to consider his position. Accordingly, we find that there was nothing in the circumstances leading up to Mr Welsh's resignation which would satisfy us that his decision to tender his resignation on 2 January, 1974, was made otherwise than with a full and considered appreciation of the probability that his resignation would be accepted. We further find that he had armed himself against that very real probability by having alternative employment immediately available.

We have therefore concluded that, when he resigned, Mr Welsh was aware of what he was doing. We find ourselves unable to accept, therefore, that Mr Welsh could have completed the form as he did and at the same time have remained unaware, either that its purpose was to signify a choice or as to the general nature of that choice. We think that these conclusions are entirely consistent with Mr Welsh's references during his evidence to the "mistake" which he made when he resigned. That mistake, of course, was not irremediable and could have been corrected, as of right, at any time within the 21 days allowed by the Act.

With respect to the arguments based on hardship, it is no doubt true that, if he is unable to make a late election, Mr Welsh will be worse off in terms of his percentage of pension on eventual retirement, assuming he remains in the Commonwealth Public Service until age 65. But that probably applies to any contributor who, having once resigned, decided not to make an election for preservation of rights and then, on subsequently rejoining the service, realised, with the wisdom of hindsight, that he would have been wiser to preserve his rights. Also., it should not be forgotten that, in the meantime, Mr Welsh has had some benefit from his refund of contributions.

For all these reasons, and having given careful consideration to all the matters raised by Mr Welsh both at and since the hearing, we are not satisfied that, conformably with the prima facie rule laid down by Parliament that an election shall be made within 21 days, there are any special circumstances in relation to Mr Welsh which would justify us in now extending the period of time for him to make an election under section 119U of the Act.

There was, however, another respect in which hardship was said to be relevant under the Act. Section 135(1) of the Act provides as follows:-

"135. (1.) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act has been or is made by an employee or pensioner before the commencement, or after





the expiration, of the prescribed period, and the Board is satisfied that -
(a) hardship would accrue to, or to the dependants of, the employee or pensioner if the election were not recognized; and

(b) in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognized,
the Board may recognize the election as if it had been made within the prescribed period."

The section was drawn to our attention by Mr Pearson during argument, but we were informed that Mr Welsh's application had not been considered by the Commissioner under that provision. Indeed, in Mr Pearson's submission, section 135 had no application in this case, having regard to the particular provision in section 119U regarding "special circumstances". Although Mr Welsh had not initially based his case on that section, he nevertheless sought to bring himself within its ambit and submitted that the Tribunal could call the section in aid as justifying a decision in his favour.

We are not satisfied, however, that section 135 of the Act has any application in the circumstances of this case. The section refers to the making of an election after the expiration of the prescribed period for so doing and confers a discretion upon the Board to recognise that election as if it had been made within the prescribed period. In the present case, an election cannot be made unless the period of time for making the election is first extended by the Commissioner pursuant to section 119U(2). Unless the Commissioner (or this Tribunal on review) considers that there are special circumstances that justify it in granting that extension of time, the contributor has no entitlement under section 119U(1) to make an election at all. In other words, section 119U appears to stand apart from those elections to which section 135 may have potential application.

We accordingly affirm the decision of the acting Commissioner for Superannuation which is subject to review, namely the decision made on 25 May, 1978, confirming the earlier decision of the Commissioner's delegate, refusing Mr Welsh's application for an extension of time in which to make an election under section 119U of the Act.
