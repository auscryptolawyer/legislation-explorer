Re Geoffrey Walter Edelsten and Max Christopher Donnelly As Trustee of the Bankrupt Estate of Geoffrey Walter Edelsten v Geoffrey Walter Edelsten; Health Insurance and Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1988] FCA 395 (10 November 1988)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: GEOFFREY WALTER EDELSTEN and MAX CHRISTOPHER DONNELLY as Trustee of the

Bankrupt Estate of Geoffrey Walter Edelsten

And: GEOFFREY WALTER EDELSTEN; HEALTH INSURANCE and DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF

TAXATION

No. P1551 of 1987

Bankruptcy

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

BANKRUPTCY DISTRICT OF THE STATE OF NEW SOUTH WALES

AND THE AUSTRALIAN CAPITAL TERRITORY

GENERAL DIVISION

Burchett J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Bankruptcy - income tax - whether moneys paid under notices pursuant to s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act during the period of relation back are recoverable by a trustee in bankruptcy - Principles relating to relation back - The effect of execution and attachment upon relation back - Whether s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act effects a statutory assignment of a debt, or effects a charge similar to that under a garnishee order - Consideration of the meaning and application of s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act - Whether s.131 of the Bankruptcy Act can affect amounts of income received during the period of relation back and before a sequestration order - Construction of s.131 - Observations as to whether s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act requires an identifiable sum to be due or accruing or payable upon a contingency - Whether future debts could be effectively assigned to the Commissioner of Taxation by the operation of s.218 when the debts only came into existence during the relation back period - Whether moneys paid pursuant to s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act were received by the Commissioner "as a result of the attachment by him ... of a debt due to the debtor" within the meaning of s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act - Meaning of "attachment" - Observations about attachment in the history of the common law and the development of the garnishee procedure - Observations about the policy considerations embodied in s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act in connection with the principle of relation back.

Bankruptcy Act 1966, ss. 115, 116, 118, 131.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s.218.

HEARING

SYDNEY

10:11:1988

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr S.G. Finch

Solicitors for the Applicant: Isenberg, Spedding & Player

For the First Respondent: Dr. G.W. Edelsten

Solicitors for the Second Respondent: Minter Ellison

Counsel for the Third Respondent: Mr J.R. Sackar, QC with Mr D.B. McGovern

Solicitor for the Third Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The applicant be directed to bring in short minutes of appropriate declarations to reflect the reasons of the court.

The matter be listed for further hearing on a date to be fixed.

NOTE: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in rule 124

of the Bankruptcy Rules.

DECISION

In Edelsten's Trustee v. Commissioner of Taxation (1987) 16 FCR 386, I discussed the consequences of two significant acts in the law which affected the parties to this case. Both of those acts have since been set aside. However, the rulings then made continue to apply, mutatis mutandis, in the new situation which now exists. They led to a decision that certain notices issued by a Deputy Commissioner of Taxation under s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, requiring the Health Insurance Commission to pay to the Commissioner 100 cents in every dollar of each payment due by it to Dr. Edelsten until a large amount of tax should be satisfied, could not continue to apply to health insurance payments which became payable to the bankrupt after his bankruptcy. The two acts in the law which occasioned that decision were the issue of the s.218 notices on 7 July 1987, and the presentation of Dr. Edelsten's debtor's petition, by virtue of which he became bankrupt on 21 September 1987.

2. The decision to issue the s.218 notices, the subject of Edelsten's Trustee v. Commissioner of Taxation, was set aside in Edelsten v. Wilcox 88 ATC 4484, but the setting aside of the decision to issue those notices had the effect of reinstating four earlier notices issued on 23 December 1986 pursuant, respectively, to paragraphs (a), (b), (c) and (d) of s.218(1). The notices which were reinstated required payment of an amount of 45 cents in every dollar of each payment to be made by the Health Insurance Commission to Dr. Edelsten, until the tax should be satisfied. Not only were the notices of 7 July 1987 set aside; the bankruptcy brought about by the presentation of Dr. Edelsten's debtor's petition was annulled: Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v. Edelsten (Burchett J., unreported, 10 March 1988); and a sequestration order was made upon a creditor's petition which had been presented on 29 July 1987: Re Edelsten; Ex parte Full Point Pty Limited (Burchett J., unreported, 10 March 1988). The period of relation back, applicable in the circumstances, produces the result that, as a consequence of the sequestration order of 10 March 1988, Dr. Edelsten's bankruptcy commenced on 22 May 1987.

3. The application, which has now been brought before me, has been made by the trustee of Dr. Edelsten's bankrupt estate seeking declarations and orders in respect of moneys paid or payable, pursuant to the subsisting s.218 notices, during the period between the commencement of the bankruptcy, by virtue of the relation back period, and the making of the sequestration order; further, or alternatively, declarations and orders in respect of moneys received by the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, pursuant to the notices, on or after 8 January 1987, claimed to be recoverable by the trustee pursuant to s.118 or s.122 of the Bankruptcy Act 1966; and further, declarations and orders pursuant to s.131 of the Bankruptcy Act, and otherwise, so as to make available to the trustee moneys payable by the Health Insurance Commission in respect of medical fees earned by Dr. Edelsten. As questions surround Dr. Edelsten's current and future ability to earn as a medical practitioner, all parties agreed that the issues arising under s.131 should be deferred until after the determination of the matters debated in respect of the relation back period and ss.118 and 122.

4. The primary submission put on behalf of the trustee was that the doctrine of relation back, as reflected in ss.115 and 116 of the Bankruptcy Act, required a decision in his favour in respect of all moneys payable by the Health Insurance Commission to Dr. Edelsten from 22 May 1987, when the relevant act of bankruptcy by failure to pay under a bankruptcy notice became complete. The broad proposition on which this submission was based is that stated by Lord Esher M.R. (with whom Lindley L.J. and A.L. Smith L.J. agreed) in In Re Pollitt; Ex parte Minor (1893) 1 QB 455 at 457-458, where he said:

"The result of the relation back is, that all

subsequent dealings with the debtor's

property must be treated as if the bankruptcy

had taken place at the moment when the act of

bankruptcy was committed. ... Then, he

being a bankrupt, all the money which he then

had, and all the money which was owing to

him, passed to the trustee in the bankruptcy

for the purpose of being distributed by him

amongst the bankrupt's creditors. ... (H)e

must be treated as if he had then already

become a bankrupt."

See also In Re Carl Hirth; Ex parte The Trustee (1899) 1 QB 612 at 623, where Lindley M.R. referred to relation back as a "doctrine ... peculiar to bankruptcy" which enabled the trustee to override transactions and dealings, following an act of bankruptcy, in order to recover the property of the bankrupt.

5. Concerning the comparable position with regard to the winding up of a company, Bowen L.J. said in Sovereign Life Assurance Company v. Dodd (1892) 2 QB 573 at 581:

"What was the defendant's position on that

day? The company was being wound up, for the

winding-up order, though not then made, dated

back when made to the presentation of the

petition ... ."

And in In Re Bostels Ltd (1968) Ch 346 at 350 Pennycuick J. said:

"It will be remembered that upon the making of

a winding-up order the commencement of the

winding-up dates back to the presentation of

the petition, so that the payment made during

the pendency of the proceedings represents a

disposition of property made after such

commencement."

(He was referring to a payment of debt and costs to a petitioner who did not proceed, where a substituted petitioner subsequently obtained an order.)

6. The answer proffered to the trustee's contentions was that the requirement of the s.218 notice, "to pay to the Commissioner, either forthwith upon the money becoming due or being held, or at or within a time specified in the notice (not being a time before the money becomes due or is held)," effected in favour of the Commissioner of Taxation what Brennan J., in Clyne v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 40; (1981) 150 CLR 1 at 26, described as "an assignment of the moneys to be paid to the Commissioner as though the taxpayer had charged the moneys otherwise payable to him with payment of his tax liability."

7. Whether s.218 confers on the Commissioner a true proprietary interest in a debt owing to a taxpayer is controversial. Mason and Wilson JJ. in F.J. Bloemen Proprietary Limited v. The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1981] HCA 27; (1981) 147 CLR 360 at 375 referred to "the garnishee power in s.218," and in Clyne's case itself Mason J. (with whom Aickin and Wilson JJ. agreed) said (at 19):

"I regard the effect of the notice as similar

to that of a garnishee order."

Mason J. (at 17-18) left open the question whether the Commissioner obtained a charge over or interest in the moneys. In holding that the taxpayer could not, after service of a s.218 notice on his debtor, the debt being owing but payable in the future, assign the debt so as to set the assignee free of the Commissioner's claim and entitle the debtor to make payment otherwise than to the Commissioner, Mason J. emphasised (at 20 et. seq.) that an assignment would be subject to equities, and the assignee would simply stand in the shoes of the assignor. He added:

"The word 'equities' is not used in its

technical sense as meaning an equitable

interest or something in the nature of an

equitable interest. It is a general

expression calculated to comprehend defences

which would have been available to the debtor

in an action brought against him by the

assignor as well as set-off and

counter-claims."

On this approach, it was clearly unnecessary to decide the question which Mason J. had left open. Nor, it seems to me, did Gibbs C.J. purport to decide this question. Both Gibbs C.J. and Mason J. point out (at 12 and 23) that the statutory protection, offered to the taxpayer's debtor paying under the notice, is the deemed authority of the taxpayer and all other persons concerned. The adoption by the draftsman of this expedient perhaps suggests that he did not contemplate a statutory assignment which would render any such authority superfluous. Varying views have been expressed concerning this question: see Tricontinental Corporation Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1987) 73 ALR 433 at 436; Huston v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (Commonwealth) [1983] FCA 200; (1983) 49 ALR 566 at 574; Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Steele 87 ATC 5050; Kerrison v. Acting Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 88 ATC 4476; Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Lai Corporation Pty Ltd 86 ATC 4085; Edelsten's Trustee v. Commissioner of Taxation (1987) 16 FCR 386 at 390-391; Kirk v. Commissioner of Australian Federal Police (1988) 81 ALR 321 at 331.

8. But it does not seem to me that the present problem can be solved by characterising the effect of s.218 as the creation of a charge in favour of the Commissioner. Although a garnishee order does not assign the debt to the judgment creditor (see Re Bismarck Australia Pty Ltd (Receivers and Managers Appointed); Sicree and Watt v. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1981] VicRp 51; (1981) VR 527; Lai Corporation case (supra) at 4093-4094), it has repeatedly been held that a garnishee order, even an order nisi, creates a charge or security in favour of the judgment creditor recognisable as such in the law of bankruptcy: In Re Caribbean Products (Yam Importers) Ltd. Tickler v. Swains Packaging Ltd (1966) Ch 331 at 345 (Harman L.J.), 350 (Russell L.J.), 353-354 (Salmon L.J.); Joachimson v. Swiss Bank Corporation (1921) 3 KB 110 at 131 (Atkin L.J.) - a passage adopted by Lord Denning M.R. in Choice Investments Ltd v. Jeromnimon; Midland Bank Ltd, Garnishee (1981) QB 149 at 155; Hall v. Richards [1961] HCA 34; (1961) 108 CLR 84 at 91-92; Galbraith v. Grimshaw (1910) 1 KB 339 at 343-4; and on appeal, (1910) AC 508 at 512.

9. The question is not whether a security was obtained; it is rather, if a security was obtained, when was it obtained? The making of the sequestration order demonstrated that, during the period of relation back, the apparent property of the bankrupt, over which security could purportedly arise, was in the eye of the law not his but his trustee's. Unless the security predated that period, or was the subject of a protective provision of the Bankruptcy Act, it would be liable to be swept aside by the retrospective tide of the sequestration. The effect, or rather the non-effect, of a security arising during the period of relation back was explained by Kitto J., with particular reference to the consequences of the issue of a writ of execution, in McQuarrie v. Jaques [1954] HCA 76; (1954) 92 CLR 262 at 306 et seq. He pointed out that the writ of fi. fa. bound the debtor's goods upon its being delivered to the sheriff for execution, and this binding effect produced a "security" within the meaning of the bankruptcy legislation, which qualified the denial to creditors of a remedy against the property of the bankrupt by providing that this should not affect the power of a secured creditor to realise his security. Kitto J. went on:

"Accordingly, in the case where the debtor's

goods were bound in an execution before the

act of bankruptcy to which the trustee's

title related back, the situation before

special provision to the contrary was made

was the same as that which existed where any

other form of valid security over the goods

had been created: the trustee took his title

subject to the security, and the bankruptcy

of the debtor therefore did not affect the

creditor's right to proceed with his

execution and thereby recover his debt:

Edwards v. Scarsbrook (1862) 3 B & S at 285

(122 ER at 108). On the other hand, where

the debtor's goods became bound after the act

of bankruptcy, the necessary result of the

doctrine of relation back was that the title

of the trustee prevailed over the execution

creditor's security, so that if the sheriff

sold the goods seized he sold goods which

turned out to be the unencumbered property of

the trustee, and the trustee could therefore

recover the amount of the proceeds, as for

money had and received to his use, from the

creditor who received them ... ."

10. As Dixon C.J. pointed out in the same case at 270-271, the situation to which Kitto J. pointed was established as far back as the time of Lord Mansfield, who held in Cooper v. Chitty [1819] EngR 27; (1756) 1 Keny 395 (96 ER 1033); 1 Burr 20 (97 ER 166) that the sheriff, as well as the execution creditor, was liable in trover to the assignees in bankruptcy where he sold under a writ of execution that did not bind the goods of the debtor prior to the act of bankruptcy to which the sequestration related back. The decision was for a time controversial, but once its doctrine became established, the legislature intervened to make the first of a succession of attempts to regulate the operation of bankruptcy in respect of executions levied prior to the making of a sequestration order. Since the availability of garnishee orders, in addition to writs of execution, for the enforcement of judgments, those provisions have been extended to attachments. At the time of the decision in McQuarrie v. Jaques, in Australia, as in England, the position was governed by a provision (here, s.92(1) of the Bankruptcy Act 1924) in the following terms:

"Where a creditor has issued execution against

the goods or lands of a debtor, or has

attached any debt due to him, he shall not be

entitled to retain the benefit of the

execution or attachment against the trustee

in bankruptcy unless he has completed the

execution or attachment before sequestration

and before notice of the presentation of any

petition by or against the debtor or before

notice of the commission of any available act

of bankruptcy by the debtor."

By sub-s.(2), it was provided that an execution against goods was completed by seizure and sale, and that an attachment of a debt was completed by receipt of the debt. "Attachment" was not defined, and there was nothing in the provision to restrict its meaning.

11. As is apparent from McQuarrie v. Jaques itself, from In Re Caribbean Products (supra), and from the discussion of both these cases, together with other cases, in a learned article by Dennis J. Rose in 43 ALJ 40, sections in the form of s.92 were far from demonstrably satisfactory. An attempt was made, on the basis of the report of the Clyne Committee, to clarify and reform the position in s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act 1966, which avoids the ambiguous expression "the benefit of the execution or attachment", and questions of notice, substituting a precise time period of six months before the presentation of a petition. Sub-s.(1) of the new section reads:

"Subject to sub-section (2), where -

(a) a creditor has, within 6 months before

the presentation of a petition, or after

the presentation of a petition, against

a debtor -

(i) received moneys as a result of

execution having been issued by

him, or on his behalf, against

property of the debtor, being

moneys that are the proceeds of

the sale of property of the debtor

that has been sold in pursuance of

the process or that were seized,

or paid to avoid seizure or sale

of property of the debtor, in

pursuance of the process; or

(ii) received moneys as a result of the

attachment by him, or on his

behalf, of a debt due to the

debtor; and

(b) the debtor subsequently becomes a

bankrupt on, or by virtue of the

presentation of, the petition,

the creditor shall pay to the trustee of the

estate of the bankrupt the amount by which

the amount of those moneys exceeds the taxed

costs of the execution or attachment, as the

case may be."

Other subsections deal with various situations which are not relevant to the present case, including the effect of a charging order. Sub-s.(4) should be noted. It reads as follows:

"Where -

(a) a creditor has, in pursuance of

sub-section (1), paid to the trustee of

the estate of a bankrupt the proceeds of

the sale of property or other moneys

that were received as a result of

execution having been issued by him, or

on his behalf, against property of the

bankrupt or of the attachment by him, or

on his behalf, of a debt due to the

bankrupt; and

(b) that property or debt would not have

been property divisible amongst the

creditors of the bankrupt if the

bankrupt had become a bankrupt

immediately before the execution was

issued or the debt was attached, as the

case may be,

the trustee shall pay those proceeds or other

moneys to the bankrupt or to a person

authorized by the bankrupt in writing for the

purpose."

12. A crucial question in the present case is whether the operation of a notice under s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act produces an "attachment" within the meaning of s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act. However, the first thing to be observed is that if s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act does not apply, because the taking of the debt is not an attachment within its meaning, there may be nothing to prevent the operation of the doctrine of relation back in its full force, according to the law laid down by Lord Mansfield well over two centuries ago. Precisely because that law would otherwise generally apply, s.118 and its progenitors have been inserted in bankruptcy legislation over most of the period since. It was sufficient, for the purposes of my previous decision in Edelsten's Trustee v. Commissioner of Taxation (1987) 16 FCR 386, to hold that s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act could not operate once the bankruptcy of the bankrupt denied the tax debt the character of an "amount due by the taxpayer" within the meaning of s.218, and converted it into a right of proof in the bankruptcy. But the operation of s.218 also depends upon the existence of another debt or obligation, that due to the taxpayer. Relevantly, s.218 empowers the Commissioner to "require ... any person by whom any money is due or accruing or may become due to a taxpayer ... to pay to the Commissioner, either forthwith upon the money becoming due ... or at or within a time specified in the notice (not being a time before the money becomes due ...) ... such amount as is specified in the notice out of each payment that the person so notified becomes liable from time to time to make to the taxpayer until the amount due by the taxpayer in respect of tax is satisfied ... ." If, at the time the notice would take effect, no money is due to the taxpayer, and the person notified has not become liable to make a payment to the taxpayer, because it can now be seen, by virtue of the doctrine of relation back, that the taxpayer was a bankrupt whose bankruptcy had already commenced, how can s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act operate? It is no answer simply to say, as counsel for the Commissioner contended in the present case, that s.218 confers a security upon the Commissioner. The section can only do that if there is a debt owed to the taxpayer upon which it can have effect. But, subject to a point about to be considered, the property in the debt, as Kitto J. demonstrated in his judgment in McQuarrie v. Jaques, was vested in the trustee in bankruptcy as from the commencement of the relation back period, unless it was already subject to the Commissioner's security prior to that period commencing.

13. The matter I set aside for further examination, when making the foregoing statement, is the effect of s.131 of the Bankruptcy Act. Sub-ss.(1) and (2) of that section provide as follows:

"(1) Subject to this section, a bankrupt who

is in receipt of income is entitled to retain

it for his own benefit.

(2) The Court may, upon the application of

the trustee, order that all, or such part as

the Court thinks fit, of the income of the

bankrupt shall be paid to the trustee for the

benefit of the bankrupt's creditors."

Under the circumstances of the present case, I have already held, in Edelsten's Trustee v. Commissioner of Taxation, that the amounts due from the Health Insurance Commission constitute income within the meaning of s.131. Does it follow that debts of this kind, which arose after the commencement of the bankruptcy, were not property of the trustee but of Dr. Edelsten personally? In that case, they would be untouched by the doctrine of relation back. It is clear that, after the making of a sequestration order, moneys due to the bankrupt, which constituted income, could be retained by him for his own benefit only "subject to this section", that is to say, subject to the power of the court to order their payment to the trustee for the benefit of the creditors. But there is a question whether s.131 has any operation prior to the date of the sequestration order. Certainly, the terms of the section appear to have been drafted with an eye to the administration of the estate of a bankrupt, which commences with the sequestration order. And the terms of sub-s.(4) of s.118, set out above, suggest an understanding that a sum, which would not have been divisible among the creditors had the bankrupt's estate been sequestrated before an attachment, might yet be caught by the doctrine of relation back, and therefore was appropriate to be brought within s.118, which both gives effect to and modifies that doctrine. An obvious example of such a sum, having regard to the terms of s.131, may be a payment of income of the bankrupt.

14. As I noted in Edelsten's Trustee v. Commissioner of Taxation (supra, at 393), there is authority that the purpose of the enactment of s.131 was to give effect to a view preferred by Fullagar J. in his judgment in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Official Receiver (1956) 95 CLR 300 at 319-320. That judgment was concerned with problems arising out of earnings by an undischarged bankrupt after the making of a sequestration order. The earnings were claimed by the Official Receiver as after acquired property of the bankrupt. Fullagar J. (with whose judgment, as well as with the judgment of Williams J., Dixon C.J. agreed) pointed out, at 318, that a qualification had been placed on the provision by which the property of the bankrupt, divisible among his creditors, included "all property which belongs to or is vested in the bankrupt at the commencement of the bankruptcy, or is acquired by or devolves on him before his discharge" (s.91 of the Bankruptcy Act 1924). That qualification had not been found in the specific section dealing with personal earnings after sequestration and before discharge, but had been placed on more general grounds. Fullagar J. referred to the statement by Lindley M.R., when delivering the judgment of the Court of Appeal, in In Re Roberts (1900) 1 QB 122 at 128. Lindley M.R. said:

"The Bankruptcy Act of 1883, like its

predecessors, excepts a bankrupt's tools and

contemplates the acquisition of future

property by a bankrupt, and he must live to

use his tools and acquire such property. The

present Act, like previous Bankruptcy Acts,

must be construed so as to enable him to do

so; and the language of (the section

equivalent to s.91 of the Bankruptcy Act

1924), clear and express as it is, must not,

therefore, be taken so literally as to

deprive the bankrupt of those fruits of his

personal exertions which are necessary to

enable him to live."

15. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Official Receiver, at 313, Williams J. said:

"The personal earnings of a bankrupt,

including his earnings by way of salary or

wages, are, on the literal reading of s.91

(i), after-acquired property of the bankrupt.

As such they would vest in the official

receiver, as and when the bankrupt received

them, and, subject to s.98, would be part of

the property of the bankrupt divisible

amongst his creditors. But it has invariably

been held that the vesting provisions of

Bankruptcy Acts must not be read literally so

as to vest the whole of the personal earnings

of the bankrupt in his official assignee

because to do so would mean that the assignee

might, in the words of Lord Mansfield in

Chippendale v. Tomlinson (1785) 1 Co Bank L

428 at 432:

'let the insolvent out to hire,

and contract himself for his

personal labour' ... ."

16. If the qualification, as Fullagar J. called it, upon the vesting in the trustee in bankruptcy of the bankrupt's after acquired property rested upon the general grounds asserted in the passages I have quoted, it clearly had nothing to do with the bankrupt's entitlement, prior to the making of a sequestration order, but during the relation back period, to the receipt of amounts representing his income. The passages deal with the bankrupt's position after a sequestration order has been made by the court. Accordingly, the attribution of s.131 to the Clyne Committee's acceptance of the views of Fullagar J. in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Official Receiver carries with it the conclusion that the section is concerned only with income of which a bankrupt is in receipt following the making of a sequestration order. Of course, this may include income previously earned, but not received. Income of which he was in receipt prior to the making of the order, but during the period of relation back, represents property to which, upon the making of a sequestration order, the trustee in bankruptcy is seen to have been entitled.

17. It follows from the foregoing discussion that, without regard to s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act, the trustee is entitled to succeed in respect of money which became due by the Health Insurance Commission to Dr. Edelsten during the period of relation back, unless s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act created a prior charge or overrode the trustee's rights. In the Lai Corporation case (supra) at 4100 Brinsden J. said of s.38 of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 1) 1930, which corresponds to s.218:

"It would, I think, be taking a big step to

conclude Parliament intended the Commissioner

should be paid his tax out of a debt which in

equity had ceased to belong to the taxpayer

at the time of receipt of a sec. 38 notice.

There is no compelling reason to be derived

from the Act why this should be so."

In that case, Brinsden J. was concerned with book debts, which were the subject of a floating charge, and the question was whether the crystallization of that charge, prior to receipt of a notice under s.38, prevented the notice from operating. He held that it did.

18. The notices served on Dr. Edelsten were served at a time earlier than the act of bankruptcy which marks the commencement of the bankruptcy. Subject to s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act, there is no argument about the effectiveness of the notices in respect of money which became due before the commencement of the bankruptcy. The question is whether, in so far as s.218(1)(a) authorizes the Commissioner to require "any person by whom any money is ... accruing or may become due to a taxpayer ... to pay to the Commissioner ... upon the money becoming due ... such amount as is specified in the notice out of each payment that the person so notified becomes liable from time to time to make to the taxpayer," the obligation imposed by the notices can affect amounts which the officers of the Health Insurance Commission only assessed and authorized to be paid after the commencement of the bankruptcy. The argument centred upon the trustee's proposition that such amounts first became due under the Health Insurance Act at a time when the only person to whom they could become due was the trustee, and not Dr. Edelsten. The Comissioner's reply was that, at the date of the notices, they were moneys which "may become due to a taxpayer", and were therefore included in the charge which he contended the section created.

19. It seems to me there may perhaps be room for debate whether the expression "any person by whom any money is due or accruing or may become due to a taxpayer" is satisfied by a person who, at a later date, becomes indebted to the taxpayer in respect of some particular amount which was not even in prospect at the date of the service of the notice. At the date of the service, nothing was "due or accruing". The words "any money ... may become due" are apt, in the context, to refer to an identifiable sum payable upon a contingency. The subsection authorizes the Commissioner to serve notices "at any time, or from time to time," which would seem unnecessary if a notice would pick up obligations not yet in prospect. It is to be noted, too, that the subsection goes on to use the expression "upon the money becoming due", which appears to refer to a specific sum or to specific sums. However, no argument was put that the notices could not extend, apart from the bankruptcy, for as long as Dr. Edelsten continued to earn fees payable, by virtue of assignments under the Health Insurance Act, by the Health Insurance Commission, and I have reached no conclusion on this question. Had the point been argued, it would have been necessary to take full account of what was said in Huston's case (supra) at 4531. The argument which was put was simply that the fees in question, becoming due after the commencement of the bankruptcy, became due to the trustee, and as they did not become due to Dr. Edelsten, they could not be affected by the notices.

20. In Performing Right Society v. London Theatre of Varieties (1922) 2 KB 433 at 454, Younger L.J. said:

"Neither at law nor in equity can there be an

assignment of that which has no existence."

When it is said that equity will enable parties to assign future property for valuable consideration, what is meant is that the transaction may be effective as an equitable assignment upon the acquisition of the property in the future. As Dixon J. explained in Palette Shoes Pty Ltd v. Krohn (1937) 58 CLR 1 at 27:

"Because value has been given on the one side,

the conscience of the other party is bound

when the subject comes into existence, that

is, when, as is generally the case, the legal

property vests in him. Because his

conscience is bound in respect of a subject

of property, equity fastens upon the property

itself and makes him a trustee of the legal

rights or ownership for the assignee."

(See the cases collected in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Booth (1986) 68 ALR 547 at 566-570.) In Booth v. The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1987] HCA 61; (1987) 164 CLR 159 at 165 Mason C.J. said:

"Although rights other than existing

proprietary rights are not capable of

immediate transfer, they may be dealt with by

contract. If they are dealt with by

contract, the contract may eventually operate

in equity to effect a transfer of them, but

only in so far as they consist of future

proprietary rights and as and when those

rights come into existence. When the

property comes into existence the assignor eo

instanti becomes trustee of it for the

assignee, the prospective right in property

which the assignee obtains being a higher

right than the right to have specific

performance of a contract, as Dixon J. noted

in Palette Shoes Pty Ltd v. Krohn (1937) 58

CLR 1 at p 27. A purported present

transfer or assignment of future property,

including a future chose in action, is

construed in equity as a contract to transfer

or assign the property when it is acquired."

(See also the citation by Toohey and Gaudron JJ. at 177 from Sir Frederick Jordan's Chapters on Equity in New South Wales.)

21. In the light of these statements of the law, even on the basis that s.218 would work an assignment of the relevant debts due to Dr. Edelsten, though prior to the commencement of the bankruptcy those debts were mere expectancies, the assignment could not be effective until the rights to the choses in action came into existence. When they came into existence, by virtue of the doctrine of relation back, they did so as rights which belonged to the trustee in bankruptcy, and not to Dr. Edelsten. The terms of s.218 are not apt to comprehend debts which are not due to the taxpayer. At the earliest moment when the obligation imposed by the notice under s.218 gained the possibility of fastening upon a sum of money, that is, "upon the money becoming due," the money did not become due to the taxpayer, but to the trustee. I should add that I have not overlooked what Mason J. said in Clyne's case (150 CLR at 23) to the effect that moneys owing to the taxpayer when a notice is given are not by the section explicitly required to continue to be owing to him when they become payable. Mason J. did not say that the moneys need never be owing to the taxpayer.

22. The next question to consider is the possible application of s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act. If it applies, it enables the trustee to extend his reach to moneys paid under the notices beyond the period of relation back, and for a full six months prior to the presentation of the petition upon which Dr. Edelsten was made bankrupt. What must be decided is whether, within the meaning of s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act, the moneys paid to the Commissioner of Taxation were received by him "as a result of the attachment by him, or on his behalf, of a debt due to the debtor."

23. It would be a confusion of thought to regard the respondent's contention that s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act creates a statutory charge over the debts referred to in the notice as in itself an answer to the application of s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act. If the Commissioner was a creditor who received moneys as a result of the attachment by him of a debt due to his debtor, the fact that the process involved the enforcement of a security would not necessarily differentiate it from other proceedings by way of attachment. Concerning a provision of the English bankruptcy legislation corresponding to s.118, in the Caribbean Products case Russell L.J., who in common with the other members of the Court of Appeal thought an attachment conferred security on the creditor, held that, upon the section applying, the attachment "is thereafter to be of no further avail as conferring security on the creditor as against a liquidator in the winding up. ... It is then that the charge created by the attachment acquires its characteristic of vulnerability." Salmon L.J. thought the creditor had lost his right to enforce his security. He said at 354:

"If he improperly obtains payment by

wrongfully enforcing the security, this is a

wrong against the liquidator."

24. So the question is simply whether what s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act effects is an attachment within the meaning of s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act. Counsel for the Commissioner argued that the reference to "the taxed costs of the execution or attachment" indicated s.118 was concerned only with attachments by garnishee proceedings under court orders. However, the history of the section is not helpful to that argument. As I have said, s.118 embodied the reforms suggested by the Clyne Committee in respect of the previous provision regulating executions and attachments, s.92 of the Bankruptcy Act 1924. The report of the committee, in paragraphs 167 - 169, is concerned with the problems which engrossed the High Court in McQuarrie v. Jaques, but gives no hint of any intention to narrow the scope of the word "attachment" as used in the provision. In s.92, there was no reference to the taxed costs of an attachment.

25. After the passing of the Bankruptcy Act 1966, the provision made by s.118 was further amended by Act No. 12 of 1980. The explanatory memorandum circulated by the Minister representing the Minister responsible for the Bill which became the Act, the Honourable P.D. Durack, confirms that a wide meaning of the word "attachment" was intended. At p 71, a comment is made on the provision, which became sub-s.(6), suspending an attachment until the court has dealt with a petition. The Minister's comment was:

"This will ensure that the income of the

bankrupt is retained by him except where the

Court makes an order under s.131."

Unless s.118 refers to all methods of attachment, this statement would of course be incorrect.

26. There is no authority in which the meaning of the word "attachment" in s.118 has been decided. But in Kerrison v. Acting Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 88 ATC 4476 at 4483, Fisher J. dealt with an argument which depended on the proposition that a notice under s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act did have the effect of an attachment within the meaning of s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act. Fisher J. rejected the particular argument, which is irrelevant to the present case, but expressed no disinclination to accept the proposition on which it was premised.

27. Consideration has been given in England to the meaning of s.40(1) of the Bankruptcy Act 1914, which applies in a case "where a creditor has issued execution against the goods or lands of a debtor, or has attached any debt due to him". In In Re Lupkovics; Ex parte The Trustee v. Freville (1954) 1 WLR 1234, Upjohn J. (as Lord Upjohn then was) had to decide the effect of a proceeding taken under s.27(1) of the Crown Proceedings Act 1947, which makes special provision for an order directing payment of money payable by the Crown in a case where, if the money had been payable by a subject, an attachment could have been obtained. At 1241, Upjohn J. said:

"Apparently, before 1947, there was no means

of attaching a debt due from the Crown: see

Robertson's Civil Procedure by and against

the Crown, at p 611. In my judgment,

however, proceedings under section 27(1) are

proceedings by way of attachment for the

purposes of section 40. Attachment is

nowhere defined in the Bankruptcy Act, 1914,

but it is not, in my judgment, a term of art

confined to attachments arising under Order

45. It is equally appropriate to include

proceedings under section 27(1) of the Crown

Proceedings Act, 1947 - a section clearly

intended to put the judgment creditor in the

same position vis-a-vis the Crown as any

other debtor of the bankrupt, i.e., to

prevent the judgment debtor from obtaining

payment of the debt and to secure payment to

himself, the difference in the form of order

obtained being due to the privileges and

immunities of the Crown."

28. There is no reason to think that s.118 uses the word "attachment" in any sense different from that in which it was used in the earlier legislation in England and Australia. If it is not used as a term of art confined to attachments under garnishee orders, there is no reason why it should not be given its widest meaning as an English word with a long history in the law. In the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary (1980), the meaning of the word "attach", in a legal sense, is given as:

"To place or take under the control of a

court; to arrest or seize by authority of a

writ of attachment: a. a person; b.

property, goods."

As an illustration of the usage of the word in this sense, the dictionary refers to Shakespeare's King Henry the Eighth (Act I, Sc. I, l. 95):

"For France hath flaw'd the league, and hath

attach'd

Our merchants' goods at Bordeaux."

Clearly, Shakespeare was not using the word in a sense limited to the procedures of a common law court - still less, to those of a modern garnishee order. In the Macquarie Dictionary (revised edition 1985), the legal meaning of "attach" is given as "to arrest (a person) or distrain (property) in payment of a debt by legal authority." This definition does not confine the requisite legal authority to a court order.

29. The explanation given by Lord Denning M.R. in Choice Investments Ltd v. Jeromnimon (supra), at 154-155, of what is meant by attaching a debt, under the rules providing for the making of garnishee orders, refers to the procedure as a mode of execution introduced by s.61 of the Common Law Procedure Act 1854. However, the explanation demonstrates that neither the word "attach", nor the word "garnish", was new to the law in 1854. Each goes back to Norman-French origins. At p 155 Lord Denning says:

"As soon as the garnishee order nisi is served

on the bank, it operates as an injunction.

It prevents the bank from paying the money to

its customer until the garnishee order is

made absolute, or is discharged, as the case

may be. It 'binds the debt in the hands of

the garnishee - that is, creates a charge in

favour of the judgment creditor' ... . The

money at the bank is then said to be

'attached' - again derived from

Norman-French. But the 'attachment' is not

an order to pay. It only freezes the sum in

the hands of the bank until the order is made

absolute or is discharged. It is only when

the order is made absolute that the bank is

liable to pay."

30. In this passage, it is made clear that the order is not the attachment. The order is a garnishee order. (Earlier, at p 154, Lord Denning had explained that the word "garnishee" is derived from the Norman-French, and "denotes one who is required to 'garnish,' that is, to furnish a creditor with the money to pay off a debt.") The word "attachment", in this usage, refers not to the order to pay a creditor (in modern practice, though not necessarily in the old law, as is illustrated by The Mayor and Aldermen of the City of London v. Cox (1867) 2 HL 239, a judgment creditor), but to the seizure of the debt, or rather the freezing of it, so as to make it available to be utilized to meet the obligation for the enforcement of which the garnishee procedure was invoked. His Lordship's usage is exactly in accordance with the meaning which the word evolved during the course of the common law. In Les Termes De La Ley (1708 edition), the word "attach" is defined as follows:

"Attach is a Taking or Apprehending by Command

or Writ."

After distinguishing attachment from arrest, capias, and distress, the entry proceeds to discuss certain specific forms of attachment as follows:

"There is also an Attachment of Privilege: and

this is twofold; either giving power to

apprehend a Man in a place privileged, or by

virtue of an Office of Privilege; as to call

another to that Court to which he himself

belongs, and in respect of which he is

privileged. ...

And there is a Process called a Foreign

Attachment, which is used to attach the Goods

of Foreigners found within any Liberty or

City for a Debt due to the party himself.

And, by the Custom of some places, a Man may

attach Goods in the Hands of a Stranger: as

if A owe to B ten Pounds, and C owes A

another Sum of Money, B may attach the Goods

of A in the hands of C to satisfy himself in

part or all, as the Debt is.

Also there is Attachment of the Forest which

is a Court there held every forty days

throughout the Year: In which the Verderors

have not any authority, but to receive and

inrol the Attachment of Offenders against

Vert and Venison taken by the other Officers,

that they may be presented at the next

Justice seat in Eyre."

31. It is clear that when the concept of attachment was taken over for the purposes of the Common Law Procedure Act 1854, which instituted the modern garnishee procedure, the word "attachment" had an established meaning in a variety of contexts, referring to a taking, of a person or goods or a debt, by compulsion of legal authority, which was normally that of a court, but was certainly not confined to the ordinary courts of the land. The Forest Courts of Attachment, for example, as appears from Blackstone vol. 3 chapter 6 could attach people "by their goods", but could only enquire into alleged offences "against Vert and Venison" and certify a presentment to be dealt with in another court. Foreign attachment, which did not lie in respect of aliens but persons "not civic", permitted debts to be attached, in some cases, prior to any judicial determination of liability between a plaintiff and a defendant, in the Lord Mayor's Court of London: see Chitty's Archbold's Practice (1866) vol. 1 p 712; The Mayor and Aldermen of the City of London v. Cox (supra) at 265.

32. I do not think the association of the word "attachment" with garnishee proceedings in the established courts requires that the word, as used in s.118 of the Bankruptcy Act, should be restricted to attachments of that kind. Generally, attachments are by court order, but the legal authority by which an attachment may be given effect may take more than one form. When the word was first used in bankruptcy legislation, the parliament may well have anticipated that various means of attaching debts might be developed, and have deliberately used a wide word. At all events, when s.118 was introduced into the Bankruptcy Act 1966, s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 already existed. The procedure for which that section provided is one so closely analogous to the attachment of debts by garnishee orders, as already known in the law, that it was called, in a passage cited above from F.J. Bloemen Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (ubi supra), "the garnishee power in sec. 218", and was described by me in Edelsten v. Wilcox (supra), at 4495, as having the purpose of "the collection of tax by the attachment of debts," and as involving "the power of attachment of debts." Also, in Huston v. Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 83 ATC 4525 at 4531, Fox J. referred to the recipient of a notice under s.218 as the "garnishee". (Cf. the language of Mason J. in Clyne's case, supra, at 19, and see Kirk v. Commissioner of Australian Federal Police (1988) 81 ALR 321 at 331, per Fox J.)

33. If s.118 is seen as part of a complex of provisions designed to overcome comprehensively problems which have emerged over a long period of experience of bankruptcy laws, and in particular of the working of the relation back period, it cannot be supposed that it was any part of the policy of the provision deliberately to exclude a form of garnishee procedure. On the contrary, the policy is to supplement the provisions in respect of voluntary preferences by a provision to enable involuntary payments, whether compelled by execution, garnishment, or the obtaining of charging orders, also to be set aside, in order to ensure a fair distribution of the assets so made available among all the creditors. As Lord Dunedin said in Galbraith v. Grimshaw (1910) AC 508 at 512-513:

"I think that the general principle which

underlies every bankruptcy system is that

after bankruptcy the bankrupt is no longer

really the owner of his own property, but

holds his own property as trustee for the

whole of his creditors for equal division.

That carries with it necessarily the idea

that some of his creditors may already have

got security or may have taken part of the

property in execution. ... It is a very

natural development of that in working out a

bankruptcy system that you should introduce a

law of relation back, and that within a

certain period, which will always be an

arbitrary period determined by positive

enactment, you should hold that the security

given or the execution effected should have

no effect and that that property should be

like the rest of the property of the

bankrupt."

The case concerned an attachment of a debt, and I think Lord Dunedin was using the word "execution" as including that species of execution which consists of the attachment of debts. The scope of the principle implies that a provision intended to carry it into effect will be intended to be taken in a broad sense, consistent with the breadth of the remedy required. Section 118 is, in truth, remedial legislation, and should be construed accordingly. In any case, the rule of construction applies, to which I referred in Estee Lauder Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1988) 80 ALR 314 at 325-326:

"Once a general purpose and policy may be

discerned in a provision, that general

purpose and that policy, together with the

consistency and fairness of the operation of

the provision, should be regarded as

important guides to its true construction:

Commissioner for Railways (NSW) v. Agalianos

[1955] HCA 27; (1955) 92 CLR 390 at 397, per Dixon C.J."

34. For these reasons, I have reached the conclusion that s.118 does apply in relation to a notice issued under s.218 of the Income Tax Assessment Act. It is unnecessary to consider the possible application of s.122. I direct the applicant to bring in short minutes of appropriate declarations to reflect these reasons, and I shall list the matter for further hearing of the application under s.131, and of the other questions outstanding, on a date to be fixed.
