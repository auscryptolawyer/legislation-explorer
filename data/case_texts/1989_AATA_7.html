ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Assessable Income - realized gains on redemption of Bank-accepted Sills purchased, it a discount - on-going investments - whether assessable income for purposes of s.25(1) whether exempt from assessment: s.23J(1) whether "profit arising from the carrying on or carrying out of any profit-making undertaking or scheme".

Inccme Tax Assessment Act1936 - ss. DDJ, 25 26(a), 26AAA.

REASONS FOR DECISION

20 January 1989 Peter M. Roach Senior Member;

1. The Applicant is a company which was incorporated in the State of Queensland in 1961. I shall refer to it as MAKE-CO. In 1970 its present holding company - a United States corporation acquired an interest in MAKE-CO and, in 1976, it acquired the outstanding interest previously held by another United States corporation. At all material times MAKE-CO had a substituted accounting period in which the year of income ended 31st December was substituted for the year of income calculated to the preceding 20 June.

2. The unchallenged evidence was that all trading in the construction business in which MAKE-CO had been engaged ceased late in 1969 with the consequence for MAKE-CO that it ceased deriving trading income from that source during the year of income ended 30 June 1980. MAKE-CO having ceased operating as a construction company it was decided by its holding company that MAKE-CO should be wound up at a convenient date but no action was taken to formally commence any winding up. That was said to be "by reason of constraints imposed by foreign exchange regulations in Australia and United States income tax considerations associated with the repatriation of funds to America". As a result, the chief financial officer of the holding company instructed the Secretary and the Financial Controller of MAKE-CO - the only witness to give evidence - as to the investment of available funds with an instruction that "the principal consideration I should bear in mind was the liquidity and security of the investments of MAKE-CO". The mode of investment was left to the discretion of the witness.

3. It was decided that a large part of the cash which the witness had calculated as being surplus to MAKE-CO's current needs would be invested, but only for periods of no more than six months. The chosen form of investment was to be Bills of Exchange to be purchased at a discount. Each Bill was to be payable at a fixed sum at a specified future date being not more than six months after purchase. Such Bills had previously been purchased by the company during the period in which it had been engaged in works of construction. The Bills MAKE-CO purchased were such that the only assurance that MAKE-CO had that the investments would prove to be sound lay in the credit worthiness of the acceptors. If an acceptor defaulted, instead of being paid in full according to the tenor of the Bill - and thereby improving its financial position - MAKE-CO was at risk that it might suffer a net loss. To minimize that risk, MAKE-CO opted for lesser discounts against face value than were available to it in the market by way of bank endorsed Bills. It chose, as that lesser risk, to purchase only Bills accepted by banks. Although it knew the identity of those seeking finance through the banks, and in that way seeking the use of the funds of MAKE-CO, MAKE-CO had no claim on those end-users.

7. The unchallenged evidence, presented on behalf of the Applicant, was that at no time when decisions were being taken to acquire Bills, was any consideration given to the possibility that the Bills might be sold: and that none of the Bills were acquired with the intention that they should be resold. I am satisfied that, at the time the Bills were purchased, it was the intention of MAKE-CO to hold the Bills until maturity and that it was then expected that MAKE-CO would realize the face-value of the Bills.

8. In its return of income the company disclosed a gain of $740,521 - not the $740,438 referred to in the affidavit – which the Commissioner treated as assessable income. The adjustment sheet which accompanied the notice of assessment which issued on 28 September 1984 recorded:-

"gain on redemption of discounted securities assessed in full $740,521".

An assessor's note on the return states:-

"In view of current assessing policy propose to assess above gains s.26(a)."

What occurred for 1981 ($201,752) was not disclosed. MAKE-CO does not dispute that the gain was the higher figure of $740,521. In assessing the Applicant, the Commissioner levied additional tax for late return in the sum of $12,950: a figure later reduced to $11,000.

  1. The first question to be determined is whether, but for the provisions of S.23J of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"), the gains in question constitute assessable income. The second question is whether, by reason of s.23J, what would otherwise be assessable income is to be exempt from taxation.
  2. The Applicant carried on business. In the conduct of that business from time to time cash surpluses were generated in its hands such as to be appropriate for investment to generate increased wealth. As a fiction of the law, the company had no "private" interests to be enjoyed or indulged, or "private" responsibilities to be discharged. When it wound up its industrial activities it came to have substantial funds on hand. It no longer intended to seek to increase its wealth by that type of business activity. But it was still concerned to increase its wealth. The cash resources then available to it constituted in potential a "profit-yielding subject". Many avenues were open to the company to invest its resources with a view to yielding a profit. It might have invested by lending a specific sum and bargaining for interest pending its return. It chose not to do so. It might have invested in an asset capable of being let to the possession of others so as to generate a profit by way of rent, with the asset being held indefinitely. It did not do so. Instead, the Applicant purchased Bank-accepted Bills. The decision to so invest sometimes called for several concurrent outlays - in order to invest all available funds - and repetitive conduct - because, so long as there was nothing more in prospect for the company than a liquidation when that course became convenient, there would be a need to re-invest funds upon the maturity of each Bill. I "am satisfied that the objective sought to be pursued by the purchase of the Bank Bills was "profit". That being so, it follows that the question to be determined is whether the "profit" realized by the Applicant was to be non-assessable as merely the realization of a growth in the value of the investments: the Bank Bills.

11. In my view, in the circumstances of this case, it is not to be so viewed. The Applicant was seized of large sums of money: potentially a "profit-yielding subject". It outlaid that fund in parcels in the purchase of Bank Bills. Pending their maturity, the Bills might rise or fall in value. Upon maturity, they might prove to be redeemable at face value. As Bank-accepted Bills, the risk of the Bills not being redeemed at full value was remote. The Bills were not intended to be productive by way of interest and were not intended to produce any other "profit" than by way of realization of face value at maturity. In entering into the transactions the Applicant had no other objective than to profit, and the only profit it sought was to come home in the form of a larger sum of money payable on maturity than had been initially outlaid by the Applicant. The increase could be separated from the amount originally invested and it could then be used for any purpose which pleased the Applicant while leaving the "profit-yielding subject" - the amount originally invested - intact and available for re-investment.

  1. This is not a situation in which one has to consider whether a gain or loss on negotiation of a lump sum non-interest-bearing Bill of Exchange constitutes assessable income or is deductible (as the case may be). Nor is it to be identified with a situation in which an interest-bearing Bill is purchased at a premium or discount and held to maturity, whereon questions arise as to the correct tax treatment of the premium or discount. This is a situation in which the only "profit" sought from the investment was a profit to be had upon the maturity of the Bill.
  2. I am satisfied that, but for S.23J, the "profits" constituted assessable income. In reaching that conclusion I have carefully considered the Reasons for Decision of my colleague Senior Member Beddoe in Case V146 (88 ATC 918). In that case it was concluded that a gain realized on the maturity of such a Bill of Exchange did not constitute assessable income. I make no comments upon the merits of the decision, save to say that there is no inconsistency in the circumstance that the conclusions reached in the two cases differ. Senior Member Beddoe was dealing with a situation in which it was "abundantly clear that the Bill transaction was an isolated transaction..." (at p.922). He went on to say:-

"If the Applicant had made a practice of acquiring Bills and holding them to maturity these operations would form part of the business of the Applicant and the profit arising from each transaction would be assessable income [see Beazley & Ors. v. Commissioner I.R. (N.2.) 1984 M.Z.T.C.3. This is not the case here. The evidence is that the Applicant has only held the one Bill so that the transaction has all the attributes of an isolated transaction on capital account."

I agree with the observations in the latter passage. It is apt to describe the dealings of the Applicant. I also reiterate what I said earlier in Case V43 (88 A.T.C. 363 at p.366):-

"... It is appropriate to observe that s.23J of the Act does not provide in express terms that a 'profit' such as arises upon the maturity of an asset acquired for a sum less than its maturity value constitutes assessable income. Whether such a 'profit' is assessable depends upon other provisions of the Act than S.23J and, in particular, the concept of 'income' which finds expression in s.25 of the Act. It might well be thought that such a profit is '... not a gain accruing to capital, not a growth or increment of value in the investments; but a gain, a profit, something of exchangeable value proceeding from the property, severed from the capital...': the classic words of Pitney in the Supreme Court of the United States of America in Eisner v. Macomber [1919] USSC 119; (1919) 252 U.S. 189. If so, it may be that a profit such as this did not constitute assessable income by reason of s.25 of the Act. It may be the correct construction of s.25 of the Act only requires that such a profit be considered as assessable income if the person deriving the profit is engaged in dealing in such assets on such a scale as to cause 'the profit' to be seen as an instance of recurrent profit generated by activities regularly or frequently undertaken with a view to profit."

In my view, in the absence of s.23J, the "gains" realized constitute assessable income within the scope of s.25(1) of the Act.

14. In the circumstances of this case I have found that s.25 of the Act does constitute the "gains" as assessable income. That being so, the exemption provided by s.23J (1)

("subject to this section, no part of an amount received by a person upon the... redemption of eligible securities purchased... at a discount..., shall, for any purpose of this Act be taken to be income derived by the person")

will avail the Applicant unless, in the circumstances, s.23J(2) or (3) applies. In so holding, I have no doubt that a bank-accepted Bill of Exchange is an appropriate subject for the operation of the Section.

15. S.23J(2) is in the following terms:-

"Sub-section (1) does not apply in relation to an amount received by a person by virtue of a transaction that is part of, or is incidental to, the carrying on by the person of a business that includes buying and selling eligible securities of any kind."

I am satisfied that the profits in question in each case constituted "an amount received" and that they were received "by virtue of a transaction". But it is not any transaction at all to which s.23J(2) applies. It is only if the transaction is

"part of, or is incidental to, the carrying on by the person of a business"

and that business

"includes buying and selling eligible securities of any kind".

I consider that the company did not cease to be in business when it brought to an end its business as a construction company. What it did thereafter constituted activities fairly described as "a business", either on the basis that it was the activity of winding up its operations or more probably on the basis that, having wholly concluded its business of construction, it had now embarked upon a business of investment. In consequence, that element of the "business" test is also satisfied. But there remains a further element. Sub-section (2) will only apply if the business "includes buying and selling eligible securities of any kind" (emphasis added). In my view, despite the fact that, on rare occasions, "and" is to be construed as if it read "or" [Associated Newspapers Limited v. Wavish [1956] HCA 69; (1956) 96 CLR 526; see Pearce "Statutory Interpretation" paras. 34 and 35], it is not appropriate to so construe "and" in the context of a phrase so apt to describe the business of trading in marketable commodities or investments as the phrase "buying and selling". For that reason I conclude that s.23J(2) does not exclude the operation of s.23J(1).

16. I now turn to consider s.23J(3). It provides:-

"Sub-section (1) does not affect the operation of s.25A, S.26AAA or S.26C".

Dealing with those sections in the reverse order to that in which they appear, one can immediately set aside as of no relevance s.26C (which is limited in its operation to Commonwealth securities) or S.26AAA which operated only in relation to profits generated by "sale". Similarly, the first limb of s.25A (1) is of no application because it too is limited to "profit arising from the sale by the taxpayer of any property..."

17. It follows then that, if the Applicant is to be denied the benefit of s.23J(1), it must be by reason of the second limb of s.25A(1), which provides:-

"The assessable income of a taxpayer shall include profit arising... from the carrying on or carrying out of any profit-making undertaking or scheme".

The question of what constitutes a "profit-making undertaking or scheme" is yet to be comprehensively addressed. Dixon, J. in Premier Automatic Ticket Issuers Limited v. F.C.T. [(1933) [1933] HCA 51; 50 C.L.R. 268 at p.298] said that the phrase:-

"... appears to cover, on the one hand, the habitual pursuit of a course of conduct, and, on the other, the carrying into execution of a plan or venture which does not involve repetition or system."

In Clowes v. F.C.T. C(1954) [1954] HCA 10; 91 CLR 209], his Honour said:-

"To enter into a contract to provide a specified sum on such terms, to pay it and then to await results cannot in my opinion be properly described as 'carrying or carrying out a scheme or undertaking'. If the case is considered apart from s.26(a), then I think the taxpayer's gain should be held to be a mere enlargement of capital. In the case of each of the two contracts, a single sum was paid in the expectation or hope of the return of a single sura, an increased sum. From the taxpayer's point of view it was nothing but a casual investment of capital in hope of enlargement at the end of many years. It was not done in the course of the taxpayer's business. There is no suggestion that it formed part of any system or practice."

In the same case, Kitto, J. said that:-

"Although the planting, nurturing, harvesting, milling and sale of pine trees constituted '... undoubtedly, a profit-making scheme... the mere purchase of a unit by an investor did not"

His Honour went on to say:-

"If it be said, as the alternative form of the argument asserted, that this investment was itself a profit-making scheme, the answer must be given that paying a sum of money in consideration of a promise of a possibly larger payment upon the happening of a future event cannot be described as carrying out a scheme, according to any ordinary use of language. If it could, every bet would be a profit-making scheme, as would every contract of life assurance. The word 'scheme' is not satisfied unless there is some program, or plan of action; and clearly there was nothing of that sort which the appellant can be said to have carried out."

18. Consistently with those views, matters which have been characterized as "undertakings or schemes" have been operations involving the perfecting of title to land so as to enable development works appropriate to residential sub-divisions to be carried out with a view to such a sub-division and to be followed by sale in lots [official Receiver in Bankruptcy v. F.C. of T (Fox's Case) (1956) 96 CLR 3703; the construction of shops on surplus land and their subsequent sale [Bernard Elsey Pty. Limited [1969] HCA 46; (1969) 121 CLR 119]; and the acquisition of a controlling shareholding in an insolvent company attended by taking an assignment from the company's creditors of debts due to them so that, having restored the company to profitability, the assignees might receive payment in full of the debts [XCO Pty. Limited v. F.C.T. [1971] HCA 37; (1971) 124 CLR 343]. But never has anything so simple as the lending of a sum of money with a view to generation of interest or the outlay of a sum of money to purchase an asset to be applied to income-producing purposes or, to use the example proposed by Kitto, J., the making of a bet been characterized as a "profit-making undertaking or scheme".

19. That being so, I am of the view that it is not appropriate to characterize the action of the Applicant in purchasing the Bank-accepted Bills, and in holding them to maturity, as a "profit-making undertaking or scheme". Profit-making was certainly an objective - indeed the principal objective - of the acquisition. But, in ray view, in the relevant sense the acquisition of the profit did not involve the "carrying out of (any)... undertaking or scheme".

20. At first impression that result would seem to be surprising. But, upon analysis, it is not so. I spoke of the inter-relationship between s.25(1) and S.25A in a recent decision (TTS8/98 of 23 December 1988) and I do not propose to repeat what was there said. Suffice it to say s.26(a) - as the predecessor to s.25A - was enacted by the Parliament with a view to closing a perceived deficiency in s.25 viewed from the stand-point of the Revenue - a deficiency which proved to be in large measure imaginary. As I have indicated by the findings I have expressed earlier in these reasons, there was no "deficiency" from the viewpoint of the Revenue in the application of s.25(1) to circumstances such as these. These gains on redemption did constitute assessable income by force of s.25(1) and it was not necessary to have regard to concepts of "carrying out (any) profit-making undertaking or scheme" in order to expose the Applicant as liable to tax.

21. But the general principles which find expression in s.25(1) must stand aside in face of the exemption expressly conferred by s.23J. S.23J(1) clearly overrides s.25(1) if s.23J(1) applies. In my view, the clear objective of s.23J(1) was to provide an exemption for those who gained a profit on the redemption of Bills such as these, provided that any business in which they were engaged did not include the buying and selling of eligible securities; provided the securities were not securities of the Commonwealth, provided the profit was not realized by a sale effected within twelve months or acquisition; provided that the securities, having been sold as a profit, had not been acquired for the dominant purpose of profit-making by sale; and provided that the dealings had not been such as to constitute the "carrying on or carrying out of (a) profit-making undertaking or scheme". I note that, should I be incorrect as to the latter point, it would seem to follow that, had there been default by the parties liable on the Bills, entitlement to a deduction would have arisen for the loss of any capital outlaid on the acquisition of the Bills.

22. Accordingly, I conclude that the Applicant is entitled to succeed upon its objection. The Order of the Tribunal will therefore be that the decision of the Commissioner upon the objection under review be varied and that the taxable income of the Applicant for the year of income ended 31 December 1982 be reduced from $1,111,307 to $370,736 and that additional tax for late return shall be reduced appropriately.
