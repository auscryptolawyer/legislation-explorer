HEARING

SYDNEY

29:7:1987

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

This is an application to review a decision by the respondent to refuse the applicant access to certain documents pursuant to a request made under the Freedom of Information Act 1982 ("the Act").

2. The taxation affairs of the applicant were and, as will appear, continue to be under investigation by the respondent. The investigation was principally carried out by an officer named Wheatley. The applicant sought access to the following documents:

"All documents in existence (currently held in

investigation by Wheatley)."

3. The respondent took this to be a request for documents relating to the affairs of the applicant that were then currently held by Mr Wheatley. Originally access was refused to all documents other than income tax returns. During a review in accordance with sub-section 54(1) of the Act, material relevant to the request was identified. In the Sydney office, the following relevant documents were found in the investigation file and associated working papers:

. Tax returns and enclosures lodged by the

applicant.

. Copies of records obtained from the applicant.

. Documents received from overseas sources.

. Documents concerned with organisations or

undertakings other than the applicant.

4. In addition, a file of records relating to the applicant was kept at head office.

5. After re-examination of these documents, access was granted to a considerable number of folios from the investigation file and associated papers, to all but five documents on the tax returns file, and to all copies of records obtained from the applicant. Access was refused (inter alia) to documents received from overseas sources and to documents relating to entities other than the applicant for reasons which will be later examined.

6. Claims for exemption were made pursuant to various sections of the Act. All the documents that were the subject of these claims, with the exception of two categories, were produced to this Tribunal pursuant to section 64 of the Act. There were 1,654 pages contained in these documents. Each and every one has been examined by this Tribunal in the process of arriving at our decisions. The two categories of documents that were not made available to the Tribunal were those in respect of which application was made for exemption under section 33(1)(b) of the Act and documents consisting of information supplied by the respondent to revenue authorities in other countries, to which reference will be made under the heading of "The section 38 exemption claim".

7. It will be convenient to consider the various claims for exemption by reference to groupings under the section under which the respective claims are made, rather than by considering each document separately as was done in a schedule tendered in evidence. Before going to the various groups of documents, however, there are some preliminary matters that can be usefully dealt with at this stage.

The Conclusive Certificate

8. At the commencement of the proceedings, counsel for the respondent produced a document which he described as a conclusive certificate under section 33 of the Act. We were told by counsel for the applicant that he had been told of the existence of this certificate only a few minutes before the commencement of these proceedings. This Tribunal was quite unaware that such a certificate was in existence or was intended to be produced. No indication of the possibility of such a certificate was given at any preliminary conference, nor was it communicated in any other way to any appropriate officer of the Tribunal's registry. This practice of late production of certificates is roundly to be condemned. It is unfair to the applicant in that he is not made aware in advance of the issues and of the case for exemption that he may have to meet. It is not fair to the Tribunal and to those who administer its arrangements because of the special requirements (to be referred to later) which such a certificate puts upon the way in which a Tribunal considering matters under that certificate may be constituted. It is true that there is nothing in the current Practice Directions requiring advance notice of any proposed conclusive certificate. However, this does not, in our view, excuse the respondent from his obligation to assist in the orderly administration of applications such as this.

9. The certificate bears the date 5 May 1987. The hearing of these proceedings commenced on 13 May 1987. Notwithstanding the late receipt by the applicant of a copy of the certificate, its counsel nevertheless made submissions during the course of the hearing that it was void and of no effect. Counsel for the respondent complained that he was not prepared for such an attack on the validity of the certificate. In our opinion, it does not lie in the mouth of the respondent to complain of such submission in view of the respondent's own tardiness.

10. Exemption was claimed for ten documents or sets of documents under sub-section 33(1)(b) of the Act which is in the following terms:

"33.(1) A document is an exempt document if

disclosure of the document under this Act would be

contrary to the public interest for the reason that

the disclosure -

(b) would divulge any information or matter

communicated in confidence by or on behalf of

a foreign government, an authority of a

foreign government or an international

organization to the Government of the

Commonwealth, to an authority of the

Commonwealth or to a person receiving the

communication on behalf of the Commonwealth or

of an authority of the Commonwealth."

11. Where such an exemption is claimed, sub-section (2) provides that a certificate may be given that disclosure is contrary to the public interest. Sub-section (2) is in the following terms:

"(2) Where a Minister is satisfied that the

disclosure under this Act of a document would be

contrary to the public interest for a reason

referred to in sub-section (1), he may sign a

certificate to that effect (specifying that reason)

and, subject to the operation of Part VI, such a

certificate, so long as it remains in force,

establishes conclusively that the document is an

exempt document referred to in sub-section (1)."

12. The powers of the Minister under sub-section (2) may be delegated under sub-section (5) which is in the following terms:

"(5) The responsible Minister of an agency may,

either generally or as otherwise provided by the

instrument of delegation, by writing signed by him,

delegate to the principal officer of the agency his

powers under this section in respect of documents

of the agency."

13. In purported exercise of this power, an instrument of delegation was signed some 19 months prior to the certificate in question by Mr P.J. Keating, Treasurer, in the following terms:

"FREEDOM OF INFORMATION ACT 1982

INSTRUMENT OF DELEGATION

I, PAUL JOHN KEATING, TREASURER, pursuant to

sub-section 33(5) of the Freedom of Information Act

1982, DO HEREBY DELEGATE to the person occupying,

or for the time being exercising and performing the

duties of the office of COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION in

accordance with the TAXATION ADMINISTRATION ACT

1953, the following powers and functions:-

Under sub-section 33(2), (3) and (4) of the Freedom

of Information Act 1982 to sign certificates that

establish conclusively that a document or a

particular part or parts of a document or that

information as to the existence or non-existence of

a document to which sub-section 33(1) of the

Freedom of Information Act 1982 applies is exempt

from release under the Act on grounds that

disclosure of the document would be contrary to the

public interest in accordance with section 33 of

that Act.

DATED THIS . . . . 30th . . . . . DAY OF SEPTEMBER 1985

(Sgd Paul Keating)

P.J. KEATING

TREASURER"

14. In purported exercise of that delegation, the certificate claiming to be a conclusive certificate was signed by Mr T.P. Boucher, Commissioner of Taxation. It was in the following terms:

"FREEDOM OF INFORMATION ACT 1982

CERTIFICATE UNDER SUB-SECTION 33(5)

This certificate is issued in response to a

request by the Association of Mouth and Foot

Painting Artists Pty Ltd c/- Westgarth Baldick,

Solicitors, 10th Floor, 39 Martin Place, Sydney,

dated 23 August 1985, under the Freedom of

Information Act 1982, for a copy of documents

described as being:-

"All documents in existence (currently held in

Investigation by Wheatley)"

I, Trevor Percy Boucher, Commissioner of Taxation,

under delegation pursuant to sub-section 33(5) of

the Freedom of Information Act 1982, HEREBY CERTIFY

that I have examined the documents in the attached

schedule to which sub-paragraph 33(1)(b) applies

and I am satisfied that the disclosure of each

document or part thereof would be contrary to the

public interest for the reason that disclosure

would divulge information or matter communicated in

confidence by or on behalf of a foreign government

or an authority of a foreign government to the

Government of the Commonwealth or to an authority

of the Commonwealth or to a person receiving the

communication on behalf of the Commonwealth or of

an authority of the Commonwealth.

DATED THIS 5th DAY OF May 1987

(Sgd T.P. Boucher)

(T.P. Boucher)

COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION"

15. Although such a certificate is often referred to as a "conclusive" certificate, application may be made to determine whether there exists reasonable grounds for the claim that disclosure would be contrary to the public interest. Powers to undertake such a review are set out in sub-sections (3) and (4) of section 58 which are in the following terms:

"58. . . . . . . .

(3) Where there is in force in respect of a

document a certificate under section 33, 33A,

34, 35 or 36, the powers of the Tribunal do

not extend to reviewing the decision to give

the certificate, but the Tribunal, constituted

in accordance with section 58B, may determine

such question in relation to that certificate

as is provided for in whichever of sub-sections

(4), (5) and (5A) applies in relation

to that certificate.

(4) where application is or has been made to the

Tribunal for the review of a decision refusing

to grant access to a document in accordance

with a request, being a document that is

claimed to be an exempt document under section

33, 33A, 34 or 35 and in respect of which a

certificate (other than a certificate of a

kind referred to in sub-section (5A)) is in

force under that section, the Tribunal shall,

if the applicant so requests, determine the

question whether there exist reasonable

grounds for that claim."

16. When such a request is made, it is to be determined by the Tribunal constituted in accordance with the requirements of sub-section (1) and (2) of section 58B which are in the following terms:

"58B. (1) Where a request is made to the Tribunal

in accordance with sub-section 58(4), (5) or (5A),

the Tribunal shall be constituted in accordance

with sub-section (2) for the purposes of any

proceeding for the determination of the question to

which the request relates.

(2) For the purposes of a proceeding

referred to in sub-section (1), the Tribunal shall

be constituted by -

(a) 3 presidential members; or

(b) a presidential member alone."

17. The present Tribunal was presided over by a senior member and was constituted by him together with two members. Notwithstanding the fact that the present Tribunal was not constituted in accordance with section 58B, we consider that we have jurisidiction to decide whether the certificate is valid, or whether it is a nullity. The terms of section 58(4) require that a certificate be in force before the operation of section 58B is triggered. In our view, it is only when a certificate is found to be a valid certificate, either after enquiry or by way of admission, that the requirement for a specially constituted Tribunal comes into effect. In order to decide whether that section becomes operative, we consider that we should first determine whether the document quoted above is a certificate within the meaning of the terms of sub-section 33(2).

18. It was assumed in Korczynski v. Quik Foods Pty Ltd 54 ALR 278, that delegations may be made in terms to the holder of an office for the time being. That assumption was not challenged on the hearing of these proceedings. It was, therefore, not disputed that a valid delegation could be made to "the person occupying, or for the time being exercising and performing the duties of the Office of the Commissioner of Taxation". It was not disputed that Mr Keating was the Treasurer and that the Treasurer was the responsible minister for the agency. It was not disputed that the "principal officer of the agency" was the Commissioner of Taxation and that Mr Boucher held that office. What was disputed was whether the instrument of delegation successfully delegated all necessary powers to the Commissioner in order to enable him to issue a valid certificate.

19. In considering this question, we have not found it necessary to decide whether the words "his powers" in sub-section (5) of section 33 means "all or any of his powers", and consequently whether the Minister was vested with authority to delegate only part of his powers under the section. Whether that be so or not, the fact is, in our opinion, he has not delegated by the instrument of delegation before us all the powers necessary for the bringing into being of a certificate with the effect set out in sub-section 33(2).

20. That sub-section contains two powers. The Minister firstly is empowered to satisfy himself that disclosure of the document would be contrary to the public interest for certain reasons. The appropriate meaning for power given in the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary is "ability to do something or anything, or to act upon a person or thing". One may therefore say that the Minister's ability to be satisfied is his power to be satisfied, and it becomes his duty to be satisfied in the exercise of that power before he signs a certificate. Regarded in the Hohfeldian sense, the exercise of this function is an exercise of a power in that it is part of the process of bringing about a change in the legal relationship between the applicant and the respondent. In the absence of its exercise, there is no presumption that a document, to which the applicant might otherwise be entitled to access, is conclusively presumed to be exempt. If the exercise of a function determines a legal relationship, that function is a power in the jurisprudential sense. The Minister's second power is to sign a certificate to the effect that he is so satisfied. The effect of such a combination of satisfaction and signature results in a document that establishes conclusively that the ground for exemption has been made out. Neither satisfaction nor signature separated from the other would have such an effect.

21. It appears to us on a literal reading of the instrument of delegation that the Treasurer has delegated merely the power to sign certificates. He has not delegated his power to satisfy himself that disclosure would be contrary to the public interest. We are unable to say whether this half delegation was intended, or whether it was assumed that in some informal way one power was linked with the other. An informal delegation by a Minister of legislative power, it has been repeatedly decided, is not a sufficient delegation, generally speaking. See, for example Hookings v. Director of Civil Aviation (1957) NZLR 929. there must be an express delegation if the intention of the delegator is to be ascertained from the documents. It is quite consistent with the instrument of delegation put before us that the Treasurer had decided to retain for himself the right to satisfy himself on matters of public interest, but to delegate the administrative consequences of such self satisfaction to his principal officer.

22. The mere signing of a certificate is not sufficient to establish the conclusive statutory effects of sub-section (2). Furthermore, an inadequately armed delegate cannot pull himself up by his own boot straps by saying that he is satisfied rather than that the Minister is satisfied of certain facts. If he has no power to exercise the Minister's "self satisfying" role, then any certificate that he may sign is authorised only by part of the pre-conditions necessary for an effective certificate.

23. In these circumstances it is not possible to view the certificate as partially valid. One cannot pick out the valid parts and rely upon those. In Morgan & Co v. Thomas (1938) 1 A11ER 696, the English Court of Appeal dealing with a purported conclusive certificate under the Workmens' Compensation Act that was defective in parts, said at page 699:

"I disagree with Mr Kerry Evans when he says that

one can find the certificate to be good in parts.

If the certificate is ambiguous, I do not think it

would be right to dissect it and say that one part

of it is conclusive and that another part of it is

not."

24. The same observations would be pertinent to the certificate presently before us, even if it were possible to argue that the certificate was valid in parts. In our view, however, even a partially valid certificate requires both powers to be exercised and in fact only one appears to have been.

25. In view of the fact that the certificate has serious effects so far as an applicant is concerned, and in view of the fact that such a certificate cuts down an applicant's rights which are conferred by section 3 of the Act dealing with its objects and by section 11, conferring the broad rights to access, we make no apology for taking such a strict approach to the certificate before us.

26. Section 59 of the Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees') Act (1971) provides for certificates to be given by medical boards in certain circumstances. Sub-section (3) provides:

"A joint certificate given by all the members of a

medical board under this section is final and is

conclusive evidence, for the purposes of this Act,

of the matters stated in the certificate."

27. The predecessor of that section has been considered in at least two High Court cases. In Bavcevic v. The Commonwealth [1957] HCA 67; (1957) 98 CLR 296, Dixon C.J. and Kitto J. assumed as a matter of course at page 302, that such a certificate would have to be strictly construed. These views were confirmed and enlarged by Windeyer J. in Commonwealth v. Mifsud [1965] HCA 56; (1964-65) 114 CLR 505. At page 515 His Honour said:

"The statutory provision that a certificate is to

be conclusive makes it imperative that its effect

be kept within narrow bounds and that it be

strictly construed."

28. We consider that these judicial observations give support to the approach we have taken to the construction of the certificate under review. Accordingly, in our view, the certificate is a nullity. It does not and has never "remained in force". It therefore fails to establish conclusively that the documents covered by the certificate are exempt.

29. This finding, of course, does not affect the claim made by the respondent for exemption under sub-section 33(1)(b). It simply takes away the reinforcement of the claim, upon which the respondent sought to rely. We will deal with the claim itself later in these reasons.

Access to Exempt Documents

30. During the course of the hearing counsel for the applicant made several applications, which would have resulted, had they been acceded to, in making available to persons other than the Tribunal, documents for which exemption had been claimed.

31. Section 64(1) of the Act is in the following terms:

"64. (1) Where there are proceedings before the

Tribunal under this Act in relation to a document

that is claimed to be an exempt document, section

37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975

does not apply in relation to the document but if

the Tribunal is not satisfied, by evidence on

affidavit or otherwise, that the document is an

exempt document it may require the document to be

produced for inspection by members of the Tribunal

only and if, upon the inspection, the Tribunal is

satisfied that the document is an exempt document,

the Tribunal shall return the document to the

person by whom it was produced without permitting

any person other than a member of the staff of the

Tribunal in the course of the performance of his

duties as a member of that staff, to have access to

the document or disclosing the contents of the

document to any such person."

32. Were it not for the terms of that section, this Tribunal would not be entitled to see the documents for which exemption is claimed. The section has been considered closely in Re Arnold Bloch Leibler and Company 6 ALD 62. The Tribunal there came to the conclusion, from an analysis of the terms of the section, that it had no power to make those documents available to any other persons than those named in the section.

33. Whether this results in "natural justice" is beside the point. The limitation on the relevant principles of natural justice applicable to tribunals such as this are set out in Mobil Oil Australia Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1963] HCA 41; (1963) 113 CLR 475, and in particular at pages 502 to 504. Counsel for the applicant was inclined from time to time to forget that these were not curial proceedings and that this Tribunal is not a court. It is, as was said in relation to another tribunal in Jolly v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1935] HCA 21; (1935) 53 CLR 206 at 214, "only another executive body in an administrative hierarchy". Kitto J. said of a Board of Review in Mobil Oil Australia Pty Ltd (supra) that the Board's "function is merely to do over again (within the limits of the taxpayer's objection) what the Commissioner did in making the assessment - not to give a decision affecting the taxpayer's legal situation, but to work out, as a step in administration, what it considers that situation to be. The Board is in the same position as the Commissioner himself . . .".

34. The reasoning in Re Arnold Bloch (supra) has been adopted by this Tribunal on a number of occasions since that decision was given. Most recently, it was specifically affirmed by a tribunal presided over by Deputy President Hall in Re Carver and Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet (3239), 19 February 1987.

35. We took the view therefore that it was not possible to make any of the documents available to counsel for the applicant. Furthermore, we ruled against his request that certain of the documents be shown to one of the respondent's witnesses so that the witness could identify certain areas and respond to questions to be put by counsel for the applicant. In our view, it is not within the power of the Tribunal to make such documents available under any circumstances to any party.

36. Counsel for the respondent applied for an order to be made under both sections 35 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act and 63, sub-section (2)(b) of the Act to enable the evidence of one witness to be taken in private hearing to the exclusion of the applicant and of its legal representatives. This application was opposed by counsel for the applicant as being contrary to the principles of natural justice which would, in a court, dictate that he and his client be allowed to hear all the evidence in the proceedings. In accordance with instructions which he said he had received, he undertook to submit to a condition that he would not pass on to his client any information concerning the documents which he might gain by reason of his presence while the evidence was being given. He also agreed to the imposition of a condition that his instructing solicitor be not present, and that his client be not present. He further volunteered a condition that he would not, in the future, accept any brief in any of the proceedings against his client that might arise out of the investigation, the subject of the present documents.

37. We considered that we were precluded from accepting any such undertaking, and we considered that in terms of section 63, on what we were told by counsel for the respondent, it was necessary to receive the evidence in the absence of the applicant and his representative in order to prevent disclosure to the applicant of the matter or information of a kind referred to in section 63. We considered that we were justified in acting on information from counsel for the respondent when the request was made that it was necessary to follow this procedure for the reasons referred to in the sub-section as there was no other practical way of dealing with the initial request. However, we continued to satisfy ourselves of the necessity of the procedure as we heard the evidence given in closed hearing.

38. The question of the giving and acceptance of such undertakings has been considered elsewhere, most notably by a Full Bench of the Federal Court in News Corporation and Others v. National Companies and Securities Commission [1984] FCA 400; 57 ALR 550. After drawing an analogy with the position in criminal trials where discovery is not available to a defendant against the prosecution, Fox J. went on to consider the type of undertaking offered by counsel for the applicant:

"A course often followed in litigation is for

access to be granted to counsel, on the footing

that he, or he and his solicitor, are the only

persons to see a document. It is a convenient but

dangerous practice, as it involves the withholding

from a client by his legal representative of

information relevant to the litigation. The

acceptance of such a course would not, I imagine,

be within the ordinary retainer of counsel, and

would as a rule need specific instructions.

Counsel relied upon the practice, as part of his

argument, and submitted that the Tribunal had erred

in not following it. An application to the effect

mentioned had been made to the Tribunal, but was

rejected by it. In the light of the statutory

provisions, I doubt whether the matter for its

discretion, and its failure to exercise the

discretion in favour of the applicant is not a

matter which would by itself involve an appealable

error of law."

39. Woodward J. took a similar view. At page 564 he said:

"The question is whether the denial to the

applicants, and more particularly to their counsel,

of access to the bulk of the schedule, constituted

a denial of natural justice. At one stage of the

hearing before the AAT, counsel offered

undertakings that if they and their instructing

solicitor were allowed access to the schedule for

the purposes of the FOI Act hearing, they would not

act for the applicants in any later proceedings

which might be brought against their clients under

the Companies Act 1981 or the Companies

(Acquisition of Shares) Act 1980 or the Companies

Ordinance 1962 of the Australian Capital Territory.

The same undertaking was offered to this court.

I should say immediately that I have grave doubts

whether the AAT, or this court, could properly

accept any such undertaking. An order to that

effect would, I believe, be contrary to the public

policy which requires a litigant to be free to be

represented by the practitioners of his choice.

And if such an undertaking could not be enforced,

it should not be accepted (see Thomson Australian

Holdings Pty Ltd v. Trade Practices Commission

[1981] HCA 48; (1981) 148 CLR 150; 37 ALR 66). The only

undertakings which could be accepted, in my view,

are those which are normally offered in cases

involving trade secrets or otherwise requiring

confidentiality as between litigants but not

between lawyers."

40. In the light of such observations, we had no difficulty in arriving at the conclusion that we should not accept the undertakings offered and that counsel for the applicant, as well as his client and instructing solicitor, should be excluded from the hearing of the evidence of one particular witness. After his evidence in chief had been given and counsel returned to the hearing room, he then made an application to be allowed to cross-examine the witness. In our view, evidence given by way of cross-examination was as vulnerable as evidence given in chief. If it was necessary to receive evidence in chief in the absence of the applicant's representatives, it would be just as necessary to ensure that cross-examination did not indirectly adduce the evidence given in camera. Notwithstanding this, on the second day of the hearing agreement was reached between counsel for the applicant and the respondent that, provided cross-examination did not touch in any way upon any of the material put before this Tribunal in camera, and provided that the cross-examination in no way tended to disclose the contents or any part of any of the documents for which exemption was claimed, then no objection would be raised to the cross-examination of the witness, Mr Wheatley, who had given evidence in closed hearing. In view of this concession, we felt constrained to consent on similar conditions, to the cross-examination taking place. From time to time, it was necessary to rule on the admissibility of questions, their relevance to the imposed conditions, and particularly on those that related to documents listed in the schedule of exempt documents. Counsel for the applicant protested that such rulings made his position impossible and eventually declined to continue with the cross-examination. However, it should be noted that the cross-examination was wide ranging and occupied almost 2 hours. The role of counsel for the applicant in proceedings under the Act is a difficult one. We can sympathise with the frustration sometimes felt by those used to the common law traditions and strict adversarial procedures. Such procedures are clearly not appropriate for at least some of the questions that arise in the conduct of broad ranging claims under the Act. The real difficulty for all of us is the delicate question of determining appropriate procedures in each case consistent both with the Tribunal's broad and flexible review power and the statutory requirement of natural justice set out in section 39 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 balanced against the specific statutory limitations imposed by the Freedom of Information Act.

41. We turn now to the various claims for exemption made under sections of the Act to which reference will be made:

The Section 38 Claim for Exemption

42. Section 38 of the Act is in the following terms:

"38. A document is an exempt document if there is

in force an enactment applying specifically to

information of a kind contained in the document and

prohibiting persons referred to in the enactment

from disclosing information of that kind, whether

the prohibition is absolute or is subject to

exceptions or qualifications."

43. It was submitted by the respondent that section 16 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 was part of an enactment falling within the terms of section 38. Section 16 obliges officers of the Commissioner to observe secrecy and prohibits the divulging of information, except in specified instances. The presence of the exceptions has led to a difference of opinion among some judges of the Federal Court. However, the better view appears to be that section 16 is an enactment applying specifically to information of a kind contained in the documents in respect of which the claim is made, and prohibiting persons referred to in the enactment from disclosing information of that kind. The majority decision in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Swiss Aluminium Australia Limited and Others 66 ALR 159, presently governs the situation so far as this Tribunal is concerned. The reasoning of their Honours, Bowen C.J. and Jackson J. (the majority in that case), was specifically approved by another Full Bench of the Federal Court in Harrigan v. Department of Health, G60 of 1986, 13 November 1986, where section 130 of the Health Insurance Act 1973 was held to have the same effect. However, as was noted in that decision, special leave has been granted by the High Court to appeal against the decision in Swiss Aluminium. For that reason, counsel for the applicant, while realising that his invitation to this Tribunal not to follow Swiss Aluminium would be declined, nevertheless offered the invitation so as to preserve his position. Of course, we do decline and acknowledge that we are bound by the two Federal Court decisions to which reference has been made.

44. In view of this present uncertainty concerning the operation of section 38, the respondent also relied upon another enactment as falling within its terms.

45. A number of conventions and agreements have been entered into by Australia with governments of other countries in relation to exchange of information relating to various taxes. Included amongst these is an agreement with the United Kingdom. Whilst it would have given information of a kind to which the respondent alleged the applicant was not entitled if the respondent nominated the country that had entered into the agreement with Australia upon which the respondent would rely, it was agreed that for the purpose of testing the point, the United Kingdom Agreement was typical in its applicable Articles.

46. The one upon which the respondent relied was Article 21 which is in the following terms:

"The taxation authorities shall exchange such

information (being information which is at their

disposal under their respective taxation laws in

the normal course of administration) as is

necessary for carrying out the provisions of this

Agreement or for the prevention of fraud or for the

administration of statutory provisions against

legal avoidance in relation to the taxes which are

the subject of this Agreement. Any information so

exchanged shall be treated as secret but may be

disclosed to persons (including a court or

tribunal) concerned with the assessment,

collection, enforcement or prosecution in respect

of the taxes which are the subject of this

Agreement. No information as aforesaid shall be

exchanged which would disclose any trade, business,

industrial or professional secret or trade

process."

47. The whole of that Agreement forms part of a schedule to the Income Tax (International Agreements) Act 1953 ("the International Agreements Act"). Section 4 of the Act defines "enactment" to mean (inter alia) an Act. The Agreement is an Act and is part of the domestic law of Australia, both because of the operation of section 13 of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 which provides that every schedule to an Act is deemed to form a part thereof, and by section 5 of the International Agreements Act itself. This is in the following terms:

"5(1) Subject to this Act, the provisions of the

United Kingdom agreement, so far as those

provisions affect Australian tax, have the force of

law in relation to tax in respect of-

(a) dividends that have been derived by

non-residents on or after 1 July 1967, and in

relation to which the agreement remains

effective;

(b) interest subject to withholding tax, being

interest that has been derived on or after 1

January 1968, and in relation to which the

agreement remains effective; and

(c) income to which neither paragraph (a) nor (b)

applies, being income of the year of income

that commenced on 1 July 1967, or of a

subsequent year of income in relation to which

the agreement remains effective."

48. The respondent relied particularly upon the second sentence of Article 21. It was argued that this was part of an enactment prohibiting persons referred to in that enactment from disclosing information of the kind referred to in section 38. It was argued that indeed the prohibition extended to disclosure to this Tribunal in the present proceedings. The applicant argued that the prohibition had no such effect and that in fact the information could be made available to his client as, so he put it, who could be more concerned with the collection of the taxes in question than the one who might have to pay them.

49. We have come to the view that the Tribunal in hearing an application under the Freedom of Information Act 1982 concerning documents involved in a taxation investigation, would not fall within the concept of the "tribunal" referred to in Article 21. The Agreement, as appears from section 5 of the International Agreements Act above, is to "have the force of law in relation to tax". The Article deals only with matters concerning the four aspects of taxes set out in that Article and possibly other matters (such as investigations) leading up to the stages they describe. This present enquiry into the question whether a decision to refuse access to documents under the Freedom of Information Act is the correct or preferable decision, it seems to us, is not an enquiry in relation to the taxation investigation, and is certainly not an enquiry in relation to the four aspects of taxes to which the Article refers. The fact that this Tribunal as an institution has jurisdiction to reconsider objections by taxpayers in other circumstances does not, in our view, bring this Tribunal, when dealing with an application under the Act, within the terms of the bracketed exception. An analogy may be drawn with the Federal Court, which also has appellate jurisdiction in relation to taxation assessments. Whilst otherwise secret information might be brought before the Court in exercising that appellate jurisdiction, a claim of privilege would probably arise if it were sought to compel the Commissioner to disclose otherwise secret information to the Court in another jurisdiction for the purpose of assessing damages. In our view the "disclosure" referred to in Article 21 is properly construed as disclosure to persons (including a court or tribunal) only in so far as they are concerned with assessment, collection, enforcement or prosecution in respect of the taxes.

50. Even more so do we consider that "persons concerned" does not include the taxpayer about whose affairs information is supplied. Both the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary and the Macquarie Dictionary give as the appropriate meaning of concerned "interested or involved". What must be demonstrated to come within the terms of this exception is that one is interested or involved in either the assessing, collecting, enforcing or prosecuting contemplated by the Article. The present taxpayer is interested or involved in receiving the assessment, paying the tax, being the subject of enforcement and being a defendant in any possible prosecutions. The sentence in other words refers to the active participant, not the passive recipient. In our view, Article 21, in so far as it forms part of Australian domestic law, must be regarded as an enactment of a type contemplated by section 38. We are satisfied, except for a small number of documents, on the evidence before us that the documents covered by claims under this section (some of which we have not seen) are of a kind that fall within one or both grounds - section 16 and Article 21. It follows that these claims must succeed. In respect of the small number of documents about which we are not so satisfied, claims under other sections succeed.

The Section 33 Claim for Exemption

51. We did not see the documents in respect of which this claim is made. However, on the hearing of the application, an affidavit of Mr Toner was read. He was the principal advising officer in the administrative law unit attached to the appeals and review group in the Australian Taxation Office. In addition to the written evidence contained in his affidavit, oral evidence was adduced by way of cross-examination. Evidence (as has been noted above) was also given in camera by Mr Wheatley, and in open hearing under cross-examination. From this evidence can be identified material relevant to the claim under section 33(1)(b) of the Act which is in the following terms:

"33.(1) A document is an exempt document if

disclosure of the document under this Act would be

contrary to the public interest for the reason that

the disclosure-

(a) would, or could reasonably be expected to,

cause damage to-

(i) the security of the Commonwealth;

(ii) the defence of the Commonwealth; or

(iii) the international relations of the

Commonwealth; or

(b) would divulge any information or matter

communicated in confidence by or on behalf of

a foreign government, an authority of a

foreign government or an international

organisation to the Government of the

Commonwealth, to an authority of the

Commonwealth or to a person receiving the

communication on behalf of the Commonwealth or

of an authority of the Commonwealth."

52. We are satisfied, particularly from the evidence of Mr Wheatley, that the documents, the subject of this claim, if disclosed would divulge material that had been communicated in confidence by an authority of a foreign government to the respondent. Having come to that conclusion there is no scope for further consideration whether disclosure would, in any event, be contrary to the public interest.

53. In Re O'Donovan and Attorney-General's Department 8 ALD 528 at 533 the Tribunal presided over by Deputy President Hall concluded that the words "for the reason that" are synonymous with the word "because". It follows, as Deputy President Hall pointed out, that whereas Parliament has been prepared to entrust a consideration of the wider public interest issues to external review where claims are made under other sections, such is not the case under section 33. The words of sub-section (1) themselves are an expression of the content of the public interest. Indeed it might almost be said that in view of this decision and in view of Re Mann and Australian Taxation Office 7 ALD 698 decided some five months earlier, there is now very little need (if any) for a conclusive certificate under section 33. The position appears to be that once an agency satisfies any of the conditions enumerated in sub-section 33(1), a document is established as an exempt document and disclosure is, in effect, deemed to be contrary to the public interest.

54. We have concluded therefore that the claim for exemption under this section must succeed.

The Section 36 Claim for Exemption

55. A claim was made under section 36(1) in respect of another category of documents. That sub-section is in the following terms:

"36.(1) Subject to this section, a document is an

exempt document if it is a document the disclosure

of which under this Act-

(a) would disclose matter in the nature of, or

relating to, opinion, advice or recommendation

obtained, prepared or recorded, or

consultation or deliberation that has taken

place, in the course of, or for the purposes

of, the deliberative processes involved in the

functions of an agency or Minister or of the

Government of the Commonwealth; and

(b) would be contrary to the public interest."

56. Evidence was given (and this was confirmed by inspection) that these documents contained material of a deliberative nature relating to the investigation of various aspects of the arrangements in which the applicant was involved. They discussed the applicability of certain sections of the tax law to the arrangements seen to have been established, and contained recommendations as to future action. There cannot be any doubt, in our view, that the documents fall within the terms of paragraph (a). The only question to be resolved is whether they fall within the terms of paragraph (b). The meaning of "deliberative processes" was considered fully in Re Waterford and Department of The Treasury (No. 2) [1984] AATA 67; 5 ALD 588. At page 606 the Tribunal said:

"58 As a matter of ordinary English the expression

"deliberative processes" appears to us to be wide

enough to include any of the processes of

deliberation or consideration involved in the

functions of an agency. "Deliberation" means "The

action of deliberating: careful consideration with

a view to decision": see The Shorter Oxford English

Dictionary. The action of deliberating, in common

understanding, involves the weighing up or

evaluation of the competing arguments or

considerations that may have a bearing upon one's

course of action. In short, the deliberative

processes involved in the functions of an agency

are its thinking processes - the processes of

reflection, for example, upon the wisdom and

expediency of a proposal, a particular decision or

a course of action. Deliberations on policy

matters undoubtedly come within this broad

description. Only to the extent that a document

may disclose matter in the nature of or relating to

deliberative processes does s36(1)(a) come into

play.

59 It by no means follows, therefore, that every

document on a departmental file will fall into this

category. Section 36(5) provides that the section

does not apply to a document by reason only of

purely factual material contained in the document

(see, in this regard, the Full Court decision in

Harris [1984] FCA 8; (1984) 51 ALR 581). See also s36(6)

relating to reports and the like. Furthermore,

however imprecise the dividing line may first

appear to be in some cases, documents disclosing

deliberative processes must, in our view, be

distinguished from documents dealing with the

purely procedural or administrative processes

involved in the functions of an agency. A document

which, for example, discloses no more than a step

in the procedures by which an agency handles a

request under the FOI Act is not a document to

which s36(1)(a) applies.

60 It is documents containing opinion, advice,

recommendations etc relating to the internal

processes of deliberation that are potentially

shielded from disclosure - documents that might,

perhaps, have been more aptly described in the

headnote as "Internal Thinking Documents". Out of

that broad class of documents, exemption under s36

only attaches to those documents the disclosure of

which is "contrary to the public interest":

s36(1)(b) of the FOI Act and cf ss35, 36, 37(1)(b)

and 37(2) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal

Act 1975."

57. These views were approved in Re Howard and The Treasurer of the Commonwealth 3 AAR 169, a decision of the then President of this Tribunal.

58. The question of public interest was considered in Murtagh v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1984] AATA 249; 54 ALR 313, a decision in which the presiding member was the then President of this Tribunal. At page 325 the Tribunal said:

"In our opinion, the "public interest" looks to

matters such as the overall need for

confidentiality within the Australian Taxation

Office and the nature of the documents to which

access is sought, whether they are documents

relating to a purely routine assessment or whether

they are documents concerned with the investigation

of taxation evasion or like matter in respect of

which there is special reason for confidentiality.

Broadly speaking, s36 can be seen as an attempt by

the legislature to protect the integrity and

viability of the decision-making process. If the

release of documents would impair this process to a

significant or substantial degree and there is no

countervailing benefit to the public which

outweighs that impairment then it would be contrary

to the public interest to grant access.

Moreover, public interest must be considered in the

light of s3(2) of the Act which provides that "...

the provisions of this Act shall be interpreted so

as to further the object set out in sub-section (1)

...". Section 3(1) provides:

"The object of this Act is to extend as far as

possible the right of the Australian community to

access to information in the possession of the

Government of the Commonwealth by-

(a) ...

(b) creating a general right of access to

information in documentary form in the

possession of Ministers, departments and

public authorities, limited only by exceptions

and exemptions necessary for the protection of

essential public interests and the private and

business affairs of persons in respect of whom

information is collected and held by

departments and public authorities."

Note the reference to "essential public

interests"."

59. The present affairs dealt with by the documents cannot be described as routine matters within an agency. They deal with a complicated investigation with international ramifications. Mr Wheatley, who had been an investigator with the respondent for 10 years, said he could remember being involved in only one or two other such international investigations.

60. The investigation relates to the possible avoidance of large sums of taxation achieved in various ways, partly by international transfer pricing policy and partly by overstatement of certain local expenditure. Normal judicial practice is to set out the facts and circumstances found by a court as the basis of its judgement. In reviews by this Tribunal under the Act, it is not possible to follow this course for obvious reasons. We can only state the principles which have guided us in our review and state the result of that review where it is based on inspection of the documents or evidence given in camera. On this basis, we are satisfied that the public interest in preserving the confidentiality of these documents at this stage clearly outweighs the general right of access to documents of an agency which is conferred by section 11 of the Act.

61. Mr Toner gave evidence that disclosure of this deliberative material would reveal the approach adopted by the Commissioner in dealing with tax avoidance and would arm the applicant with information which might enable it to take "countering measures". Notwithstanding the use of the term "countering measures", which may or may not indicate lawful measures, we accept this evidence and the apprehensions it details as reasonable. We consider that these are important considerations and add up to a clear demonstration on the part of the respondent that disclosure would be contrary to the public interest.

The Section 37 Claim for Exemption

62. This claim is made under the above section which is in the following terms:

"37.(1) A document is an exempt document if its

disclosure under this Act would, or could

reasonably be expected to-

(a) prejudice the conduct of an investigation of a

breach, or possible breach, of the law, or a

failure, or possible failure, to comply with a

law relating to taxation or prejudice the

enforcement or proper administration of the

law in a particular instance;

(b) disclose, or enable a person to ascertain, the

existence or identity of a confidential source

of information in relation to the enforcement

or administration of the law; or

.... "

63. The documents came into existence or came into the respondent's possession in the course of the investigation of the applicant's taxation affairs. It was contended by the respondent that if the information were disclosed, it would be possible and indeed could reasonably be expected that if the applicant had engaged in an arrangement to avoid tax, it might frame responses to queries which do no more than remain consistent with the information which is known to be already in the possession of the respondent. Furthermore, not only would the trend of enquiries be revealed, but the applicant could be made aware of the extent of information which the respondent possessed. This might indicate to the applicant how much information the respondent had not yet acquired, thereby facilitating concealment of relevant facts and implementation of counter measures.

64. The applicant relied heavily on the fact that shortly before the hearing of this application, assessments had been issued to the applicant for the years ended 30th June 1980 to 1984 inclusive. Counsel for the applicant sought to argue that in view of the fact that the Commissioner was not entitled to issue arbitrary assessments but must make a reasoned calculation before issuing any assessment, it was reasonable to conclude that at any rate so far as the matters covered by those assessments were concerned, the investigations were at an end. He further argued that if this was accepted, then the whole basis for a claim under section 37 disappeared. In our view, the issue of the assessments can have no bearing on the relevant considerations, both as a matter of law and as a matter of fact.

65. Evidence was given that notices had been served on the applicant under section 264 of the Income Tax Assessment Act requiring the applicant to provide certain information. It was only when this information was not forthcoming that the assessments were issued. They were accompanied by adjustment sheets accounting for the large additional amounts of tax assessed against the applicant. These adjustments were confined to the disallowance of amounts claimed for artists' salaries, entertainment expenses and legal expenses. The fact that the assessments were issued in default of response to a notice, and the fact that they deal only with some of the issues outstanding, in our view, clearly demonstrates that investigations of these aspects of the applicant's returns has by no means been concluded. Quite apart from the proceedings one would expect by way of objection and further appeal, there is evidence (uncontradicted by any evidence called by the applicant) that investigation even into these limited areas is still continuing.

66. However, a much larger area of possible exposure to additional tax is set out in what was referred to as "Statement of Proposed Adjustments". This is a document suggesting that the transfer pricing by the applicant of colour separations imported from one or more foreign companies was not appropriate, and should be adjusted. Bases were suggested for possible adjustment founded on certain enquiries the respondent had made. The document was no more than an agenda for further discussion of the applicant's taxation liability. At the time of the hearing of this application, it was proposed that meetings take place between representatives of both parties to discuss this document. Mr Wheatley was quite positive in his evidence that none of the investigations were by any means complete. Furthermore, consideration was being given to commencement of proceedings against the applicant for failure to comply with the requirements of notices given under section 264 to supply additional information. In passing it may be noted that in Re Briggs (No. 1) and Australian Taxation Office (2736), 30 June 1986, the Tribunal was not prepared to hold that an investigation was no longer on foot simply because an assessment had been issued in that case.

67. The present evidence leaves us in no doubt that an investigation of a breach or possible breach of the law relating to taxation is in being. Whether disclosure of the documents for which exemption is claimed would, or could reasonably be expected to, prejudice the conduct of such an investigation, is the only question to be determined.

68. The meaning of "prejudice" was considered by all the judges in News Corporation Limited v. National Companies and Securities Commission [1984] FCA 400; 57 ALR 550. The observations of Woodward J. at page 561 are apposite:

"... I think that the words "would, or could

reasonably be expected to ... prejudice" mean more

than "would or might prejudice". A reasonable

expectation of an event requires more than a

possibility, risk or chance of the event occurring.

On the other hand, if the legislature had required

a probability of prejudice it could easily have

said so. In my view it is reasonable to expect an

event to occur if there is about an even chance of

its happening and, without attempting to suggest

words alternative to those chosen by the draftsman,

it is in that general sense that the phrase should

be read."

69. In our view, disclosure would pose more than a possibility or small risk of impairment to the continuing investigation. It certainly would have a grave effect, which could very well lead to the aborting of the investigation. It is not necessary, as their Honours have observed, to put a gloss upon the statutory words "could reasonably be expected to". Nevertheless, we feel that disclosure would certainly come within the terms of that phrase and then extend even further.

The Section 40 Claim for Exemption

70. Claims for exemption were made in respect of certain documents under sub-sections (c), (d) and (e) of section 40(1) of the Act. These are in the following terms:

"40.(1) Subject to sub-section (2), a document is

an exempt document if its disclosure under this Act

would, or could reasonably be expected to-

. . . . .

(c) have a substantial adverse effect on the

management or assessment of personnel by the

Commonwealth or by an agency;

(d) have a substantial adverse effect on the

proper and efficient conduct of the operations

of an agency; or

(e) have a substantial adverse effect on the

conduct by or on behalf of the Commonwealth or

an agency of industrial relations."

71. The claims refer to those parts of documents where names of staff of the Australian Tax Office have been deleted. Mr Toner gave evidence that generally where staff of the respondent contact a taxpayer, either by personal interview or by telephone, it is the policy of the respondent that the officers concerned should disclose their names to the taxpayer. Apart from this, the respondent does not disclose staff names unless the officer is a delegate of the Commissioner under the Income Tax Assessment Act or equivalent legislation, and is acting in the exercise of the power delegated to him, or the officer holds a position, the duties of which include signing in his or her own name correspondence to taxpayers or to outside organisations.

72. Mr Toner gave evidence that he was aware of indications from staff associations that the identification of officers by name or otherwise would lead to staff industrial action. He was aware of representations that had been made to the respondent by the Federated Clerks' Union on the issue of disclosure of the names of staff officers, which representations included indications of proposed industrial action if this practice were to be encouraged. If these results were to follow (and we have no doubt that they could well do so), they would amount to a substantial adverse effect within the meaning of the three sub-paragraphs quoted above.

73. In more recent times, counsel for applicants in other cases have seen fit to concede this claim (see for example Re Briggs (supra)). It was dealt with succinctly and on a general basis in Re Mann and Australian Taxation Office (supra) at page 708:

"20 The claims in this category relate to the

deletion of the names of officers of the Australian

Taxation Office. Evidence in this respect was

given by Mr JJ. Daly who is Senior Assistant

Commissioner, Operations Division, Australian

Taxation Office. We are of the opinion that there

would be very real dangers of misuse of the names

of officers of an agency, at least and in

particular the names of officers of the Australian

Taxation Office, if disclosure were made. It would

be one thing for the names of officers holding

delegations from the Commissioner of Taxation to be

released, but at lower levels we see a distinct

danger of mischievous use of officers' names, with

no countervailing advantage to the public. While

we do not suggest for a moment the presence of any

such danger of misuse in the present case, the

principle is a general one."

74. Nothing has been put before us to persuade us that this general principle should not be adhered to in the present circumstances. This claim, then, must also succeed.

Overlapping Claims

75. Claims for exemption of certain documents were also made under certain other sections. A claim was made in respect of documents said to affect personal privacy under section 41(1) of the Act. It is not necessary to consider these claims as claims under section 38 have been made in respect of the same documents and have been dealt with above.

76. Similarly, the claim made under section 43(1)(c)(i) that disclosure could reasonably be expected to unreasonably affect a person adversely in respect of his lawful business or professional affairs, overlaps with claims made under both section 41(1) dealing with personal privacy, and, in the circumstances of this application, with claims made under section 38 dealing with the secrecy requirements of the Income Tax Assessment Act.

77. Claims made under section 45 in respect of documents containing material obtained in confidence are similarly covered by claims made in respect of the same documents under other sections which have been dealt with above.

78. In the case of the great majority of the documents, claims for exemption were made under multiple sections of the Act. In some cases there could be some doubt as to whether a claim under a particular section or sections should be sustained, but for all documents there was at least one section under which, for the reasons given above, the claim should succeed.

79. In the circumstances we consider that the respondent has made out a sufficient case to support all the claims for exemption dealt with above, and accordingly the decision under review is affirmed.

I have had the opportunity to settle a draft of the decision in this matter and fully concur with the jointly settled decision in all but paragraphs 19-28 inclusive. These paragraphs refer to the question of the validity of the certificate issued pursuant to sub-section (2) of section 33 of the Act.

2. I agree with my colleagues that jurisdiction exists in a Tribunal constituted in the ordinary way under section 21 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 to review such a certificate for validity. Such a certificate cannot in my view be said to be in force unless it is valid yet in terms of sub-section 58(3) Freedom of Information Act 1982 ("the Act") such a certificate must actually be 'in force' before a Tribunal, especially constituted under section 58B of that Act is needed to review the reasonableness of the grounds upon which it was issued. It is not the mere existence of the certificate that provides one of the pre-conditions necessitating a specially constituted Tribunal but the state of that certificate being 'in force'.

3. Notwithstanding this view, it is still desirable as a matter of fairness to an applicant, courtesy to the Tribunal and to prevent unnecessary complication and delay in proceedings, that the respondent advise the Tribunal of the existence of a certificate such as one issued pursuant to sub-section 33(2) of the Act as early as possible prior to the hearing so that the question of whether a specially constituted Tribunal might be needed can be addressed in preliminary conferences convened pursuant to section 34 Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975.

4. Turning to the question of validity of the certificate, sub-section 33(2-7) of the Act provides:

"33. . . . .

(2) Where a Minister is satisfied that the

disclosure under this Act of a document would be

contrary to the public interest for a reason

referred to in sub-section (1), he may sign a

certificate to that effect (specifying that reason)

and, subject to the operation of Part VI, such a

certificate, so long as it remains in force,

establishes conclusively that the document is an

exempt document referred to in sub-section (1).

(3) Where a Minister is satisfied as mentioned in

sub-section (2) by reason only of matter contained

in a particular part or particular parts of a

document, a certificate under that sub-section in

respect of the document shall identify that part or

those parts of the document as containing the

matter by reason of which the certificate is given.

(4) Where a Minister is satisfied that information

as to the existence or non-existence of a document

as described in a request would, if contained in a

document of an agency, cause the disclosure under

this Act of the last-mentioned document to be

contrary to the public interest for a reason

referred to in sub-section (1), he may sign a

certificate to that effect (specifying that

reason).

(5) The responsible Minister of an agency may,

either generally or as otherwise provided by the

instrument of delegation, by writing signed by him,

delegate to the principal officer of the agency his

powers under this section in respect of documents

of the agency.

(6) A power delegated under sub-section (5), when

exercised by the delegate, shall, for the purposes

of this Act, be deemed to have been exercised by

the responsible Minister.

(7) A delegation under sub-section (5) does not

prevent the exercise of a power by the responsible

Minister."

5. The terms of the instrument of delegation and the certificate issued in these proceedings have been set out in the reasons of my colleagues. In my view the separate powers provided for in sub-sections 33(2), (3) and (4) of the Act are each properly considered to be single powers. Whilst as a matter of fact the mental achievement of a state of 'satisfaction' and the physical act of the signing of a certificate are two separate events, as a matter of statutory construction they are in my view an exercise of a single power. To use terms more usually applied in a different domain the signing of a certificate seems best understood as the outward and visible sign of an inward state - of satisfaction. They are both part of a unitary power, the exercise of which is only ever manifested to the world by way of the signing of a certificate. For all practical purposes it is the existence of a certificate signed either by the Minister or the person validly exercising the delegated authority set out in sub-section 33(5) of the Act that will conclusively establish that the requisite mental state of satisfaction has been achieved.

6. No point has been taken in these proceedings as to the validity of the Treasurer's signature, the Commissioner's signature, the delegation to "the holder of an office for the time being" or the question of whether the delegation remained in force at the time the certificate was signed. The only question is whether it was necessary for the instrument of delegation to make reference to both the duty of being satisfied and the signing of a certificate so specifying. In view of the position I have adopted above, I have no difficulty in holding there is no necessity to specify both elements as the act of signing the certificate can only occur when the requisite state of satisfaction has been achieved and it is the only way in which the power referred to in sub-section 33(2) of the Act can be given a form which has legal effect.

7. To adopt a different approach to the construction of section 33 has the consequence that for the certificate in a case such as this to be valid it would be required to state and reflect the reality that the Commissioner of Taxation was certifying that the Treasurer was satisfied in his own mind that the documents were exempt from disclosure as contrary to the public interest. Such an outcome would defeat the whole legislative intention of being able to delegate the power as it would necessitate the Minister continuing to give personal attention to every document said to be exempt. In the alternative, a different approach to construction of section 33 would necessitate the instrument of delegation itself expressing the delegation of both the power of being satisfied and the signing of certificates to that effect. Whilst this would clearly eliminate any room for argument it would in my view be mere surplusage.

8. Accordingly, I find that the certificate issued by the Commissioner of Taxation and dated 5 May 1987 is a valid exercise of the delegation made by the Treasurer on 30 September 1985 pursuant to sub-section 33(5) of the Act.

9. Notwithstanding these differences, since I concur with the remainder of the reasons of my colleagues I too would affirm the decision under review.
