ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Allowable Deductions - Interest on borrowed money Moneys borrowed back after gifting them to lenders - Whether transaction a "sham"

Income Tax Assessment Act (1936) (Cth)

Income Tax - Allowable deductions - "Interest" on moneys borrowed used to earn income - "Interest" paid gratuitously - Whether deduction allowable

Income Tax Assessment Act (1936) (Cth) - sections 51(1), 7B (1)(c), 170(4) , and 185

REASONS FOR DECISION

17 July 1987 Mr Peter M Roach (Senior Member)

  1. These applications relate to the years of income ended 30 June 1983 and 1984 respectively. In the latter year there is a claim to a further deduction of $42 arising out of the purchase of the Sydney Morning Herald at a cost of $98.90. In both years there is a claim to deduct amounts ($11,876 and $10,400 respectively) claimed to be "interest" paid in consequence of "borrowings" of $394,000 used in the course of income generation. All claims arise out of the intense_ interest of the Applicant in profiting by investing in financial markets.
  2. The Applicant (born 1924) is a married woman and mother of five adult children who were born in the period 1950 to 1960. She described herself as a grazier and retired school-teacher. During the years in question she worked a farming property in partnership with her husband; worked casually as a school teacher and for the electoral office; and invested. She is intensely interested in increasing her wealth; she tries to avoid spending money unnecessarily ("I never pay people to do things I think I can do for myself"); and dislikes needlessly paying any form of tax. Encouraged by some unidentified success before a Taxation Board of Review in another matter and confident in the merit of her own assertions and evidence, she represented herself at the hearing and called no-one but herself to give evidence.
  3. In 1974 the Applicant decided upon a course of action which was intended to reduce the liability to estate duties which she expected her executors to bear in the event of her death. She decided upon a "gifting" program. Not wishing to expose herself to liability to gift duty (Commonwealth), she determined upon making the maximum gifts she could as frequently as she could without becoming liable to pay Commonwealth gift duty. She decided upon cash "gifts".
  4. To accommodate those ambitions she decided to "give" $1,999 to each of her children on 1 July 1974. The children were then aged from 23 years to 14 years. One difficulty remained. The Applicant was not willing to forgo the opportunities she had enjoyed in the past of being able to invest the monies she wished to give to the children. She implemented her plans by having each child execute a document in the following terms:-

"1st July, 1974.

This is to certify that, on this day, 1st July, Nineteen Hundred and Seventy four, my Mother, (the Applicant), has gifted me the sum of One thousand, nine hundred and ninety-nine Dollars. This amount I have loaned back to my -Mother, to be repaid on 1st July in the year Two Thousand and fourteen".

5. Her evidence was that by the year 2014 A.D. when she would be 90 years of age, she would no longer be so concerned about her investments. However, at the time of making the initial "gift" and "borrowing" back the amount "gifted", her view was that it was necessary to borrow back in order that she could "have my love of life ...., business". Although her evidence was that she could not bear to part with the money, I accept that her objective was "to make the most of it during my life time.... (so that) I will return them a lot more when I die ... but it will be far greater than if I had just given it to them. I might as well have died myself if I did not have my investments." No monies changed hands. The only record of the "gifts" and the "loans" were the documents signed by the children. She gave notice of the "gifts" to the Commissioner and that was in due course acknowledged. (Calculated at the rate of 10% per annum, the present value at that date of $1,999 payable in the year 2014 did not exceed $45).

  1. Before the next "gift" was due on 2 January 1976 it was suggested to the Applicant that something more should be done in way of effecting the "gift" and that that should be by way of depositing the amount of the "gift" to a bank account for each donee. So it was that on 2nd January 1976, not only did the Applicant have the children execute documents in the same terms as previously, but she also engaged the assistance of her banker. On 29 December 1975 she arranged for accounts to be opened with a savings bank in the names of her children, and deposited 10c to each account. Having opened an account in the name of each child, but reserving to herself complete and exclusive control over the account as sole signatory, on 3 January, 1976 she deposited $1,999 from funds of her own to each account and withdrew it immediately leaving a credit balance in the account of 10c. Whether she did this by using one amount of $1,999 five times was not disclosed, but there was no suggestion that she was not in a position to dispose of $9,995 from cash resources at that time. What is clear is that -when the exercise of that day was completed her cash resources were not in any way diminished. At most she had assumed an obligation to pay to each child $1,999 in the year 2014, having no obligation to pay any interest in the interim. (Calculated at the rate of 10% per annum, the present value at that date of $1,999. payable in the year 2014 did not exceed $54).
  2. On 5 July 1977 the exercise was repeated, the only material difference being that "on" 1st July, 2014 became "by" 1st July 2014. (Calculated at the rate of 10% per annum, the present value at that date of $1,999 payable in the year 2014 did not exceed $60).
  3. Before the next "gift" was due, Parliament repealed the Gift Duty Act (Commonwealth). Fearing that a future Parliament might re-introduce such legislation, the Applicant determined to accelerate the "gifting" program, both as to time scale and as to quantum. On 28 September 1978 she made five "gifts" of $20,003 each, using the same procedure as previously. (Calculated at the rate of 10% per annum, the present value at that date of $20,003 payable in the year 2014 did not exceed $712). The bank accounts were then closed. Before any further "gifts" were made, one son (the fourth child) went into partnership with his father to farm some of the lands previously farmed in partnership by the parents. As a result of that, thereafter the "gifting" was limited to the other four children.
  4. The next "gifts" were made in October 1982. On 19 October 1982 the Applicant opened new savings bank accounts with another Bank, the accounts being entitled "Mrs (Applicant) in trust for (named child)". As before, each account was opened with a deposit of 10c. That was followed that day by a deposit of $36,000 followed immediately by withdrawal of $36,000. Acknowledgments were signed by the donee-children as usual. There was no suggestion on that - day that she did not have $144,000 available to be gifted. (Calculated at the rate of 10% per annum, the present value at that date of $36,000 payable in the year 2014 did not exceed $1,876).
  5. "Gifts" of $10,000 to each of those four children were made on three further occasions: 9 June 1983; 14 September 1983; and March 5 1984 (Calculated at the rate of 10% per annum, the present value at those dates of $10,000 payable in the year 2014 did not exceed $521; $574; and $574 respectively). However, on these occasions there were differences. In each case and in relation to each child $5,000 was deposited and withdrawn and was followed immediately by a second deposit and withdrawal of $5,000. The significance of those matters was not pursued in evidence. Secondly, in relation to the "gifts" of December 1983 and March 1984 the Applicant did not trouble the donees to sign any documents. Instead the Applicant signed a certificate according to the following form:-

"This is to certify that on this day, 14 December 1983, I, (Applicant), have gifted to my daughter, ..., the sum of $10,000. This amount has been loaned back to me, to be repaid by 1st July, in the year Two Thousand and Fourteen".

11. Meanwhile, as the year of income ended 30 June 1983 neared its close, the children had aged by 9 years. Their ages then ranged from 32 years to 23 years. With the exception of the son now working in partnership with his father on the land, they had left home. Further, with the exception of one daughter, they were all heavily indebted for monies borrowed in order to advance their domestic and business interests.

12. At this point the Applicant, motivated by a desire to provide some assistance to her children in their need and influenced by the thought that she had been using what she considered as "their money", and doing so interest free, she decided to make some "interest" payments to them in relation to the monies she had "borrowed" from them. Those "borrowings" and the "interest" payments to be made in that year and in the year following were as follows:

A B C D E Total

Sum of

"borrowings" 92,000 92,000 92,000 26,000 92,000 394,000

1983:

"Interest" 2,000 3,800 2,800 1,276.59 2,000 11,876.59

1984:

"Interest" 3.000 1,200 2,200 1.000 3.000 10,400

5,000 5,000 5,000 2,276.59 5,000 2,276.59

13. In the first year the amounts paid to her son B and her daughter C were greater than the amounts paid to her son A and her daughter E to whom she was equally indebted, and in the first year the amount paid to B was greater than the amount paid to C to whom she was equally indebted. However, reduced payments were made to B and C in the second year, with the result that over the two year terra A, B, C and E each received $5,000. The amounts paid to D were not pro-rata to the payments to others. In the thinking of the Applicant nothing was being paid to compensate the children for her use of "their monies" in "the first nine years" (sic) but only for the subsequent period. Further, no thought was giving to calculating the amounts to be paid to the children by reference to any combination of factors such as amounts "borrowed"; duration of "borrowing"; or interest rates on other investments. The evidence of the Applicant is that in each case the "interest" was paid to, or to the use of, the children, and I accept it. I am satisfied that each child received immediately and fully the benefit of the payments. In relation to the payments so made the claim of the applicant to deductions is wholly in dispute.

14. In relation to the latter year there is a further claim. In her return of income the Applicant claimed a deduction for "periodicals and newspapers necessary for share inform” ($204.27). By her objection the latter claim was detailed as:-

"Rydges" (subscription) $ 10.00

"Herald" (everyday) $ 98.80

"Financial Review" $ 62.40

"Business Review Weekly" (once a month) $ 20.40

"The Bulletin" (once a month) $ 20.40

Total $212.00

In assessing the Applicant the Commissioner had disallowed the claim to the extent of $102. Following the objection he issued an amended assessment so that by the time of hearing all subscriptions claimed had been allowed save that only 50% had been allowed in relation to the Sydney Morning Herald. The Applicant claims a further $42.

The Newspaper Claim

15. The argument of the Applicant was summarised in her notice of objection. She then said "if I were not a share investor, I certainly wouldn't need to buy newspapers as the everyday local and overseas news coverage on television and on radio, is so comprehensive. The type of periodical included in this claim is not for enjoyment, but for study. Therefore these purchases are made for share advice and market trends only. If the above publications are considered to be "private expenditure", then one could argue that shares are a hobby with me, and, as a result, my $13,519.71 for dividends should be non-taxable". I do not need to be persuaded - nor does the Commissioner - that "to the extent to which" the losses and outgoings so incurred they are to be allowed" pursuant to s51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. Insofar as the subscription to the Sydney Morning Herald is concerned, I cannot accept that, even for this taxpayer and her husband and son living with her, there was no use or value to be " had from the Sydney Morning Herald other than the investment information obtained by the Applicant. In my view the 50% already allowed was generous. I would allow no more.

The "Interest" Claim

  1. There was no suggestion in the evidence that the Applicant might not had had financial capacity to make the "gifts" contended for. That being so I accept that there was financial capacity in her to make each of the "gifts" contended for. Further, having regard to the decision I have reached, it is not necessary to consider whether the "gift" was to be regarded as being in amount equal to the related "borrowing"; or whether each transaction should have been regarded as if involving only an assumption by the mother of an obligation to pay a sum in future: an obligation having the effect of diminishing her nett worth by amounts of "present value" such as I have cited. However, I observe that on either view the "plan" of the Applicant was unlikely to have been effective for "estate planning" purposes (cf Muir v Commissioner of Stamp Duties (1963) SR (NSW) 221.)
  2. As to the children, I accept that they assented to their mother's wish that they agree to her proposals that she would "give" to them if they would "lend" back to her the amounts so given interest free until the year 2014. The concept envisaged by the mother did not require that they should do anything more, and there was no reason why they should do either less or more than that. I am not persuaded that the transactions were "shams" and, in consequence, nullities. (cf Jagues v FCT [1923] HCA 70; (1924) 34 CLR 328).
  3. Insofar as amounts were actually paid (as I accept they were), they constituted "losses or outgoings incurred". The fact that the "payment" was made satisfies the first test as to whether a liability was "incurred", which means only that the expense "...defrayed, discharged or borne,...(New Zealand Flax Investments Limited v FCT [1938] HCA 60; (1938) 61 CLR 179 at 207 per Dixon J). The real question is not whether the liability was "incurred" but rather whether, in the sense contemplated by section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act the expenditure was "...incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income...". That requires that the expenditure be incurred "in the course of gaining or producing" the assessable income. (Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavav's) Limited v FCT [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295 at 309 per Dixon J.). As the case for the taxpayer is that she was not in the business of trading in investments - a proposition not disputed by -the Commissioner in these circumstances - it is unnecessary to consider the relevance of the second limb of section 51(1).
  4. Having implemented with her children the several transactions deposed to, the Applicant had diminished her net worth, and augmented the worth of her children as a group, by an amount equal to the present value (calculated at the date of the transaction) of the sums payable in the year 2014 - a sum in all initially of the order of $16,000. As the years passed the value of the entitlement to future payment increased. Those transactions were in all respects complete and entire. I find that there never was a disposition independent of the borrowing or any borrowing independently of the "gift". The only legal obligation remaining was an obligation in the Applicant to pay in the year 2014. In the event of the death of the Applicant before 2014, in absence of any other action by the Applicant, her executors would be entitled to have the benefit of the interest-free loans; to use them to the advantage of the estate in accordance with the provisions of her Will or, if appropriate, as on an intestacy. The transactions gave rise to no other legal or, I suggest, moral obligations. Nor did the use by the Applicant of the "borrowed" monies give rise to any legal or moral obligation in her towards her children. If she wished to further benefit some or all of her children, whether equally or not, that was something she was entitled to do if she wished, whether prompted by a sense of maternal moral obligation arising out of their status as her children or because of the financial difficulties in which they found themselves. But the action of the Applicant in doing as she did was entirely a gratuitous act but that alone is not a reason for denying her the deduction she claims. Voluntary payments made in the course of business and in carrying out the duties of an employment are often deductible. But, what is far more important, is that the expenditure was only related to the derivation of her income in so far as her trading gave her increased capacity to be generous. The expense was in no sense a "loss or outgoing incurred in gaining or producing (her) assessable income..."

20. The Applicant further contends that she is entitled to a deduction in these circumstances on the basis that her action in effecting payment to her creditors was to be equated with the action of an employer in gratuitously effecting payment of a bonus or retiring allowance to employees. In my view the analogy is unsound. An employer qualifies for a deduction for a performance or annual bonus "gratuitously" paid to employees on the basis that it is expended in respect of on-going operations in the course of business, being expenditure which not only rewards the employee for past service but encourages him, and also his fellow employees, to continued reliable service. "Commercial expediency" may found entitlement to a deduction (British Insulated and Helsby Cables Ltd v Atherton (1926) AC 205). But the payment must be "in the course of" earning assessable income. That that is so becomes particularly clear upon considering the subject of "retiring allowances", because a retiring allowance can hardly be said to be related to the prospect of continuing service from its recipient. Any claim to a deduction pursuant to section 51(1) of the Act would have to rest on the basis of a contention that the amount was paid in discharge of an existing obligation to the recipient, or as an expense directed to the continuing service of others as employees, or otherwise to promote the derivation of income (cf. W Nevill & Co Ltd [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 CLR 290). It is no doubt for that reason that specific provision has been made for deductibility of "retiring allowances" by section 78(1)(c) which allows as deductions:

"Sums which are not otherwise allowable deductions and are paid by the taxpayer during the year of income as pensions, gratuities or retiring allowances to persons who are or have been employees or dependents of employees, to the extent to which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, those sums are paid in good faith in consideration of the past services of the employees in any business operations which were carried on by the taxpayer for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income".

21. The Applicant also protests that upholding of the Commissioner's assessment would be to support in effect "double taxation". She says that the effect of the Commissioner's action is to tax her on her investment profits without allowing a deduction for interest paid on the monies necessarily borrowed in order to obtain that income; and that the Commissioner then taxes the children on the portion of those profits distributed to them by way of interest. If the children have been so taxed - and I make no such finding - it is likely that that has occurred only because the children represented to the Commissioner that the amounts they had received from the Applicant constituted "interest" and, therefore, assessable income; and that they did so because of advice given to them by their mother. I have already held that she was incorrect in her appreciation of her own affairs. If in fact she advised her children as I suggest she may have, it would then seem that she was incorrect in advising them as to their affairs too. If such assessments have issued, and if all relevant information has been placed in evidence before me, it would seem to follow that what the children received did not constitute assessable income at all. If that is so then they should seek to protect their interests by making appropriate applications to the Commissioner. Such applications might be by way of objection to any assessment yet to come or in relation to which any assessment might yet be made as of right (section 185); to seek leave to object out of time, if such leave may be given (section 188 - cf. s. 221 Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986); or to request the Commissioner to exercise his power to amend existing assessments to reduce liability, assuming that there is still sufficient time for such an application to be made (section 170(4).

23. I affirm the determinations of the Commissioner upon the objections.
