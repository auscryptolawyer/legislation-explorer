Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. The Commissioner of Taxation (Respondent) amended the income tax assessment of Mrs Gui Feng Cai (the Applicant) for the year ended 30 June 2017 (the 2017 Year) to include additional assessable income of $545,376. The Applicant had originally filed her income tax return for the 2017 Year on the basis that her taxable income was $42,018, based on salary and wages from working in a grocery store.
  2. The Respondent had obtained information from three casinos which suggested that the Applicant had deposited substantial sums in accounts with two of the casinos, and that she had made significant losses gambling at the third casino. The Respondent’s view was that the ability of the Applicant to put several hundreds of thousands of dollars through these casinos was inconsistent with an individual who earned a modest amount of salary and wages. Therefore the Respondent believed that the Applicant had not disclosed all of her income from all sources.
  3. However the Applicant says that the Respondent has double counted or duplicated some of the amounts it has included in her assessable income, and also that her gambling at the casinos was funded by loans from friends rather than from income she had not disclosed in her tax return.

BACKGROUND

Applicant’s personal circumstances

  1. The following factual background is drawn from the Applicant’s witness statement dated 2 December 2024, her Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions (SFIC) dated 21 February 2025, other documents filed with the Tribunal as noted and her oral evidence at the hearing.
  2. The Applicant was born in Shanghai and moved to Australia when she was in her mid-30s. At that time she was married to her ex-husband, Mr X. They divorced in 2024. The Applicant told the Tribunal that her gambling activities, and the financial and legal difficulties that followed, contributed to the divorce. Her English language proficiency, both written and spoken, is limited.
  3. The Applicant said that after arriving in Australia, she and her ex-husband worked in various retail and hospitality jobs until 2008 when they bought a 21.25% interest in an Asian grocery store operating at 200 Victoria Street, Richmond in Victoria.[1] They each drew a salary of approximately $1,000 per week (before tax) from the store but did not receive any dividends as the business required ongoing working capital.[2]
  4. Around February 2009 the Applicant and her former husband bought a residential property in the suburb of Abbotsford (the Abbotsford Property) for which they paid $885,000. A loan from HSBC Bank for $670,000 funded part of the purchase price with the remainder funded from the couple’s savings and gifts from family. The Applicant, her former husband and their daughter lived at the Abbotsford Property until January 2016 when, due to financial difficulties, they rented out the Abbotsford Property. The Applicant initially moved into a property in Docklands owned by her friend, Ms Tong Wu while the Applicant’s husband and daughter moved into a residence above a gelato shop they now operated in Carlton. The Applicant has since moved to an apartment in Melbourne owned by her daughter and son-in-law.[3]
  5. The Applicant said that her only source of assessable income in the 2017 Year was her salary and wages of $42,168 from working in the grocery store.[4] She ceased working at the grocery store in May 2024 and has been working as a shop assistant at a butcher’s shop for the past 4 to 5 years, earning approximately $250 per week after tax.[5]
  6. During the hearing the Applicant told the Tribunal that she also earned rental income during the 2017 Year from the Abbotsford Property which was rented out for $950 per week. The Applicant said she did not know why this was not included in her income tax return for the 2017 Year.[6] The rental income issue is considered further below.

Entities associated with the Applicant

  1. During its audit and review activities the Respondent identified certain entities of which the Applicant had been an officer or shareholder, or businesses with which the Applicant otherwise had an association. Some of these entities or businesses were also referred to by the Applicant in her witness statement and SFIC. During the hearing the Applicant told the Tribunal that she was unsure about some of the details of her relationship with these entities or businesses, or that she did not believe the Respondent’s information was correct. This is discussed further below.
  2. The entities and businesses identified by the Respondent and discussed at the hearing were:
    (a) 200 Victoria Pty Ltd, which operated an Asian grocery store.[7]

    (b) Il Dolce Freddo Enterprise Pty Ltd as trustee for the Jin Yang Family Trust, which operated an ice cream business.

    (c) Richmond Grocery Pty Ltd.

    (d) 118 Lygon Street Pty Ltd as trustee for the 118 Lygon Unit Trust, which operated a fish restaurant business.

Applicant’s gambling activities

  1. It is not disputed that the Applicant gambled at three casinos, being The Star Sydney, The Star Gold Coast and Crown in Melbourne. In addition to information and records obtained by the Respondent from the casinos during its audit,[8] the Tribunal issued summonses to produce documents to each of The Star Sydney and The Star Gold Coast in August 2023.
  2. The Applicant identified two friends with whom she gambled at these casinos, Ms Tong Wu and Ms Mei Zhu. She said that she met Ms Wu in 2007 and met Ms Zhu (through Ms Wu) in around 2014. The Applicant described Ms Wu and Ms Zhu as regular and experienced gamblers. The Applicant said that she only gambled socially and in the company of her friends, and learnt to play Blackjack and Baccarat from Ms Wu and Ms Zhu. Ms Wu and Ms Zhu each provided witness statements in this proceeding and were cross-examined by the Respondent’s counsel at the hearing.
  3. The Applicant referred to particular gambling events or trips she took with her friends during or around the time of the 2017 Year. In December 2016 she gambled at Crown and The Star Sydney with Ms Wu and Ms Zhu. During January 2017 she spent ‘many days’ gambling with Ms Wu and Ms Zhu at Crown. In February 2017 she again went with Ms Wu and Ms Zhu to The Star in Sydney as well as continuing to gamble frequently at Crown (which had a Chinese New Year promotion at the time). In April 2017 she travelled with Ms Zhu to The Star Gold Coast, and also gambled at Crown.
  4. The Applicant said that on some occasions she won but on many other occasions she lost. The amounts of particular winnings or losses that she recalls varied from a couple of thousand dollars to $137,000.
  5. The Applicant described various promotions, offers and programs of the casinos which provided benefits such as free accommodation, flights and meals, bonus or special ‘VIP’ casino chips and cash amounts. Ms Wu and Ms Zhu were members of a program at Crown and Ms Wu was also a member of a program with The Star. The Applicant opened a deposit account with Crown and also signed up for two VIP and premium player programs with The Star.
  6. The Applicant said that when she first started gambling she did not have much money. Some of the casino programs had minimum chip purchase amounts. Ms Wu and Ms Zhu had substantial credit limits at one or more of the casinos and they would draw from their credit to fund gambling by the three of them. She said that the three friends would share their winnings and losses equally, and lend or give chips to each other so that the three could continue playing. Sometimes one ‘would play a hand on the others’ behalf’.[9]
  7. The Applicant said that the three friends did not formally document amounts or chips lent or returned as between themselves, but kept informal records on their mobile phones, for example by using WeChat or other messaging apps on their phones. The Applicant acknowledged that she no longer has these notes or messages as she has changed phones since that time.
  8. The Applicant told the Tribunal that she now regards her gambling as a big mistake and she lost a significant amount of money. She said that after losing money she undertook further gambling in an attempt to win back amounts, and she borrowed money from various people to fund this further gambling. Amongst other things this contributed to her estrangement from her former husband and other family. She said that she ceased to gamble completely around September 2017, however she continues to owe money to friends and associates arising from her gambling losses.

Respondent’s audit and review processes

  1. The Applicant lodged her income tax return for the 2017 Year on 1 August 2018,[10] and the Respondent issued a notice of assessment to her on 8 August 2018,[11] reflecting the information provided by the Applicant in her return.
  2. In late October 2018 the Respondent commenced an audit of the Applicant’s income tax affairs for the 2017 Year and the year ended 30 June 2016.[12]
  3. The Respondent had obtained records from the three casinos which set out:
    (e) A number of deposits said to have been made by the Applicant between December 2016 and April 2017 into accounts she held with The Star Sydney[13] and The Star Gold Coast,[14] and

    (f) Net losses made by the Applicant at Crown Casino in Melbourne in the 2017 Year Year and the previous year.[15]

  4. The Applicant provided further information about her financial circumstances to the Respondent in a ‘Personal living expenses questionnaire’ dated 7 November 2018[16] and attended an interview with officers of the Respondent on 13 November 2018.[17] At this time the Applicant was represented by her accountant and tax agent, Ms Huang.
  5. The Respondent finalised its audit in September 2019 and on 20 November 2022 issued to the Applicant a ‘notice of amended assessment’ for the 2017 Year (the Amended Assessment).[18] The Respondent determined that the amount of income disclosed by the Applicant in her income tax return for the 2017 Year, together with loans she said had been made to her, were not enough to meet her living expenses, gambling activities, home loan interest expense and expenses relating to associated entities. The Respondent included in the assessable income of the Applicant the amount of the deposits into her account with The Star and the amount of her losses at Crown.[19]
  6. The Applicant was also issued with a ‘notice of assessment for a shortfall penalty’ on the same day (the Penalty Assessment).[20] The amount of the shortfall penalty was worked out on the basis that the Applicant’s tax shortfall resulted from ‘intentional disregard’ of a taxation law by the Applicant, and therefore a penalty of 75% of the tax shortfall amount should be imposed.
  7. On 3 August 2022 the Applicant lodged an objection against the Amended Assessment and Penalty Assessment.[21]
  8. In December 2022 the Applicant wrote to the Respondent saying that she had not evaded any tax and that it was wrong for her taxable income to be based on her casino losses. She said that her casino losses were from personal savings and money that she had borrowed money from friends.[22]
  9. By its decision dated 8 September 2023 (the Objection Decision) the Respondent disallowed the Applicant’s Objection against the Amended Assessment. However the Respondent decided that it was appropriate to reduce the amount of the penalty imposed on the Applicant. The Respondent determined that a penalty of 50% of the tax shortfall amount should be imposed on the basis that the Applicant’s tax shortfall arose from ‘recklessness’ by the Applicant as to the operation of a taxation law, rather than a penalty of 75% for ‘intentional disregard.’

RELEVANT LAW AND GUIDANCE

Review of objection decisions relating to assessments

  1. It is not disputed that the Objection Decision is an objection decision and a reviewable objection decision: see sections 14ZL and 14ZQ of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (TAA 1953). The ART Act therefore applies in relation to the Objection Decision: subsection 14ZZA(1).
  2. Where the Tribunal is reviewing an objection decision concerning an assessment, subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(i) of the TAA 1953 says that the Applicant has the burden of proving that the assessment is ‘excessive or otherwise incorrect’ and ‘what the assessment should have been.’
  3. In the present case the Objection Decision concerns the Amended Assessment and the Penalty Assessment. For the Applicant to succeed she must prove, on the ‘balance of probabilities’, that the Amended Assessment and Penalty Assessment are excessive or otherwise incorrect, and what these assessments should have been. It is not sufficient for the Applicant to demonstrate only that the Amended Assessment (and/or Penalty Assessment) are excessive or incorrect. She must also show what the relevant assessment should have been. [31]
  4. The Respondent is not required to prove that the Amended Assessment and Penalty Assessment are correct or were correctly made. The Respondent is entitled to rely on ‘any deficiency in proof of the excessiveness of the amount assessed.’[32]
  5. A taxpayer may be able to prove the relevant matters where they provide ‘acceptable evidence’ and ‘a reasonable explanation’ for those matters, ‘unless some positive reason is shown why [the taxpayer] is to be disbelieved.’[33] However the taxpayer will not satisfy their burden of proof by merely criticising elements of the Respondent’s calculations or facts, or by pointing out that parts of the assessment were incorrect.[34]
  6. The Full Court of the Federal Court made the following observations in relation to a case where the Respondent had assessed ‘unexplained deposits’ as ordinary income and the taxpayer said that the deposits reflected loans and other contributions:[35]

‘... this is not a case in which the transaction giving rise to the [trust’s] entitlement to the Deposits was known. In these circumstances, it was for the taxpayers to disprove the basis of that assessment by establishing the relevant facts. It was not for the Commissioner to posit or prove a basis from which it might be inferred that the Deposits were income but for the taxpayers to place the relevant facts before the Tribunal.’

Assessment of income

  1. Under the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (ITAA 1936) if the Respondent is not satisfied with the tax return lodged by a taxpayer, the Respondent may make an assessment of the amount upon which, in the Respondent’s judgement, tax should be paid. That amount will then be the taxable income of the taxpayer for the purposes of the assessing provisions.[36]
  2. Although the Respondent is not required to prove the validity of the process or basis for reaching its assessment, the Tribunal sets out below provisions of the taxation legislation relating to assessment of income.[37]
  3. There is no dispute that the Applicant is a resident of Australia for taxation purposes. Therefore her assessable income for the 2017 Year includes all ordinary income she derived, directly or indirectly, from all sources during that income year: section 6-5 of the ITAA 1997. Salary and wages, director’s fees and rental income are examples of ordinary income.
  4. A person’s income will also include their statutory income which are amounts that are included in assessable income by provisions of taxation legislation about assessable income: section 6-10. Dividends are an example of statutory income.[38]

Administrative penalty

  1. Division 284 in Schedule 1 to the TAA 1953 sets out circumstances in which administrative penalties may apply to a taxpayer and how the amounts of those penalties are worked out.
  2. A person will be liable for an administrative penalty where they make a statement to the Respondent and that statement is ‘false or misleading in a material particular, whether because of things in it or omitted from it’: subsection 284-75(1). This also applies to a statement made by the person’s agent (for example, their tax agent) as if it had been made by the person: section 284-25.
  3. The person will not be liable to a penalty where:
    (a) The person or their agent took ‘reasonable care’ in connection with the making of the statement;[39] or

    (b) The person engaged a registered tax agent, the person gave their agent all relevant taxation information and the agent made the false or misleading statement. However this exception does not apply where the ‘false or misleading’ nature of the statement arose from ‘intentional disregard’ of a taxation law by the agent or ‘recklessness’ by the agent as to the operation of a taxation law.[40]

  4. Sections 284-85 and 284-90 contain provisions for working out the amount of the penalty to be imposed. A ‘base penalty amount’ is calculated having regard to the circumstances in which the false or misleading statement was made. In circumstances where a tax shortfall amount resulted from ‘recklessness’ by the taxpayer (or agent) as to the operation of a taxation law, the base penalty amount is 50% of the shortfall amount. Where a tax shortfall amount resulted from ‘intentional disregard’ by the taxpayer (or agent) of a taxation law then the base penalty amount is 75% of the shortfall amount.
  5. The Federal Court has stated that:[41]

Recklessness in this context means to include in a tax statement material upon which the Act or regulations are to operate, knowing that there is a real, as opposed to fanciful, risk that the material may be incorrect, or be grossly indifferent as to whether or not the material is true and correct, and that a reasonable person in the position of the statement-maker would see there was a real risk that the Act and regulations may not operate correctly to lead to the assessment of the proper tax payable because of the content of the tax statement. So understood, the proscribed conduct is more than mere negligence and must amount to a gross carelessness.

And:[42]

‘the context makes it clear that recklessness means something more than failure to exercise reasonable care ... but less than an intentional disregard of the Act.’

  1. A person can be ‘reckless’ in the context of these penalty provisions without being dishonest.[43]
  2. The Respondent has published various pieces of guidance[44] which set out the Respondent’s view of how concepts and terminology used in Division 284 such as ‘reasonable care’, ‘recklessness’ and ‘intentional disregard’ should be interpreted. The Tribunal is not bound by this guidance, although the guidance may assist the Tribunal in understanding why the Respondent imposed a penalty, and in some instances the guidance refers to case authority which the Tribunal must or should follow.
  3. Under subsection 298-20(1) the Respondent may remit all or part of a penalty. The TAA 1953 does not set out particular things which the Respondent (or the Tribunal standing in the shoes of the Respondent) must take into account when considering whether to remit a penalty. There are cases previously decided by the courts which suggest that the Tribunal should consider whether it is appropriate in ‘all the circumstances’ to remit all or part of the penalty.[45] The discretion to remit a penalty must also be exercised for a proper purpose, in accordance with the objects of the TAA 1953.[46]
  4. The Respondent has also issued guidance for its staff as to the factors which they must look at when deciding whether to remit all or part of a penalty.[47] The Tribunal is not bound by this guidance but many of the factors set out in it reflect matters considered by courts and tribunals in previous cases.

Findings on gambling activities

  1. The Tribunal makes the following findings in relation to the gambling activities of the Applicant:
    (a) The Tribunal accepts, and it does not appear disputed, that the Applicant gambled frequently at Crown, and also at The Star Sydney and The Star Gold Coast with her friends Ms Wu and Ms Zhu across 2016 and 2017.

    (b) The documentary evidence provided by the casinos, in response to section 353-10 notices issued by the Respondent in 2018, privacy applications made by the Applicant and summonses issued by the Tribunal, show that substantial sums passed through the Applicant’s accounts with those casinos and that she made substantial losses, such amounts being consistent with amounts contended by the Respondent as forming part of the taxable income of the Applicant for the 2017 Year.

    (c) The Tribunal accepts that Ms Wu and Ms Zhu ‘lent’ chips to the Applicant on many occasions around that period and that the Applicant returned some chips or transferred money from her bank account to the bank accounts of Ms Wu and Ms Zhu by way of ‘repayment.’ However there was little evidence indicating the amount of such loans and the dates on which they were made.

The correspondence provided by the casinos contains disclaimers as to the accuracy or completeness of their records.[77] The Tribunal also appreciates that the casino records will only reflect an activity conducted in the name of a particular casino account or membership card, and may not take into account circumstances where one person uses the membership card or account of another person. However the Tribunal rejects the Applicant’s contention that the casino records are entirely or substantially incorrect or misleading. The Applicant did not provide evidence or reasoning for such a claim, other than to state that the casino records do not align with her recollection or views.

    (h) With the exception of the amount of $26,000 borrowed from Ms Zhu, the Tribunal is unable to make any findings, even on the basis of a ‘balance of probabilities’ as to dates on which loans were made by either Ms Wu or Ms Zhu to the Applicant and/or repayments by the Applicant occurred or the amounts involved.[78]

    (e) In relation to the 118 Lygon Unit Trust the Applicant’s witness statement and recollection of the fish restaurant being unprofitable is not entirely consistent with the business making a profit in its first full year of operation (being the 2017 financial year) and sales increasing in that year. The Applicant acknowledged that in addition to her being a director of the corporate entity, she was directly involved with the establishment and operation of the restaurant around this time and therefore the Tribunal would expect her to be able to recall and explain the financial circumstances of the entity around the 2017 Year.

    (f) The Tribunal acknowledges that some of the financial reports for these entities were unsigned. However the Applicant’s signature appears on other documents, such as the contract for the purchase of the ice cream shop business by Il Dolce Freddo Enterprises. Although the Applicant denied knowledge of the activities, particularly financial activities, of these entities or businesses the Tribunal is not convinced that she was completely unaware of or at a distance from these entities or businesses during the 2017 Year and surrounding periods.

  2. Although this proceeding is not about the taxation treatment of these entities, the factual findings above are relevant to the issue of the Applicant’s proximity to these entities and business, whether she might have obtained benefits from those entities or businesses and the reliability of her evidence as it related to her financial affairs in the 2017 Year.

Contentions and conclusions on Amended Assessment

  1. In her SFIC the Applicant said that:
    (a) Her taxable income and therefore her assessment for the 2017 Year should have been $42,018. This amount represents assessable income from her salary or wages from her job at the grocery shop of $42,168 less a deduction of $150 for work related expenses.

    (b) The amounts she deposited into her casino accounts at The Star Sydney and The Star Gold Coast in the 2017 Year, totalling $480,000 were not sourced from assessable income but were funded by loans from friends and acquaintances, from her winnings at Crown casino or represented conversions of one type of casino chip into another (rather than being a deposit of fresh chips or funds).

    (c) Her gambling activities at Crown during the 2017 Year were funded by loans from friends or acquaintances, by way of cash or in the form of chips lent by her friends when they were gambling together.

  2. The Applicant also told the Tribunal that she had not received any payments or other benefits from entities or businesses during the 2017 Year, other than her wages from 200 Victoria Pty Ltd for her work in the grocery store. During the hearing the Applicant told the Tribunal that she also received rental income from the Abbotsford Property during the 2017 Year.
  3. The Respondent viewed this case as turning on whether the Applicant had discharged her burden of proof in relation to the Amended Assessment. The Respondent said that it was for the Applicant to establish any facts she relied upon to prove that the Amended Assessment was excessive or otherwise incorrect, and what it should have been. The Respondent said that it was not required to show that the Amended Assessment was correctly made, however it did draw to the Tribunal’s attention certain matters which it believed contradicted or raised doubts about the Applicant’s arguments and evidence. The Respondent contended that the evidence given by the Applicant was unreliable. On that basis the Respondent said that the Tribunal could not be satisfied that the Applicant had been lent cash or chips or had accessed bank loans or savings as claimed. The Respondent also told the Tribunal that it was possible that the entities or businesses with which the Applicant was associated had provided financial benefits to her, whether as a shareholder, trust beneficiary or otherwise.
  4. The Respondent believed the amount of the Amended Assessment was ‘conservative’ as the Respondent had only looked at the gambling related deposits and losses and had not taken into account amounts which might have been used by the Applicant for personal living and other expenses. The Respondent had also decided not to assess any amounts which might have been sourced in the 2017 Year but which were reflected in further substantial gambling losses by the Applicant in July 2017.
  5. For the purposes of subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(i) the Respondent said that the amount assessed in the Amended Assessment might not be correct, having regard to the potential non-inclusion of rental income, but this was not enough for the Applicant to succeed.
  6. The Tribunal is required under section 55 of the ART Act to give each party a ‘reasonable’ opportunity to present their case, make submissions and adduce evidence, although it is unable to provide legal advice to parties. However, mindful that the Applicant had limited English language skills and is not legally trained, the Tribunal has spent significant time reviewing the large volume of documentary materials provided by her (and her previous representatives) to see what evidence might support her claims and contentions. In relation to the oral evidence, the Tribunal found it problematic that the Applicant, Ms Wu and Ms Zhu suggested during the hearing that they were not aware of some of the contents of their witness statements.[140]
  7. The Tribunal refers to its findings on factual and evidentiary matters in relation to the Applicant’s gambling activities at paragraphs 100 and 101, in relation to entities and businesses associated with the Applicant at paragraphs 128 and 129 and in relation to other transactions involving the Applicant at paragraph 144.
  8. Having regard to these findings the Tribunal cannot find that the Applicant has discharged her burden of proof in relation to the Amended Assessment. The Applicant has not provided adequate evidence or explanation for the Tribunal to be satisfied, on the balance of probabilities, that the Amended Assessment was excessive, and what the amount of the Amended Assessment should have been. In particular the Tribunal cannot discern evidence supporting the Applicant having access to borrowed funds, whether from banks, friends or associates, in the 2017 Year or surrounding years, of an amount that is consistent with the amounts shown to have been gambled in the name of the Applicant at the three casinos in the 2017 Year. The Amended Assessment must therefore stand.

ADMINISTRATIVE PENALTY

  1. During the course of the hearing and at an earlier directions hearing the Tribunal became aware that the Applicant believed she had been accused of fraud or evasion, or some type of criminal activity by the Respondent. In her submissions to the Tribunal she appeared to be very distressed about this. The Tribunal wishes to reassure the Applicant that it did not see any such contentions or allegations in the Objection Decision and did not understand the Respondent to be raising such matters before the Tribunal.[141]
  2. The Tribunal understands that some of the terms used in the taxation legislation such as ‘false or misleading,’ ‘intentional disregard’ and ‘recklessness’ have very negative meanings when used in ordinary conversation. However when they are used in the context of the Australian taxation law they have a specific legal meaning. As noted above, a taxpayer might be regarded as having made a ‘false or misleading statement’ or having been ‘reckless’ but this does not necessarily mean that they have been dishonest or that they have engaged in criminal behaviour. The definition of a false or misleading statement is such that it may simply mean that the taxpayer has not prepared their tax return in the way that the Respondent believes they should have, or that the Respondent disagrees with how they have prepared their tax return.
  3. The Tribunal also observes that the Respondent partly allowed the Applicant’s Objection as it related to the penalty, finding that the Applicant had not demonstrated ‘intentional disregard’ but had been ‘reckless’ such that the amount of the penalty should be reduced from 75% to 50% of the tax shortfall amount. The Respondent is therefore no longer arguing that the Applicant intentionally disregarded a tax law. The Tribunal must consider whether the taxpayer made a false or misleading statement, and if so whether any tax shortfall amount from the statement resulted from the Applicant being ‘reckless’ in relation to the operation of a taxation law.
  4. As set out above, the Applicant also has the burden of proving that the Penalty Assessment was excessive or incorrect, and if so, what it should have been. In relation to a request for remission of any penalty the Applicant must satisfy the Tribunal that remission is appropriate.
  5. The Tribunal has had regard to the oral submissions made by the Applicant at the hearing, including her closing statement. The Tribunal has also had regard to the written materials prepared by her previous legal representatives, as filed with the Tribunal.[142]
  6. The Applicant told the Tribunal that her assessable income for the 2017 Year was as set out in the tax return she filed, and that the Respondent had incorrectly assessed her on the additional amounts. Therefore penalties should not be imposed. She did not have a shortfall amount arising from making a false or misleading statement.[143] In her SFIC the Applicant said that she exercised the reasonable care expected of a person in similar circumstances to her when she made statements to the Respondent, and that she did not behave recklessly, in that her conduct did not fall significantly short of the standard of care expected of a reasonable person in her situation.[144]
  7. The Applicant said that the way the Respondent had relied on the casino records to attribute income to her was wrong. She believes that the Respondent did not consider all of the documents and evidence that she provided carefully, including a large number of documents provided by her previous lawyers, and the Respondent had made errors about the four companies it said she was associated with. She said that she had told the Respondent about rental income from the Abbotsford Property because it was shown in the bank statements she had provided to the Respondent.
  8. She also rejected any suggestion that she had prepared her tax returns herself, telling the Tribunal that an accountant and tax agent had prepared and filed her income tax returns and she had relied on the accountant. She had also spent significant sums on obtaining legal advice in relation to her dispute with the Respondent. Towards the end of the hearing she asked to provide the Tribunal with additional documents, including a copy of an email from her former accountant which enclosed her income tax returns for the 2016 to 2025 years (without the copies of those returns attached), as proof of her having engaged a registered tax agent to prepare her returns. She said that she did not have enough English language skills to lodge her own tax returns.
  9. She also said that where a penalty was imposed, it should be remitted in whole or in part as the imposition of penalties would result in an unjust result.[145] She told the Tribunal that she was only a small shareholder in the companies identified by the Respondent and therefore if she was regarded as having evaded tax, the other larger shareholders would have evaded more tax than her. She referred to her lack of English language skills, as well as having no legal knowledge. She acknowledges that her gambling was a big mistake and said that she now leads a simple life. She said that the stress from her dispute with the Respondent has led to physical and mental health difficulties. She referred to her inability to pay the tax debt arising from her Amended Assessment and Penalty Assessment and believes that the Respondent is wasting public resources by pursuing these tax liabilities. She said that relieving her of these liabilities would be fair and just.
  10. The Respondent said that the Applicant made a false or misleading statement by omitting the amount of $545,367 from her assessable income and only disclosing income of $42,018 for the 2017 Year. The Respondent said that the Applicant was reckless in doing so, in that she disregarded her taxation obligations or was otherwise indifferent or careless.[146] The Respondent also referred to the statements made by the Applicant during the hearing that she had received rental income in relation to the Abbotsford Property during the 2017 Year of $950 per week (or a share thereof), which was also not included in her tax return for that year.
  11. The Respondent also contended that the Applicant had not demonstrated that the imposition of a penalty would produce an unjust result or otherwise pointed to any circumstances which would justify any remission of the penalty the subject of the Penalty Assessment.
  12. For the reasons given above, the Tribunal has found that the Applicant did not prove on the balance of probabilities that the Amended Assessment was incorrect or excessive, and what it should have been. The Respondent acknowledges that, due to the lack of evidence on key matters, such as loans made to the Applicant, the additional amount of $545,367 included in the Applicant’s taxable income for the 2017 Year may not be correct. However the Applicant has not demonstrated with appropriate evidence what the taxable income should be. The Tribunal has accepted the Respondent’s argument in this regard and therefore the Applicant made a false or misleading statement to the Respondent in that she must be treated as having omitted income from her tax return in the 2017 Year. The Applicant’s disagreement with the methodology used by the Respondent in amending her assessment does not alter this conclusion.
  13. There was the further issue raised during the hearing of rental income from the Abbotsford Property. Rental income was not disclosed in the Applicant’s tax return for the 2017 Year. The Applicant referred to having received such income on a monthly basis and this is consistent with amounts deposited in her bank account as disclosed in her bank statements. During her closing statement the Applicant suggested to the Tribunal that the Abbotsford Property was actually in her former husband’s name and the income was reported in his tax return and paid into a joint bank account. Her former husband was not called to give evidence and no evidence had been provided of his tax returns or bank accounts. The Tribunal is unable to make any findings other than to observe that the status of rental income on the Abbotsford Property remained unclear at the conclusion of the hearing. The Respondent did not include undisclosed rental income amounts in its calculation of the amount of administrative penalty in the Penalty Assessment, and therefore even if the Applicant made a false or misleading statement by omitting rental income in the 2017 Year it did not give rise to a (further) tax shortfall amount for the purposes of that Penalty Assessment.
  14. The Applicant did not provide any evidence or explanation of how she might be regarded as having taken reasonable care in relation to the preparation of her income tax return for the 2017 Year. The Tribunal acknowledges the Applicant’s view that the Respondent was wrong to include an additional amount in her assessable income and that she believes the records provided by the casinos are not accurate. These assertions do not demonstrate how the Applicant took reasonable care in relation to her tax returns. The Tribunal also acknowledges that the Applicant provided a significant volume of documents to the Respondent during the audit and objection processes. The Tribunal has made factual findings above in relation to the reliability of some of the documentary evidence provided by the Applicant. Further, this material was collated and provided in response to the Respondent’s audit. The Tribunal is unable to find that the Applicant took reasonable care on the basis of this material.
  15. The Tribunal will accept that the Applicant engaged a tax agent to lodge her tax return or at least assist her with lodgement for the 2017 Year.[147] However the Applicant did not provide any evidence or explanation of having provided all relevant taxation information to her tax agent. For example, when asked whether she had provided information about rental income to her accountant she said that the accountant would have known but she would need to call them to check.[148] It was necessary for the tax agent to be provided with this information by the time of lodging the income tax return for the 2017 Year. The fact that the tax agent may have obtained further information from the Applicant during the audit and objection processes with the Respondent is not sufficient. The exception to making a false or misleading statement where there is reliance on a tax agent therefore does not apply to the Applicant.
  16. The Tribunal has taken similar matters into account in considering whether the tax shortfall amount from the omission of the additional income resulted from recklessness by the Applicant as to the operation of a taxation law. The Applicant has not provided evidence or explanation to the satisfaction of the Tribunal as to why that those amounts should not have been included in her return, and asserts at a general level that the Respondent’s approach is wrong and the records of the casinos are incorrect. In circumstances where she acknowledges that she was gambling significant amounts at casinos in the 2017 Year and she cannot provide satisfactory evidence of the sources of these amounts, the Tribunal believes that a reasonable person in the Applicant’s circumstances should have realised that there was a real risk that taxation consequences would arise or was indifferent as to whether her tax return was true and correct. The Tribunal therefore finds that it was appropriate for the penalty to be determined at the rate of 50% of the shortfall amount on the basis of recklessness.
  17. The Tribunal has considered the difficulties and challenges faced by the Applicant in considering whether there are circumstances which would warrant remission of any of the penalty. The Tribunal accepts that the Applicant has limited English language proficiency, that she suffers from a number of medical conditions, that she has experienced marital breakdown and that she is unlikely to have the money to pay her tax debt in full. The Tribunal also accepts that the Applicant is genuine when she describes her previous gambling as a serious mistake. However the Tribunal does not believe that she has demonstrated circumstances which mean that imposition of the penalty would produce an unjust result or that the penalty should otherwise be remitted. An inability to pay a tax debt is not of itself sufficient to justify remission of the penalty.[149] The Applicant’s health concerns and relationship breakdown appear to have arisen after, and possibly as a consequence of, her gambling and tax debts rather than being a reason for her lacking capacity to comply with her taxation obligations. The Tribunal accepts that the Applicant has not previously been subject to penalties or other sanctions for non-compliance with her taxation obligations, however the Respondent had undertaken audit activities in relation to the previous year and was unable to amend the Applicant’s return for that year as the period of review had passed. The Tribunal does not view the Applicant’s limited English language proficiency of itself as justifying a remission of the penalty in circumstances where she has provided evidence of having engaged a registered tax agent to assist her with her tax lodgements.
  18. On balance the Tribunal therefore has determined that it is not appropriate to remit all or part of the Penalty the subject of the Penalty Assessment.

CONCLUSION

  1. The Tribunal has found that the Applicant has not proven, on the balance of probabilities that:
    (a) The Amended Assessment was excessive and what it should have been.

    (b) The Penalty Assessment was excessive or incorrect, and what it should have been.

  2. The Tribunal has also determined that it will not remit all or part of the Penalty.
  3. The Objection Decision is therefore affirmed.
