ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



INCOME TAX - amended income tax assessment - inclusion of difference in value of trading stock at beginning and end of financial year - business of pearl farming - whether pearl oysters are “live stock” and “trading stock” - statutory interpretation.



Income Tax Assessment 1922-1925, s.17

Income Tax Assessment Bill 1935

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss.6(1), 28, 29, 32, 34, 51(1), (2), 92

Income Tax and Social Services Contribution Assessment Act 1958

Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935 First Schedule Div 1

Taxation Administration Act 1953 ss.14ZU, 14ZW

Perpetual Trustee Co (Canberra) Ltd v Commissioner for ACT Revenue 94 ATC 4403

REASONS FOR DECISION

26 May 1995 Deputy President G L McDonald

Mr T E Barnett (Senior Member)

Associate Professor R D Fayle (Senior Member)



1. The taxpayer company (“the applicant”) is a member of a partnership carrying on a pearling operation business in northern Australian coastal waters. The applicant’s net income, pursuant to section 92 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”) from the partnership for the year of income ended 30 June 1991 was increased by $30,317 which amendment consequently increased its taxable income as previously assessed, by the same amount. An objection, in terms of section 14ZU of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (“the TAA”) was lodged on 1 June 1994 by the applicant against the amended assessment issued on 26 May 1994, pursuant to section 14ZW of the TAA. The sum of $30,317 represents the applicant’s 2.935 per cent interest in the increased partnership net income. The total adjustment to the partnership net income in this respect was $1,032,957, being the difference between what the respondent claims to be the value of trading stock on hand at the beginning of the year of income ($134,118) and that at the end of the year of income ($1,167,075).



2. The applicant was represented by Mr R.K. O’Connor, QC, and the respondent by Mr M. Buss, QC.

3. The matter proceeded on the basis of a statement of agreed facts and documents before the Tribunal under section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. Several other documents to assist the Tribunal were handed up by counsel.



AGREED FACTS



4. The agreed facts set out in the statement of agreed facts include:



(a) The applicant was at all material times a partner in the partnership known as “Pearlers” (“the partnership”) with its interest in the net income of the partnership for the year ended 30 June 1991 being 2.935 per cent.



(b) The partnership carries on pearl farming at (a specified location).



(c) Pearl farmers are licensed to carry on operations pursuant to the Pearling Act. Pearling licences, hatchery licences, pearl diver licences, pearl boat licences and pearl boat master’s licences are all granted under provisions of that Act.



(d) The pearl oyster farmed by the partnership is the “Pinctada Maxima”. They are farmed for the purpose of growing cultured pearls for sale and also for selling their meat and their shells.



The Tribunal notes in respect of the previous agreed fact that it requires further explanation and it was the subject of critical focus in the Tribunal’s reasons.



(e) An oyster is a sea animal with a soft body inside a hard two piece shell. It is a mollusc of the subgroup known as bivalves. The oyster itself has:



(i) gills which it uses to breathe and capture food;



(ii) a heart which is responsible for pumping blood throughout the animal’s body;



(iii) a digestive system;



(iv) the ability to respond to changes in its surroundings; and



(v) the ability to reproduce.



(f) The oysters farmed by the partnership exist in a free-ranging state on a sandy seabed in depths of 20-30 metres. After they reach their minimum legal size of 120mm they are collected by divers to be used in the farming operations. The divers are towed behind special catching vessels and pick up the oysters from the seabed.



It was later explained during submissions that the oysters are initially gathered by a contractor, from outside the partnership’s sea bed leases and sold to the partnership.



(g) After cleaning and grading, the oysters are stored in net panels in waters specified by the pearling licence. They are left there until operating time, between June and September each year, when the shells are recovered and delivered to the operating vessel.



(h) A technician then conducts the seeding operation. This involves placing into the oyster a nucleus which forms the basis for the development of a pearl sac which secretes the nacre onto the nucleus to form a pearl. [This is referred to later as seeding.]



(i) After the operation, the oyster is placed back into a net panel and returned to the seabed lease for post-operative handling. Following a rest period of between 7 and 10 days a turning programme is commenced. New panels of oysters are turned over every few days. The period of turning may extend to two months or more after seeding. The oysters are then left on the sea floor until late October or early November when they are recovered and transported to the farm sites for the remainder of the culture period.



(j) After two years the oysters are removed from the sea and taken to the operation vessel for harvesting. After removal of the pearl the oyster is often re-seeded. An oyster may be operated on two or three times.



(k) When no longer useful for growing round pearls, the oyster is used to grow half pearls. To harvest the half pearls it involves the oyster shell being cut to remove them. Half pearls can be sold on the half pearl shell or separated from the shell. It is at the harvest stage that the oyster may be killed with the related by-products, being the oyster meat and the mother of pearl [“MOP”] being sold.



The Tribunal was told that there is no dispute as to the valuation of the oysters for the purpose of Part III, Subdivision B of the Act.



5. There is no dispute that the disclosure annexed to the statement of agreed facts sets out the revenue from sales by the partnership for the two years of income ended 30 June 1990 and 1991, (that is, the preceding year of income and the year of income in question), as follows:

At the request of the Tribunal the applicant provided further information about the operations and the age distribution of shells on hand at 30 June 1991, none of which was challenged by the respondent. That information is that the operations of the partnership commenced in 1979 and that the first pearls were produced in 1984. As at 30 June 1991 the age distribution of live shells on hand was:

Further, it was submitted by the applicant that the amount of MOP and oyster meat produced is in inverse proportion to the quality of pearl shell husbandry.



THE ISSUES



6. The two issues for determination are:



Whether the Pinctada maxima oysters on hand as at 30 June 1991 (and therefore inferentially as at 1 July 1990) are:



(a) “trading stock” other than “live stock”, according to the definition of that term in subsection 6(1) of the Act; and if not,



(b) whether the oysters are “live stock” and therefore “trading stock” according to the definition of the latter term in subsection 6(1) of the Act?



THE LEGISLATION



7. The relevant section 6(1) definitions are:



“ 6(1) In this Act, unless the contrary intention appears -



‘trading stock’ includes anything produced, manufactured, acquired or purchased for purposes of manufacture, sale or exchange, and also includes live stock;



‘live stock’ does not include animals used as beasts of burden or working beasts in a business other than a business of primary production;”



ARE THE PINCTADA MAXIMA “TRADING STOCK” OTHER THAN LIVE STOCK?



8. The definition of “trading stock” is not exhaustive; cf FCT v St Hubert’s Island Pty Ltd (1978) 138 CLR 210; 78 ATC 4104. Even though something may not fall directly within the inclusions listed it may nevertheless be trading stock for the purposes of the Act if it is trading stock, (or, in contemporary language, inventory) according to ordinary parlance. Stephen J observed in St Hubert’s Island, that the use of the term “anything” enlarges the ordinary meaning of the term by including “things acquired not only for sale but also for purposes of manufacture” and without that expansion would not extend to include raw materials acquired for the purpose of manufacture (see CLR p.216).



9. Mr O’Connor submitted that Pinctada oysters, (Pinctada maxima, Pinctada margaritifera and the Pinctada carchariarum) are used in the pearling industry and are of a different genus to the Ostreidae oysters used in the oyster farming industry, referring to the Microsoft (R) Encarta which states:



“Scientific classification: Oysters make up the family of Ostreidae of the order Ostreoida. Pearl oysters belong to the family Pteriidae of the order Arcidae.“



And in the World Book Encyclopedia, World Book Inc it states in relation to oysters:



“Scientific classification: Oysters used for food make up the oyster family, Ostreidae. . . . The pearl oyster belongs to the pearl oyster family, Pietidae. It is Pinctada margaritfera.”



The pearl oysters used by the partnership are Pinctada maxima which grow to about 30 centimetres in diameter and weigh approximately 5.5 kilograms; (Farn A.E., Pearls: Natural, Cultured and Imitation, Butterworths Gem Books series, Sydney, p.41).



10. The sub-section 6(1) definition of “trading stock” provides that the item be either trading stock according to ordinary parlance or be included specifically as provided. It is clear that the partnership’s oysters are not themselves trading stock according to ordinary parlance as they are not sold. They are purchased for the purpose of culturing pearls. The product of the culturing operations being pearls, are sold. The sale of the MOP and oyster meat is comparatively insignificant and cannot be seen as a purpose of acquiring the oysters in the first place. To this extent, ground 4 of the agreed facts is misleading. The Tribunal must be satisfied that the facts as agreed are adequate Perpetual Trustee Co (Canberra) Ltd v Commissioner for ACT Revenue 94 ATC 4403 per Wilcox J at 4414. In this instance, the Tribunal is satisfied that the oysters are acquired to facilitate the pearl culture, or pearl production, and any other revenue obtained as a result of the sale of the by-products of the oysters once they have ceased to be capable of culturing is insignificant and incidental.



11. Mr Buss submitted an alternative argument that if the “Pinctada Maxima” oysters were not acquired for the purposes of sale, then they could be considered to have been acquired for the purposes of manufacture. Whilst accepting that the word “manufacture” must be given its natural and ordinary meaning, Mr Buss went on to submit that it “describes a process and not the means by which the process is affected”. In manufacture, those two things are the same: the manufacturing process is the means by which raw, or semi-processed, materials are transformed into products of a different kind. Usually, that process is carried out either by skilled or unskilled (manual) labour or by machinery, or a combination of both. Here, while the seeding of the oyster could be described as being carried out by skilled labour, that process and the process by which the pearl subsequent develops is not readily understood as amounting to manufacture.



12. For these reasons, the oysters are not trading stock unless they can be properly classed as “live stock” in terms of that definition in section 6(1) of the Act.



ARE THE OYSTERS “LIVE STOCK”?



13. The definition of “live stock” in section 6(1) of the Act does not expand on what is included within its bounds, but rather specifies that beasts of burden or working beasts would only be live stock if used in a business of primary production. What can be gleaned from the definition is that animals are capable of being live stock but with those exclusions. According to the World Book Encyclopedia, “young oysters are about as big as a needle point and swim in the sea”. Further, in the same publication it states that “oysters live on the sea bottom”. These attributes class them as within the genus of “animals” as distinct from minerals or vegetables.



14. But, Mr O’Connor submits, not all animals are intended to be “live stock” for the purpose of the Act. In support he argues that the amendment to include “live stock” within the definition of “trading stock” arose from the Income Tax Assessment Bill 1935. The explanatory memoranda to the Bill said, in this regard:



“By reason of the proposal to tax all sales of live stock, including the sale of breeding stock whether or not sold in a walk-in walk-out sale, it is necessary to extend the definition of trading stock to include live stock.” (Taxation Laws of Australia, Butterworths, p.255)



The amendment was to overcome the anomaly in the predecessor legislation, the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922-1925, (which was then being replaced by the 1935 Bill), as highlighted in Robinson v FCT [1927] HCA 8; (1927) 39 CLR 297. That case concerned the assessment of that part of the sale proceeds from a walk-in walk-out sale of a wool producing sheep station, so far as it was apportioned to the value of a number of breeding weaners. The appellant claimed that the amount was not assessable income under section 17 of that Act. The amendment resolved the problem by deeming breeding stock “live stock” as defined and hence trading stock, even though not held for sale or exchange in business operations.



15. Mr O’Connor submits that to discover the extent to which animals fall within the definition of “live stock” one needs to refer to the ordinary meaning of the terms which infer that only animals in a business of primary production are live stock, a conclusion reached by Dixon and Fullagar JJ in FCT v Wade [1951] HCA 66; (1951) 84 CLR 105. That case concerned the assessability of compensation received when the taxpayer’s diseased dairy cattle were compulsorily destroyed. The relevant issue of that case was whether the dairy cattle were “live stock” under the Act. Their Honours said (at p.110):



“The definition of ‘trading stock’ . . . brings ‘live stock’ within s36(1). There is a definition of live stock which, by inference, makes it clear that all animals are to be included in the case of a business of primary production.”



Primary production is defined in section 6(1) of the Act as:



“[In this Act, unless the contrary intention appears] ‘Primary production’ means production resulting directly from -



(a) the cultivation of land;



(b) the maintenance of animals or poultry for the purpose of selling them or their bodily product, including natural increase;



(c) fishing operations;



(d) forest operations; or



(e) horticulture;



and includes the manufacture of dairy produce by the person who produced the raw material used in that manufacture.”



16. Mr O’Connor submits that the Act was amended in 1958 to introduce, in effect, paragraph (c) to include fishing operations since fish would not otherwise have been construed to have been within the concept of “animals” as that word is used in the definition of “live stock”. In support he submits that in construing that word it is necessary to apply the maxim of statutory interpretation known as noscitur a sociis, that the meaning of a doubtful word may be ascertained by reference to the meaning of words associated with it; [Mozley & Whiteley’s Law Dictionary, John B. Saunders, Butterworths, 9th ed,1977]; or: “a maxim in statutory interpretation to the effect that the meaning of an unclear expression can be gathered from the context. [L: it is known from its companions]”; [The CCH Macquarie Concise Dictionary or Modern Law].



17. Mr O’Connor pointed to the use of the distinguishing word “poultry” in sub-paragraph (b) of the definition of “primary production”, submitting that it was necessary to ensure that poultry would be included since the word “animals” in that context is associated with customary farm animals such as sheep, cattle and pigs[1]. For this reason, he submitted, the contextual meaning of the word “animals” is delimited and would not extend to animals generally.



18. The 1958 Bill amended the Act, then titled Income Tax and Social Services Contribution Assessment Act 1958, to include definitions of “fishing operations” and consequently of “pearling operations” which was within the scope of “fishing operations” as defined. The accompanying explanatory notes stated that a principal purpose of the Bill was to extend the provisions dealing with averaging of income to individual taxpayers carrying on a business of fishing or pearling. Averaging provisions of the Act extended concessional tax rates to primary producers and the Bill ensured that those be availed of by pearl producers. The Bill did not address the question of whether the oysters of a cultured pearl operation were animals or indeed live stock.



19. The Tribunal was referred to the decision in a sales tax matter, DFCT (NSW) v Zest Manufacturing Company Pty Ltd [1949] HCA 61; (1949) 79 CLR 166. In that case, the defendant manufactured fish food for sale to government and other commercial breeders of trout, perch, carp, gold fish and other ornamental fish. It claimed that the sales were exempt from sales tax because the goods were food for live stock, exempted under a specific provision of the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935.



20. Under that Act “livestock” was defined as “domestic animals generally; animals of any kind kept or dealt in for use or profit”. Latham CJ (dissenting) said in relation to the submission that whether it would not have occurred to the legislators at the time that fish were to be construed within the definition of livestock, is irrelevant. His Honour said, by way of obiter dicta:



“The question is not what the Court thinks Parliament thought it was doing when it passed a bill. The mind of Parliament is to be ascertained by construing the words which Parliament has placed in the statute. The question is simply what the words contained in the Act mean. The Court should interpret the words of a statute as it finds them, paying attention to any context which shows that they were used in a particular sense of subject to a particular limitation.”



Each of the majority judgments of Rich, Dixon, McTiernan, Williams and Webb JJ decided that fish were not to be construed as livestock under the legislation. All employed a similar synthesis. Rich J said that no assistance can be gathered from dictionary definitions and it is necessary to determine the meaning of the word (“livestock”, one word in that legislation) by reference to the sense in which it is used in the statute. His Honour concluded that because there was another specific provision in the statute dealing with fish they were outside the intended class for the particular item in question. In Dixon J’s opinion the context was opposed to assigning to the word “livestock” a meaning wide enough to include “fish”. His Honour makes specific reference to the context where the exemption appears. That exemption was found in clause 6 of Division 1 of the First Schedule which read:



“Goods (and parts therefor) for use in the maintenance of livestock, viz. : - (1) Bullnose punches (2) Dips and washes for cattle or sheep (3) Drenching guns and syringes (4) Foods for livestock (5) Lamb-marking cradles (6) Marking and branding oils (7) Preparations for use in the prevention, cure or eradication of diseases or pests in livestock (8) Rock salt and licks for livestock (9) Rugs for horses, cattle, sheep and pigs ( 10) Sheep and stock feeders for use in agricultural industry (11) Sheep jetting plant (12) Tar brands, fire brands, tattoo brands, ear pliers, ear tags and ear markers, for marking or branding livestock (13) Veterinary instruments, appliances and materials of a kind ordinarily used by veterinary surgeons.”



21. McTiernan J relied on a similar analysis of the exempting item and that poultry and bees, also animals, were specifically dealt with under a separate and distinct item in the legislation. Therefore, His Honour concluded that by parity of reasoning fish would be excluded from the term livestock as used in the relevant item. Williams and Webb JJ each confined their reasons to the limitations placed on the construction of the term in the context of the legislation and the particular item quoted above. The majority, for these not dissimilar reasons found that in the context of the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935 livestock did not include fish.



22. The definition of “live stock” in section 6(1) of the Act is not in the same constrained setting as was the similar word “livestock” in the legislation under review in Zest. For this reason the Tribunal is not bound by the decision in that case. Because “live stock” as defined in section 6(1) of the Act, by inference, includes animals used in the business of primary production[2], animals used in the pearling industry would be within the definition. This would hold unless, in the context of the legislation it would be doubted or unclear that “animals” include live oysters used in the cultured pearling business of the partnership. The Tribunal, in noting Mr O’Connor’s point about the inclusion of “poultry” with “animals” in sub-paragraph (b), finds that does not necessarily delimit the definition of “live stock”, nor the scope of “fishing operations” which are included as “primary production”, but rather serves only to expand that particular subparagraph of the definition of “primary production”.



23. Having regard to what Latham CJ said in Zest and mentioned above, it is relevant in this matter, where a dictionary definition is unhelpful, to consider the overall context of the Act. In the context of calculating the taxable income of a business, such as a pearling operation, the Act allows a deduction under section 51(1) for outgoings incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of deriving assessable income, providing it is not an outgoing of capital or of a capital, private or domestic nature. If the outgoing is incurred in the purchase of stock used by the taxpayer as trading stock it is deemed, by section 51(2), not to be an outgoing of a capital nature. By a combined operation of sections 28, 29, 32 and 34 the difference in the value of trading stock, being live stock, on hand at the beginning and end of a year of income is taken into the taxable income calculation as either assessable income or an allowable deduction, depending on whether the closing stock value exceeds the opening stock value or vice versa; Part III, Division 2, Subdiv B - Trading Stock.



24. The relevant context of the Act is therefore to ensure that where an item of expenditure in a business setting achieves the acquisition of trading stock that expenditure is to be allowed as a deduction despite the possible conclusion that it may be of a capital nature. Should that item remain unsold at the end of the year then the deduction is effectively negated by the trading stock adjustment of section 28. Similarly, should the item still be on hand at the end of a following year by taking its value at the beginning and end of the year of income its value is not taken in as either assessable income or an allowable deduction. There are variations on this theme depending on the elected values chosen for trading stock at the end of the year, but those are not necessarily relevant to live stock.



25. The partnership’s purchase of oysters provides it with the means by which it can culture the pearls, a process running over more than one year of income. In the context of the legislation it is appropriate to treat the live oysters as trading stock to ascertain the true income of the partnership. In C of T (SA) v Executor Trustee & Agency Company of South Australia Limited [Carden’s case] [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108; 5 ATD 98, Dixon J said, in the context of discussing the framework of the Act to measure assessable income, that:



“Speaking generally in the assessment of income the object is to discover what gains have during the period of account come home to the taxpayer in a realised or immediately realisable form. . . . The computation of profits from manufacture and trading has always proceeded upon the principle that the profit may be contained in stock in trade and ‘outstandings’. Whether this is to be explained on some view that the purpose is to ascertain what is the detachable increase in circulating capital, or more simply on the ground of common sense and the teachings of experience, the result for the purposes of taxation is the same. The result is that a tax upon the profits or income of such a business must be understood as a tax upon the profit or income computed according to the system, because according to common understanding and commercial principles, that is the method of determining profits. The basis of a trading account is stock on hand at the beginning and end of the period and sales and purchases.” (CLR p.155; ATD p.132)



26. The Tribunal, whilst observing that it would run counter to commercial common sense not to bring to account the value of the live oysters on hand at the end of the year of income of the partnership whilst allowing a deduction for the cost of acquiring them, is mindful that the authority for any such adjustment must be found squarely in the legislation.



  1. The Tribunal finds that in the context of the Act, the partnership is carrying on a business of primary production, namely pearling operations. Pearls are cultured by a natural process assisted by a specific manual and technical operation to seed the live oyster. The live oyster is an animal. It is logically analogous that the live oyster is live stock in the same sense that dairy cattle would be live stock. That is, although the live oysters are not held for breeding or sale they are held for the purpose of producing a natural product under proper conditions of management, nurture and husbandry. The section 6(1) definition of “live stock” whilst couched negatively, can appropriately be construed in the context of the legislative provisions to which it relates, and in particular to cultured pearling operations, so as to extend the notion of “animals” to live oysters, without straining the ordinary meaning of “animals” in context.

28. For the above reasons, the decision under review is affirmed.
