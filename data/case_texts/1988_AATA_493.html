ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - undisclosed income - whether interest received by husband and wife from a finance company was derived by a trust for their children - no written trust document - failure to establish element of intention to create a trust -.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss 25(1), 99A, 102(1)(b), 226(2)

Swiss Bank Corporation v Lloyds Bank Limited (1980) 3 WLR 457

REASONS FOR DECISION

9 May 1988 Mr B J McMahon (Senior Member)

1. These are two applications heard jointly to review amended assessments of income tax against a husband and wife for the years ended 30 June 1982 and 1983. The amended assessments showed as additional income amounts of interest received by each of the husband and wife from a finance company in each of the years under review. Because of non-disclosure of these amounts, a penalty was added to the additional tax, pursuant to the provisions of s 226(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act"). The applicants submitted that the interest was not derived by them as income within the meaning of s 25(1) of the Act, but was derived by a trust for their children. They therefore submitted that the amended assessment should not have brought the additional interest into account as part of their income, and that no additional tax, by way of penalty, should have been imposed. Evidence was given by the husband intended to establish that a trust existed. There was no written trust document nor were any accounting records kept of the alleged trust. The husband's evidence was given honestly, as I believe, but in a quite confused manner when it came to details. I have found the following facts as a result of my understanding of his evidence and as a result of cross-examination by the Commissioner's representative supported by documentation obtained by the Commissioner.

  1. The husband and wife have 5 children. In 1982 the ages of the 4 daughters were 17, 16, 13 and 7 respectively, and the age of their only son was 12. Then, and now, all the children lived with their parents in one household, and all were and are unmarried.
  2. The husband gave evidence that the family had received child endowment regularly since the birth of their first child (although it is a notorious fact that child endowment was not payable in respect of the first child when she was born in 1965). This is an example of the husband's hazy recollection of details.
  3. In 1973 or 1974 the husband and wife, together with a cousin of the husband and his wife, purchased a fruit shop. The husband gave evidence that the child endowment, which had been set aside for some years prior to that date, was used by him in this purchase. The business lasted for 5 or 6 years and ended in disagreements between the 2 families. The husband gave evidence that he did not sell out. Rather, he walked out, taking the $7000 or $8000 which he had put in and no more. This amount was mixed with moneys standing to the credit of either the husband or the husband and wife with the Commonwealth Bank. He bought a car from the total and "put the balance back into the children's account". He said in evidence that he felt he had a moral obligation and that this money was theirs and not his.
  4. Nevertheless, it appears from cross-examination that the first use of this money was the purchase of an unsecured note from the finance company on 24 June 1981. It was purchased in the name of the husband and the wife. The amount was for $6000 and the term was for one year. When that expired, a second note for the same amount in the same names was purchased on 24 June 1982 for a period of 6 months.
  5. On 24 December 1982, another unsecured note was purchased, but this time the proprietors were shown as the 5 children who were named and there referred to as children of the wife. On 17 March 1983, another note was purchased showing the proprietors in a similar manner but, on this occasion, stating only the names of the 3 of the daughters. On 30 July 1982, another note was purchased showing the proprietors as the son and the youngest daughter, who were described as children of the husband. Again, on 17 September 1982, a note was purchased in the name of the second eldest daughter and the youngest daughter, both being described as daughters of the wife. The husband could give no explanation as to why these variations occurred. On one occasion he said that he had taken advice from an accountant and had simply carried out that advice. On another occasion he said that the finance company must have made a mistake. He confirmed that no moneys from his wife were contributed to the purchase price of the notes.

7. On the face of these documents, there is no indication of a trust until 24 December 1982. The husband said in evidence that he did not know when he decided to start a trust. He said that he had no other investments in the joint names of himself and his wife, although he had previously been in partnership with "his wife building home units before they went into the fruit shop business.

  1. Interest from the unsecured notes was paid to an account with a building society. The husband gave evidence that it was paid into one of three accounts, being an account in the names of himself and his wife. In the years in question, there were 3 cheques drawn on the account in favour of the finance company, evidently in payment for further unsecured notes. In addition to these 3 cheque withdrawals, there were a number of cash withdrawals of amounts ranging from $50 to $500. The husband could not account for each withdrawal but thought that probably they were for clothes and food for the family. Food for the children was not kept in a separate stock from that of the family as a whole.
  2. Some time later, (the exact date was not established), an account was opened with ANZ Savings Bank Ltd in the name of the wife in trust for 3 of the daughters. The husband could not explain why only 3 of the 5 children were mentioned in the title of the account. From the time of establishment of this account interest from the finance company was paid into that fund. The signatory on that account was the wife alone.
  3. The husband gave evidence that, from time to time, moneys from one or other of these savings accounts was also used to pay for ' food and clothes for the family. In addition, specific sums were drawn on behalf of the husband or the wife or both. For example, moneys were used towards payment of their income tax and in payment of water rates. Specific sums were disbursed for the purposes of specific children on other occasions. For example, an amount was paid to help the eldest daughter buy a car and a fishing boat was bought for the son. The husband said that whatever moneys he drew out for his own purposes, he repaid 3 times over.
  4. He gave evidence that in his view the children were to benefit equally, none being favoured above the other. However, he could not account for the fact that some of the unsecured notes and some of the relevant savings accounts were in some rather than all the names of the children. In the absence of any books of account, or other records, he was also unable to demonstrate any balancing payments to children who were not favoured by specific advances.
  5. In fact it seems to me that the funds have been used quite indiscriminately for the benefit of the family, including the husband and wife. As the husband said in evidence "there is no difference between myself, my missus and my children. We all live together as a family". Evidence was given by the husband's parish priest. Although he could say nothing about the specific financial affairs of the husband, he confirmed that the husband had, on occasions, said to him " I have something for my kids". The priest also gave evidence that the husband and wife were "very honest people" and that the husband was a "good man". I do not doubt any of these encomiums. However, the objective facts do not, in my view, indicate more than a general intention to set aside something for the children in the future, in an approximate way, when the opportunity offered itself, and when other pressing demands did not require other use of the money.
  6. The applicant husband left school at the age of 16 in Lebanon. Although he gave evidence that his English was not very good when he came to this country, it was certainly clear enough to make his evidence understood on the hearing of this application. I am also mindful of the fact that the husband has apparently had wide business experience since he came to this country. I do not consider therefore that any particular regard should be paid to the argument that his "simple" origins and education did not enable him to understand the legal requirements of a trust. Indeed, what taxpayer other than one with legal or accounting training does understand such a concept?
  7. However, as Swiss Bank Corporation v Lloyds Bank Limited (1980) 3 WLR 457 at 467 illustrates, it is not necessary to the creation of trusts that their creators should appreciate hat their acts had the legal consequences inherent in a trust. In this context, the creators are taken to intend, not so much the natural and probable consequences of their acts, as the legal consequences that would be apparent to one acquainted with the legal concepts.
  8. On the facts as I have found them the taxpayers have failed to establish a fixed trust for all 5 children in equal shares. Indeed, in my view, they have failed to establish a trust at all. The necessary element of intention, illustrated by objective facts, that is necessary to the creation of a trust has not, in my view, been established. All that has been shown is a, no doubt praiseworthy, course of conduct by a husband in putting aside moneys for his family where he can, and where there are no other immediate competing needs. The evidence, in my view, does not show that the children had acquired a vested interest in the subject matter of the alleged trust. That being so the income derived from the investments remains the income of the husband and wife, and should be taxed as such.
  9. Even if a trust had been demonstrated, this would, in my opinion, have been a proper case for the Commissioner to assess tax under s 102(1) (b), having regard to the loose and rather indiscriminate way in which the assets have been invested and the income used. If there were not a fixed trust in favour of the 5 children equally, but a discretionary trust, then the income ought to have been taxed under s 99A of the Act. In my view, however, it is not necessary to resort to either of these sections. On the facts as put before me there is no trust, in the legal sense, and accordingly the income is properly assessed to tax in the hands of the husband and wife.

17. The amended assessments are therefore affirmed.
