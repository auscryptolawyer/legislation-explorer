Date of judgment:

Catchwords:
PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE – ex parte applications for freezing orders – whereas the applicant seeks freezing and ancillary orders be made in respect of the respondents in each of these proceedings – whereas the Court’s discretion to make such orders is enlivened pursuant to rr 7.32-35 of the Federal Court Rules 2011 (Cth) – whereas prospective claims for judgment against the respondents are justiciable in this Court – whereas a risk of dissipation of assets exists referable to the respondents – balance of convenience weighs in favour of the making of freezing and ancillary orders – orders made in the form sought



PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE - application for orders allowing substituted service of originating and interlocutory applications pursuant to r 10.24 of the Federal Court Rules 2011 (Cth) – whereas ordinary service on the respondents is impracticable owing to the risk of dissipation of assets – whereas the freezing and ancillary orders are considered urgent – noting the ex parte nature of these applications – whereas proposed methods of substituted service likely to bring the documents to the respondents’ attention – orders made in the form sought

RELEVANT LEGAL PRINCIPLES

27 Many of the legal principles arising in the present proceedings were recently summarised by me in Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Raptis [2021] FCA 1192. It is convenient to repeat them:

46 The Court is empowered to make freezing orders by r 7.32 of the Federal Court Rules, which provides as follows:
7.32 Freezing order

(1) The Court may make an order (a freezing order), with or without notice to a respondent, for the purpose of preventing the frustration or inhibition of the Court’s process by seeking to meet a danger that a judgment or prospective judgment of the Court will be wholly or partly unsatisfied.

(2) A freezing order may be an order restraining a respondent from removing any assets located in or outside Australia or from disposing of, dealing with, or diminishing the value of, those assets.

Note: Without notice is defined in the Dictionary.
47 Orders ancillary to freezing orders are the subject of r 7.33, which provides:
7.33 Ancillary order

(1) The Court may make an order (an ancillary order) ancillary to a freezing order or prospective freezing order as the Court considers appropriate.

(2) Without limiting the generality of subrule (1), an ancillary order may be made for either or both of the following purposes:
(a) eliciting information relating to assets relevant to the freezing order or prospective freezing order;

(b) determining whether the freezing order should be made.
48 Rule 7.34 extends the powers of the Court in rules 7.32 and 7.33 to persons who are not otherwise parties to the substantive proceedings, namely third parties. For completeness I note that r 7.34 provides:
7.34 Order may be against person not a party to proceeding

The Court may make a freezing order or an ancillary order against a person even if the person is not a party in a proceeding in which substantive relief is sought against the respondent.
49 Rule 7.35 anticipates reezing orders against a judgment debtor, or prospective judgment debtor, or a third party. In particular r 7.35 (1)(b) states that:
(1) This rule applies if:
(a) ...

(b) an applicant has a good arguable case on an accrued or prospective cause of action that is justiciable in:
(i) the Court; or

(ii) ...
50 Further factors guiding the exercise of the Court’s discretion are set out in r 7.35 (4)-(6) as follows:
(4) The Court may make a freezing order or an ancillary order or both against a judgment debtor or prospective judgment debtor if the Court is satisfied, having regard to all the circumstances, that there is a danger that a judgment or prospective judgment will be wholly or partly unsatisfied because any of the following might occur:
(a) the judgment debtor, prospective judgment debtor or another person absconds;

(b) the assets of the judgment debtor, prospective judgment debtor or another person are:
(i) removed from Australia or from a place inside or outside Australia; or

(ii) disposed of, dealt with or diminished in value.


(5) The Court may make a freezing order or an ancillary order or both against a person other than a judgment debtor or prospective judgment debtor (a third party) if the Court is satisfied, having regard to all the circumstances, that:
(a) there is a danger that a judgment or prospective judgment will be wholly or partly unsatisfied because:
(i) the third party holds or is using, or has exercised or is exercising, a power of disposition over assets (including claims and expectancies) of the judgment debtor or prospective judgment debtor; or

(ii) the third party is in possession of, or in a position of control or influence concerning, assets (including claims and expectancies) of the judgment debtor or prospective judgment debtor; or
(b) a process in the Court is or may ultimately be available to the applicant as a result of a judgment or prospective judgment, under which process the third party may be obliged to disgorge assets or contribute toward satisfying the judgment or prospective judgment.


(6) Nothing in this rule affects the power of the Court to make a freezing order or ancillary order if the Court considers it is in the interests of justice to do so.
51 Having regard to these rules I note the following.

52 First, Freezing Orders Practice Note (GPN-FRZG) issued by the Chief Justice on 25 October 2016 applies to any proceeding involving an application in this Court for a freezing order, and to that extent supplements the provisions of the Federal Court Rules relating to the making of such orders.

53 Second, the DCT seeks interim orders ex parte. I note however that r 7.32 (1) empowers the Court to make a freezing order with or without notice to a respondent.

54 In relation to the making of orders ex parte, I note the following observation of Gageler J in Aristocrat Technologies Australia Pty Ltd v Allam [2016] HCA 3:
15. It is an elementary principle of our ordinarily adversarial system of justice that full and fair disclosure must be made by any person who seeks an order from a court ex parte, with the result that failure to make such disclosure is ordinarily sufficient to warrant discharge of such order as might be made. The principle is not confined to particular types of interlocutory orders. Its rationale lies in the importance to the administration of justice of the courts and the public being able to have confidence that an order will not be made in the absence of a person whose rights are immediately to be affected by that order unless the court making the order has first been informed by the applicant of all facts known to the applicant which that absent person could be expected to have sought to place before the court had the application for the order been contested.
(footnotes omitted)

55 (See also Rumsley v Vegas Enterprises Pty Ltd [2016] FCAFC 84 at [20].)

56 Third, as the Practice Note states at 2.6, a freezing order should be viewed as an extraordinary interim remedy because it can restrict the right to deal with assets even before judgment, and is commonly granted without notice. In this respect I further note the following observation of Gaudron, McHugh, Gummow and Callinan JJ in Cardile v LED Builders Pty Ltd [1999] HCA 18; (1999) 198 CLR 380 at 403-404:
We agree with the tenor of what was said with particular respect to Mareva relief before judgment by the Court of Appeal of New South Wales (Mason P, Sheller JA, Sheppard A-JA) in Frigo v Culhaci:
"[A Mareva order] is a drastic remedy which should not be granted lightly....

A [Mareva order] is an interlocutory order which, if granted, imposes a severe restriction upon a defendant's right to deal with his or her assets. It is granted at the suit of a plaintiff whose status as a creditor is in dispute and who need not be a secured creditor. Its purpose is to preserve the status quo, not to change it in favour of the plaintiff. The function of the order is not to 'provide a plaintiff with security in advance for a judgment that he hopes to obtain and that he fears might not be satisfied; nor is it to improve the position of the plaintiff in the event of the defendant's insolvency' ... Many authorities attest to the care with which courts are required to scrutinise applications for [Mareva orders]...”
(footnotes omitted, emphasis added)

57 In view of the extreme seriousness of a freezing order it is also appropriate for the Court to take into account the balance of convenience of making an order in these terms: Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Shi [2018] FCA 1915 at [20].

58 Fourth, the Court must be satisfied that a freezing order is necessary to prevent the frustration or inhibition of the Court’s process by seeking to meet a danger that a – in this case – prospective judgment of the Court will be wholly or partly unsatisfied. In respect of prospective judgment debtors, the Court must be satisfied that there is a danger of a prospective judgment being wholly or partly unsatisfied because the debtor (or another person) might abscond, or relevant assets will be removed, disposed of, dealt with or diminished in value (r 7.35 (4)).

59 Fifth, r 7.32 (2) contemplates orders restraining a respondent in respect of assets located in or outside Australia.

60 Sixth, the purposes for which ancillary orders may be made are limited by r 7.33 (2), namely for eliciting information relating to assets relating to the freezing order or determining whether the freezing order should be made.

61 Seventh, plainly either freezing orders or ancillary orders may be made against a person even if not a party to the substantive application. However the circumstances in which the Court may make such orders against third parties are subject to r 7.35 (5). Rule 7.35 (5)(a) requires that there must be a danger that a judgment or prospective judgment will be wholly or partly unsatisfied because:
  • the third party holds or is using, or has exercised or is exercising, a power of disposition over relevant assets; or
  • the third party is in possession of, or in a position of control or influence concerning, relevant assets.
62 Alternatively, r 7.35 (5)(b) enlivens the Court’s power to make freezing or ancillary orders against third parties where, having regard to all the circumstances, a process is or may ultimately be available to the applicant as a result of a judgment or prospective judgment, wherein the third party may be obliged to disgorge assets or contribute towards satisfying the judgment or prospective judgment.

63 Eighth, an applicant for a freezing order bears the onus of satisfying the Court that the order should be made, and in satisfying the Court as to the amount which is to be the subject of the order: Zhen v Mo [2008] VSC 300 at [24].

64 Ninth, an order can only be made on the basis of admissible evidence which supports the contentions made by the party seeking the order: Zhen at [25].

65 Finally, and critically, an applicant for a freezing order must demonstrate that it has a good arguable case on an accrued or prospective cause of action that is justiciable in the Court.

Substituted Service of Material

48 I understand that service of relevant material on the Recorder of Titles (Tasmania) and the Registrar of Titles (Queensland) is not an issue of concern to the DCT.

49 However, the DCT submitted that, as matters presently stand, the originating application, the interlocutory application, the affidavit material in support of these applications, the proposed interlocutory orders, and written submissions have not been served on either Mr Farid or Ms Farid. The DCT has sought substituted service of this material in terms of r 10.24 of the FCR.

50 Rule 10.24 of the FCR allows substituted service in instances where it is not practicable to serve a document on a person in a way required by the Rules. It need not be shown that service in the ordinary way is impossible; it is sufficient to show that ordinary service is not realistic or sensible. The urgency of a freezing order, in addition to the risk of dissipation of assets, have been sufficient to meet the threshold for orders allowing substituted service to be granted: see Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Seabrooke [2012] FCA 1158 at [24]; Commissioner of Taxation v Regent Pacific Group Limited [2013] FCA 36 at [26]; Unilever Australia Ltd v PB Foods Ltd [2000] FCA 798 at [14]; NPP Australia Ltd v Ripple Labs, Inc [2020] FCA 1237 at [36].

51 In my view it is appropriate to order substituted service in light of the risk of dissipation of property, the accompanying urgency of a freezing order in the present case, and the ex parte nature of the orders sought. However the method of substituted service must be one through which there is a reasonable probability that the relevant documents will be brought to the attention of the person to be served: Commissioner of Taxation v Oswal [2012] FCA 1507 at [37], referring to Porter v Freudenberg [1915] 1 KB 857 at 889. In this case the methods of substituted service suggested are:

  • by leaving a copy of the relevant materials at 5 Chessington Court, Sandy Bay TAS 7005. In my view this is an appropriate method, being that this address is (on the DCT’s case) the home address of Mr and Ms Farid;
  • by leaving a copy of the relevant materials at MKP Consulting & Co, Level 5, 219-223 Castlereagh Street, Sydney NSW 2000. In my view this is an appropriate method, being (on the DCT’s case) the address of the registered tax agent of Mr and Ms Farid;
  • by emailing the relevant materials to [email protected]. In my view this is an appropriate method, given that (on the DCT’s case) Yuliana Halim is recorded on the system of the Australian Taxation Office as the authorised contact for Mr Farid as his tax agent;
  • by emailing the relevant materials to [email protected] . In my view this is an appropriate method, given that (on the DCT’s case) this was Mr Farid’s recorded address in his application to American Express.

52 I am satisfied that service by these methods is likely to bring the freezing orders to the prompt attention of Mr Farid and Ms Farid.

QUD 78 OF 2022

53 In respect of QUD 78 of 2022 I am satisfied that freezing and ancillary orders should be made against Mr Williams, Ms Williams and the Registrar of Titles (Queensland). I have formed this view for the following reasons.

Mr Spencer Williams

54 First, for reasons I have explained referable to s 39B (1A)(c) of the Judiciary Act and s 255-5 of Sch 1 to the TAA53, I am satisfied that the DCT’s prospective claims for judgment against Mr Williams, pursuant to the originating application filed yesterday, are justiciable in this Court.

55 Second, I am similarly satisfied that notices of assessment as claimed by the DCT have been issued to Mr Williams. I note evidence of Mr Paino in his affidavit as follows:

4.5 I am informed by ATO officer Nicholas Gunaratnam and believe that the Assessments were served on the Taxpayer in accordance with the provisions of the TAA and the Taxation Administration Regulations 2017 (Cth) (TAR) at approximately 10.00am on 21 March 2022, by delivering them to the registered office of the Taxpayer's authorised contact and tax agent, MKP Consulting & Co, at Level 5, 219 Castlereagh Street, Sydney 2000.

4.6 A copy of an ICR screenshot showing the Taxpayer's service address is at page 485.

4.7 The total amount payable by the Taxpayer to the Commonwealth of Australia, including in respect of amounts not yet due and payable, is $3,907,935 (as at 21 March 2022).

56 In accordance with such authorities as Futuris, the accuracy and existence of the tax liabilities of Mr Williams as claimed by the DCT is conclusive.

57 Third, I consider that there is a danger or real risk that any judgment in favour of the DCT against Mr Williams will go unsatisfied if the freezing orders sought by the DCT are not made. On the case of the DCT there is a significant level of funds in Mr Williams’ Bendigo Bank Account (noting that there was $3,280,000.00 worth of unexplained deposits made into that account in 2021), as well as his ownership of a number of luxury motor vehicles. Additionally, the applicant submits that a number of factors give rise to a ready inference that the Mr Williams’ assets may be disposed of, dealt with or diminished in value:

  • on the case of the DCT, Mr Williams has grossly understated his income over a number of years thus showing an intention to not pay tax, culminating in an extremely large tax shortfall;
  • Mr Williams’ tax liabilities exceed his known assets;
  • there are no registered mortgages over the title to the property located at 44 Britannic Crescent, Paradise Point QLD 4216(Paradise Point Property), such that he has the capacity to raise borrowings against the equity in that property (and redirect any funds so borrowed);
  • none of Mr Williams’ luxury vehicles appear to be encumbered by security interests, and could be sold to generate cash (which again could be redirected by Mr Williams);
  • any cash held by Mr Williams could be transferred to third parties (including, for example, Ms Williams);
  • Mr Williams has a history of significant lifestyle expenditure; and
  • Mr Williams is not an Australian citizen, and there is the prospect that he might seek to move cash offshore.

58 In my view the apparent prior conduct of Mr Williams, and the relatively liquid nature of his assets, are such that the risk of dissipation of assets is real.

59 Fourth, insofar as concerns the balance of convenience, a factor militating against the grant of freezing orders is that on 21 March 2022 the DCT issued a notice to the Bendigo Bank pursuant to s 260-5 of Sch 1 to the TAA53 requiring compliance no earlier than 14 April 2022 (s 260-5 notice (2)). The s 260-5 notice (2) required the Bendigo Bank to “deduct money you may hold on behalf of or owe to Spencer Williams up to the value of $3,907,943.04 or 75% of the available money, whichever is the lesser amount.” This form of notice is arguably an effective alternative remedy available to the DCT.

60 I am nonetheless satisfied that, notwithstanding the s 260-5 notice (2), the balance of convenience favours the freezing orders sought. This is because:

  • the s 260-5 notice (2) does not apply to other assets of Mr Williams, including his real property and motor vehicles;
  • the s 260-5 notice (2) does not require all money held by the Bendigo Bank to be paid to the DCT;
  • the evidence supports a finding that Mr Williams has extensive tax liabilities;
  • there appears to be a real risk of asset dissipation by Mr Williams;
  • the DCT is prepared to give the usual undertakings as to damages.

61 The DCT has also proposed that sums be made available to Mr Williams in respect of living expenses ($1,500 per week) and legal costs ($50,000). On the evidence before me such sums appear to be reasonable.

Ms Bronwyn Williams

62 The evidence before the Court is that Ms Williams is a joint tenant of the Paradise Point Property with Mr Williams.

63 While there is no specific evidence before the Court concerning the likelihood of dissipation of assets by Ms Williams, or pointing to a risk that any judgment against Mr Williams would be frustrated by conduct of Ms Williams, I am satisfied that such a risk exists in respect of the Paradise Point Property. In particular:

  • the purchase of the Paradise Point Property was, on the DCT’s case, funded by a loan or mortgage issued by ANZ Bank, which in turn was paid off with funds deposited into Mr Williams’ accounts by way of unexplained deposits;
  • given that Ms Williams is a joint tenant of the Paradise Point Property with Mr Williams, she satisfies the description of a third party in possession of, or in a position of control or influence concerning, assets (including claims and expectancies) of the judgment debtor or prospective judgment debtor within the meaning of r 7.35 (5)(a)(ii) of the FCR; and
  • given that, on the evidence before the Court, Ms Williams is the spouse of Mr Williams, at this interlocutory stage the Court can draw the inference that Ms Williams might be prepared to assist Mr Williams should he choose to deal with his assets.

64 I note that the DCT seeks freezing orders against Ms Williams limited to her interest in the Paradise Point Property.

Registrar of Titles

65 The DCT submitted that ancillary orders were sought against the Registrar of Titles (Queensland) to prevent dealings in respect of the Paradise Point Property, in circumstances where the Registrar may be under a statutory duty to process dealings lodged in respect of that property.

66 That the Court has power to make such orders, and that it may be appropriate to make such an orders, was confirmed in such cases as Commissioner of Taxation v Zou [2021] FCA 433.

67 In the absence of such orders there is a risk that the Paradise Point Property could be disposed of, or subjected to encumbrances, potentially diminishing it as an asset available to satisfy any judgment in favour of the DCT. Orders in the form sought by the DCT so restraining the Registrar are appropriate.

Substituted Service of Material

68 I understand that service of relevant material on the Registrar of Titles is not an issue of concern to the DCT.

69 However, the DCT submitted that, as matters presently stand, the originating application, the interlocutory application, the affidavit material in support of these applications, the proposed interlocutory orders, and written submissions have not been served on either Mr Williams or Ms Williams. The DCT has sought substituted service of this material in terms of r 10.24 of the FCR.

70 As I explained earlier in this judgment, r 10.24 of the FCR allows substituted service in instances where it is not practicable to serve a document on a person in a way required by the Rules. The method of service however must be such that there is a reasonable probability that the relevant documents will be brought to the attention of the person to be served

71 To that end, the applicant seeks the following methods of substituted service by:

  • leaving a copy of the documents at the Paradise Point Property. In my view this is an appropriate method, being that this address is (on the DCT’s case) the home address of Mr and Ms Williams;
  • leaving a copy of the Documents at MKP Consulting & Co, Level 5, 219-223 Castlereagh St, Sydney NSW 2000. In my view this is an appropriate method, being (on the DCT’s case) the address of the registered tax agent of Mr and Ms Williams;
  • emailing the documents to [email protected]. In my view this is an appropriate method, being (on the DCT’s case) Mr Williams’ email address; and
  • emailing the documents to [email protected]. In my view this is an appropriate method, being (on the DCT’s case) an email address used by Mr Williams in correspondence with Bendigo Bank.

72 Given the nature of the relief sought, the potential of the risk of dissipation of assets, and the suitability of the methods of substituted suggested by the applicant, I am prepared to grant the orders sought in this context.

CONCLUSION

73 It follows that I am prepared to make orders substantially in the terms sought. It is appropriate that costs be reserved.
