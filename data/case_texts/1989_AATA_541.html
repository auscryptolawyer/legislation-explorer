WT88/262 and Commissioner of Taxation [1989] AATA 541 (30 August 1989)

Last Updated: 8 April 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Zone Allowance - whether "resident" within zone and whether temporary absences therefrom destroyed concept of being resident. Living Away from Home Allowance -failure to demonstrate Commissioner's assessment excessive.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - Sections 51A, 79A, 109

Gregory v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation (WA) [1937] HCA 57; (1937) 57 CLR 774

Koitaki Para Rubber Estate Ltd v Federal Commissioner of

Taxation [1941] HCA 13; (1941) 64 CLR 241

Commissioner of Taxation v Miller [1946] HCA 23; (1946) 73 CLR 93

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Applegate (1979) ATC 4307 - followed

cases F61 (1974) ATC and P82 (1982) ATC distinguished

Applicant COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

No. WT88/262

AAT Decision No 5378

Mr M.D. Allen (Senior Member)

Perth

30 August 1989

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION ) WT88/262

- and -

DECISION

Tribunal : Mr M D Allen, Senior Member

Date : 30 August 1989

PLACE : Perth

DECISION : That part of the objection relating to Zone Allowance is upheld and this matter remitted to the respondent with the direction that the applicant was a resident of Zone A pursuant to Section 79A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (as amended) for the whole of the period 4 March 1985 to 13 September 1985.

The decision as to Living Away from Home Allowance is affirmed.

(Sgd) M.D. ALLEN

SENIOR MEMBER

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. WT88/262

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

30 August 1989 Mr M.D. Allen (Senior Member)

At the conclusion of the hearing of this application for review Mr M.D. Allen, the Senior Member who constituted the Tribunal, stated orally the terms of the decision intended to be made and the reasons therefor. After service upon the Respondent of a copy of the decision that was in fact made, the Respondent pursuant to Sub-section 43(2A) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 requested the Tribunal to furnish to the Respondent a statement in writing of the reasons of the Tribunal for its decision. Those reasons are now restated below accordingly, and will be furnished to the Applicant and to the Respondent.

1. The Applicant taxpayer objected to his assessment for the tax year 1986. The specific grounds of the original objection referred only to a refusal by the Commissioner to grant to him a rebate pursuant to Section 79A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (as amended) consequent upon his alleged residence within Zone A, the actual place of alleged residence being the Argyle Diamond Mine Construction Site which site is some 210 road miles from Kununurra in Western Australia. It was not contested that the Argyle Diamond Mine Construction Site (hereinafter referred to as "Argyle") was within Zone A.

  1. At the hearing with the concession of the Commissioner's representative, the matter of a Living Away from Home Allowance was also canvassed.
  2. Insofar as the Living Away from Home Allowance is concerned the relevant section of the Act as it then stood is at paragraph 51A(2)(c):

"Such amount, if any, in respect of each week for which the allowance is paid or granted not exceeding the amount by which the weekly rate of value of the allowance exceeds $2, as the Commissioner considers reasonable in the circumstances."

  1. The facts of this matter are within a relatively small compass and are encapsulated by the statement of agreed facts which was taken in and marked as Exhibit 1.
  2. The Applicant commenced employment with Minenco Pty Limited at the Argyle Diamond Mine Construction site pursuant to a Contract of Employment dated 19 February 1985. His actual period within the Zone A commenced on 4 March 1985 and terminated on 13 September 1985, a total period of 194 days.
  3. During this period of 194 days the Applicant took leave (referred to as R & R) as provided by his Contract of Employment. This leave was in 4 periods of 4 days each and was spent at 11 Windich Road, Bullcreek which is a Perth suburb. During his period at Argyle the Applicant had maintained his wife and children at the matrimonial home at 11 Windich Road.
  4. Exhibit 1 states the pertinent facts thus; commencing at paragraph 3:

"3. Prior to commencing employment with Minenco Pty Ltd (sic), the Applicant resided at 11 Windich Road, Bullcreek, a suburb of Perth. The Applicant's home had been at this address since 1972.

4. The property at 11 Windich Road, Bullcreek was owned by the Applicant and his wife, and the Applicant's family resided at 11 Windich Road, Bullcreek for the period from 1972 to early 1988."

  1. The Applicant was provided with barrack accommodation and rations at Argyle. For this he paid the sum of $15 per week.
  2. The Applicant's Contract of Employment is silent as to any Living Away from Home Allowance. His remuneration is at page 13 of the Contract of Employment (Exhibit 2) and is set out thus:

“ SCHEDULE 1

(Details of Remuneration - Clause 2)

BASE SALARY : $ 25,000 per annum

PROJECT ALLOWANCE : 8,750 per annum

LOCATION ALLOWANCE : 2,100 per annum

TOTAL : $ 35,850 per annum"

  1. In the Group Certificate issued for the tax year ended 30 June 1985 the Applicant's employer nominated an amount of allowances in the following terms, namely "Travel/Other Live Away $1698.49". The 1986 Group Certificate expresses allowances in the following terms, namely "Living Away from Home Allowance $1065.91".
  2. The Commissioner rejected the full amount claimed as Living Away from Home Allowance and applied a formula which granted as Living Away from Home Allowance the sum of $60.23 less $2.00.
  3. Sub-section 51A(3) of the Income Tax Assessment Act as it then stood in 1986 read:

"'living-away-from-home allowance' means so much of any allowance or benefit paid or granted in money or otherwise as the Commissioner is satisfied is in the nature of compensation to the employee for the additional expenses (not being expenses which are allowable as a deduction under section 51) incurred by him, or which would be incurred by him if the allowance or benefit were not received, through having to live away from his usual place of abode in order to perform his duties as an employee."

13. Sub-section 190(b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act reads:

"In proceedings under this Part on a review before the Tribunal the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive shall lie upon the taxpayer."

Notwithstanding that the Applicant taxpayer had no part in the framing of his Group Certificates I find that he has not discharged the burden of proof incumbent upon him and that I am not persuaded that the formula adopted by the Commissioner relative to the assessment of the sum nominated as a Living Away from Home Allowance is excessive.

15. As to the rejection of the claim for a rebate pursuant to Section 79A, paragraph 79A(3B)(d) states:

"For the purposes of this section, a taxpayer is a resident of Zone A if the following conditions are satisfied:

(i) the taxpayer resided, or actually was, in the relevant area in the year of income for a period of not more than one-half of the year of income;

(ii) the taxpayer resided, or actually was, in the relevant area in the next preceding year of income for a period of not more than one-half of the next preceding year of income;

(iii) for the purposes of this section, the taxpayer was not a resident of the relevant area in the next preceding year of income; and

(iv) the sum of -

(A) the number of days in the period mentioned in sub-paragraph (i) ; and

(B) the number of days in the period mentioned in sub-paragraph (ii), other than days included in a period to which sub-section 23AB(8) or 79B(3) applied in relation to the taxpayer in relation to the next preceding year of income, exceeds 182."

16. It was contended for the Commissioner that at no time could it be said that the Applicant was a "resident" of Zone A but that if the Tribunal were to find that the taxpayer were a resident of Argyle whilst he was physically at that place then the periods spent by him with his family at Perth destroyed his status of resident so that he was resident within Zone A for 178 days only and not the required 182. The total of 178 days being calculated by 194 days days of employment less 16 days in total spent on R & R in Perth.

17. The concept of residence has been dealt with in many cases. Perhaps the best discussion of the concept is in Nygh "Conflict of Laws in Australia" at page 150. There His Honour says:

"Another difficulty is that residence is not a term of art like domicile. It is usually a question of fact, the determination of which is coloured by the purposes for which it is relevant. Thus residence may mean one thing for the purposes of founding jurisdiction in personam over a defendant, another thing in taxation matters, and yet another thing in matrimonial causes. In essence the Court must, when it has to consider whether a person is resident in a certain country for the purposes of a particular rule, eg the recognition of a foreign adoption, enquire what degree of connection was envisaged by the legislature when enacting that rule: Re an infant (1980) Qd R225.

In its usual meaning, however, residents require something more than a mere physical presence. A person who is in a country as a casual visitor or traveller is not resident there, Firebrace v Firebrace (1878) 4 PD 63. But no particular length of stay is required. A person who comes to a foreign country for a particular purpose and stays for a limited period, even it lasts only weeks or days, until that purpose is achieved, can be said to be resident in that country during that period. Examples are students studying at a foreign university or a husband going to the country where his wife is a resident to seek a reconciliation: Armytage v Armytaqe (1898) P 178; Hoy v Hoy (1906) 25 NZLR 857. Nor is an intention to reside required. A prisoner-of-war is resident in the place where he is interned: Keil v Keil [1947] VicLawRp 56; (1947) VLR 383. Nor does residence require a continuous physical presence. A person remains resident in a country even if he has left it for a temporary purpose: Norman v Norman (No 3) (1969) 16 FLR 231; Rowley v Rowley (1959) NZLR 213, and, in certain circumstances, a man be held to have maintained a residence, at least for matrimonial purposes, in the place where he has maintained the matrimonial home even if he himself has left it to spend most of his time elsewhere: Sinclair v Sinclair (1968) P 189. It is obvious that a person can have more than one residence at the same time."

  1. I note that the sub-section under review uses the term "resided" as opposed to the term "residence" or "resident", however to my mind nothing turns upon this semantic differentiation. The concept under review is that of what is required to constitute a taxpayer a "resident", ie one who "resides".
  2. It is well accepted that a person may have more than one residence, see for example Williams J. in Koitaki Para Rubber Estate Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1941] HCA 13; (1941) 64 CLR 241 at 249:

"The place of residence of an individual is determined, not by the situation of some business or property which he is carrying on or owns, but by reference to where he eats and sleeps and has his settled or usual abode. If he maintains a home or homes he resides in the locality or localities where it or they are situate, but he may also reside where he habitually lives even if this is in hotels or on a yacht or some other place of abode ...

In Inland Revenue Commissioners v Lysaght (1928) AC at p244 Viscount Sumner said: 'Grammatically the word 'resident' indicates a quality of the person charged and is not descriptive of his property real or personal.'"

20. In Gregory v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation (WA) [1937] HCA 57; (1937) 57 CLR 774 his Honour Mr Justice Dixon as he then was said at page 777:

"A question of much importance in the present case is whether the word 'resident' in sec. 5A should be interpreted in the same way as similar expressions are interpreted in the British Income Tax Acts. I link that the answer is that the word should receive the same meaning and application as 'person residing' and 'ordinary resident' have been given in England. No technical or artificial meaning has been placed upon these expressions and parallel expressions under the British income tax law. But certain principles have been laid down by judicial decision for interpreting and applying the expressions.

That was first settled in Attorney-General v Coote (1817) 4 Price 183; 146 ER 433. ...

In the present case I would not deny that the taxpayer had retained his residence in Broome in Western Australia. He had done nothing that would be enough to divest himself of the character of a resident of Broome which, speaking figuratively, clung to him from long association and usage, an association and usage which he had done nothing to dissolve or destroy. But I think he did most definitely acquire the concurrent character of a resident of Darwin. ..."

21. See also Commissioner of Taxation v Miller [1946] HCA 23; (1946) 73 CLR 93. In that case it is perhaps pertinent to start first of all with the facts. It was, after all, an appeal to the High Court from a Board of Review. The Board had held that the taxpayer, whilst at Milne Bay in the Territory of Papua was a resident of Papua and not Australia. Upon the matter being taken on appeal by the Commissioner Latham CJ said at p.99:

"Next, it was said that his residence was in Australia because he left his wife there. But whether or not a husband must be held to have a home wherever his wife may happen to be from time to time, that fact is immaterial in the present case. It is well established that an individual may have two residences: See the cases cited by Dixon J. in Gregory v Deputy Federal Commission of Taxation (WA) [1937] HCA 57; (1937) 57 CLR 774, at pp. 777, 778.

I should have thought that there was no doubt that a man resided where he lived, and I do not think that there is any interpretation of the word 'reside' by the courts which makes it impossible to apply the ordinary meaning of the word 'reside' in the present case. In Levene v Inland Revenue Commissioners [1928] UKHL 1; (1928) AC 217, at p. 222 Cave L.C. said:

'... the word 'reside' is a familiar English word and is defined in the Oxford English Dictionary as meaning 'to dwell permanently or for a considerable time, to have one's settled or usual abode, to live in or at a particular place'. No doubt this definition must for present purposes be taken subject to any modification which may result from the terms of the Income Tax Act and Schedules; but, subject to that observation, it may be accepted as an accurate indication of the meaning of the word 'reside'. In most cases there is no difficulty in determining where a man has his settled or usual abode, and if that is ascertained he is not the less resident there because from time to time he leaves it for the purpose of business or pleasure.'"

22. At page 103 of Miller's case supra Dixon J. said:

"Having regard to the character of the place, I do not think that, had I been in the Board's place, I should have regarded the facts I have stated as leading to the conclusion that the taxpayer was a resident of Papua. But I am not satisfied that their decision involved any question of law. It is not legally impossible for a man to reside in a country, though he lives on a moving craft plying upon its rivers or within its territorial waters. Nor is it legally impossible for a man to reside at a military base, even a forward one."

And continued at page 104:

"The Board have given their reasons and no misapprehension of the meaning of the provision in question is disclosed and no misconception appears as to what amounts to 'residence' as a general proposition."

23. Mr Preshaw who appeared for the Commissioner sought to distinguish Gregory's case on the basis of the degree of involvement in the community of Darwin adopted by the taxpayer in that case as opposed to the transitary nature of this Applicant's relationship to Argyle. Whilst I agree that the Applicant was a transitary employee at that place that does not mean he was not "resident" there for the time he was there. As was said by Nygh J. in the passage quoted earlier:

"no particular length of stay is required."

24. The Applicant was living in barracks where his necessary clothing and tools of trade etc were kept. During his absences from the site his room was kept for him with his goods and chattels therein. His was not the case such as a traveller putting up at a motel or hotel and vacating his room when not physically present.

  1. Mr Preshaw also submitted that later decisions of Boards of Review have restricted the authority of Gregory's case supra and other High Court cases to the particular sections therein dealt with, namely whether a taxpayer was a resident for tax purposes. He cited cases F61 in 1974 "Australian Tax Cases", and P82 in 1982 "Australian Tax Cases" as authorities.
  2. It seems to me however that case P82 supra is distinguishable on its facts being a Mariner's case and turning on the specific wording of what constituted Zone A. See for example Dr Gerber at page 406 and Dr Beck at paragraph 3 of page 406.
  3. Notwithstanding those authorities I am of course bound by decisions of the Federal Court. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v APPlegate (1979) ATC 4307 the Full Court of the Federal Court made it clear that even on the more stringent legislative provision, namely that of a "permanent place of abode" an intention to return in the future from whence the taxpayer had originally come is not sufficient to destroy a degree of permanency.
  4. Case F61 in (1974) ATC concerned the special case of a child at a boarding school and is thus also distinguishable on its own facts.
  5. I would however refer to the statement of reasons by Mr Gordon Thompson, Member, at p363 of 1974 ATC, namely:

"The policy of the legislature is to be discerned clearly from the actual language used."

29. In this regard it is interesting that, as available to me, Section 79A(1) commences:

"For the purpose of granting to residents of the prescribed area an income tax concession in recognition of the disadvantages to which they are subject because of the uncongenial climatic conditions, isolation and high cost of living in Zone A ..."

It seems to me that those disadvantages are suffered by the fact of being present, that is "residing" in that place and not diminished by the fact a family is maintained elsewhere.

30. The language used in Sub-section 79A(3B)(d) also used two phrases - "the taxpayer resided" and "or actually was". The use of the phrase "or actually was" indicates that the parliament had recognition of the legal situation that one can be resident in a place whilst having periods of absence therefrom. If otherwise other phrases could have been used.

  1. As stated above I consider that the authorities establish first of all that a person can have one or more residences and that a person can be resident in a place notwithstanding temporary absences therefrom. I consider in this case that the taxpayer has established that he resided at Argyle during the relevant period and that his temporary absences therefrom were not sufficient to destroy that status of his residing there.
  2. That part of the objection relating to the Zone Allowance will be upheld and the matter remitted to the Commissioner with the direction that the Applicant was a resident of Zone A pursuant to Section 79A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 as amended for the whole of the period from 4 March 1985 to 13 September 1985. The decision as to the Living Away from Home Allowance is affirmed.
