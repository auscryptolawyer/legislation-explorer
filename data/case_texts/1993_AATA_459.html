Negative Instruments Pty Ltd and Commissioner of Taxation [1993] AATA 459 (2 April 1993)

Last Updated: 25 August 2008



INCOME TAX - Petroleum exploration expenditure - Joint venture exploration agreement - Drilling Agreement - Authority to prospect (ATP) - Failure to have grant of interest in ATP stamped - Failure to obtain Ministerial approval - Whether exploration expenditure deductible - Plant depreciation - What constitutes "disposal" - Whether plant abandoned on land which had become an inseparable part of the land when sold with the land can constitute "property" for purposes of depreciation - secs 59, 124AH, 124AJ Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1985; Petroleum Act 1923-1983 (Qld).

REASONS FOR DECISION



1. The years of income in dispute between the parties are 1981 and 1985. The correct names of the various entities and individuals have been included, the applicant having agreed to waive his right to confidentiality. The main issue between the parties in the 1981 tax year was, after much argument on the first day, finally reduced to a Statement of Agreed Facts and Issues as follows:



A. Facts



1. That an amount of $1,231,216 was outlaid by XL Petroleum Pty Ltd ("XLP") in the year of income ended 30 June, 1981, in relation to petroleum exploration expenditure on the East Windorah hole located in the area covered by Authority to Prospect 270P ("ATP 270P"), as shown in the amended accounts of XLP furnished to the Commissioner of Taxation in respect of the 1981 year.



2. That at all relevant times for the purposes of the present application, XLX NL ("XLX") held ATP 270P.



3. That at no time has XLP held a registered interest in ATP 270P.



B. Issues In Contention



1. Whether XLP had, at any time, an equitable interest in ATP 270P.



2. Whether on a proper construction of the following agreements, namely:



(a) a loan agreement, as recorded in the Minutes of a meeting of directors of XLP, dated 10 (sic) December, 1980, under which XLX loaned an amount of $1,108,815 to XLP;



(b) a joint venture petroleum exploration agreement, dated 10 July, 1980, between Abrolhos Oil Investments Pty Ltd ("Abrolhos") and XLX;



(c) a joint venture petroleum exploration agreement between XLX and XLP, dated 14 May, 1980;



(d) a drilling agreement, dated 27 June 1980, between XLP and OD & E Pty Ltd (Oil Drilling & Exploration (Qld.) Pty. Ltd - "OD & E");



and whether, given that at no time XLP held a registered interest in ATP 270P; and whether, given that the agreement in (c) above between XLX and XLP was not stamped; and on a proper construction and application of relevant law to the facts, XLP incurred, for the purposes of section 124AH(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"), expenditure in the amount of $1,231,216 in relation to the East Windorah hole located on the area covered by ATP 270P, during the year of income ended 30 June, 1981.



2. On the second day of the hearing, and well into the cross-examination of Mr Sykes, the principal witness for the taxpayer, Mr F, the Commissioner's representative, indicated that he now wished, in addition to the agreed statement of facts and issues, to rely on subsec 124AJ(1) of the Act, pointing to cl 1 of the Abrolhos agreement. This had been discussed with Mr Sykes and was, in any event, relied upon in the decision on the objection. Mr Sykes was thus not taken by surprise.



3. In the 1985 tax year, the only issue in dispute is the disallowance of some $65,770, claimed by XLP as a loss on sale of fixtures. (The "T" documents do not contain the 1985 return).



4. Dealing with the 1981 tax year, a brief history of events and the relevant documentation is necessary to understand the somewhat complex dispute between the parties. The applicant XLP (now known as Negative Instruments Pty Ltd) described its income producing activity in its 1981 return as "Petrol Retailer - Petroleum Exploration". All the issued shares in XLP were, at all relevant times, owned by Mr Sykes. XLX, having exploration as its aim and purpose, was owned as to 75.3% Sykes directly and 24.7% by XLP. Exhibit "B" lists all the members of the XL group of companies as follows:



XL Petroleum Pty Ltd

XLX NL

XL Petroleum (NSW) Pty Ltd

Jervois Sulphates (NT) Ltd

Orex Pty Ltd

Opal Supplies Australia Pty Ltd

Australasian Prospect Digest



5. Mr Sykes claimed in his opening that, with the exception of Jervois and Orex, which were publicly listed companies, Orex being a subsidiary of Jervois, the remaining companies were "100% Sykes". As already noted, the authority to prospect ATP 270P was owned by XLX.



6. The various events (set out in chronological order) reveal the following:



(i) On 14 May 1980, an agreement (exh "H") was entered between XLX and XLP on OD & E letterhead, Mr Sykes signing on behalf of both parties. It is common ground that this agreement was not stamped. The agreement provides:



[XLX] hereby agrees that [XLP] will earn in XLX's ATP 270 a 50% working interest in graticular blocks Nos 1361 and 1289 on the Queensland Government Map Series B near and East of Windorah, Queensland in return for drilling one hole to about 3000 metres or less at about shot point 230 on line AB of XLX during 1980-81 at XLP's sole cost risk and expense.



(ii) On 10 July 1980, XLX entered into an agreement ("T10") with Abrolhos Oil & Investment Limited ("Abrolhos"), the relevant parts of which provide:



W H E R E A S:



A. XLX is registered as the owner of ATP 270P ... hereinafter referred to as `the tenement'.

B. Abrolhos had agreed to pay moneys to XLX in connection with the exploration of the tenement ...

C. XLX proposes that a well to be named `XL Flank East of Windorah Number One Well' shall be drilled on the tenement ... by XLP for and on behalf of XLX and which well is hereinafter referred to as `the Well'. (my underlining).

D. The said XLP has entered into a contract for the drilling of the Well with OD & E which contract is dated 27th day of June 1980 a copy of which is annexed hereto and marked Schedule B and which contract is hereinafter called "the drilling contract".



(The aforementioned drilling contract with OD & E was not annexed to "T 10", nor made an exhibit - an unfortunate omission).



E. It has been agreed between XLX and Abrolhos that XLX or its nominee shall be the Operator for the purposes of drilling the Well.



NOW THIS AGREEMENT WITNESSETH as follows:-



1. Abrolhos hereby agrees to pay to XLX a sum equal to all costs incurred by XLX or by XLP in drilling the Well in accordance with the drilling contract and in particular but without limiting the generality thereof to pay to XLX all costs of logging testing casing completing abandoning fuelling production testing siting positioning and all other costs payable by XLX or XLP under the drilling contract to the said OD & E or to any other person or corporation in connection with the drilling of the Well.



2. (1) XLX shall be and is hereby appointed the Operator of the joint venture constituted by this agreement and shall have responsibility for and shall subject to the provisions of this agreement carry out manage and control or cause to be carried out managed and controlled all work and operations required or permitted to be carried out hereunder in relation to the tenement and XLX shall be entitled to remain as operator whilst it shall hold greater than 30% undivided interest in the tenement.



(2) For the purposes of the carrying out of the work and operations referred to in sub-clause (1) of this Clause 2 XLX may carry out the same by itself or through its servants agents and such independent contractors as it may engage.



3. (1) XLX shall not more than thirty (30) days prior to the 14th day of October 1980 or such other date as the parties hereto shall agree submit to Abrolhos a budget of expenditure to be incurred in connection with the drilling of the Well.



(2) Abrolhos shall within 14 days of the receipt of such budget or by the 14th day of October 1980 or such other date as shall be agreed by the parties hereto whichever shall be the later pay to XLX the sum shown on such budget as being the estimated cost of drilling the Well for and upon payment of which sum (which may later be adjusted in accordance with the provisions of sub-clause (3) of this Clause 3) Abrolhos shall subject to the consent of the Minister of Mines of the State of Queensland be deemed to be and become the owner of a ten percentum (10%) undivided working interest in the tenement.



(3) If it should be found upon completion of the Well that the costs thereof properly incurred for the purposes of this agreement shall be more than the sum shown on the budget referred to in sub-clause (1) of this Clause 3 Abrolhos shall upon receiving notification of any additional cost within fourteen (14) days of receipt of such notification pay the amount of such additional cost to XLX ...



4. Upon the payment to XLX of the budget sum referred to in Clause 3(2) hereof Abrolhos shall subject to the consent of the Minister of Mines of the State of Queensland be entitled to and shall be the owner of TEN PERCENTUM (10%) undivided working interest in the tenement and in any other tenement production licence or right issued in lieu of or in substitution therefore or any part thereof and shall further be entitled to and shall be the owner of a 10% undivided interest in all economic geological and geophysical information so that XLX and Abrolhos shall subsequently thereto hold both the interest in the tenement and such economic geological and geophysical information as tenants in common in unequal shares.



(The remainder of the agreement is not relevant to this application. It should be noted, however, that the agreement does not record the fact that XLX had transferred a 50% interest in ATP 270P to XLP).



(iii) The Authority to Prospect No 270P File No 79/8042 (as per copy on folio 47 "T" documents) contains the notation "The Hon the Minister on 8/10/80 approved farm-in agreement dated 10.7.80 between Abrolhos Oil & Investments Limited and XLX NL.

(iv) A letter, addressed to the Directors of Orex, dated 6 November 1980 and signed by Mr Ferguson on behalf of Abrolhos and witnessed by Mr Sykes (exh "M") states inter alia:



The deductions in respect of XL Flank Windorah East will vest with XL Petroleum Pty Limited and not Abrolhos.



(v) The minute of the meeting of directors of XLP, dated 18 December 1980, (T 7) records a resolution that:



... the Company accept a loan from [XLX] at call and subject to a deferred payment of interest at a rate to be determined by the Directors upon call of loan monies but being at a rate up to and not in excess of three percentage points above that of trading bank overdraft rates on the $1,108,815.



(vi) A minute of a meeting of directors of XLX, also dated 18 December, 1980, (exh "O") records a resolution that:



... the amount due to the Company from Abrolhos in respect of ATP 270P be advanced to [XLP], at call by the Company and subject to a deferred payment of interest at a rate to be determined by the Directors upon call but not in excess of three percentage points above trading bank overdraft rates.



7. In the taxpayer's original return for the 1981 year, Mr Sykes, who signed the return on behalf of the company, stated (at folio 17 of "T3") that:



"[XLP] acquired a 10% interest in ATP 270 from [XLX] by drilling a well at a cost of $1,108,815. [XLP] then sold this 10% interest to Abrolhos at cost price i.e. $1,108,815. (my underlining). This expenditure was in respect of oil exploration, and is therefore claimed under s.124. No expenditure has been recouped, therefore s 124AQ applies. Also as the sale of property is involved s.124AM may also apply."



8. In its profit and loss statement attached to the 1981 return (folio 21 of "T3"), under the heading "Drilling of East Windorah Hole", an amount of $1,108,815, i.e. the same amount as was claimed as a deduction for "oil exploration", was returned as income, derived from the "sale of drilling information". Next to the amount of $1,108,815 appears the following information:



On the basis of reply to [question] in 1980, it is assumed (and it seems reasonable having regard to the amount of expenses listed here) that the costs involved in earning this income have been included in the claims made opposite.



9. Against the amount of income said to have been earned with respect to the sale of drilling information relating to the drilling of the East Windorah Hole are claimed deductions totalling $861,302, claimed to have been expended on such items as chemicals, equipment, wages, technical advice, etc. In all, the taxpayer returned a net profit of $1,064,919.



10. On 14 October 1983, the Commissioner issued an assessment which, for present purposes, accepted the statement in the return that the company had received as income some $1,108,815 from the sale of seismic information and, after some adjustments, arrived at a taxable income of $969,682.



11. On receiving the assessment, it soon dawned on Mr Sykes that there was something radically wrong with the return, and in the company's notice of objection, dated 13 December 1983, signed by one GG Collins, Accountant, it was stated, inter alia,

That the assessment of income tax for the year ended 30th June, 1981 has been calculated on an incorrect and erroneous amount of Net Profit, albeit reported by this Company. Specifically, an amount was incorrectly included as Sale of Seismic Information (in respect of Drilling of East Windorah Hole) -$1,108,815. This receipt was in fact a loan from the original holder of the Permit ATP 270P, XLX. XLX sold a 10% interest in this prospect to Abrolhos Oil & Investments Ltd for $1,108,815 which was remitted direct to XL Petroleum Pty Ltd as operator. XL Petroleum Pty Ltd acquired a 50% working interest in the Authority to Prospect 270P in return for being Operator and drilling the petroleum exploration well referred to as `XL Flank East of Windorah No 1 Well". This error in the accounts of the Company was made by a former accountant to the Company, long since dismissed from service, and is the subject of a Letter of Amendment of this date in respect of the 1981 income tax return of both this Company and XLX. ... That pursuant to the facts outlined (above), the Taxable Income of the Company be reduced to nil and that the assessment referred to above be withdrawn.



12. In the events that occurred, before the objection could be determined, the taxpayer lodged a fresh return on or about 25 June 1984, which set out a very different set of figures and expenses relevant to the drilling the East Windorah Hole. These changes were, according to Mr Sykes, said to be "the net result of a series of adjustments and corrections to the books of account following a complete review of accounts. Both the company accountant and auditor were appointed subsequent to the lodgment of the original 1981 income tax return."



13. On receipt of the new set of accounts, the Commissioner issued an amended assessment which issued on 22 October 1986, allowing the original objection in part (i.e. to the extent that it accepted that the allowable expenditure recouped in the 1980 tax year be reduced by $186,032 with a consequent increase of $186,032 in the amount of allowable expenditure to be carried forward into 1981 tax year) and, after some adjustments, including the addition of some $2,166,310 "sec 124 expenditure" (which included the disputed amount of $1,108,815) and deducting some $1,507,752 "sec 124AH deductions", the amended Notice of Assessment left the taxpayer with a taxable income of $966,475. At the end of the day, and for purposes of this hearing, the Commissioner relies on the original figures as returned by Mr Sykes, save that he now accepts that the drilling costs on the East Windorah Hole were $1,231,216.



14. This amended assessment led to a further notice of objection, dated 12 February 1990, claiming, inter alia, that the amount of $1,231,216 or alternatively $1,108,815 was necessarily incurred in carrying on the business of exploration and prospecting, and was thus an allowable deduction pursuant to sec 124AH(1). It further stated that an amount of $1,108,815 was received by the taxpayer by way of loan from XLX during the year of income and that neither that amount nor any part thereof was received by the taxpayer by way of recoupment of the drilling expenditure.



Further, if a deduction was allowed in respect of the drilling expenditure or some part of it, the Taxpayer did not enjoy the benefit of such deduction because of the erroneous inclusion in its income as returned of the sum of $1,108,815 ...



15. It seems that the confusion in the accounts was due to the employment of a new accountant, who, according to Mr Sykes:



was only a young chap ... and he just posted according to how he saw the amounts coming in. In other words, he did not refer at all to the contracts. And I probably should have told him, or something like that, so that he had got that right. And for two years I could not understand why we had a dispute with the Taxation Commissioner at all. ... But eventually we pulled the files down and we got the contracts which were there to say what was said. And then, of course, we had to amend our accounts and show the position as it really was. (p 14 tr).



16. The Commissioner's case (at that time) is neatly summarised in his decision on the later objection. I propose to set out the relevant parts in full:



Your objection, dated 12 February, 1990, against the amended notice of assessment which issued on 22 October, 1986, in respect of the year of income ended 30 June, 1981, has been considered and has been disallowed for the following reasons:



(a) on the balance of probabilities, the company did not intend to create legal relations when it entered into a purported agreement, dated 14 May, 1980, with XLX, under which the company was to drill a petroleum exploration well at East Windorah, in consideration for which XLX agreed to convey to the company an interest in ATP 270P; and accordingly the company did not incur any expenditure on exploration or prospecting for the purpose of discovering petroleum which would entitle the company to claim a deduction pursuant to the provisions of section 124AH(1) of the Act;



(b) on a proper construction of the Joint Venture Agreement, executed under seal on 11 November, 1980, in relation to the drilling of a petroleum exploration well at East Windorah, between XLX and Abrolhos; and on the facts; the company entered into an agreement, dated 27 June, 1980 with O D & E Ltd for the drilling of a petroleum exploration well at East Windorah in the capacity of agent, and not principal; and accordingly the company did not incur any expenditure on exploration or prospecting for the purpose of discovering petroleum which would entitle the company to claim a deduction pursuant to the provisions of section 124AH(1) of the Act;



(c) in any event, the 14 May 1980, agreement referred to in sub-paragraph (a) above was not performed by XLX, in that XLX has not transferred, and can not now transfer, an interest in ATP 270P to the company; and moreover, as a matter of law the company could not have obtained an equitable interest at the relevant time in ATP 270P; and accordingly, because of the operation of the provisions of section 124AJ(1), the company could not claim a deduction under section 124AH(1) in the event that it had acted as principal in relation to the 27 June, 1980, agreement with O D & E Ltd referred to in sub-paragraph (b) above.



17. The relevant sections dealing with exploration and prospecting expenditure are set out hereunder:



124AH.(1) Subject to this section, expenditure incurred by the taxpayer during the year of income on exploration or prospecting in Australia for the purpose of discovering petroleum is an allowable deduction.



124AJ.(1) For the purposes of this Division, where the holder of a petroleum prospecting or mining right has, for a consideration provided or to be provided by him, not being a payment of a share of income derived by him from the sale of petroleum or of the products of petroleum or a consideration by way of an assignment or sub-lease of a petroleum prospecting or mining right, procured the performance of work which, if it had been performed by him, would have constituted prescribed petroleum operations or would have constituted exploration or prospecting -



(a) the work shall constitute prescribed petroleum operations or exploration or prospecting, as the case may be, carried on by him and shall not constitute prescribed petroleum operations or exploration or prospecting carried on by the person by whom the work was performed; and



(b) any such consideration shall be deemed to be expenditure incurred by him in the carrying on of prescribed petroleum operations or of exploration or prospecting, as the case may be.



18. Turning to the evidence, Mr Sykes alleges that the amount of $1,108,815 was paid "in two sums, one of $900,000 if I remember it correctly, and another of $108,815 (sic), which was paid on my instructions by Abrolhos to XLP ... two specifically tagged payments by Abrolhos for the cost of buying the 10% interest in the XLX tenement."



19. Pausing here, it will be recalled that it was stated in the original return that XLP had acquired a 10% interest in ATP 270P from XLX which it on-sold to Abrolhos "at cost price" (see para 7 above) which would explain why Abrolhos paid the amount to XLP. However, on the facts as we now know them, the vendor was XLX. However, when it became obvious that the payment was, in fact, made to XLP, I commented to Mr Sykes that it would appear that he seemed to ignore in the various books of account that XLP and XLX were two different legal entities, He replied:



Well, no, they should not have been done that way, but the point is that in practice - if, for instance, - there is one crucial letter that Sykes sort of says to Abrolhos, the person buying into the XLX tenement; "pay this money into XLP's account". And the reason is that XLP needs the money, whereas XLX does not need the money, and had it been paid into there, it would have simply been loaned out to XLP again. So that was short circuited. (p 11 tr).



Later, Mr Sykes stated:



But what was wrong in the books was when Abrolhos paid the $900,000 and the $108,815 - which was XLX's money - I said: `Look, your obligations under the contract ... will be satisfied to XLX for buying this percentage of the lease, by paying this money into XLP'. And the simple reason I did that is that XLP was the active operating account, paying out things like this, and we needed the money there. (p 14 tr).



20. Exh "H" is the holograph (and unstamped) agreement between XLP and XLX, described by Mr Sykes as:



a contract for drilling the exploration hole at East Windorah. ... Now that piece of paper - remember XLP and XLX were owned by Ian Sykes - and that piece of paper could have been lodged with the Mines Department after being stamped at the Queensland Stamp Duties Office for $5 or whatever, then sent to the Mines Department for entering on the title. Now, that was not done, and there was, in my opinion, no need to. Had, for instance, for some reason the companies been sold off to some other party - to separate entities - and oil had been found in the well and XLX had refused to transfer 50% in those two blocks to XLP for having paid for the hole, XLP then could have sued in equity. So, we say that under mining law ... where petroleum is treated more leniently than with mining tenements, that would have entitled us to be registered at any time. In fact, at any time where you have sent a document in, it would have been stamped. And that process is quite automatic by the Minister. I have personally done that perhaps 30 times in my life, and he just stamps it. (p 33-34 tr).



21. In due course, a hole was drilled which, at a certain depth, contained nothing but water. In the result, XLP did not register its 50% interest and when Abrolhos made an offer to take over the entire tenement jointly with Crusader, Mr Sykes gladly accepted the offer ("drilling in that area was enormously expensive"). In the result, XLX transferred the whole tenement to Crusader as the operator on terms that XLX would receive 18.75% of the oil or gas produced or 37.5% of the profits, whichever was the greater in any year, together with the right to buy all the oil at market price should any be found. (In theory, of course, XLX merely owned a 50% interest in ATP 270P, having divested itself of "a 50% working interest in said tenement". I was not informed to what extent - if any - XLP, which "owned" 50% of ATP 270P, was a party to this formal agreement). This evidence was adduced in answer to my enquiry whether it was possible, even at this late stage, to have the agreement stamped and lodged with the Mines Department, Mr Sykes adding wryly: "XLP threw away its interest really at the end of the hole, because what is the use of owning 50% in two blocks dividing up the tenement as to equity when there is nothing on those two blocks in your opinion". (p 37 tr)



22. Mr Sykes' main submission is to the effect that, having spent the money on drilling a well in search of oil or gas, it becomes an allowable deduction pursuant to subsec 124AH(1) ("Subject to this section, expenditure incurred by the taxpayer during the year of income on exploration or prospecting for the purpose of discovering petroleum is an allowable deduction"). The right to enter upon land and/or prospect, so it is submitted, is irrelevant to obtaining the deduction, which is said to depend purely on evidence that what is claimed can be shown to have been expended on oil exploration. ("I do not think that failure to lodge a document for stamping for $5 matters, and I do not think it also matters, whether - say - we had a bad agreement; say, somebody could have argued these two blocks did not exist or something, or whatever, it still does not matter. We say there is consideration in that agreement, namely that we earned 50% by doing certain work, we did that work. Had we been successful, we would have gained 50% interest by registration in the normal way").



23. To test the proposition that mere expenditure on exploration qualifies for deduction, I asked Mr Sykes whether he is submitting that he could claim the cost of drilling for petroleum in Collins Street without any claim of right? To which he replied "yes". In support for that view, Mr Sykes referred me to IT 2642, which deals with mining exploration and prospecting expenditure. That ruling states, so far as relevant:



7. As far as general mining entities are concerned, subsection 122J(1) only allows deductions for expenditure incurred on exploration or prospecting if it is "on any mining tenements" in Australia. A mining tenement is taken to mean a prospecting licence, exploration licence, mining lease, general purpose lease or a miscellaneous licence granted or acquired under ... a State Act ... and includes the specified piece of land in respect of which the mining tenement is so granted or acquired. As indicated, subsection 122J(1) - see also subsection 122J(4D) - specifically requires the existence of a mining tenement at the time exploration or prospecting is carried out. It does not require that the person incurring the expenditure to be the holder of the mining tenement. Hence, this Office accepts that it is sufficient if the taxpayer is given authority or permission to enter and explore by the holder of the mining tenement over the area Taxation Ruling IT 363 reflects this view.



8. Accordingly, preliminary expenditure incurred by a mining or exploration company on surveys (mainly aerial surveys including the use of satellites, regional aerial photographic and geophysical studies and the study of heat flows) over broad areas of the Australian continent before the acquisition of a mining tenement is not deductible under subsection 122J(1) as it cannot qualify as expenditure on "any mining tenements".



9. This contrasts with the position with petroleum mining, where subsection 124AH(1) does not require exploration or prospecting expenditure to be incurred specifically on mining tenements. Subject to subsection 124AH(4C), and provided the expenditure satisfies the qualifying activities of "exploration or prospecting" as defined in subsection 124AH(7), the expenditure is an allowable deduction under subsection 124AH(1) regardless of whether a mining tenement exists at the time the expenditure is incurred. Consequently, similar expenditure to that in paragraph 8 above qualifies as a deduction under subsection 124AH(1). (my underlining)



24. Mr Sykes next submitted that the money expended on petroleum exploration (referred to at all times during the hearing as being "$1,108,815") was actually paid by XLP. He stated (at p 45 tr) that, having prepared a budget on or about 5 November 1980 (exh "L"), he knew that the dry hole cost would be $1,108,815 ("we were entitled to submit a budget and within 30 days of that, they (i.e. Abrolhos) had to pay the money and they paid it in two amounts, I remember - $900,000 and $108,815 - and that is the money that went straight into XLP's account and was missed by me in the account. So that is the cause of the court case. I suppose that we messed up our books because the accountant did not look at the rig notebooks or the contracts. He just posted them up as the figures apparently were. Therefore the accounts had to be corrected, and two or three years later proper accounts were prepared and resubmitted, taking into account the actual documents at the time").



25. Exh "J" is a letter written by Mr Sykes as Chairman of XLP to the Commonwealth Bank, Cabramatta, dated 5 November 1980, advising the bank that XLP expected to receive from Abrolhos an amount of $1,108,815 on 1 December 1980, and requesting that the amount be paid into its (i.e. XLP's) account No 123-124 and to be dealt with in accordance with the instructions contained in the letter. That money, as we now know, belonged of course to XLX, being the consideration received from Abrolhos for its 10% interest in ATP 270P acquired from XLX pursuant to the agreement dated 10 July 1980.



26. It will be recalled that a meeting of directors of XLP, attended by Mr Sykes and a Mr Kenneth R Turner, resolved "to accept a loan from XLX at call ... on the $1,108,815" (see para 6(v) above). At p 49 of the transcript, Mr Sykes volunteered that this meeting "was a telephone meeting. It was our authorisation on the phone". Furthermore, it appears that at the relevant time, Mr Sykes was working on a rig somewhere near Barcoo Junction with the nearest telephone in Quilpie (see pp 54-55 tr). This notwithstanding, the formal minutes of this "meeting" record that it was held at 324A Bell Street, Coburg at 10 a.m. on 18 December 1980 (see exh "H").



27. Exh "O" records the formal minutes of meeting of directors of XLX held at 324A Bell Street Coburg on 18 December 1980 at 10.15 a.m. (see para 6(vi) above). Present were Mr Sykes and a Mr Chesleigh W Langdon, a director of both XLP and XLX. The minutes of the previous meeting were recorded as having been read and confirmed as a true record. It was then resolved "that the amount due to the Company from Abrolhos in respect of ATP 270P be advanced to XLP at call by the Company and subject to a deferred payment of interest at a rate to be determined by the Directors upon call but not in excess of three percentage points above trading bank overdraft rates. The meeting then closed". The minutes were confirmed by Mr Sykes as Chairman who, it will be recalled, was then somewhere near Barcoo Junction, as was Mr Langdon (p 76 tr) and not at 324A Bell Street, Coburg, Victoria.



28. It seems Abrolhos did not pay the $1,108,815 to XLP until January 1981. It came in two payments - $910,000 on 2 January and the balance on 12 January. Both payments were paid into the Cabramatta account of the taxpayer. At the conclusion of the evidence, I asked Mr F to address me first and, in all the circumstances, I thought it best to afford Mr Sykes the opportunity to mull over the various points raised in argument and to permit him to submit a written response to Mr F's address, a suggestion he adopted with alacrity.



37. Mr F's first submission is to the effect that the wide interpretation given to sec 124AH(1) by Mr Sykes cannot be sustained; that the section is not a self-contained code but must be read subject to the other sections in Div 10AA, and particularly sec 124AJ(1). By way of illustration, and using the example I put to Mr Sykes, Mr F submitted that a person drilling a hole in Collins Street at his/her expense in search of petroleum and without a claim of right would not - contrary to Mr Sykes' assertion - be entitled to deduct the cost involved in that activity. For this submission, Mr F relied on the Petroleum Act 1923-1983 (Qld) which, he claimed, rendered it unlawful "for a person to go upon land to prospect for petroleum". For good measure, he pointed to clause 19 of the ATP 270P, which provides that:



Any interest in this Authority to Prospect shall not, except with the written approval of the Minister first had and obtained, be capable of being assigned, transferred, mortgaged, charged or created.



38. The tax consequences of such "illegality", Mr F submitted "...I am relying entirely on arguing from general principle - the principle is that it would be contrary to public interest to permit a deduction for unlawful acts". (p 110 tr) I gather the "unlawfulness" stems from a lack of ministerial approval rather than constituting a trespass vis-à-vis the owner of the land. I must say that I find so widesweeping an assertion unattractive. Whilst the Petroleum Act 1923-1983 (Qld) requires ministerial authority to prospect, I am unable to read the Act and Regulations as creating an offence in the circumstances here prevailing, where XLP was authorised to explore the Windorah well for petroleum by both Abrolhos and XLX. Even if I am wrong in this, and assuming that the Minister had launched a successful prosecution against XLP for a breach of the Petroleum Act and had been fined, it is one thing to say that such a fine is not deductible; cf Mayne Nickless v FC of T 84 ATC 4458 (which held that parking fines, albeit necessarily incurred in a taxpayer's business operations, are not deductible), and quite another to argue that exploration costs, tainted by the kind of "illegality" alleged by Mr F, are disqualified from deduction under Division 10AA of the Tax Act. Let us take a homely example. Let us assume that a professional home renovator has been engaged to renovate a house. It is a Trust Home and requires ministerial approval for carrying out any alterations to the property. The renovator renovates the house, but it turns out the owner had failed to obtain ministerial consent. The result, if Mr F's argument is correct, is that whilst the proceeds from the renovation are subject to tax, the deductions otherwise allowable fail on the grounds of "illegality". With due respect to Mr F, I must reject so wide-sweeping a proposition. The Mayne Nickless case makes clear that fines are imposed as deterrents. The court was of the view that to permit the offender to share his/her burden with the revenue, i.e. to characterise fines as legitimate business deductions, would offend against public policy. That is a far cry from the position of this taxpayer.



39. On the other hand, to give sec 124AH(1) the wide interpretation claimed for it by Mr Sykes would provide a deduction to a petroleum prospector with no claim of right whatever, whilst the deduction available to the holder of a petroleum prospecting right is severely proscribed by sec 124AJ(1). That cannot be right. I am satisfied that subsec 124AH(1) must be read as limiting the deductibility of expenditure incurred on exploration or prospecting for the purpose of discovering petroleum to holders of a petroleum prospecting or mining rights. Having satisfied myself that a document which requires stamping by the revenue laws of Queensland is inadmissible in proceedings, even before a Tribunal not bound by the rules of evidence; cf the very strong pronouncement on the subject by Hill J in Davis v FC of T 89 ATC 4377, these exploration costs are thus disqualified from deduction, not because of any "illegality", but because XLP, having failed to get the XLP/XLX contract stamped and/or to obtain ministerial approval of its 50% transfer, is unable to establish that it is "the holder of a petroleum prospecting or mining right", nor otherwise able to bring itself within the ambit of sec 124AJ(1).



40. For the sake of completeness I will note that Mr F's final - and what appeared to be his principle - submission is grounded on the law of contractual joint venturers. The first case I was taken to was Muschinski v Dodds [1985] HCA 78; (1984-1985) 160 CLR 583. In that case, an unmarried couple purchased land pursuant to a contract under which they were jointly and severally liable. They intended to renovate the cottage on the land to be used by the woman for an arts and crafts centre and to build a prefabricated house upon it. The woman paid the price for the land from her own funds and agreed to include the man's name on the title if he undertook to renovate the cottage and pay for the prefabricated house. The land was transferred to them as tenants in common in equal shares. The parties separated without the cottage having been renovated or the house built. The woman claimed the sole beneficial ownership of the land. In the result, the whole court held that the presumption of resulting trust arising from the fact that the whole purchase price was paid by the woman was rebutted. For present purposes, I need only refer to the decision of Deane J, who (with Mason J as he then was) concluded that the parties held their respective legal interests as tenants in common upon trust to repay to each her or his contribution and as to the residue for them both in equal shares, because it was unconscionable after the failure of the joint venture for the man to assert his legal entitlement without recognising the woman's payment; cf Atwood v Maude (1868) 3 Ch App 369. Per Deane J at p. 619:



If the relevant relationship is not a partnership but takes the form of a contractual joint venture for the pursuit of some commercial advantage, a similar prima facie rule of equity applies in the event of the premature collapse of the joint venture and the consequent preclusion of the attainment of the commercial advantage, namely, that, to the extent that the joint funds allow, the joint venturers are entitled to the proportionate repayment of their capital contributions to the abortive joint venture. This is so notwithstanding that it was the common understanding or agreement that the funds advanced were to be applied for the purposes of the joint venture and that the return from them would take the form, not of a repayment of capital contributed but of a share in the proceeds for the joint venture when it was carried to fruition:, eg, Allen v Kent (1957) A.2d 540, at p 3541; Ewen v Gerofsky (1976) 382 NYS (2d) 651 at p 653; Legum Furniture Corporation v Levine (1977) 232 SE (2d) 782 at pp 785-786; and cf, generally, "Joint Ventures", Corpus Juris Secundum, vol. 48A, pp. 452-453, 463.



41. This gave Mr F the opening to refer me to the Corpus Juris Secundum vol 48A. The Restatement notes, at p 446, that:



Where one party to a joint venture is given managerial authority requiring the affirmative consent of another party only in specified cases, or is entrusted with control of the property of the enterprise or conduct of the venture, he not only stands in a fiduciary relationship to his co-adventurers but owes them one of the highest fiduciary duties recognised in law. Towards his associates such co-adventurer occupies the position of a trustee and he is obliged to use the utmost good faith in dealing with them.



42. I was also referred to a number of US authorities, including Kelly v Lang 62 NW (2d) 770 and the cases listed in 62 American Law Reports at p 23-24. One case - Drews v Drews (1925) 214 App Div 758, 209 NY Supp 82, summarised in the ALR - involved two brothers who had entered into a joint venture for the purchase of a home with their mother, each putting in his time and money, and one of them taking title to the property, attempted to appropriate it to his own use. Alas, the court held that this appropriation could not succeed. The case is said to illustrate that where one taking title to property in his own name, in pursuance of an agreement of a joint enterprise entered into with another, later conceives the idea of appropriating the proceeds of the enterprise to his sole benefit, the court will compel him to abide by his agreement.



43. What is sought to be deduced from these cases is the proposition that when one looks at the agreement between Abrolhos and XLX of 10 July 1980 as a whole, it is said to be a joint venture agreement to prospect for petroleum, rather than - as is submitted by the applicant - a contract pursuant to which Abrolhos merely acquires a 10% interest in ATP 270P. In a written submission in response to Mr F's legal argument, Mr Sykes seeks to persuade me "that this agreement is not all that there is and must be read in relationship to all other documents". Alas, given the parol evidence rule, extrinsic evidence is generally inadmissible if it would, if accepted, have the effect of adding to or vary a document constituting a valid and effective contract; cf Bank of Australasia v Palmer [1897] AC 540, at p 545. Although not strictly bound by the rules of evidence, I find the Abrolhos/XLX agreement is clear and unambiguous and contains nothing which would persuade me to accept extrinsic evidence as to its true meaning.



44. Once I accept Mr F's proposition that the Abrolhos/XLX agreement is indeed a joint venture agreement, it follows, Mr F submits, that:



Given that it is a joint venture and the joint venture involves - between the joint venturers - the drilling of the hole under the management of the operator, which was XLX, then XLX as operator, when funds were involved by Abrolhos, was holding the funds of the joint venture, and in so holding the funds of the joint venture, (it) had a fiduciary duty towards its co-venturer and (therefore held these funds) as trustee for the two co-venturers, and on that basis had no power in its own right to treat the monies as if they were its own.



45. I have little doubt that without the interposition of Abrolhos, (and leaving aside the failure to obtain ministerial approval and/or the stamping of the XLX/XLP contract of 14 May 1980), XLP could have legitimately claimed the deduction for the cost of exploration of the East Windorah Well. However, once Abrolhos acquired a 10% interest in the venture, the whole undertaking took on a different legal complexion. Thus the agreement between Abrolhos and XLX undoubtedly had the effect of bringing about a joint venture between the parties; cf clauses 1 and 2 of the Agreement (para 6 above). It follows that, contrary to Mr Sykes' assertion, XLX could not deal with the Abrolhos monies "as it liked". In my view these monies - earmarked for the exploration of the XL Flank East Windorah No 1 Well - were held by XLX in trust for the joint venturers. It follows that XLX could not lend these funds to XLP without the consent of its co-venturer Abrolhos. In the absence of such consent, it would appear that XLP must be deemed to have held these "loan" funds on a constructive trust; the fact that both companies were owned by Mr Sykes is irrelevant.



46. If I am correct in the above, it follows that whilst XLX was authorised to carry out the exploration "by itself or through its servants and agents and such independent contractors as it may engage", and notwithstanding that XLP drilled the hole "for and on behalf of XLX", XLX remained the "operator" for purposes of the Act - one does not become an "operator" merely pursuant to a cosy "loan" arrangement which had, as its sole purpose, the "transfer" of whatever available tax deduction enures for the benefit of the "operator". Nor does one become an "operator" simply by paying for the drilling operation. Once it is established that the $1,108,815 which XLP paid OD & E for the drilling the well had as their origin the "loan" from the joint venture, it is disqualified from claiming the deduction pursuant to sec 124AJ(1), if only because it fails to pass the requirements of the preamble of the section, viz



For purposes of this Division, where the holder of a petroleum prospecting or mining right has, for a consideration provided or to be provided by him, not being a payment of a share of income derived by him from the sale of petroleum ... not being a share of income derived by him from the sale of petroleum ... or a consideration by way of assignment or sub-lease of a petroleum prospecting or mining right, procured the performance of the work ...

The consideration receivable being "nil", Mr Sykes submitted the claim ran as follows:

$65,770



50. Subsec 59(1) provides:



Where any property of a taxpayer, in respect of which depreciation has been allowed ... is disposed of, lost or destroyed at any time in the year of income, the depreciated value of the property at that time, less the amount of any consideration receivable in respect of the disposal, loss or destruction, shall be an allowable deduction.



51. There can be little doubt that the plant in issue in this case was "disposed of" within the meaning of the section. As it was put in Henty House Pty Ltd v FC of T [1953] HCA 54; (1953) 88 CLR 141 at 110):



The entire expression `disposed of, lost or destroyed' is apt to embrace every event by which property ceases to be available to the taxpayer for use for the purpose of producing assessable income, either because it ceases to be his, or because it ceases to be physically accessible to him, or because it ceases to exist. In the context of s. 59 there is ample reason for rejecting a narrower construction.



52. Although the point was not relied on by the Commissioner, I am satisfied that the term "property" in subsec 59(1) cannot be applied to the sale of fixtures attached to land which have no value independently of the land itself and are sold as one piece of property. Indeed, it is a long-standing principle of law that when a parcel of land is sold with its fixed improvements, there is indeed only a disposition of one property. The Commissioner did not rely on - or even refer me to - the decision in Ferling v FC of T [1966] HCA 85; (1965-66) 115 CLR 603, presumably because the case runs counter his established policy. However, if Ferling governs the facts before me, I cannot be swayed by "policy" and must apply the law as I see it.



53. In Ferling differing opinions were expressed by some of their Honours on the question whether sec 59 applies to depreciation of units of property which are fixtures of no value outside the land on which they are erected, e.g. fences, water bores, etc. Suffice it to note that Owen J stated (at pp 649-650):



These contentions, if they be correct, certainly produce a surprising result but the answer to them is, in my opinion, to be found in the fact that the 'property' of which the taxpayer disposed was his interest in Springvale and that interest was not, within the meaning of s 59(1) or of s 59AA, property in respect of which depreciation had been allowed or was allowable. It is unreal to say that the fencing, water improvements and buildings in respect of which depreciation had been allowed or was allowable were 'property' sold with other assets or to attempt to distinguish between the land and the improvements which became an inseparable part of it. It would be just as unreal to say that the taxpayer had disposed of improvements such as 'timber treatment' and 'scrub pulling' at figures greatly in excess of the amounts shown against those items in the Rigby contract and had therefore made a substantial profit on the disposal of those improvements.



Taylor J noted, albeit by way of obiter dicta, that:



... it is necessary to point out that what s 59 deals with is 'property' of the taxpayer, upon which depreciation has been allowed or allowable, which is disposed of, lost or destroyed, at any time during the year of income. This, of course, raises the initial difficulty in the way of the appellant for it is difficult to regard improvements upon land which, although they add to the value of the land, do not in themselves constitute property and have no value independently of the land itself. The next difficulty is that, in spite of the apportionment of the purchase money which each agreement purported to make, the purchase price specified in each agreement was the consideration receivable in respect of the land in the condition in which it stood at the time of each sale. No part of it was the consideration receivable for the improvements which could not be, and therefore were not, made, independently, the subject of a sale. In these circumstances there is, in my view, much to be said for the proposition that the provisions of s 59 have no application to a case such as the present. I appreciate the difficulties which would flow from such a view but it is one to which, as at present advised, I would be prepared to subscribe since it seems to me to follow inevitably from the language of the section. (at pp 630-631).



A different view was expressed by Menzies J, his Honour stating (at p 635):



Nevertheless, it is inescapable that s. 59 does apply to some extent to 'plant' which is in reality part of the taxpayer's land. For instance, if a house has been built for the accommodation of employees and, after some years of depreciation, that house is destroyed by fire and insurance moneys are paid, it could not be questioned that s. 59 would apply. The difficulty with which we are concerned arises because disposals are within the scope of s. 59 and, when land is disposed of, that disposal carries with it all that is part of the land. There is something unreal in treating what is simply part of the property as a whole as disposed of 'with other assets'. The difficulty caused by the words 'is disposed of' in s. 59 is perhaps mitigated to some extent by the provisions of s. 59, subs. (3), which provides for the ascertainment of 'the consideration receivable in respect of the disposal, loss or destruction' of depreciated property. Thus, if an agricultural property on which there is a house which has been depreciated in accordance with s. 54 has been disposed of by sale, it seems to have been within the contemplation of those who framed s. 59 that the Commissioner could determine the value of the house for the purposes of s. 59 in circumstances where 'no separate value is allocated' (see s. 59(3)(c)). It would, I think, by yielding too much to difficulties of a formal nature and having too little regard to the substance of the matter to treat s. 59 as inapplicable in the case where a property is disposed of which consists in part of 'plant' upon which depreciation has been allowed in accordance with s. 54. I have, therefore, and against what I may say was my initial reaction, come to the conclusion that s. 59 does apply to cases such as the disposal of 'Springvale' ..."



54. A similar issue arose again in FC of T v Mullins 81 ATC 4643. I that case, the taxpayer (with his wife) purchased a farm and sold it within 12 months at a profit. The Commissioner taxed the husband's share of that profit in reliance on the (then) capital gains provisions contained in sec 26AAA. Mr Mullins claimed that the effect of sec 26AAA(5) was to exclude the profit from the sale of the land from assessability under sec 26AAA because depreciation had been allowed in the sum of $7 on fencing, and, on the sale of the farm by Mr Mullins, the $7 had been brought to account under sec 59(2) in calculating his assessable income. At first instance before Board of Review No 3 (Case L20 79 ATC 101), Mr Hogan (Chairman) and I in a joint decision held, in reliance on Ferling, that the profit was excluded from assessability under sec 26AAA by the terms of sec 26AAA(5)(b) because the fences, despite their treatment as "plant" for the purposes of the Act, were an inseparable part of the land.



55. The case went on appeal to the Supreme Court of Queensland (81 ATC 4192) and thence to the Full Federal Court, each Court dismissing the appeal. It is of interest to note that in the Federal Court, Deane J stated:



As has been indicated, the Commissioner expressly conceded that, following the sale, the amount representing recoupment of allowable depreciation in respect of fences was assessable income of the taxpayer pursuant to sec 59(2). This concession, no doubt, accorded with the Commissioner's established administration of the Act. With some hesitation, I am of the view that the Court should, for purposes of this appeal, accept the concession as properly made. (at pp 4645-6).



56. In light of that concession, it is not, perhaps, surprising that the decision of the Full Federal Court contains no reference to Ferling. Doing a headcount of Ferling, and noting that this Tribunal is bound by decisions of the High Court in pari materia, I am satisfied that the plant abandoned by XLP on the land when the garage was sold with the land formed an inseparable part of the land and cannot, for purposes of sec 59, be treated as "property" separately and apart from the "property" represented by the land itself. Since no attempt was made in this case to treat any of the items so abandoned other than as fixtures, I will not attempt to do so unaided. It follows that subsec 59(1) can have no application to this case.



57. In the circumstances, whilst I do not need to deal with the interesting argument presented to me by Mr F, viz that subsec 59(3)(c) applies in this case, with the effect that Commissioner was able to determine the consideration receivable for the abandoned fixtures, I do so out of courtesy to his well presented argument.



58. The meaning of the term "consideration receivable" in respect of the disposal, loss or destruction of depreciable property is, for present purposes, defined in subsec 59(3):



... the consideration receivable in respect of the disposal, loss or destruction means:



(a) in the case of a sale of the property - the sale price less the expenses of the sale of the property;

(b) ...

(c) in the case where the property is sold with other assets and no separate value is allocated to the property - the amount determined by the Commissioner;

(d) in the case where property is disposed of otherwise than by sale - the value, if any, of the property at the date of disposal.



59. Mr F submitted that, having placed a nil value on the plant sought to be depreciated, the taxpayer had failed to allocate a separate value to the property within subsec 59(3)(c). "Value", so it was submitted, must be expressed in money or moneys worth; there can be no "$nil" said Mr F; "value" can only be expressed in dollars or cents. No authority was cited for this proposition. I will merely content myself with the observation that I have not been persuaded by Mr F's submission on this aspect.



60. For the above reasons the objection decisions in respect of the 1981 and 1985 years of income are affirmed.
