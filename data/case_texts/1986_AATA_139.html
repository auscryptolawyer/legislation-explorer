Re Phillipa Christine Weeks and Commissioner of Superannuation [1986] AATA 139 (22 May 1986)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: PHILLIPA CHRISTINE WEEKS

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A85/129

AAT No. 2684

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.K. Todd (Deputy President)

Dr D.B. Travers (Member)

N.J. Attwood (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - benefit classification certificate - not likely to continue to be an eligible employee - otosclerosis

Superannuation Act 1976 - s. 16

Re Bewley and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 8 ALD 293

HEARING

CANBERRA

22:5:1986

ORDER

The Tribunal

(i) sets aside the decision under review, namely the

decision of a delegate of the above-named respondent

taken on 2 December 1985 varying a decision of a

delegate taken on 21 March 1984 to issue, pursuant

to s. 16(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976, a benefit

classification certificate in respect of the

above-named applicant specifying the condition

"hearing loss" and certain other conditions by

deleting such conditions and adding to it the

condition "otosclerosis"

(ii) in substitution therefore sets aside the

above-mentioned decision taken on 21 March 1984 to

issue a benefit classification certificate in

respect of the applicant.

DECISION

This is an application for review of a decision of a delegate of the above-named respondent. That decision varied a decision of a delegate to issue pursuant to s. 16(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") a Benefit Classification Certificate ("BCC") in respect of the applicant specifying the condition "hearing loss" and certain other conditions by deleting the condition "hearing loss" as well as the other conditions, and adding the condition "otosclerosis" to the BCC. These other conditions having been so deleted they are no longer in issue, and it is unnecessary to refer to them in any way. It is thus with a BCC specifying the condition "otosclerosis" that we are now concerned.

2. Section 16 of the Act so far as relevant is as follows:

"(2) The Commissioner may, for the purposes of

this section, require a person (other than a person

to whom section 184 applies) who proposes to become

or becomes an eligible employee to undergo, within

such period as the Commissioner specifies, such

medical examination or examinations by an approved

medical practitioner or practitioners as the

Commissioner determines.

(3) A report or reports of the result or results

of the medical examination or examinations shall be

furnished to the Commissioner.

(4) The Commissioner shall consider the report or

reports, and such other matters (if any) as the

Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of

the opinion that the person is not likely, by

reason of or for a reason connected with a physical

or mental condition or conditions referred to in

the report or reports, to continue to be an

eligible employee until the person attains his

maximum retiring age, the Commissioner shall issue

a benefit classification certificate to that

effect, being a certificate in which the relevant

condition or conditions is or are specified.

(5) In the application of this Act to an eligible

employee at any time, a reference in this Act to a

benefit classification certificate that is in force

in respect of an eligible employee shall, in the

case of an eligible employee whose period of

contributory service would, if he ceased to be an

eligible employee at that time, be not less than 20

years or an eligible employee who has attained his

maximum retiring age, be read as not including a

reference to a benefit classification certificate that has been issued

in respect of him.

3. The applicant was born in April 1953 and became an "eligible employee" in January 1983 on her appointment as a lecturer within the Australian National University ("ANU") in the Faculty of Law. She had been teaching in that Faculty since 1979. In 1981 the applicant had become aware of a degree of hearing loss, and having been referred by her general practitioner to Dr Hilton Stone, who is a specialist otolaryngologist, his diagnosis was that her condition was most likely "otosclerosis". In October 1984 Dr Stone was, for the purposes of a reconsideration by the Commissioner of the decision to issue a BCC including the condition "Hearing Loss", asked to report on the applicant's condition. He reported as follows:

"I saw Miss Weeks on the 30th of June 1981 for

assessment of a recently noted hearing loss.

Examination showed her to have a mild hearing loss which

I attributed to otosclerosis that is poor conduction of

sound vibrations via one of the three bones involved in

transmission of such vibrations to the inner ear. She

was subsequently reviewed six months later and her

hearing then had shown a slight deterioration.

Otosclerosis is a type of hearing loss usually occurring

in young adults and can continue to progress to cause

quite significant problems though in most cases the

defect plateaus out and can be satisfactorily managed

with a hearing aid or possibly by surgery. Apart from

the hearing loss and occasional noise in the ear it is

not known to affect a person's fitness in any other way;

clearly in an individual case, it is not possible to

prognosticate the severity of the hearing loss that can

be expected.

. . ."

4. The General Medical Standard that was in force at the time of the delegate's decision to add the condition "otosclerosis" to the BCC was as follows:

"14. Ear, Nose, Throat and Mouth Diseases

. . .

(2) Ears

(a) Standard met where an applicant uses a hearing

aid provided a specialist report indicates

that he is likely to retain effective hearing

(with the aid) until normal retiring age and

the duties of his office do not require normal

hearing (unaided).

(b) Specialist advice should be obtained where a

hearing defect or other ear condition exists

which may have a future prejudicial effect on

the fitness of the applicant

. . .

(3) Nose, mouth and throat

. . .

(b) Standard met where a person has hay fever or

allergic rhinitis which is not severe."

5. The General Medical Standard is of course a guide only, but at all events having regard to the Standard quoted the delegate took the view that a person with a hearing aid would meet that standard only if a specialist report indicated that effective hearing (with the aid) was likely to be retained until normal retiring age. Dr Stone's report was seen as clearly falling short of such a prognosis. Reference having also been made to the possibility of surgery, a senior medical officer had recommended that the condition "otosclerosis" should be retained on the BCC. The delegate had also had regard to the applicant's occupation as a university lecturer, and had formed the view that there could be a serious obstacle to her ability to continue to perform her duties in the event of a total or very substantial hearing impairment. In view of what was seen to be the unpredictable nature of the applicant's condition, in particular the possibility that the condition could deteriorate, the delegate formed the opinion that the applicant was "not likely by reason of or for a reason connected with the condition 'otosclerosis', to continue to be an eligible employee until she attains her maximum retiring age". Accordingly, while the condition "hearing loss" that had originally been included in the BCC was deleted, the condition "otosclerosis" was added.

6. After postgraduate studies the applicant had been appointed to the ANU as a Lecturer in the Law School, as previously stated, in 1983. She had detected some hearing loss in March 1981 as a result of difficulties experienced in tutorials. She explained that her duties within the faculty were distributed between formal lecturing, tutorials, seminars, research (including preparation for teaching), and administration. The time spent in formal lectures was, and was expected to continue to be, no more than about 6 - 8 hours per week. She also said that while nothing is laid down in contractual terms, guidelines used in relation to promotion criteria envisage a breakdown roughly along the lines of 40 per cent teaching and 40 per cent research, with the remaining 20 per cent divided between administration and "extra campus" activities related to employment. The applicant said that the research and administration components of her work take up more time than teaching. The ANU Law School has a policy of keeping class sizes small, and a compulsory subject with a large number of students is divided into smaller groups. The largest lecture theatre accomodates about 100 persons.

7. The applicant has been wearing a hearing aid since late 1981. Until late 1984 she used an older type hearing aid in one ear only. About 18 months ago she upgraded to a more modern and sophisticated hearing aid, and accepted advice to wear an aid in each ear, that is to say a binaural hearing aid. This has improved, she said, her discrimination in locating sound, so that when a student asks a question in class she knows where to look. Previously, she had to ask a questioner to wave, because although she heard the sound she did not know in which direction to look. She has other assistance, such as an amplification device on the telephone.

8. The applicant states, and we accept, that since she commenced to wear a hearing aid she has not noticed any impairment of her functions across the range of her duties in her employment. The evidence of Dr Stone, referred to below, strongly supports that this should be so. The crucial question is of course the extent to which, down the years, there may be deterioration, for upon this prognosis hangs the question whether the opinion should be formed that the applicant is not likely, by reason of the condition otosclerosis, to continue to be an eligible employee until her maximum retiring age.

9. Dr Hilton Stone, who as previously stated had examined the applicant in 1981, had also seen her in January and November 1982 and again two days before this hearing, which took place on 30 April 1986. In the circumstances his evidence is crucial, and constitutes the whole of the medical evidence to which the Tribunal should have regard. Dr Stone said that having examined the applicant again and conducted hearing tests it was apparent that there had been a further deterioration in her hearing between 1981 and 1986. Using the National Acoustic Laboratory Tables of 1976, the degree of hearing loss was expressed as having been 17.9 per cent in 1981, whereas in 1986 it was 34.8 per cent. He said that the relevant kind of hearing loss could "plateau out" or could be part of a steady deterioration. Dr Stone referred to two major series of statistics, one from the United Kingdom and one from the United States of America. The United Kingdom statistics suggested an 85 per cent chance of a steady deterioration, and the US statistics a 75 per cent chance of such. He emphasised again however that a deterioration could "plateau out", or alternatively could "nose dive", at any time. Both series indicated that 5 per cent of patients would go on to a severe to profound hearing loss. The figures presented in the series indicated to Dr Stone that deterioration may bring the applicant down to a moderate to severe hearing loss, but that it was unlikely to become a severe to profound hearing loss. A moderate to severe loss was said to be represented by a loss in the area of 60 to 90 decibels ("dB"), a profound loss being represented by a loss worse than 90 dB. The applicant's loss is now in the area of 45-50 dB in the range of speech frequencies, whereas in 1981 it was in the area of 30-35 dB. Around 40 dB is the stage at which, Dr Stone said, it would be difficult to function adequately without help in the form of a hearing aid. Another option is surgery, and he said that the criteria for surgery are all met in the applicant's case. There is an approximately 85 per cent chance that her hearing would be significantly improved by surgery, a 12 to 13 per cent chance of a lesser degree of improvement, and a 1 to 3 per cent chance that her hearing could be made worse.

10. Dr Stone said that hearing aids are now fairly sophisticated and that they can offer a lot of benefit even in a severly to profoundly deaf person. The fidelity of them was said to be being continually improved. New types of amplification circuits were being brought in with the aim of filtering out a lot of unwanted low frequency noise such as shuffling of feet. The gain of a hearing aid can be 40, 50, or 60 dB, so that in theory a hearing loss that has deteriorated to 90 dB can be effectively brought back to 30 dB. The problem with hearing aids is that they are, or rather have been, non-specific in what they amplify. In an auditorium where there is a lot of background noise that noise is amplified disconcertingly. Turning the hearing aid off can lead to decrease in intensity of the voice. That would be the case in the applicant's situation, where the nerve of her ear is good. Where there is on the other hand sensori-neural loss the voice tends to get louder. Nevertheless Dr Stone said that he considered that with experience the applicant could turn the hearing aid off in a noisy lecture theatre and learn to modulate her voice. In any event, we have noted above the improvements that are being made, and that can be expected, in the performance of hearing aids in this regard. The applicant is not in the category of moderate to severe loss at the moment, but if there came to be a loss in the range 60 to 90 dB a hearing aid should bring her back to an acceptable level, excepting the stated problem of a lot of background noise.

11. Asked whether the applicant might enter the moderate to severe range, Dr Stone said that "one tends to talk about decades rather than single numbered years in the condition of otosclerosis, so within a couple of decades she may well be in that moderate to severe loss range, yes". The only problem with the applicant's hearing is in the conduction of sound from the eardrum to the inner ear, and "this degree of hearing loss cannot get worse than 60 decibels. For it to go worse than 60 decibels there has to be deterioration in the nerve . . . There is no indication at the moment that this has happened". Dr Stone said that he would expect there to be some degree of hearing loss due to the nerve, but unless this were the dominant feature it would not be possible to get a severe to profound hearing loss. There was probably one chance in four of her having trouble with the nerve component, but she would be unlucky, he said, to get into that sort of trouble.

12. Dr Stone considered that, in assessing as a matter of probability the question whether the applicant was likely to be able to continue her work if she should desire to persist in it until her retiring age, it was a situation where motivation came into it as well. If she were highly or well motivated it was more probable than not that she would function satisfactorily.

13. The question before the Tribunal is not one simply as to whether the applicant's hearing loss will worsen or not. The question is whether having regard to the circumstances of the applicant's employment in her chosen career, and to the medical evidence indicating the likely progression or otherwise of her hearing loss and the extent to which that loss can be ameliorated, it can be said that by reason of, or for a reason connected with, otosclerosis, the applicant is not likely to continue to be an eligible employee until she attains her maximum retiring age. It follows that the issue before the Tribunal raises the question that was fully discussed in the Reasons for Decision in Re Bewley and Commissioner for Superannuation (1985) 8 ALD 293. It was there held that in order to form the opinion referred to in s. 16 of the Act there must be a positive satisfaction that the person in question is not likely to continue in employment until retirement, that is to say it must be more probable than not that the person in question will not continue to his or her maximum retiring age by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition. On this footing it is therefore not enough for the purpose of forming the requisite opinion that there be no more than a real or substantial risk that the person will not attain maximum retiring age.

14. Mr Peters offered in argument an alternative possible construction of s. 16(4) which was not considered by the Tribunal in Re Bewley. This was that there was a grammatical analysis of s. 16(4) of the Act which if correct would cast doubt on the approach adopted in Re Bewley. As we understood it, the submission was that the words "is not . . . to continue to be" are a "compound verb"; that the word "likely" is an adverb modifying that verb, or alternatively that the words "likely, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the report or reports" is an adverbial phrase modifying that verb; that it is permissible to split a "compound verb" for the purpose of attaching an adverb or adverbial phrase to part of it; and that such a splitting had been adopted in the relevant portion of s. 16(4) so that the word "not" was conceptually connected not with the word "likely", but rather with the words "to continue to be".

15. There appear to us to be a number of difficulties with this submission. We think it enough however to say that in grammatical terms the word "not" is an adverb. As the Shorter Oxford Dictionary puts it, it is "the ordinary adverb of negation". We are not clear as to what was meant by the term "compound verb" or as to the characteristics of such an entity; perhaps it might ordinarily be understood to mean an auxiliary verb together with a participle, but this meaning is not applicable in the present case. At all events it seems basically unsound, if not eclectic, to remove certain words from an adverbial phrase and to regard the one remaining as having a special attachment to the verb which the entire adverbial phrase was intended to qualify. While the English language is flexible as to the placement of different parts of a sentence, generally speaking an adverb or adverbial phrase will immediately precede the verb that it is modifying. Alternatively, where there are one or more auxiliary verbs, the adverb will be found immediately after the first auxiliary verb. That order may be changed for the purpose of emphasis. It remains to be said, however, that both "not" and "likely" are adverbs, and since an adverb may modify either a verb, an adjective or another adverb (see Shorter Oxford Dictionary for "adverb"), and bearing in mind the ordinary principles relating to placement of adverbs, it seems to us clear that the word "not" was intended to qualify the word "likely", and that that was how the phrase was interpreted in Re Bewley.

16. It is convenient here to mention another point made by Mr Peters. This was that the "real risk" test, which had been rejected in Re Bewley, "better achieves protection of the fund than does the more probable than not test and section 16(4) should be seen as primarily a provision for protecting the fund". That the fund should be protected against the assertion of rights that were not intended to be within the provisions of the Act is undoubted. But the primary question is the rights intended to be created by the Act, not the protection of the fund by limiting claims as far as possible. That question can only be answered by construing the words of the Act, in this case by construing in particular the words of s. 16(4). Despite the length of the discussion of the point in Re Bewley, a length dictated not least by the importance of the point and by the full and careful submissions that were put by Mr Peters on behalf of the Commissioner in that case, in the last resort the Reasons for Decision therein reveal that the Tribunal considered that the critical words in s. 16(4) have an unambiguous and clear meaning according to the ordinary understanding of the English language. That being so, there was seen to be no alternative to the construction adopted. The final general comment may be made that as far as concerns the object, if object there be, of protecting the Fund, the long title of the Act is "An Act to make provision for and in relation to an Occupational Superannuation Scheme, for persons employed by the Commonwealth, and for certain other persons". The objects of the Act should be seen as including the protection not only of the Fund, but also of the rights of those by whose contributions it is, in part, comprised and for whose benefit it was created.

17. The Tribunal as presently constituted is of the opinion that it should follow the decision in Re Bewley. In these circumstances we are equally clearly of the view that on the evidence placed before us the requisite opinion that the applicant is not likely by reason of the condition "otosclerosis" to attain maximum retiring age should not be formed. There is certainly a substantial chance that the applicant's hearing will deteriorate to the stage of a moderate to severe loss. Indeed it is, we consider, more probable than not that it will. There is only a very small chance that it will deteriorate to a severe to profound loss, but in any event, in the light of the evidence as to the ways in which the applicant can ameliorate the extent to which her hearing loss will have an adverse effect on her duties, we are of the view that there is only a small risk that the applicant will not, by reason of her condition of otosclerosis, be able to complete a normal working life, and that rather than it being more probable than not that she will not, by reason of that condition, continue to maximum retiring age, it is, assessing the matter in terms of that condition, far more probable than not that she will, despite that condition. It is obvious that the most significant factor in amelioration of the condition will be the wearing of a hearing aid, and there was evidence that continuing refinement and greater effectiveness of such aids will lead to increasing benefit. Nor will the applicant be confined to mechanical aids for the purpose of making the necessary adjustments to enable her to carry on a normal working life. Dr Stone agreed that the applicant's own motivation, a motivation that was apparent to us in her presentation before the Tribunal, will be a vital factor. It is also clear that her chosen profession will afford her a high degree of flexibility in choosing modes of teaching and research, a flexibility in work modes not always available in other areas of employment. In all the circumstances we are of the view that we should not form the opinion that the applicant is not likely, by reason of the condition of otosclerosis, to continue to be an eligible employee until she attains her maximum retiring age, and accordingly the decision to include such a condition in the BCC should be set aside. That being the only remaining condition, the decision to issue the BCC should be set aside, in the formal terms of the Decision that accompanies these Reasons.
