Re Denys Andre Leon Lennox Clarke and Commissioner of Taxation [1990] AATA 240; 21 ATR 3736 (15 October 1990)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: DENYS ANDRE LEON LENNOX CLARKE

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. NT90/108

AAT No. 6288

Income Tax

[1990] AATA 240; 21 ATR 3736

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

B.J. McMahon(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Annuity s 27H(4) Income Tax Assessment Act - Whether "Variable income annuity" policy is an annuity contract within the meaning of the Act - Whether entitled to concessional method of assessment provided for in s 27H - Assessable income s 27H(1) - Deductible amount s 27H(2) - Legal meaning of annuity - Review of basic legal requirements - Whether "sum certain" - Objection decision under review affirmed

HEARING

SYDNEY

15:10:1990

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the objection decision under review.

DECISION

The applicant asked that these proceedings should be heard in public and this procedure accordingly was followed. He retired as a teacher on 17 July 1987, the day before his 60th birthday. He received a lump sum payout from the State Superannuation Fund on 27 July 1987. This sum was invested in various securities by way of roll-over. The present application concerns an investment of $50,000 with Capita Financial Group Ltd in a security or policy named a Variable Income Annuity. The policy, written on 28 July 1987, was for a term of 5 years. In consideration of a stated purchase price of $50,000, annuity payments of $133-34 per month ($10,000 per year) were to be made on the 28th day of August and on the corresponding day in each subsequent month. Additional terms were set out in the Conditions annexed to the schedule to the policy. As reference will be made to these conditions in detail, it is necessary to set them out in full -

2. The applicant gave evidence that he made extensive enquiries before deciding to invest part of his superannuation in this policy. Although he interviewed Mr McCormack of Capita, he relied eventually upon advice given to him by his financial adviser. He was fully conversant with the conditions and discussed them with Mr McCormack. He was particularly attracted by the varying levels of payment provided for in Condition 6. He said that he understood a minimum level of payments ($10,000 per year) was set, but that upwards variations would be available without any difficulty so long as there was a credit balance in the capital fund.

3. He said that he needed a cashflow between the ages of 60 and 65 and for this reason took out a policy for a term of 5 years. At the age of 65, he intended to roll the policy over into another acceptable security. In the meantime he appreciated that he could cash in the policy should the need arise. He also appreciated that the value of that policy would increase in accordance with the investment performance of Capita. He believed that its then reputation was that it was one of the best performing funds available. At the end of the 5 years he would take advantage of this investment performance by receiving credit for any capital growth and credited income.

4. One of the big attractions of the arrangement was the taxation advantages which the applicant perceived, on advice that he received from his financial consultants. The schedule to the policy contained 2 clauses, misdescribed as special conditions. The first clause said that the undeducted purchase price of the annuity was $50,000 and that "this information will assist you in your taxation return". The second condition stated : "The annuity payment to an annuitant shall be such that this policy continues to be an eligible annuity within the meaning of s 27A of the Income Tax Assessment Act". This was a reference to the definition of "Eligible Annuity" in the section referred to and to the general taxing provisions relating to annuities to be found in s 27H. The relevant parts of that section are as follows -

"s 27H(1) (Assessable income) The assessable

income of a taxpayer of a year of income shall include -

(a) the amount of any annuity

derived by the taxpayer during the year of

income excluding, in the case of an

annuity that has been purchased, any

(b) the amount of any payment made

5. Between August 1987 and May 1988 the applicant was paid annuity payments of $8,333-40. On 30 April 1988 he was credited with an amount of $4,443-16, which was referred to as "interest", and which was allocated pursuant to Condition 15. In his assessment for the year ended 30 June 1988, these two amounts were added back to the income he returned. The amount of $8,333 was described as "income from Capita Financial". The amount of $4,443 was described as "interest". Both were included in the applicant's assessable income. Following considerable negotiations, an amended assessment was issued in which the amount of $8,333 was excluded. The applicant objected to the inclusion of the amount of $4,443 in his assessable income and claimed to be entitled to have that amount taxed in accordance with the terms of s 27H. The respondent took the view that the policy was not an annuity contract and that the applicant was therefore not able to take advantage of the concessional method of assessment provided for in s 27H.

6. In presenting his case before me, the applicant's first position was that he had complied with Income Tax Ruling 2480. This was a Ruling issued by the respondent on 16 June 1988. It set out the Commissioner's view of the type of contract of which the present policy was an example. In paragraphs 15 and 16 he said -

"Para 15 Despite the terminology used,

contracts that display the above

features do not provide for the payment

of annuities. The authorities show that

the description given to a contract under

which an annuity is purported to be purchased

is not conclusive that it is an annuity -

"The name given to payments by the

parties to the transaction is of

course not conclusive as to their true

nature and in considering whether they

are of an income or of a capital

character the court is entitled to

have regard not only to the

contractual documents, but to all the

facts which are relevant for

determination of the question."

Lord Fraser of Tullybelton in IR

Commrs v Plummer (1979) 3 All ER 775

at p 793.

Para 16 The essential feature of the contracts

examined is that the capital sum invested is

never lost sight of. It is contained in an

account the balance of which at any time

represents that investment plus the purchaser's

share of the issuer's actual performance (based

on the investment balance) less any withdrawals

previously made by the purchaser (or loss of

capital where it is not guaranteed) and any

charges or fees levied. The liability of the

issuer is, at all times, referable to and limited

to the balance of the funds at the particular

time. Once the investment account is exhausted

the contract is terminated. Furthermore, the

level of payments made out of the fund is at the

investor's discretion. The amount of a payment

is not determinable from the contract. The

arrangements are not materially different to a

term deposit, subject to variable interest rates,

the sums drawn therefrom depending upon agreement

between the parties."

7. In the same Ruling, the Commissioner indicated that he would be prepared to allow holders of unacceptable contracts to convert them within a given period. In paragraph 29 of the Ruling he said -

"Para 29 It has been claimed that investors in

products that have been marketed as

variable annuities have done so in good

faith and in the expectation of the

taxation results claimed to be available

by the issuers, that much of the money

invested has been eligible termination

payment money, and that many investors

are retired persons. Because of this,

the action proposed in para 26 and 27

will not be taken for the year ended 30

June 1988 and subsequent years in respect

of any particular investor where, within

90 days of the date of this Ruling, that

investor terminates his/her existing

contract and invests the total proceeds

in an annuity that is the subject of an

"eligible policy" within the meaning of

the Taxation Laws Amendment Act 1988."

8. That period of 90 days was subsequently extended by one month.

9. The applicant gave evidence that he terminated his policy with Capita on 13 October 1988, without any difficulty, and was credited with an amount of $44,303-60 which he rolled over in to an eligible policy with the same company. This was not contested by the respondent. The applicant said that having complied with the Ruling, he should not now be taxed in the year ended 30 June 1988 on the amount of interest credited to his account but should be given the benefit of the Commissioner's expressed policy.

10. The applicant was not legally represented. Nevertheless I am satisfied that he understood that Rulings issued by the Commissioner do not have the force of law even though, in my experience, some people are misled by the name given to these opinions. If the Commissioner is correct in his view of the law as set out in paragraph 16, it is a little difficult to see how he can exempt taxpayers from the consequences of the law by an administrative arrangement, as set out in paragraph 29. It is even more difficult to understand why, having gone to the trouble of making such an administrative arrangement, the Commissioner denies the procedure to a taxpayer who has apparently complied with the arrangement. There were many conferences and exchanges of correspondence between the applicant and the respondent on this question. It seems to me, however, that I have no jurisdiction to embark upon an enquiry as to these matters. I must take the facts to which I have referred, and about which there is little dispute, and consider whether in law, the assessment under review is excessive.

11. Whether the Commissioner issued a Ruling which is misleading in law, whether the Commissioner failed to follow his own administrative guidelines, whether the Commissioner is in fact guilty of "wrong conduct" (within the meaning of s 15 of the Ombudsman Act) is a matter which may be considered by the Ombudsman. Although not legally represented the applicant was articulate and was conscious of his rights. I have no doubt that this matter will be pursued.

12. Whether or not the assessment under consideration is to be affirmed however depends, in my view, upon whether the variable income annuity policy is an annuity contract. The fact that the issuing house calls the contract an annuity does not make it so (per Pollock B in HRH Nizam State Railway Co v Wyatt (1890) 24 QBD 548 at 552). It is necessary to look at "the real nature of the transaction" (Scoble v Secretary of State for India (1903) 1 QB 494, confirmed in the House of Lords and reported at 1903 AC 299 at 302). More recently the High Court has observed in Atkinson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1951] HCA 64; (1951) 84 CLR 298 that whether certain payments form an annuity or not is governed by substantial considerations disclosing the true nature of those payments. Similar observations were made in Egerton-Warburton v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1934] HCA 40; 51 CLR 568 at 572 (in which their Honours quote verbatim, without acknowledgement, from the judgment of Rowlatt J in Jones v Commissioners of Inland Revenue (1920) 1 KB 711 at 714).

13. It is therefore necessary to look at the conditions, and at the way in which those conditions were expected to be interpreted and administered, in deciding whether the real nature of the transaction is that of an annuity. The only definition in the Act of that word is to be found in s 27H(4). This is of no assistance in the present circumstances.

14. The applicant submitted that the meaning of annuity should not be arrived at by reference to past decisions and past practices. Account should be taken, he submitted, of the dynamics of language and the changing meaning which the financial community attributes to the term. A sales document from Pembroke Financial Planners Limited, which was tendered by the applicant, indicated a wide range of investments that is now available and which are marketed under the names of annuities. These include -

"Last Survivor Annuity -

Payments continue after the death of the

first person either at the same or at a

reduced rate, then cease on the death of

the second person.

Last Survivor Annuity - with

guaranteed period -

Identical to a Last Survivor Annuity but

payments continue for a guaranteed

period, usually 5 or 10 years, even if

both annuitants die.

Single Life Annuity - both with

or without guaranteed period -

A Single Life Annuity is on one person's

life only and, unless there is a guaranteed

period, would normally be considered too

high a gamble as payments cease on death.

Capital Protected Annuity -

Payments until death, when any remaining

part of the purchase price is refunded.

However, this type of annuity is costly and

means reduced income and it still counts as

an asset under the Assets Test.

Joint Life Annuity -

Not generally recommended because

payments cease when either party dies.

Escalating Annuity -

Payments increase by a nominated percentage

to counteract inflation.

Indexed Annuity -

Payments rise in line with the CPI to

provide an inflation hedge. But indexing is

costly - a flat rate annuity initially pays

almost twice as much income.

Term Certain Annuity -

Payments are made for a set period (say, 10

years) and then cease. Uses include early

retirement, divorce settlements and school fees."

15. The use of a technical term for marketing purposes however does not, in my view, change the meaning of the term. In Gamer's Motor Centre (Newcastle) Pty Ltd v Natwest Wholesale Australia Pty Ltd (1985) 3 NSWLR 475, Kirby P., in dissent, cautioned against what he referred to as the "encrustation" of ordinary words with legal doctrines. The majority judges, however, considered that the intention of the Parliament had been to depart from the popular sense of the words in question and use them in their legal sense. At pages 483 and 484 Priestly J.A. said -

"In considering the appropriate meaning of the words in their

setting, it is my view that if there is one ordinary and natural

meaning of the words then that meaning must be given to them, but if

as is the case here the words have a range of meanings, then the

construction to be given to the words used must take into account

the legal as well as the "ordinary" uses to which they have been

put. No matter how hard a draughtsman tries to keep the language of

a statute clear and simple, the statute is a legal document. The

Sale of Goods Act 1923 ("the Act") is a legal instrument using words

with legal significance in an overall context where all concerned

with its passage through Parliament knew the past history of the

words used in it and knew also that the meaning to be put upon the

words in cases of such doubt as would lead to litigation would be

decided by lawyers. Thus when this court comes to consider the

meaning of the words in s 28 of the Act, it seems to me necessary to

make the kind of survey made by McHugh J.A. in his reasons (ie a close

analysis of the cases interpreting the words in question). The

object of the approach is not to find the legal as opposed to the

"ordinary" meaning but to find from the range of legal and ordinary

meanings, which in any event will seldom be in watertight compartments,

the meanings best suited to the statutory document as a whole."

16. In my view, the word "annuity" has a legal meaning and it must therefore be taken that the legislature has intended to use that word with that meaning, there being no contrary intention clearly appearing from the context. What is that meaning?

17. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Knight 83 ATC 4096 at 4106, Kelly J (at first instance) reviewed the basic legal requirements of an annuity -

"In DFC of Land Tax, Sydney v Hindmarsh [1912] HCA 27; (1912) 14 CLR 334 at p 338

Barton J said -

"'What then is the meaning of 'annuity' as a legal or technical

term? According to Co Litt 144b, an annuity is 'a yearly payment of

a certain sum of money granted to another in fee for life or years,

charging the person of the grantor only'. Viner's Abridgment, vol

II, p 504, repeats the definition, with further passages showing

that the sum need not be payable each year if only it is a yearly

sum. Bacon's Abridgment, vol I, p 233, says that 'an annuity'

strictly taken, is a yearly payment of a certain sum of money

granted to another in fee simple, fee tail, or life or years,

charging the person of the grantor only: if payable out of lands, it

is properly called a rent-charge; but if both the person and estate

be made liable, as they most commonly are, then it is generally

called an annuity.'

The text books generally adopt the definition in Co Litt; no

case was found in which any other definition was offered; nor any

case in which an indeterminate sum was held to be an annuity.

It is therefore a characteristic of an annuity that it be of a sum

certain."

Isaacs J also adopted the definition in Co Litt, 144b, and went on

to say at p 340 -

'... in saying an annuity charges the person only, that is merely

as to its primary effect. It may also be charged on the land, and

... an action for debt will in certain cases lie for a rent charge.'

It follows, I think, that payment of the sum agreed to be paid as an

annuity need not in all cases be charged upon land or personalty of

the grantor for an action may lie upon the personal covenant of the

grantor. This accords with the definition of the word "annuity" in

Jowitt's Dictionary of English Law, 2nd ed, where it is defined to

mean "a yearly payment of a certain sum of money granted to another

in fee, or for life, or for a term of years, either payable under a

personal obligation of the grantor or charged upon his pure

personalty, although it may be a charge upon his freehold or

leasehold land ..."

The element of certainty in the sum payable is sufficiently

satisfied, I think, because by calculation and upon the happening of

certain events it becomes certain even though it may vary from year

to year. The basis figure is certain. It represents 51.75% of Mr

Knight's final salary. The provisions of Pt X of the 1976 Act

ensure that Mr Knight's entitlement to a pension may be calculated

with certainty upon the publication of the relevant all groups

consumer price index number and the application of the provisions of

Pt X. The amount of the pension is no more uncertain than was the

rent in Ex parte Voisey; In re Knight (1882) 21 Ch D 442 at p 458,

per Brett L.J. (as he then was)."

18. It will therefore be seen that an annuity must provide for a "sum certain" but that the dollar amount may be varied by reference to a formula, so long as the formula is certain in its application. In my view, the policy under consideration does not comply with these basic requirements for 7 reasons, notwithstanding the fact that in Condition 1 the document is described as an "annuity certain policy".

19. Firstly, the reality and substance of the transaction is that it is not the sale of an annuity to the applicant for a capital sum which disappears, to be replaced by annuity payments over a stipulated period. The purchase price is credited to an investment account (Condition 8) presumably in the name of the applicant. An annual statement is prepared and sent to the policy owner each year showing the amount of the investment account calculated in accordance with the conditions attached to the policy (Condition 24). The transaction therefore has the hallmarks of a term deposit with capital and income benefits accruing to the investor. The capital sum diminishes with withdrawals but is still available to the purchaser, subject to certain conditions, on early withdrawal.

20. Secondly, the account continues to exist except to the extent that it is depleted by withdrawals, and increased by earnings of a capital or income nature. It can not, therefore, be characterised as a purchase price, which would become the property of the vendor.

21. Thirdly, annuity payments cease if the balance of the investment account becomes negative (Conditions 20 and 18). This is inconsistent with the element of certainty which an annuitant is entitled to expect.

22. Fourthly, payments of annuity may be varied by the application of Condition 6. Although the terms of the clause require agreement on the part of both parties, it is clear from the evidence that provided the extent of the payments is increased, and provided that there is a balance remaining to the annuitant's credit in the investment account, then no objection will be raised to the anticipated variations. This again is contrary to the requirement of a sum certain.

23. Fifthly, the element of certainty in arriving at the cash value of the policy, pursuant to Condition 18, is not present. Ongoing expenses charged pursuant to Condition 11 may be varied, pursuant to Condition 13. Administration charges may be varied pursuant to Condition 25. The allocation of surplus can be cancelled or reduced by the issuing house. Indeed, the whole of the basis of arriving at cash value may, according to Condition 18, be varied or suspended on certain conditions. The rate of investment income is not fixed by reference to any formula. According to Condition 16, it is varied by reference to the investment performance of the fund. This leaves the "rate of interest" entirely at large at any given time. For all of these reasons the number of variables render nugatory any element of certainty in calculating capital worth, which an annuitant is entitled to expect.

24. Sixthly, the amount of the annuity is exactly the same as the amount of "purchase price" paid by the annuitant. The nature of an annuity is that payments are calculated by reference to life expectation or to the likelihood of the happening of other events during the proposed term of the annuity. Simply to provide that payments of an annuity are the same as the purchase price spread out over the period of an annuity, is to characterise the transaction as a deposit and repayment of capital.

25. Seventhly, this construction is reinforced by a consideration of Condition 20. Once the capital has been repaid to the annuitant, either at the agreed or at an accelerated rate, the policy is terminated. If it is terminated in any other circumstances, the uncertain cash value to which reference has been made will be payable. There is therefore not an income stream that is characteristic of an annuity. There is simply repayment of a capital sum together with interest earned on the funds deposited by the annuitant.

26. That being so, the arrangement made between the applicant and the issuing house must be seen in reality to be an arrangement for depositing funds at interest, at any rate during the year ended 30 June 1988. From a legal point of view therefore, the amount of interest was correctly included in the applicant's assessable income. The objection decision under review is therefore affirmed.
