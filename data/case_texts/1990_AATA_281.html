Re Jennifer Margaret Bainrot and Commissioner of Superannuation [1990] AATA 281 (20 November 1990)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: JENNIFER MARGARET BAINROT

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A89/189

AAT No. 6420

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.K. Todd(1) (Deputy President), N. Attwood(1) (Member) and Dr E. Stephenson(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - issue of benefit classification certificate - applicant involved in two car accidents - failure to disclose injury sustained in earlier car accident - applicant pain-free for some time - earlier injury posed minimal and not a real risk to attainment of maximum retirement age

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.16(2)(4)(11), 66(2)(c)

Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1990

Re Nicholson and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 14 ALD 217

Re Rowlands and Commissioner for Superannuation (1988) 16 ALD 589

Re Gelsthorpe and Commissioner for Superannuation (1988) 18 ALD 793

Re Bergin and Commissioner for Superannuation (1989) 19 ALD 279

HEARING

CANBERRA

20:11:1990

ORDER

The Tribunal varies the decision under review namely the decision to issue pursuant to s.16(11) of the Superannuation Act 1976 a Benefit Classification Certificate in respect of the applicant specifying the conditions "Whiplash Syndrome with Sequelae" and "Recurrent Headaches" by deleting from the Benefit Classification Certificate the words "Whiplash Syndrome with Sequelae and".

DECISION

This is an application for review of a decision to confirm the issue, pursuant to s.16(11) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") of a benefit classification certificate ("BCC") in respect of the applicant specifying the conditions "Whiplash Syndrome with Sequelae" and "Recurrent Headaches", and of a decision to be of the opinion under s.66(2)(c) of the Act that the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the applicant was caused, or was substantially contributed to by, a condition specified in the BCC or by a condition or conditions connected with such condition. The issue of the headaches may be put on one side, for the respondent in fact decided that there was no support in any of the evidence before him for a possible connection between the applicant's "Recurrent Headaches" and either of the two conditions which substantially contributed to the applicant's eventual incapacity.

2. The basic facts may be taken from the Statement of Reasons, a Statement which, as is usual with the present respondent, was extremely detailed and comprehensive. Because of our conclusions drawn from the evidence given at the hearing, these Reasons may be rather more concise.

3. The applicant was born in September 1942. She became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act on 8 February 1979. Pursuant to the provisions of s.16(2) of the Act she was required to undergo a medical examination by a Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO"), and that examination took place on 19 March 1979. In his report of the examination conducted on that day the CMO, now deceased, indicated that he considered that the applicant met the General Medical Standard ("GMS"), which is used as a guide in classifying applicants for entry into the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme. On 6 February 1981, the medical report was considered pursuant to s.16(4) of the Act and it was decided that a BCC was not to be issued.

4. The applicant was retired from the Public Service on the ground of invalidity on 1 August 1986. We shall in due course have to go back further, but the event ultimately responsible for the problems that led to her retirement, subject to our later discussion of an earlier accident, was a car accident which occurred on 5 February 1981 and which caused an injury to her neck, a so-called "whiplash injury". As the Statement of Reasons put it, the applicant thereafter "experienced exacerbations of pain and tightness in her neck, and also complained of continuing headaches and right-sided paraesthesia in her face, arm and hand". She continued to display a wide range of symptoms, underwent many medical examinations and had a good deal of absences from work, some on sick leave and some on compensation leave. She was re-deployed at one stage and at another was put on restricted duties. In view of the conclusion to which we have come on a vital issue, as set out below, it is unnecessary to say more than that after an examination which he conducted on 26 June 1986 a CMO considered that the applicant was suffering from "neck pain 100%" and commented:

"Whiplash injury 1972 and again February 1981.

Pain has become worse over past 18 months. Has had

extensive sick leave in that time and has been off

(work) continuously since February 1986.

(Prognosis) Very poor according to specialist."

5. The CMO commented elsewhere in his report:

"Whiplash injury 1972. Joined Super Scheme 1979

(Feb). Employee claims that her neck had

completely recovered at time of joining the scheme

and that she had no further problems until February

1981. Letter from her local doctor indicates that

he was treating her for neck pain in April 1979."

6. It was subsequently established in evidence given by Dr Jones that he had made a mistake in a written report and that he had in fact first treated the applicant for neck pain on 14 June, not April, 1979. The applicant's counsel placed a good deal of weight on the significance of this error, the entry medical examination having occurred in March 1979.

7. At all events the CMO concluded that the applicant was unfit for continued employment and that she should be retired on the ground of invalidity. A doctor who oversaw the CMO's report and signed as "Advisor to the Commissioner for Superannuation" wrote: "I concur. Review in 12 months recommended". As previously stated, the applicant's retirement date was 1 August 1986.

8. Resolution of the case depends on the significance or otherwise of the accident in 1972, a car accident which occurred in Perth where the applicant and her family then lived. We do not really know a great deal about it. It cannot have been a very severe accident. No action for damages appears to have followed it, although there was some kind of compensation claim, but the applicant was certainly affected by it. After the accident she was treated in Outpatients at the Fremantle Hospital and afterwards by her own general practitioner. She wore a cervical collar all of the time for one or two months, then was allowed by her doctor to leave it off for a while because she was by now nursing a newly born infant. She wore it on and off for "a few more months" until about eight months had passed from the accident. She had some medication, the only item of which that she could remember being Valium. She and her husband were keen on beach fishing. She had to give up using a heavy rod but continued all the time with lightweight gear. Her husband would cast for her and she "would do some fishing, not a great deal". She was back fishing properly by late 1973. After she gave up the collar she used to feel stiff and sore in her neck from time to time, but in mid to late 1974 she had to pack up the house because of a move to Melbourne when her husband joined the Army. She said that she did this and had no problem. After Melbourne she moved with her husband to Canberra and she said that she used to play tennis, badminton and a little bit of squash without any trouble. For some 12 months the applicant had to mind two young children of her brother's, as well as her own, so that she was managing three children under the age of five. Our conclusion is that from the beginning of 1975, if not before, the applicant was leading an active physical life untroubled by any effects from the 1972 accident.

9. In 1977 the applicant obtained employment with the High Commission of India, and then entered the Public Service at the end of October 1978, working as a clerical assistant in the Department of Defence. In the period up to the accident in February 1981 the applicant had a number of periods of sick leave but none relating to back or neck ache. One was for "headache".

10. The applicant's entry medical examination on 19 March 1979 must now be dealt with. We need not go through all the ramifications of it. The first issue is whether she disclosed the 1972 accident. To the question in the Medical Report Form which she had been required to complete and bring to the examination which asked "Have you had any operations, surgical treatment or accidents" the applicant answered "Yes" and gave as details "Ceasearean" (sic). Somebody, not the CMO and, we would surmise, possibly a Sister whom the applicant might have seen before going into the examination, then wrote against that: "and Tubal Ligation Foetal distress". There must have been some conversation to give rise to this. Did the applicant mention the 1972 accident to some such person? The applicant's evidence was that she was not sure whether she had to refer to the 1972 accident because she thought that she only had to disclose anything occurring within five years previous to the examination. No explanation was given as to the source of this notion. She said however that she had said to the CMO that she "had had an accident - a motor vehicle accident - involving my neck, and there was a discussion about it and he ... asked me if I was having any problems from it, and I said 'No' that I was not having any problems. And that was about it".

11. The provisions of the Act in relation to disclosure were amended in important respects by the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1990 (Act No.40 of 1990). A written submission by the respondent as to the application of the amendments is of general interest and is attached as an Annexure to these Reasons. Because of these amendments the Tribunal was in fact requested by the respondent to delay hearing of this matter until the resolution of a matter before the Federal Court in which the Tribunal has stated a Case to that Court in relation to the interpretation of the legislation as amended. We declined to do so, considering that we could at least take the evidence. Then after that we were asked to delay a decision until the Federal Court matter had been resolved. We do not consider that we should do so. We are prepared to assume in favour of the respondent, without deciding the point, that the amended legislation applies in this case with its arguably heightened standard of disclosure. It is unnecessary to decide the point because the fact is that we are not prepared to find on the facts that the applicant made the verbal disclosure to the CMO that she claims to have made. We do not find positively that she did not. Her credit was not otherwise damaged, but there is an inherent unlikelihood that she did so, having regard to the total absence of any reference to it by the CMO. As stated below, we are of opinion that the CMO would as a matter of probability have discounted the significance of the 1972 accident, but we find it very hard to accept that having had it disclosed to him he would have made no reference to it at all. It is more likely that if anything was said about it it was said to some possible third person, the person who wrote "Tubal Ligation" etc. But that is mere surmise. We simply say that we feel bound to approach the matter on the footing that disclosure was not made.

12. The next and crucial question is whether, had the applicant made the required disclosure, a BCC would have been issued in which a particular physical or mental condition or conditions would have been specified. We have to approach the matter on the evidence before us. That evidence does not accord with all of the evidence relied upon by the then Director of Health in her memorandum of 19 June 1987 (T.29 pp 117-118). In particular, we have already found that Dr Jones saw the applicant in relation to a history of neck injury in June and not April 1989. Further, we do not agree with the Director's critical finding that the 1972 accident "probably caused intermittent symptoms up to the time of the second accident in 1981". The evidence before us was to the contrary.

13. The medical evidence before us on this point was that of Dr Danta, a neurologist, and Dr Chandran, a neurosurgeon. We refer first to those parts of these reports which lend support to the respondent's case. Dr Danta said in a written report T35/138:

"(iii) The patient did have a whiplash injury

to her neck in 1972 which was severe enough for her

to have to wear a cervical collar for eight months

and she continued to complain of symptoms but then

made a complete recovery in about three years. In

the sense that she was, according to the history

given by her, asymptomatic at the time the second

accident happened, one has to say that there was no

pre-existing condition present at the time of the

second accident, but there certainly is a

connection between the effects of a neck injury

such as Mrs. Bainrot received and a previous

injury. The connection is the following: Patients

who have a single whiplash injury have a variable

amount of pain and disability which also lasts a

variable time, but for approximately equivalent

severities of injury, as far as this can be judged,

pain and disability after whiplash injuries is more

severe and lasts much longer in those patients who

have had a previous similar injury, and the story

given by Mrs. Bainrot is very typical with full

recovery from the first injury and then severe

disability after the second one. The cause for

this is not known, but it is a very common finding

amongst patients with multiple such injuries. It

is, therefore, possible that the patient would not

have had to be retired after the 1981 injury had

the first one not occurred. This is entirely

hypothetical and there is no way of establishing

this fact firmly."

14. In oral evidence Dr Danta said that the cause of the applicant's neck problem "was the whiplash injury that she sustained in 1981". Asked about the involvement of the 1972 accident he said:

"Well, she told me that the effects of that injury

had completely resolved after three years which is

about 1975. If one accepts this then any direct

effects of that injury should not play any part in

the subsequent symptoms and disabilities except

that in patients who have had previous injuries

subsequent ones make them more liable to have more

disability and symptoms."

Then he was asked and answered in evidence-in-chief:

"If you had been looking at Mrs Bainrot's

circumstances, say in 1979, some seven years after

the whiplash injury and where she had claimed that

there had been no symptoms for the previous three

or four years, would you be able to say that there

was a greater likelihood that she would develop

neck problems later in life than if she had not had

the 1972 accident?---I think one would have to say

slightly higher but it is very difficult to

estimate that.

Would there be some probability or possibility that

she could develop symptoms in the neck which would

prevent her from continuing to work? I mean, one

may be looking here just at a possibility rather

than a probability?---As a result of the 1972 accident?

As a result of the 1972?---I think had she

developed neck pain after a period of freedom of

pain without any further injuries one would have to

ascribe this to cervical spondylosis and simply

regard the 1972 accident as probably a minor

contributing factor to that."

He was then asked if it would be accurate to say that there was some risk of developing complications later in life that may prevent a person from working and he answered: "Some risk as I mentioned before in the accident contributing to the later development of spondylosis"... (H)ad she developed, without any further accidents, symptoms of cervical spondylosis, we would then have to regard this 1972 accident only as a minor contributing factor."

15. In cross-examination Dr Danta confirmed that X-rays were unremarkable. He was also asked and answered as follows:

"If you assume that the history that you received

is correct, namely that there was a 72 whiplash

injury, that after about 3 years, we will say by

early 75, there were no symptoms in the sense of

pain, restriction of movement, or anything of that

nature. Assume, would you please, that Mrs Bainrot

then went about her ordinary duties, which included

for a period, looking after - running a household,

looking after five children, three of them under

five years of age at one stage, that in 1977, for

part of 77 and 78 she worked at the Indian High

Commission, full time, 9 until 5, five days a week,

performing clerical type duties without difficulty,

that she joined the Public Service on 31 October

1978, and between that date and 19 March 1979, had

no time off work for any sick leave and indeed,

apparently during that period did not consult a doctor.

Now, if you were examining her with that history in

March of 1979 and you were considering whether at

that particular time with that history there was a

real risk - without more, just on those symptoms,

would you consider, examining her, that at that

time there was a real risk of her not being able to

perform or continue to perform her duties in the

Public Service for another 20 years on the basis of

anything in that history?---Well, I probably would

have said that there was no or only minimal risk.

Yes. And that is the minimal risk that you

referred to in relation to the cervical

spondylosis?---Contribution of the accident to

cervical spondylosis later in life."

He also said, in answer to a question from the Tribunal, that "patients with neck pain and backache in the past, are very prone to develop it again with even minimal injury."

16. Dr Chandran had first seen the applicant in February 1982. This initial consultation and subsequent visits up to August 1984 were, Dr Chandran said, largely about numbness in the face, which was part of the applicant's symptomatology after her 1981 accident. He did not at the time of her first visit have any knowledge of the 1972 accident. He became aware of it later after he was given some documents by the Commissioner for Superannuation. In his written report of 27 May 1988 Dr Chandran set considerable store on the 1972 incident, saying that there was evidence of a neck injury in 1972, resulting in neck pain and stiffness and numbness in the right arm intermittently". He went on however to say: "It is noteworthy that these informations were not given to me during the various consultations".

17. The evidence upon which Dr Chandran relied was a reference in a document which had been supplied to him but which had not been supplied to the Tribunal. It was so supplied during the hearing and became Ex.6. It should have been included in the s.37 documents, for it was a vital cog in Dr Chandran's reasoning. What it said was:

"f.102 1972 '...she mentioned that she had a

neck injury 7 years prior to

(1979)' (Reported to Dr Jones, GP,

in April 1979)

f.70 'she had had similar pains

(intermittent episodes of numbness

R arm) which had dated back to an

injury to her (R) shoulder eight

years ago.' (Dr Greenhalgh, GP,

6/8/81)."

18. We have already noted that the report to Dr Jones was in June, not April, 1979. More important however is the reference to Dr Greenhalgh. His report of 6 August 1981, referred to as "f.70", is T8/63 in the Tribunal documents. The relevant passage is:

"On 16 February 1981 she also told me that she

had been having intermittent episodes of numbness

of her right arm and hand which lasted ten to

fifteen minutes, which had been happening for the

past six weeks and it had in fact improved after

her car accident. She had had similar pains which

had dated back to an injury to her shoulder eight

years ago. This is no doubt due to right sided

brachial plexus irritation from an old injury to

the right shoulder."

19. The statement in the passage quoted from Dr Greenhalgh: "She had had similar pains which had dated back to an injury to her shoulder eight years ago" is in our opinion quite equivocal. Does it for instance mean any more than that after the first accident she had had similar pain? The "intermittent episodes" of numbness are said to have been "happening for the past six weeks" (that is to say up to 6 August 1981) but to have "in fact improved after her car accident". Against the applicant's evidence these statements have to be said to be equivocal and Dr Greenhalgh was not called to give evidence. There was no opportunity for him to explain them or for them to be tested by the applicant's representative. For that matter the evidence of the applicant and of her husband that she was asymptomatic from early 1975 was not challenged as to its truth in cross-examination. In this situation we consider that the only proper course is to accept the applicant's evidence that she had been symptom-free from early 1975 at the latest.

20. In fact it is important to note also the following passage from Dr Chandran's evidence, at Tr.65, where he is being examined-in-chief by Mr A. Browne, who appeared for the respondent:

'MR BROWNE: Based on the information you received

about the 1972 accident, with that sort of a

history, what sort of conditions could perhaps have

developed later in life in which the 1972

(accident) could have been involved?---Degeneration

in the disc and in the facet joints could have

arisen if there was a major injury to the neck in

72, and that could lead eventually to degeneration

which can be picked up on x-rays some years later.

Were you able to form an opinion, on the basis of

the information you were given, as to the extent of

the 1972 injury?---There were no abnormalities to

be found in the x-rays of the cervical spine that

were available to me. There was no degeneration

evident, therefore I could not state, on that

basis, that there was a continuing injury in the neck.

Would the 1972 incident, though, have had any

involvement in subsequent development of

degeneration?---There has been no degeneration so

far, so that injury has not produced - if an injury

is going to produce degeneration in the neck, then

we should see that within a few years. Well, by

that I mean at least about four to five years we

should see changes. So, since we did not see that,

I can only say that the injury of 72 has not

produced any degeneration in the neck, and will not

produce any degeneration.

It had not produced any degeneration within four or

five years?---No."

21. Finally, we should set out the cross-examination of Dr Chandran, in which Mr G.A. Stretton of counsel, for the applicant, asked one question and got one answer:

"MR STRETTON; Doctor, I want you to assume for the

moment that Mrs Bainrot had been involved in a

motor vehicle accident in which she sustained what

is commonly called a whiplash injury to the neck in

1972. I want you now to assume that by February

of 1975 there were no symptoms, either of pain,

limitation of movement, para(e)sthesia, or anything

else, relating to that earlier accident and that

that situation of no symptoms continued up until

and beyond but at least up until March of 1979. So

we have in excess, I want you to assume, of four

years symptom free. I want you also to place in to

this hypothetical that during that period, for a

period of approximately 12 months at least, Mrs

Bainrot had looked after some five children, three

of them being under five years; that she had worked

full time without symptoms performing clerical work

for a period of six to 12 months. I want you to

further assume that she had commenced work in the

public service on 31 October 1978 and between the

date of her commencement and 19 March 1979 there

were no absences of any description from her

employment. I want you to further assume that in

that period before 1979 Mrs Bainrot had returned to

sporting activity, including playing tennis, some

squash, badminton, and engaged in beach fishing,

including casting one of those long rods used for

beach fishing, without difficulty or apparent

symptoms. If that hypothetical situation were the

position then, would you agree with me, doctor,

that any effect of the 1972 accident had ceased by

1979 and that in that event it played no

contribution in this lady's eventual

retirement?---Yes.

22. The critical question then is whether, all this having been known, a BCC would have been issued or, under the amendments made by Act No. 40 of 1990 would have been in force, which must mean would properly have issued or would properly have been in force. The first point is that we consider, on the evidence before us, that the applicant would have satisfied the General Medical Standard as applicable at the time (Ex.4):

"21. Skeletal and Muscular System

(1) All deformities call for the most careful

scrutiny by the CMO. The deformity should be

considered in terms of its degree of

severity, the nature of the work for which

the application is made, and the possibility

of it being the cause, indirectly, of

incapacity sufficient to result in retirement

prior to the normal retiring age.

(2) Standard met where the medical examination,

or specialist report if considered necessary,

indicates that:

(a) the applicant could perform the full

duties of the position,

(b) a high degree of adaptation to the

deformity or disability has occurred,

(c) pain is not present,

(d) the disability or deformity is stationary

and further progression is not likely to occur,

(e) there is no associated active arthritis,

(f) in the case of monarticular arthritic

diseases the condition is stabilised,

e.g. ankylosed, and

(g) an applicant previously deferred for an

appropriate period has had no significant

treatment during that period."

23. The fact is that as at the time of her medical examination there was no deformity present. An X-ray would have revealed nothing, and even if somehow there could have been said to be one the applicant would have satisfied the criterion in (2)(a) to (e), and 2(f) and (g) would have been inapplicable.

24. The question under the Act for the purpose of considering whether a BCC would have issued is whether it could be considered that at the material time there was "a real risk" that the applicant would not continue to be an eligible employee until attaining maximum retiring age by reason of a relevant physical or mental condition or conditions or by reason of a condition or conditions connected with such condition or conditions. This is the verbal expression of the test whether it be found under s.16 of the Act prior to its amendment by Act No.40 of 1990 or as amended. In our opinion no such risk could have been identified. There was no radiological evidence of any disability. Dr Chandran, in the passage set out above, said that if the 1972 accident were to have caused any degenerative condition it would have occurred within "four or five years" from the accident. There was no symptomatology suggestive of any condition that might deteriorate so as to produce early retirement.

25. Dr Danta considered that there was a "minimal risk", based on the contribution of the 1972 accident to cervical spondylosis later in life. Dr Danta's view was that "if one has more or less equivalent physical injuries, it is a very common experience for the effects of subsequent accidents to be much more severe than (that) of the initial one. The patients continue with pain much longer, they are more disabled for what would seem to be very similar injuries." But accepting fully what Dr Danta says, what is the "risk" that must be a "real risk" in this case? The applicant faced no threat of shortened employment by reason of any existing physical or mental condition as such. On the evidence there was none. At its highest from the point of view of the respondent's case, there was a risk that the applicant might have another accident, and if that did happen a further risk that the consequences of the second accident would be more serious than they would have been had the first accident never occurred. The second of these risks having been expressed by Dr Danta as being "no or only minimal", that assessment has to be placed against the occurrence of the first risk, that is to say of another accident occurring, and viewed in that light the reality of risk in this case as a whole has to be seen as having been very low indeed. The threat to continued employment came because the second accident occurred. It all depended on the severity of that accident, should it happen at all, even although the first accident could have had some effect on its consequences. Another person with no previous history of trauma might have had a far more serious whiplash injury, and so on. In all the circumstances we do not consider that a BCC could properly have issued because there could not have been said, at the material time, that there was a real risk that the applicant would not continue to be an eligible employee until attaining maximum retiring age. To find otherwise in the circumstances of this case would be to substitute for "real risk" the meaning "any risk at all however minimal", and that in a case where there was not only no existing condition but no history of any condition as such, only a history, later ceased, of production of symptoms from a soft tissue injury, a form of injury of which it has been said in countless medical cases before the Tribunal that it heals up within a space of weeks or months, as it did here.

26. In coming to this conclusion we have considered the Tribunal decisions in Re Nicholson and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 14 ALD 217; Re Rowlands and Commissioner for Superannuation (1988) 16 ALD 589; Re Gelsthorpe and Commissioner for Superannuation (1988) 18 ALD 793; and Re Bergin and Commissioner for Superannuation (1989) 19 ALD 279. We do not propose to refer to these authorities in detail or to add any glosses to the expression "real risk". We would only add that not too much store should in our opinion be set on the fact that an entrant to the scheme has the comfort that a BCC ceases to have any effect after 20 years. That is indeed a matter of comfort and no more. It does not entitle the issue of a BCC for any condition, however minor, that has ever been suffered: see paragraphs 33-34 of the Reasons for Decision in Re Rowlands.

27. In these circumstances no further issue remains to be considered. The Tribunal will vary the decision under review to issue pursuant to s.16(11) of the Act a BCC in respect of the applicant specifying the conditions "Whiplash Syndrome with Sequelae" and "Recurrent Headaches" by deleting from the BCC the words "Whiplash Syndrome with Sequelae and". Because the respondent decided that there was no support in any of the evidence before him for a possible connection between the applicant's "Recurrent Headaches" and either of the two conditions which substantially contributed to her eventual incapacity, namely "Soft Tissue Injury to Neck" and "Functional Overlay" the matter is of no moment, but as no evidence was given, or submissions made, to the Tribunal as to the applicant's history of headaches it is preferable to leave the BCC to stand in so far as it referred to "Recurrent Headaches".
