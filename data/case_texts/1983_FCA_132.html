Re Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v Peter Joseph Walsh and Beatrice Joan Walsh As Trustees of Lisa Marie Walsh Trust [1983] FCA 132; (1983) 69 FLR 240 (5 July 1983)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

And: PETER JOSEPH WALSH and BEATRICE JOAN WALSH as trustees for

LISA MARIE WALSH TRUST [1983] FCA 132; (1983) 69 FLR 240

No. G14 of 1983

Income Tax

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

QUEENSLAND DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

McGregor(1), Lockhart(2) and Fitzgerald(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Company trustee with other companies engaging in successful profit making transaction - relationship of companies said to be that of partnership - receipt by company trustee of share of profit - trust property held by company trustee for unit holders - unit holders holding units as trustees for beneficiary presently entitled but under legal disability - whether sum received as trustees for beneficiary income to which s.94 applied and liable to further tax thereby.

"No uncontrolled partnership income" - "Share in the net income of a partnership" - "Income of a particular class" - "Trust estate".

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s.6, s.94, s.98

Acts Interpretation Act 1901 s.15AA

Partnership Act (1891) (Qld.)

Income Tax - Uncontrolled partnership income - Trustee of unit trust engaging in profit making scheme with other companies - Whether partnership - Profit distributed to beneficiaries of unit trust who were trustees for infant - Whether uncontrolled partnership income - Whether partnership income if partnership dissolved - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), s. 94. The taxpayers were trustees of a family trust for the sole benefit of one of their children who was aged under sixteen. The family trust owned units in a family unit trust the trustee of which was Syncarpia Pty Ltd. Syncarpia in its capacity as trustee of the unit trust bought together with two unrelated companies an area of land and sold it at a profit. This was the only business done together by the three companies. Part of the profit was distributed to the taxpayers as owners of units in the unit trust in their capacities as trustees of the family trust. The Commissioner assessed the taxpayers on that income on the basis that it fell within s. 94 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth). It was net uncontrolled partnership income because the sole beneficiary of the family trust was under sixteen. The taxpayers successfully appealed to the Supreme Court of Queensland, and the Commissioner appealed.

Held (by majority): Appeal dismissed. The receipt by Syncarpia of part of the profit was on the dissolution of the partnership. It was received by Syncarpia as a trustee in extinguishment of its interest in the partnership. Section 94 therefore did not apply to the income (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Everett [1980] HCA 6; (1980) 143 CLR 440).

DECISION

THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA (the Commissioner) appealed against a decision of a judge of the Supreme Court of Queensland wherein he directed that an assessment of income tax imposed pursuant to the provisions of s.94 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (the Act) for the year ended June 1973, be set aside. The income referred to in the notice of assessment was said to be "net uncontrolled partnership income of $1,768." In a notice accompanying the assessment the Commissioner advised the taxpayer as follows -

"INCOME TAX - SECTION 94

A further tax of $751.02 has been imposed in accordance with the provisions of section 94 on the net uncontrolled partnership income of $1768 included in your taxable income.

2. The net uncontrolled partnership income was arrived at by deducting from the gross uncontrolled partnership income included in your assessable income the proportion of expenses and apportionable deductions applicable thereto.

3. The following calculations were involved:-

Gross uncontrolled partnership income included in the assessable income $1768.00

Less

to the uncontrolled

partnership income $ -

Proportion of expenses

applicable to the total

partnership income $ - -

Net uncontrolled partnership income included in the assessable income $1768.00

Less Proportion of apportionable

section 6 $ -

income included in the

taxable income $1768.00

________

4. The rate of further tax has the effect of imposing on the net uncontrolled partnership income included in the taxable income a total rate of 50 cents in the dollar."

The essential facts and findings which are not in dispute or, in my view, are to be accepted, include:-

(i) Syncarpia Pty. Ltd. (Syncarpia) was, pursuant to a deed dated 22 November 1972, the holder of trust property and trustee thereof as therein defined for certain unit holders who were holders of numbers of units; the trust thus constituted being styled "The P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust".

(ii) The said deed provided that each unit holder was to be presently entitled to the income of the unit of which he was registered as the holder.

(iii) There were 55 units of equal interest in the trust property.

(iv) In the year ended June 1973 Syncarpia with two other companies, Wise Investments Pty. Ltd. and Glen Gar Don Pty. Ltd., otherwise unassociated with it, entered into a profit making venture described in detail in the reasons for judgment of the judge at first instance in the Supreme Court of Queensland. The oral agreement between the partners was that they should purchase a property, sell it at a profit, and split the profit after taking out costs equally; if there were any loss they were also to be split equally. As a result, and still within that income year, Syncarpia made a profit of $19,447.92. The profit, it seems, was referred to in the tax return for the year ending June 1973 of Syncarpia.

(v) Peter Joseph Walsh and Beatrice Joan Walsh were, by deed dated 21 November 1972, constituted trustees of property, as to capital and income thereon to be held absolutely for Lisa Marie Walsh as cestui que trust or beneficiary.

(vi) Peter Joseph Walsh and Beatrice Joan Walsh as trustees aforesaid for Lisa Marie Walsh held five units in the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust.

(vii) The profit referred to in (i) above was distributed by Syncarpia to unit holders under the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust; P.J. and B.J. Walsh as trustees for Lisa Marie Walsh received $1768.00.

(viii) P.J. and B.J. Walsh as trustees for Lisa Marie Walsh in their Return for year ending June 1973, included in their net income, inter alia, the distribution of the said sum of $1768.00. The Return was stated to have been prepared by reference to the Accounts of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust.

(ix) The Commissioner, relying on s.94 of the Act, assessed P.J. and B.J. Walsh to further tax on the said sum.

(x) The trial judge found that there was a partnership at least within the meaning of s.6(1) of the Act between Syncarpia and the two other companies mentioned in (iv) above.

(xi) Lisa Marie Walsh was presently entitled to the whole of the income being held by her parents under the deed dated 21 November 1972; but, being in the year of income aged 15 years, was under a legal disability.

Before this court, as before the trial judge, there was an issue as to whether there was any partnership. I am satisfied, with respect, that the learned trial judge was correct in the conclusion he reached, i.e. that there was a partnership at least within the extended meaning of s.6(1) of the Act. Further, I agree with him also that P.J. & B.J. Walsh are assessable to tax on the share of the net income of the trust estate of Lisa Marie Walsh pursuant to s.98 of the Act.

The question here is whether the further tax imposed in reliance on s.94 of the Act is payable. It will be if that section or any sub-section which is in an entire form applies to these facts. Section 94 is in Division 5 of the Act referring to Partnership; but sub-sections in it refer to Trust Estates. Division 6 was headed "Trustees". There appears to be some affinity or continuity between the Divisions.

Parsons and Kenneally in Principles of Income Tax Law in Australia (which professes to state the law as at 5 March 1974) wrote of s.94 in Ch.XIII at p.343 -

"Broadly the purpose of the section is -

(1) To impose further tax on the share of partnership income to which a person under 16 years of age on the last day of the year of income is presently entitled whether as partner or as beneficiary under a trust estate (Subsections (3) and (4) of s.94); and

(2) to impose further tax on the share of partnership income to which a person other than a company is entitled as partner or as beneficiary under a trust estate (whether immediately or through a series of trusts) whose trustee is a partner or which is assessable to that trustee or to a trustee in another trust as income to which no person is presently entitled, if the partnership is so constituted or controlled or its operations are so conducted that the partner or trustee-partner has not the real and effective control and disposal of the share of partnership income or of a part of that share (Subsections (1) and (2) of s.94)."

Section 94(1) may be discarded from consideration. The net income of the partnership was the net income of Syncarpia plus companies named. The relevant partner is Syncarpia; but, contrary to s.94(1)(a)(i) it is a company, and a "person in the capacity of trustee"; and, contrary to s.94(1)(b), the partnership was not so constituted or controlled or its operation so conducted that the partner (Syncarpia) had not the real and effective control and disposal of its share. There was no evidence or finding by the learned trial judge of any aspect in the agreement between the partners as a result of which the partnership could be said to be so constituted or controlled nor as to the way its operations were conducted. In my opinion, the words of Latham C.J. in Robert Coldstream Partnership v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1943] HCA 33; (1943-44) 68 C.L.R. 391 (Coldstream) at pp.392,393 (referring to the then s.94) are appropriate, viz. -

"The section refers in the first place to three circumstances or conditions. First, the constitution of the partnership, which must be determined upon a consideration of the terms of the partnership agreement; second, the control of the partnership, which must be determined by reference to the facts of management and the conduct of the business of the partnership, and, thirdly, the conduct of the operations of the partnership, which I have some difficulty in distinguishing from the second head."

Implication (so far as there is any) is rather to the contrary of there being any such constitution control or conduct. The partners were entitled, so far as the agreement between them revealed to dispose of their share as they chose. It is not to the point, I suggest, that, quite separtately from the agreement for partnership, Syncarpia was bound by the trust deed - and, in particular, by its clauses 4, 5 and 23 - which might be thought effectively to affect the said control, or at least the disposal, after its receipt, of its income. I note also the words of Latham C.J. at p.394 as to a "going concern".

The considerable shorter form of s.94 which was considered by Latham C.J. had no equivalents to e.g. s.94(4) (5) or (11); and did not refer to trustees or further tax. Yet its opening words were as to partnership constitution control and conduct similar to those introduced by the amending Act No. 110 of 1964. Section 94(1) is said to be "subject to this section"; it is necessary to consider the effect of later sub-sections should the facts in this matter fall within them or any of them.

Section 94(2) of the Act reads -

"Subject to the succeeding provisions of this section, where -

(a) a partnership is so constituted or controlled, or its operations are so conducted, that a partner in the partnership, being a trustee of a trust estate, has not the real and effective control and disposal of his share in the net income of the partnership or of a part of that share (which share or part of a share, as the case may be, is in this sub-section referred to as "uncontrolled partnership income"); and

(b) in calculating in accordance with section 95 the net income of that trust estate or of any other trust estate, there is included in the assessable income of the trust estate any uncontrolled partnership income,

then -

(c) if -

(i) a beneficiary, not being a company or a person in relation to whose share of the income of the trust estate sub-section (4) applies, is presently entitled to the whole of the income of the trust estate otherwise than in the capacity of a trustee; or

(ii) there is no part of the net income of the trust estate that is included in the assessable income of a beneficiary in pursuance of section 97 or in respect of which the trustee is assessed and liable to pay tax in pursuance of section 98,

this section applies to the portion of the net income of the trust estate that was derived from uncontrolled partnership income;

(d) if a beneficiary, not being a company or a person in relation to whose share of the income of the trust estate sub-section (4) applies, is presently entitled to a share of the income of the trust estate otherwise than in the capacity of a trustee, this section applies to so much of that share of the net income of the trust estate as bears to that share the same proportion as the portion of the net income of the trust estate that was derived from uncontrolled partnership income bears to the net income of the trust estate; and

(e) if there is a part of the net income of the trust estate that is not included in the assessable income of a beneficiary in pursuance of section 97 and in respect of which the trustee is not assessed and is not liable to pay tax in pursuance of section 98, this section applies to so much of that part of the net income of the trust estate as bears to that part the same proportion as the portion of the net income of the trust estate that was derived from uncontrolled partnership income bears to the net income of the trust estate."

I have already expressed the view that the partnership here was not so constituted, controlled or its operations so conducted as is referred to in s.94(2)(a). Thus, in my opinion, there is no income which can, within the meaning of s.94(2) appropriately be referred to or called "uncontrolled partnership income".

Section 94(3) refers to a share in the net income of a partnership being included in the assessable income of a partner who is at the relevant time under the age of 16 years; it has no application here.

Section 94(4) reads -

"(4) Subject to the succeeding provisions of this section, where, in calculating in accordance with section 95 the net income of a trust estate, . . . . .there is included in the assessable income of the trust estate a share in the net income of a partnership of a year of income and a beneficiary who was under the age of sixteen years on the last day of the year of income of the partnership is presently entitled to, or to a share of, the income of the trust estate, this section applies -

(a) if the beneficiary is presently entitled to the whole of the income of the trust estate - to the portion of the net income of the trust estate that was derived from the share of the trust estate in the net income of the partnership; or

(b) if the beneficiary is presently entitled to a share of the income of the trust estate - to so much of that share of the net income of the trust estate as bears to that share the same proportion as the portion of the net income of the trust estate that was derived from the share of the trust estate in the net income of the partnership bears to the net income of the trust estate.

but, if during that year of income of the partnership, the beneficiary rendered any services, without remuneration, in the production of assessable income of the partnership, this section applies to the portion of the net income of the trust estate referred to in paragraph (a) or the part of the share of the net income of the trust estate referred to in paragraph (b), as the case may be, reduced by an amount that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, would constitute reasonable remuneration by way of salary or wages for those services.

The sub-section envisages that there is included in the assessable income of the trust estate held by P.J. and B.J. Walsh i.e. trust property referred to in the Deed dated 21 November 1972 i.e. the Lisa Marie Walsh trust, what may be regarded as a share ($1768) of or in the net income of a partnership. This, in the context, is a share in the income the partnership of Syncarpia and the two other companies. With respect, I do not agree with the learned trial judge's view that for s.94 to apply the Commissioner must demonstrate that P.J. and B.J. Walsh as trustees for Lisa Marie Walsh were partners. In the circumstances present here, I consider "this section applies", the beneficiary, Lisa Marie Walsh, being, as s.94(4) (a) contemplates, "presently entitled" to the whole of the income of that trust estate, to the portion of its net income derived from the net income of the said partnership, viz. $1768. The qualification in the section introduced with the word "but" has no application. However, the sub-section is expressed to be "subject to the succeeding provisions of this section; which must therefore be considered.

Section 94(5) read -

(5) For the purposes of this section -

(a) where -

(i) the assessable income of a trust estate includes the net income or a share of the net income of another trust estate; and

(ii) the assessable income of the other trust estate by reference to which that net income is calculated included income of a particular class (including an amount that is to be deemed by an application or applications of this paragraph to be income of a particular class),

the assessable income of the first-mentioned trust estate shall be deemed to include income of that class of an amount equal to so much of the net income or share of the net income of the other trust estate that is included in the assessable income of the first-mentioned trust estate as bears to that net income or share of that net income the same proportion as the portion of the net income of the other trust estate that was derived from income of that class bears to the net income of the other trust estate; and

(b) the portion of the net income of a trust estate that is derived from income of a particular class that is included in the assessable income of the trust estate is the amount remaining after deducting from the income of that class that is included in the assessable income of the trust estate -

(i) any prescribed deductions that relate exclusively to that income of that class;

(ii) so much of any other prescribed deductions (other than apportionable deductions) as, in the opinion of the Commissioner, may appropriately be related to that income of that class; and

(iii) the amount that bears to the prescribed deductions (being apportionable deductions) the same proportion as the amount that, but for this sub-paragraph, would be the portion of the net income of the trust estate that is derived from that income of that class bears to the sum of the net income of the trust estate and those last mentioned prescribed deductions.

The conditions for the application of s.94(5) are met here i.e. (making appropriate substitution) -

(i) The assessable income of the Lisa Marie Walsh Trust estate included a share of the net income of Syncarpia.

(ii) The assessable income of Syncarpia, by reference to which the net income of the Lisa Marie Walsh Trust is calculated, included income of a particular class. This it happens is one of the classes of income referred to in Division 2 of the Act viz. s.26(a) i.e. ". . . profit arising from the sale by the taxpayer of any property acquired by him for the purpose of profit making by sale . . . ."

These preconditions having been fulfilled, as in my view they are, the sub-section determined what was the assessable income of the Lisa Marie Walsh Trust; i.e. what that income shall be deemed to include, viz. income of the particular class mentioned in (ii) above. The amount of such income is to be so much of the net income of Syncarpia as is included in the Lisa Marie Walsh Trust estate (i.e. $1768), measured against an equation with which this court on this appeal is not presently concerned, but subject to reduction as sub-para.(b) provides. It has not been suggested that there are any deductions as referred to in (i), (ii) or (iii) of para.(b).

Like others, I have found difficulty with the expression "income of a particular class". Since undefined, it is arguable, as counsel contended, to regard the phrase as a reference to something which, in the end, the draftsman failed to define. Even so, if possible an effect should be given to the section. I agree that its meaning, to say the least, could have been made plainer. But eventually I have concluded that "income of a particular class" is no more than a convenient tag to refer to a category of income, thus derived by "another trust estate". I accept the contention of counsel for the Commissioner that partnership income passing through successive trusts does, by the section, retain its "category". According to accepted canons of construction, the section should be made to work if reasonably possible. See e.g. Craies on Statute Law 7th ed. p.103 et seq. And the words of Barwick C.J. in Tickle Industries Pty. Limited v. Hann [1974] HCA 5; (1974) 130 C.L.R. 321 at p.330 warn us against "undue literalism". I suggest that a consideration of the Explanatory Memorandum at p.66 et seq to which we may refer to assist understanding the object of the sections, does not discourage the approach to interpretation taken above. Counsel for the Commissioner cited in this are of his argument, Cooper Brookes (Wollongong) Pty. Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4,292 (Cooper Brookes) at pp.4,295-6, 4,299, 4,300: Reg. v. Smith (1974) 2 N.S.W.L.R. 586 at p.589; Scott v. Moses 75 W.N. (N.S.W.) 101 at p.102; Dunlop v. Milton Timber and Hardware Company Limited: Dunlop v. Thomas 1960 N.Z.L.R. 1096 at p.1098.

Section 94(6) and (8) has no application. There is no suggestion or evidence that the Commissioner has the opinion referred to in sub-sections.

Section 94(7) was not the subject of any submission. It is an exclusionary provision which does not apply to the circumstances here.

Section 94(9), (10), (11) and (12) provide for further tax at the rate declared by Parliament upon the portion of taxable income derived from income to which s.94 refers and other sections refer. Sub-s.(11) read -

"Where -

(a) section 98 applies in relation to the net income of a trust estate of a share of that net income; and

(b) this section applies to a portion of that net income or of that share of that net income, as the case may be,

the trustee of the trust estate shall be assessed and is liable to pay further tax, at the rate declared by the Parliament for the purposes of this section, upon that portion of that net income or of that share of that net income, as the case may be, reduced by an amount equal to so much of the sum of the concessional deductions that are taken into account for the purposes of section 98 in relation to that net income or that share of that net income as bears to that sum the same proportion as that portion of that net income or of that share of that net income bears to that net income or that share of that net income, as the case may be."

Section 94(13) read -

"(13) In this section -

"prescribed deductions", in relation to a trust estate, means the deductions that are allowable in calculating in accordance with section 95 the net income of the trust estate;

"prescribed trust" means a trust that resulted from -

(a) a will, a codicil or an order of a court that varied or modified the provisions of a will or codicil; or

(b) an intestacy or an order of a court that varied or modified the application, in relation to the estate of a deceased person, of the provisions of the law relating to the distribution of the estates of persons who die intestate;

"share in the net income of a partnership", in relation to a partner, means the individual interest of the partner in the net income of the partnership and any income derived by the partner from the partnership otherwise than as a partner."

FCT v. Everett [1980] HCA 6; (1979-1980) 143 C.L.R. 440 (Everett) offers assistance on some aspects of this matter. The majority of that court point out that a partner has no title to any specific property owned by the partnership, but a beneficial interest in its assets - in each and every asset and a right to a proportion of the surplus after the realisation of its assets and payment of debts and liabilities. This does not deny that the amount a partner may receive from the partnership may include, as here, a share of income (which may itself be assessable income) earned by the use of assets, cf. the observations of the majority at p. 452/5, referring to reasoning of Kitto J. in Dawson Holdings Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1965) 39 A.L.J.R. 300 at p. 301. Latham C.J. in Coldstream, in his reference to the partnership as a going concern (p. 394) thought the section - and he referred there to the earliest s. 94 - referred to income and not to disposal of assets upon a dissolution of partnership. Nor do I consider that to describe the interest of a partner as did the learned trial judge as an equitable chose in action, prevents one from recognising a sum of money as a share of income. Livingstone v. Commissioner for Stamp Duties [1960] HCA 94; (1960) 107 C.L.R. 411 does not decide to the contrary, preoccupied as it was with the problem where is situate the right of a residuary legatee or next of kin to have an estate duly administered. So far as Kitto J. in that case referred at p. 453 to the interest of a partner, the reference was to a right to a proportion of the surplus after the realisation of assets and payment of debts and liabilities of the partnership. Kitto J. at p. 453 was not attempting to distinguish between or to assert there was no distinction to be made between an asset in the ordinary sense and accrued income; s. 94 was not being considered at all. Here the Trust estate before sale of the land was the land the sale of which generated a net income. Following sale there remained with the partners, after deduction of capital sums and expenses, a profit, in ordinary circumstances, assessable to tax. In the hands of Syncarpia all the amounts received may be regarded in one sense as the Trust estate i.e. the sum total of assets held by it as the P.J. Walsh Family trust unit. Yet the totality of the account in the tax return of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust shows identifiable components and in this case reveal the "net income" of the (Syncarpia) trust estate. If it is relevant to say so, no part of it was in income tax law a capital sum, all of which had been accounted for before Syncarpia received its share of the profit. The amount finally received by Syncarpia was thus its profit from the real estate venture, even if it was also in another sense the trust estate. The unit holders - P.J. and B.J. Walsh - are then, in their capacity as trustees for Lisa Marie Walsh, entitled to receive their share of that trust estate. Again, even if it is a share of a trust estate, it is also, identifiably, income. See clause 5 of the Trust Deed of 21 November 1972. The sum they received, $1768, was assessable income in their hands as trustees pursuant to s. 98 of the Act. In my opinion, with such assistance as is available from the Explanatory Memorandum to which we were referred, the assessment to further tax quoted earlier by reference to s. 94 (11) is justifiable. Encouragement for this approach is, I consider, to be found in s. 15AA of Acts Interpretation Act 1901 and passages in Cooper Brookes referred to above. But so far as the Notice relies on there being "net uncontrolled partnership income", I consider it is not supportable. A reading of the Reasons for Judgment of the learned trial judge suggests that he found the use of that phrase mystifying. I note there does not appear any reference to "uncontrolled partnership income" or s.94 (2) in issues said to have been raised by the Commissioner in the judgment of the learned trial judge; though he mentions it in his Reasons.

Accordingly, the order I would propose is that the appeal should be upheld. The matter should be remitted to the Commissioner for his further consideration. Each side should pay its own costs.

The Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 is no stranger to intricate sections. It is difficult, however, to imagine a more convoluted provision than s.94 which arises for consideration in this appeal.

1972 and 1973 witnessed a land boom throughout much of Australia. In November 1972 Charles William Wise, a United States citizen, suggested to a business associate and friend of his, Peter Joseph Walsh, a Brisbane solicitor, that they should buy land in the Shire of Beaudesert, Queensland, with a view to selling it quickly for a profit. Mr Walsh agreed and they approached another friend of Mr Walsh, a Mr Brown, and suggested that he join them in the venture. On 23 November 1972 a contract of purchase was entered into for the purchase of the Beaudesert land, the purchasers being Mr Wise, Syncarpia Pty. Limited and Glen Gar Don Pty. Limited 'and/or their respective nominees'. Mr Walsh is the governing director of Syncarpia Pty. Limited and Mr Brown controls Glen Gar Don Pty. Limited. The land was purchased by the three purchasers as tenants-in-common. Soon after the contract was signed, Mr Walsh arranged for the incorporation of Wise Investments Pty. Limited, a company controlled by Mr Wise. The purchase price was $110,770. A deposit of $5,000 was paid. A cheque for one-third of that amount was drawn by Syncarpia in favour of Mr Walsh's firm of solicitors and a cheque for two-thirds of the amount was drawn by Mr Wise, also in favour of Mr Walsh's firm. The two cheques were deposited to the credit of a trust account opened by Mr Walsh's firm in the names of the venturers. Mr Walsh's firm then drew a cheque on its trust account in payment of the deposit. Later Glen Gar Don Pty. Limited reimbursed Mr Wise or Wise Investments Pty. Limited for one-third of the amount of the deposit. $85,000 was borrowed from the Commercial Bank of Australia Limited which took a first mortgage over the land as security. The balance was paid by the three venturers equally. The purchase was settled on 5 January 1973.

On 5 February 1973 a contract was entered into between Wise Investments, Syncarpia and Glen Gar Don as vendors and Mr Barry Maranta as purchaser for the sale of the Beaudesert land. The sale was settled on 6 March 1973. The purchase price was $177,910.

Wise Investments, Syncarpia and Glen Gar Don opened a bank account with the Commercial Bank of Australia in their joint names. The proceeds of sale were paid into that account on 6 March 1973. The amount owed to the Bank was paid to it. The balance was divided equally between the three companies involved in the venture, save for the sum of $1191.05 which was paid on 12 March 1973 to Mr Walsh's firm to meet legal costs and disbursements for acting on the real estate transactions. The account was then closed. The three companies had no other bank account with any Bank. There have been no other business dealings between the three companies.

There is no written agreement between Mr Walsh, Mr Wise and Mr Brown or their respective companies. Their agreement was oral and described in evidence by Mr Walsh as follows:-

'Q. I take it also _ I think it is common sense but I think it should go on the record _ that that agreement would be, 'We will purchase this property with a view to turning it over quickly and selling it at a profit, and we will split the profit after taking out costs equally."?-A. Yes.

Q. And if there are any losses per chance, we will split losses equally? A-That was assumed but never said, yes.

Q. That is your understanding of it? A-Yes.

Q. And any costs associated with the land such as rates, taxes,

The question to which I shall now turn is where the sum of $1768.00 recieved by Mr and Mrs Walsh as trustees for Lisa Marie Walsh was income to which s.94 of the Assessment Act applied.

Under the former s.94, repealed by Act No. 110 of 1964, a partnership was itself liable to tax where it was so constituted or controlled, or its operations were so conducted, that any partner did not have the real and effective control and disposal of his share of the net income of the partnership. In those circumstances the Commissioner was authorised to assess the additional amount of tax that would be payable if the share of that partner had been received by the partner who had the real and effective control of that share, and the partnership was liable to the tax so assessed. Section 94 operated only if it was established that a partner did not have the real and effective control of his share, and that another partner had the complete and exclusive control of the whole of that uncontrolled share. The meaning and operation of the former section were discussed in Robert Coldstream Partnership v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1943] HCA 33; (1943) 68 C.L.R. 391.

Many taxpayers took advantage of this situation to avoid tax. The mischief which the present s. 94 was designed to cure was referred to in the report of the Commonwealth Committee on Taxation, June 1961, para. 698 (the Ligertwood Report) in these terms:-

'We are informed that because of the defects in s.94, businesses are being rapidly converted into family partnerships. In many instances, little or no capital is introduced by the incoming family partners and only token services are rendered. Household responsibilities of the wife, and school attendance or lack of physical capacity of the children, often preclude active and full participation by these partners. A partnership deed is executed and provisions specifically inserted which permit family partners to give reduced services, use their share of profits to pay for their capital interest, and draw amounts against profits. The provisions of the deed effectively dispose of the possibility of the application of s.94.'

The present s.94, which applies as from 1 July 1965, operates very differently from the former section. It does not impose a liability to tax on the partnership. Instead, it makes the person who does not have the real and effective control and disposal of his share in the net income of a partnership liable to additional tax.

The section applies to a share in the net income of a partnership in the following four circumstances:-

1. Where a partner has included in his assessable income a share of partnership income and he does not have the real and effective control and disposal of that share or of a part of that share. Section 94 applies to that share or that part of that share: sub-s. 94(1).

2. Where a trustee, who is a partner in a partnership, does not have the real and effective control and disposal of his share in the net income of the partnership or of a part of that share, the section applies to what is termed the 'uncontrolled partnership income', that is the amount over which the trustee does not have real and effective control, which is included in the assessable income of a trust estate: sub-s. 94(2). Three cases are dealt with:-

(a) where a beneficiary is presently entitled to the whole of the income of the trust estate or where no beneficiary is presently entitled to any of the income of the trust estate. In this case s.94 applies to the portion of the net income of the trust estate which was derived from uncontrolled partnership income;

(b) where a beneficiary is presently entitled to a share of the income of the trust estate, in which case s.94 applies to so much of that share of the net income of the trust estate as bears to that share the same proportion as the portion of the net income of the trust estate that was derived from uncontrolled partnership income bears to the net income of the trust estate; and

(c) where there is a part of the net income of the trust estate to which no beneficiary is presently entitled. In this case s.94 applies to so much of that part of the net income of the trust estate as bears to that part the same proportion as the portion of the net income of the trust estate that was derived from uncontrolled partnership income bears to the net income of the trust estate;

3. Where a partner is under the age of 16 years on the last day of the year of income of the partnership, s.94 applies to his share of the net income of the partnership. But if, during the year of income of the partnership, the partner rendered any services in the production of assessable income of the partnership, s. 94 applies to that share reduced by an amount that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, would constitute a reasonable remuneration by way of salary or wages for those services if they had been performed by an employee of the partnership: sub-s. 94(3).

4. Where a beneficiary in a trust (other than a 'prescribed trust', that is a trust arising from a will or intestacy) is under the age of 16 years on the last day of the year of income of the partnership and is presently entitled to a share in the income of a trust estate which includes a share in the net income of a partnership, s.94 applies:-

(a) if the beneficiary is presently entitled to the whole of the income of the trust estate, to the portion of the net income of the trust estate that was derived from the share of the trust estate in the net income of the partnership; or

(b) if the beneficiary is presently entitled to a share of the income of the trust estate, to so much of that share of the net income of the trust estate as bears to that share the same proportion as the portion of the net income of the trust estate that was derived from the share of the trust estate in the net income of the partnership bears to the net income of the trust estate: sub-s. 94(4). Allowance is made for services rendered by the beneficiary as in 3. above.

Sub-section 94(5) is intended to apply,where there are successive trust estates,by providing a basis for tracing the net income of a partnership in respect of which s.94 may operate, and which is initially income of a trust estate, through each estate to which the section applies in succession If there was any doubt about s.94 being a convoluted provision, that doubt would by now be dispelled.

The purpose of s.94 is to impose additional tax on a partner who does not have the real and effective control of a share of partnership income. Sub-section 94(9) provides that where the assessable income of a taxpayer, other than a taxpayer in the capacity of a trustee, includes income to which s.94 applies, the taxpayer is to be assessed and is liable to pay further tax, at the rate declared by Parliament for the purposes of that section, upon the portion of his taxable income that is derived from income to which the section applies. The rate is 50 cents in the dollar. Sub-section 94(10) describes how the portion of a taxpayer's taxable income that is derived from income to which the section applies is to be ascertained. Sub-sections 94(11) and (12) create a liability for further tax in assessments of trustees in respect of income which includes income to which s.94 applies.

Sub-section 94(13) defines the expression "share in the net income of a partnership" in relation to a partner as meaning "the individual interest of the partner in the net income of the partnership and any income derived by the partner from the partnership otherwise than as a partner."

Where the Commissioner is of the opinion that, by reason of special circumstances, it would be unreasonable for s. 94 to apply to any income, the section does not apply to that income: sub-s. 94 (8).

The Commissioner contended that the assessable income of the trust estate under the Lisa Marie Walsh Trust included a share in the net income of the trust estate under the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust, that Lisa Marie Walsh was a beneficiary presently entitled to the whole of the income of the Lisa Marie Walsh Trust, was under the age of 16 on the last day of the income year and therefore the Commissioner correctly assessed the sum of $1768.00 to further tax by reason of sub-s. 94 (4) and (5). It was agreed that sub-s. (4) cannot apply to this case without the aid of sub-s. (5).

In my opinion the question whether sub-ss. 94 (4) and (5) apply in this case turns on one point namely, whether the sum of $1768.00 paid by Syncarpia as trustee under the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust to Mr and Mrs Walsh as trustees for Lisa Marie Walsh under the Lisa Marie Walsh Family Trust answers the description of "a share of the net income of" the trust estate under the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust for the purposes of sub-s. 94 (5) (a). If it does not, then the Commissioner must fail.

The term "trust estate" is not defined in the Assessment Act. It is generally taken to mean the estate which is vested in a trustee, that is the trust property. In my view this is the meaning of the term in Divisions 5 and 6.

The relevant trust estate held by Syncarpia as trustee under the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust was a one-third interest in the real estate venture within co-venturers Wise Investments and Glen Gar Don, which I will assume for present purposes was a partnership between the three within the meaning of the word "partnership" as defined in sub-s. 6 (1) of the Assessment Act.

The word "partnership" is defined in sub-s. 6 (1) as an association of persons carrying on business as partners or in receipt of income jointly, but does not include a company. The first limb of this definition merely reproduces the language of most Partnership Acts including The Partnership Acts (1891) (Qld) which defines "partnership" as the "relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit".

The taxpayer contended that the relation between the three venturers did not fall within either limb of the statutory definition. The Commissioner contended that both limbs applied. I do not find it necessary to decide this question because of the view I have formed on the point presently under discussion.

Syncarpia's interest in the partnership was a beneficial interest in the partnership asset, namely the Beaudesert land: Canny Gabriel Castle Jackson Advertising Pty. Limited v. Volume Sales (Finance) Pty. Limited [1974] HCA 22; (1974) 131 C.L.R. 321 at pp. 327-438; Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v. Everett [1980] HCA 6; (1980) 143 C.L.R. 440 at p. 446. Syncarpia's share in the partnership was a right to a one-third proportion of the surplus after the sale of the land and payment of the debts and liabilities of the partnership: Darby v. Darby [1856] EngR 328; (1856) 3 Drew. 495 at pp. 503-504; [1856] EngR 328; 61 E.R. 992 at p. 995; Rodriguez v. Speyer Bros. (1919) A.C. 59 at p. 68; Bakewell v. Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation (S.A.) [1937] HCA 11; (1937) 58 C.L.R. 743 at p. 770; Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v. Everett (supra) at p. 446.

The partnership came to an end when the land was sold, the liabilities discharged and the net proceeds of sale distributed to each partner. The sum of $19,565.09 was received by Syncarpia in extinguishment of its interest in the partnership It was not received as income from its interest in the partnership. Thereafter the $19,565.09 became part of the trust property under the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust. It itself became "trust estate".

It is not to the point that the profit gained by the partnership from the sale of the land was for fiscal purposes, net income of the partnership and that Syncarpia's interest in that income was assessable income pursuant to s.90 and sub-s. 92(1). The receipt by Syncarpia of the $19,565.09 was upon the dissolution of the partnership and in discharge of the mutual obligations of the partners. It was the antithesis of the receipt of income from its interest in the partnership: See Everett's Case, especially at p. 452, Ewing v. Ewing (1882) 9 App. Cas. 822 at p. 826.

The $1768.00 paid by Syncarpia to the trustees of the Lisa Marie Walsh Family Trust was not "a share of the net income of" the trust estate constituted by the $19,565.09 under the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust. It was a distribution of the very trust estate itself.

In view of this conclusion it is unnecessary to consider the other arguments advanced by the parties. I would dismiss the appeal with costs.

Counsel for the taxpayer launched a sustained attack on s. 94, including a submission that sub-s. 94 (5) made no sense. However, it seems to me possible to identify, in broad outline, the general scheme of s. 94 as it stood during the period now in question although the extent to which the details of the legislative purpose found expression in the statutory language, and more particularly whether the section applied to the subject transaction, will, of course, be matters for further consideration.

(i) Sub-sections (9) to (12) inclusive were the charging provisions in the sense that it was those sub-sections which provided for additional tax to be payable in the specified circumstances. Sub-section (9), which was complemented by sub-s. (10), related to the situation in which the assessable income of a taxpayer other than a trustee included income to which s. 94 applied; sub-s. (11) related to the situation in which the net income of a trust estate included income to which s. 94 applied and the trustees of that estate were assessable by virtue of s. 98; and sub-s. (12) related to the situation in which the net income of a trust estate included income to which s. 94 applied but it was s. 99 and not s. 98 which imposed the liability on the trustees. It is sub-s. (11) which is of present significance.

(ii) Each of sub-ss. (1) to (4) specified a class of income to which s. 94 applied. All four were concerned with partnership income. The effect of each was qualified by sub-s. (7) in a manner which is not presently directly material. Sub-sections (1) and (3) concerned partnership income where the partner was not a trustee (cf. sub-s. (9)). Sub-sections (2) and (4) concerned partnership income which formed part of the income of a trust estate (cf. sub-ss. (11) and (12)). Of these provisions, only sub-s. (4) falls for detailed consideration in these proceedings.

(iii) Sub-section 94 (5) was a difficult provision but I do not think that it was nonsensical as was suggested. The submission was made for the taxpayer that sub-s. 94 (5) omitted to specify what consequences were to ensue "Where" sub-paragraphs (a) and (b) were fulfilled. However, that seems to me to mistake the role of sub-s. 94 (5) which was broadly analogous to the role played by sub-s. (10). Sub-section (5) was concerned with partnership income which constituted income of a trust estate (cf. sub-ss. (2), (4), (11) and (12)), while sub-s. (10) was concerned with partnership income received by a partner who was not a trustee (cf. sub-ss. (1), (3) and (9)). The functions of sub-s. (5) included the expansion of what was income to which s. 94 applied.

(iv) Sub-section (13) defined certain of the terms used in s.94. None of the definitions assume importance on the facts of the present matter.

(v) Sub-sections (6) and (8) gave the Commissioner discretions in respect of the application of s.94. Neither discretion was exercised in respect of the subject assessment.

It was common ground that the Commissioner could not succeed by reference to sub-s. 94(4) alone but needed to rely also on sub-s. (5). Paragraphs (a) and (b) of sub-s. (5) both referred to "income of a particular class", a phrase which was nowhere defined, and depended for their operation upon a meaning being attributed to that phrase. In the Supreme Court, Shepherdson J. held against the Commissioner because, in his view, it would have offended established rules of construction to give such an uncertain provision in a taxing statute a meaning which would expose a taxpayer to liability. Whilst his Honour's reasons have obvious force, and the imprecise and otherwise unskilled drafting is to be deplored, I would prefer to leave the question open. The assumption implicitly underlying para. 94(5)(a)(ii) is that the assessable income of a trust estate may be income of a "particular class", e.g. rent, royalties, dividends or perhaps a share in the net income of a partnership, or indeed whatever the "particular class" may be. That assumption does not appear inherently objectionable. Nor is there any reason why, when a trust makes a distribution and what is distributed is income in the hands of the recipient, that income of the recipient should not be "deemed" by the legislature to be, for income tax purposes, of the same "particular class" as was the income of the trust estate which made the distribution, with whatever consequences, for or against the taxpayer, as may follow. It is, of course, necessary to attend to the actual language of sub-s. 94(5) to see whether it accomplishes this or some other and if so what object. My remarks thus far are confined to the question whether the sub-section's failure to define the concept of "a particular class" is itself a sufficient reason for finding in favour of the taxpayer and dismissing the appeal.

Before dealing further with s.94 of the Act, it ought be noted that the Commissioner's argument was founded upon the following propositions, each of which needed to be determined in his favour before any question of s.94 arose for consideration:

(i) a partnership, within the meaning of sub-s. (6)(1) of the Act, existed between Syncarpia and the other two companies which were tenants in common of the land;

(ii) as a partner in that partnership, Syncarpia's assessable income relevantly consisted of its interest in the net income of that partnership by reason of ss. 90 and 92 (1) of the Act;

(iii) however, Syncarpia was a partner in its capacity as trustee of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust so that its assessable income as a partner in the partnership was derived by it in its capacity as trustee of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust;

(iv) therefore, the assessable income of the trust estate of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust within the meaning of Division 6 of Part III of the Act relevantly consisted of the assessable income of Syncarpia as a partner in the partnership, i.e. Syncarpia's interest in the net income of the partnership;

(v) therefore, the net income of the trust estate of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust relevantly consisted of Syncarpia's interest in the net income of the partnership, less any deductions provided for in the definition of "net income" in s.95 of the Act;

(vi) Mr and Mrs Walsh as trustees of the Lisa Marie Walsh Trust were beneficiaries of the trust estate of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust who were not under any legal disability and who were presently entitled to a share of the income of the trust estate of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust;

(vii) therefore, by virtue of s.97 of the Act, the assessable income of Mr and Mrs Walsh as trustees of the Lisa Marie Walsh Trust relevantly consisted of the share of the net income of the trust estate of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust which they received.

Briefly stated, the Commissioner's claim is that, given the correctness of the propositions above, it has been shown that:

(a) the assessable income of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust included income of a particular class, namely, a share (Syncarpia's interest) in the net income of a partnership;

(b) a share of that net income of the trust estate of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust was included in the assessable income of the trust estate of the Lisa Marie Walsh Trust.

The Commissioner submitted that the requirements of sub-paragraphs (i) and (ii) of paragraphs 94(5)(a) of the Act had therefore been satisfied. Further, according to the Commissioner, by that paragraph the assessable income of the trust estate of the Lisa Marie Walsh Trust was deemed to include "income of that class" (i.e. a share in the net income of the partnership) "of an amount" calculated in accordance with that paragraph.

The next stage, according to the Commissioner, requires reference to sub-s. 94(4). He said that, by the operation of sub-s. (5), in the calculation of the net income of the Lisa Marie Walsh Trust estate in accordance with s.95, there was deemed to be included in the assessable income of that trust estate a share in the net income of the partnership. If that were so, then, since the beneficiary Lisa Marie Walsh, was under the age of 16 years but presently entitled, the consequence of sub-s. 94(4) was that s.94 applied to a portion of the net income of the trust estate of the Lisa Marie Walsh Trust, the portion being calculable by reference to paragraph (b) of sub-s. 94(5).

It is plain that there are problems occasioned by s.94 even in relation to the extremely simple transaction which gave rise to these proceedings. This matter raises only a fraction of the difficulties created, one would think quite unnecessarily, by the language of the section. For example, the present proceedings do not raise any question under para 94(5), either as to apportionment or as to the operation and effect of the latter part of sub-para. 94(5)(a)(ii), which seems to envisage that a series of consecutive distributions of income may be within the scope of the provision. I do not wish to say anything which might unnecessarily inhibit any later consideration of its meaning and operation.

We were pressed with the Treasurer's Explanatory Memorandum to Parliament, which accompanied the Bill by which s.94 was introduced into the Act, as an aid to construction. It may be that such material is admissible for the purpose of disclosing the object of the section, thus providing a basis in appropriate cases for the implementation of s.15AA of the Acts Interpretation Act in addition, of course, to the rules of the common law which permit and require due regard to legislative intention which is manifest despite inappropriate phraseology: see, e.g. Tickle Industries Pty Ltd v. Hann [1974] HCA 5; (1974) 130 C.L.R. 321, 330; Cooper Brookes (Woollongong) Pty Ltd v. F.C.T. [1981] HCA 26; (1981) 55 A.L.J.R. 434; Shah v. Barnet Borough London Council (1983) 1 All E.R. 226, 238. However, it seems to me obvious that such an approach is worth little and indeed will seriously impede the efficient operation of the courts, adding to costs, length of hearings and delays in the judicial system if the extrinsic material itself is unclear and requires debate as to its meaning. Further, even if the extrinsic material does reveal the legislative purpose, there will continue to be boundaries beyond which the words used will not stretch even where it is known that they were intended to do so.

I have found it unnecessary to form a concluded opinion as to the meaning and effect of s.94. In my view, the Commissioner failed to make good the series of propositions, detailed above, upon which his attempt to resort to s.94 depended. Without suggesting that all or any of the Commissioner's other propositions are correct, it is sufficient that propositions (iv) and (v), which are central to the Commissioner's case, are unsustainable. In F.C.T. v. Totledge Pty Ltd (1982) 82 A.T.C. 4168, the Commissioner sought to uphold assessments upon a trustee on the basis, inter alia, that there was no "trust estate" for the purposes of Part 111 Division 6 of the Act so that the assessments were justifiable pursuant to s.25 of the Act. The argument was rejected because the Full Court was satisfied that there was such a trust estate: see pp. 4177-4178. In the present case, however, the point taken by the Commissioner in F.C.T. v. Totledge, supra, is, in my opinion valid and destructive of the Commissioner's case. I shall attempt to demonstrate why as briefly as possible.

Sub-section 6(1) of the Act defines "partnership" to mean, unless the contrary intention appears, an association of persons carrying on business as partners or in receipt of income jointly, but not to include a company. There is no definition in the Act of "carrying on business" although "business" is defined in sub-s. 6(1) to include "any profession, trade, employment, vocation or calling" but not to include "occupation as an employee". Because the first part of the definition of "partnership" in sub-s. 6(1) of the Act speaks of carrying on business "as partners", it seems that it imports the requirements of a partnership under the general law. Sub-section (5) (1) of the Partnership Acts 1891, as amended, (Qld), provides that partnership is the relation which subsists between persons carrying on a business in common with a view of profit. Section 6 of the Queensland Partnership Acts requires that regard be had to rules which it sets down in determining whether a partnership does or does not exist.

On any view of the matter, there was no partnership for present purposes unless Syncarpia and the other two companies constituted an association of persons (i.e. legal persons) which were:

(i) carrying on business; or

(ii) in receipt of income jointly.

I propose to assume that there was a partnership and to consider the consequences of a partnership under each alternative separately.

Carrying on Business

If, in their isolated land dealing for profit, Syncarpia and its co-venturers were carrying on business in partnership, the only asset of such partnership was the land. The partners held the legal title to the land as tenants in common. However, the land as a whole was, on this hypothesis, a partnership asset. The interest of each partner, including Syncarpia, was not, or was not merely, a legal tenancy in common in the land but a beneficial interest in the whole land commensurate with its right to a proportion of the surplus after the sale of the land and the payment of partnership debts and liabilities: F.C.T. v. Everett [1980] HCA 6; (1979) 54 A.L.J.R. 196 If there was such a partnership, it was its interest in the partnership which Syncarpia held on trust and which constituted the presently relevant trust estate of P.J. Walsh Family Trust Unit.

The partnership sold its assets - its sole asset - the land. It paid off its debts, principally at least what it had borrowed to purchase the land. It was left with its profit. Whether that profit was income of the partnership by virtue of sub-s. 26 (a) of the Act (as the Commissioner contended), or the proceeds of sale were income of the partnership by virtue of sub-s. 25(1), the profit constituted the partnership's "net income" as defined in s.90 of the Act. Syncarpia's "assessable income" relevantly consisted of its interest in the profit; sub-s. 92(1) of the Act. Because Syncarpia was a partner in its capacity as trustee of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust, its assessable income as a partner in the partnership was derived by it in its capacity as trustee of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust.

Upon the division between the partners of the partnership profit from the sale of the partnership asset, the partnership came to an end. Further, the relevant trust estate of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust (the interest in the partnership held by Syncarpia as trustee of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust) then ceased to exist. Although, for income tax purposes, the partnership profit was net income of the partnership and Syncarpia's interest in the partnership profit was assessable income, Syncarpia did not receive its share of the partnership profit as income from, i.e., as it is sometimes put, as the fruit of, its interest in the partnership. Syncarpia received its share of the partnership profit in satisfaction and extinguishment of its interest in the partnership which as I have said, was the relevant trust estate of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust.

Receipt of Income Jointly

The other basis upon which the Commissioner contended that, for income tax purposes, a partnership existed between Syncarpia and the other tow companies was that they were in receipt of income jointly. The assumption upon which it is necessary to consider this aspect of the matter is, of course, that a partnership did not otherwise exist; that is to say, Syncarpia and the other two companies were not, in the transaction relating to the land, carrying on business as partners according to usual concepts.

Thus, it is not immediately relevant that, where there is a true partnership (i.e. a partnership which satisfies the requirements of a general law and not merely the latter part of the definition in sub-s. 6(1) of the Act), the gross income of the partnership is earned jointly by the partnership, the outgoings of the partnership are joint outgoings of the partners, and the profits of the partnership are the profits of the partners: see Rowe v. F.C.T. (1982) 82 A.T.C. 4243. The hypothesis, for the moment, is that there was an isolated transaction by three tenants in common resulting in the sale of their land at a profit. Sub-section 25(1) of the Act therefore has no current relevance; the material income of the partnership was not the gross proceeds of the sale of the land. The only income which might have been received jointly by Syncarpia and its co-venturers for present purposes is the profit arising from the sale: ss. 26 (a) and 26AAA of the Act.

The only asset of any such partnership was again the land. Again, the partners held the legal title to the land as tenants in common. There is nothing in the Act of which I am aware which attributes to such a notional partnership for income tax purposes any of the consequences of a true partnership under the general law. Each of the partners accordingly also held the beneficial title to its interest in the land as a tenant in common. Syncarpia of course held its interest in the land on trust for the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust. It was its interest in the land which, on this hypothesis, constituted the presently relevant trust estate of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust.

The partnership, or at least the partners, sold the land. The partners paid off their joint debts, and were left with the profit. That profit was income of the partnership by virtue of sub-s. 26 (1) of the Act and constituted the partnership's "net income" as defined in s. 90 of the Act. Therefore, Syncarpia's "assessable income" relevantly consisted of its interest in the profit (sub-s. 92 (1) of the Act). However, Syncarpia was a partner in its capacity as trustee of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust so that its assessable income as a partner in the partnership was derived by it in its capacity as trustee of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust.

Once again, upon the division between the partners of the partnership profit from the sale of the partnership asset, the partnership came to an end. Further, the relevant trust estate of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust, (Syncarpia's interest in the land which it held as trustee for the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust) then ceased to exist as such. Although, for income tax purposes, the partnership profit was net income of the partnership and Syncarpia's interest in the partnership proper was assessable income, Syncarpia did not receive its share of the partnership profit as income from, i.e. as the fruit of, its interest in the land. Syncarpia received its share of the partnership profit in return for its interest in the land which, as I have said, was the relevant trust estate of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust.

Income of the trust estate

In Everett's Case, supra, a partner in a firm of solicitors assigned to his wife for valuable consideration a proportion of his share in the partnership including his right to a share in the profits. A majority of the High Court (Barwick CJ, Stephen, Mason and Wilson JJ) held that the assignment created a trust of the portion of the share in the partnership in favour of the assignee. The majority rejected a contention (which as in Totledge, supra, was advanced by the Commissioner) that the assignee's share of the profits was not income of a trust estate. At p. 199 2nd col. G to p. 200 1st col. E, their Honours said:

"The appellant's contention is that the income payable to the respondent's wife was not, as the majority in the Federal Court held, 'the net income of a trust estate' within the meaning of s.95 of the Act. The argument is based very largely on the proposition founded on the judgment of Kitto J. in Stewart Dawson Holdings Pty Ltd v. Commissioner of Taxation (1965) 39 A.L.J.R. 300, at p.301, that income derived by a trustee from his own property or by means of his personal exertion, 'income with respect to which a trust arises at the moment of derivation', does not answer the statutory description. Kitto J. was making the point that when a person establishes a trust of his future income simpliciter, the income when it is derived is the subject matter or corpus of the trust, not the fruit of it. To use the terminology of s.95, it is because the income is the 'trust estate' that it cannot be 'the net income of' that trust estate. His Honour's remarks do not touch the case where an immediate trust is established of a proprietary right which yields or earns future income. Then the income is accurately described as income of a trust estate. For reasons which we have already given, this is the situation which obtains here.

Likewise, the observations of Rich and Dixon JJ in Howey v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1930] HCA 45; (1930), 44 C.L.R. 289, at p.293, where a rather different question was considered, have no application here. Their Honours were dealing with a case in which the appellant did not hold the trust estate in virtue of which the income arose. After referring to the statutory definition of 'trustee' in the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922-1928, their Honours said:

'. . . it is therefore difficult to apply the definition in order to overcome the effect of the references in sec. 31 to 'income of the trust estate.' These references suggest that the person who answers the description 'trustee' must stand in some relation to the proprietary right in virtue of which the income arises, even although he need not be a trustee in the proper sense.'

Here, the interest in the partnership in virtue of which the income arises is vested in the respondent as trustee."

For the reasons already advanced, Syncarpia as trustee of the P.J. Walsh Family Unit Trust did not here receive its share of the partnership profit in virtue of its interest in the partnership or the land but in "exchange" for it. The income was the proceeds of realization of the trust estate and became the trust estate of that trust, so that it could not have been "the net income of" that trust estate.

Accordingly, in my opinion, all else aside, the Commissioner's argument foundered on propositions (iv) and (v) supra. There is no reason to consider any of the other questions which might have arisen. The appeal should be dismissed with costs.
