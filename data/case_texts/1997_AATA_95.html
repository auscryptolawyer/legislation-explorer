     REASONS FOR DECISION

21 March 1997 J Block, Senior Member



1. (a) Cases NT95/348 and NT95/349 were listed as related matters for hearing by the Tribunal on 5 February 1997 and 6 February 1997. Although the Taxpayers are two separate companies, the issues in each case are the same; the cases relate to sales of vehicles for use in respect of a Garbage Services Contract (the “Contact”) made on 27 December 1989 between a company which is referred to as the “Contractor” and a shire council which will be referred to as the “Council”.

(b) It was agreed that the Tribunal would concern itself only with the documents and evidence in respect of Case NT95/348, on the basis that the Tribunal’s decision in that case would be treated as binding also on the parties in Case NT95/349. Accordingly, references to the Applicant and the Respondent respectively should be treated as references, respectively, to the Applicant and Respondent in Case NT95/348.

(c) The Applicant, in Case NT95/348 sought the review of a decision by the Respondent made on 18 October 1995 disallowing the Applicant’s objection dated 15 September 1993 against a decision by the Respondent made on 13 July 1993 disallowing the Applicant’s application dated 10 May 1993 (“refund application”) for a refund of sales tax in respect of certain refuse vehicles (“the Vehicles”), in an amount of $225,495.00.

2. (a) The Applicant was represented by Mr A. Gibbs of KPMG Solicitors, and the Respondent was represented by Mr R. Hamilton of Counsel.

(b) The Applicant received into evidence the T Documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and also certain exhibits; being in particular:

  • Exhibit A1 which is a statement dated 28 January 1997 by a senior employee of the Council, and who is referred to in these Reasons as “P”; by agreement between the parties Exhibit A1 was tendered and admitted on the basis that paragraph 11 and the second sentence of paragraph 13 were treated as deleted.
  • Exhibit R1 which is an agreement entitled “Termination and Release of Garbage Services Contract” made between the Contractor and the Council on 21 October 1992.
  • Exhibit R2 which is a letter by the Accountants (as referred to hereafter) to the Council dated 9 July 1990.
  • Exhibit R3 which is a statement by a gentleman who was at the relevant time a senior executive of the Contractor, and who is referred to in these Reasons as S. (S gave evidence by video-link; at the commencement of his evidence S confirmed that his statement, which had previously been submitted to the Tribunal was true and correct in all details (Transcript page 30). It is possible that that statement was inadvertently, and during the hearing, not formally tendered or allotted a specific exhibit number; it is plain that it was intended that it be tendered as an exhibit and was thus admitted and numbered R3.)

(c) Oral evidence was given before the Tribunal by P and S; the evidence of S was (as set out previously) taken by video-link because he is now resident in the United Kingdom and employed by a United Kingdom company which is a member of the group of companies of which the Contractor (and also LCo, referred to hereafter) are members.

(d) In the refund application the Applicant claimed that the Vehicles were exempt from sales tax under Item 78(i) contained in the First Schedule to the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935 (the "Act") which provides an exemption from sales tax for,

"goods for use (whether as goods or in some other form) and not for sale by:

1. a municipal, shire or district council constituted for the general purposes of local government under any law of the Commonwealth or of a State or Territory; ......"


It was common cause that the Council falls within Item 78(i) as a shire council; it was common cause also that the goods (being the Vehicles) were for use and not for sale.

3. (a) Each of the parties furnished the Tribunal with a comparison between the Contract and the Brambles Contract (referred to hereafter); Mr Hamilton in addition furnished the Tribunal with a helpful written outline of the Respondent's submissions.

(b) The comparisons referred to in subclause (a) above were made by reference to the Contract and those provisions of the Brambles Contract which are referred to in the Brambles case (both referred to hereafter). Subsequent to the hearing of the matter, and at the request of the Tribunal, each of the parties obtained a copy of the Brambles Contract, and submitted a further comparison of the Brambles Contract and the Contract. The two comparisons submitted by the parties respectively after the conclusion of the hearing are referred to as the "full comparisons". (In addition to its full comparison, the Applicant furnished the Tribunal with further submissions on the basis that it considered it necessary to do so for the purpose of considering its full comparison.) The parties acknowledged that the only issue to be decided by the Tribunal was as to whether the Vehicles sold by the Taxpayer for use in connection with the Contract fell within the relevant exemption (as set out in clause 2(d)) so that a decision favourable to the Taxpayer would result in the grant of the refund claimed; it was not in other words necessary for the Tribunal to consider whether the sales tax in question was refunded by the Taxpayer to LCo, a company associated with the Contractor.

(c) Although there are references in the T Documents which would suggest that the Vehicles were sold by the Taxpayer to the Contractor, the evidence indicated that the parties accept that the Vehicles were sold by the Taxpayer to LCo; the Vehicles were then the subject of a finance lease arrangement between LCo as lessor and the Contractor as lessee. No written instrument or agreement recording the terms of any such finance lease arrangement was furnished to the Tribunal; it may be, given that LCo and the Contractor are members of the same group of companies, that the arrangement was not reduced to writing. Although that finance lease arrangement will be referred to again in the context of clause 38 of the Contract, nothing very much turns on whether or not it was recorded in writing.

4. The T Documents, the Exhibits and the Statements of Facts, Issues and Contention by each of the parties made it clear that, having regard to the decision of the Full Federal Court in FC of T v Brambles Holdings Limited 91 ATC 4285 (the "Brambles case") the Applicant would be entitled to succeed only it if could establish (having regard to the decision in the Brambles case) either:

(a) that the Vehicles were leased or hired by the Contractor to the Council; (this contention is referred to as the "leasing contention”); or

(b) that pursuant to the Contract, and having regard also to the decision in D.F.C. of T. v. Stewart & Anor 84 ATC 4146 (“Stewart’s case”), the Council, in accordance with the Contract, obtained control (not necessarily exclusive control) sufficient to enable the Tribunal to distinguish the facts in this case from those of the majority judgment in the Brambles case; this contention is referred to as the “control contention”.

5. All references to the “Brambles case” should be construed inclusively of the original decision by Davies J. in Brambles Holdings Ltd v F.C. of T. 90 ATC 4584 (separately, where relevant, referred to as the “original Brambles decision”); the original Brambles decision was the subject of an appeal and cross-appeal, which was dismissed by the Full Federal Court. The Brambles case was also concerned with a garbage services contract (“the Brambles Contract”) between a contractor (Brambles Holdings Ltd, and referred to as “Brambles”) and the City of Knox (referred to as the “Knox Council”); it was held in respect of the Brambles Contract that while the bins therein referred to were hired to the Council thus attracting the sales tax exemption, the vehicles therein referred to were not; the Brambles Contract, in respect of the vehicles referred to therein, constituted a contract for the supply of garbage services and not a supply of the vehicles used in providing those services.

6. (a) The Tribunal is concerned, in respect of the Contract, only with the Vehicles sold by the Taxpayer to LCo and in respect of which a refund is claimed. It is thus necessary in the first instance to consider whether, as contended by the Applicant, both in the refund application and in the proceedings before the Tribunal, the Contract constituted a lease or hiring of the Vehicles by the Contractor to the Council.

(b) In respect of the leasing contention, the evidence on behalf of the Contractor and the Council respectively is contradictory. Clause 9 of Exhibit A1 (the statement by P) may be contrasted with clause 6(i) of Exhibit R3 (the statement by S); those two paragraphs are (amended only to preserve anonymity) quoted in full as follows;

Clause 9 of Exhibit A1:

“9. In the tender process and in the preparation of the Contract, the Council sought advice from the Accountants regarding the sales tax issues concerning the cab/chassis units for refuse vehicles (“vehicles”) and mobile garbage bins to be purchased by the Contractor under the Contract. The Council was advised that the agreement between the Council and the Contractor should be structured such that the amount payable by the Council to the Contractor for the provision of garbage services consisted of two elements namely:

(a) A hire or lease charge in respect of the provision of the vehicles and mobile garbage bins; and

(b) A separate waste disposal service fee

...”


Clause 6(i) of Exhibit R3:

“(i) A weekly bin pick-up which constituted a large capital investment on the part of the Contractor involving the purchase of mobile garbage bins and appropriate vehicles. The vehicles were purchased from the Taxpayer by LCo and leased to the Contractor. In respect of this item there was a charge for the service of $1.26 per bin.

It was necessary to substantiate to the Council the proportion of certain costs to the total costs. In relation to this particular service the amount of 39.57% was for the lease charges in respect of the provision of the vehicles. It was not the intention of the Contractor to hire the vehicles to the council and the reference to “hire and/or lease charges in respect of the vehicles...” in clause 2(kk)(iii)(a) of the contract is a reference to the Contractor’s internal apportionment of equipment costs to total costs. The balance of the costs referred to in 2(kk)(iii)(b) i.e. 60.43% was for other associated costs such as wages, fuel maintenance etc.”

(c) The respective opposing contentions by the parties in this regard should be considered in relation to questions by the Respondent and answers by the persons questioned, and contained in T8-T14 (both inclusive) of the T Documents. It is unnecessary, in respect of the leasing contention, to deal in specific terms with the T Documents referred to; suffice it to say that each of the Council and the Contractor in effect adhered to the contentions advanced on their respective behalves in the statements which are Exhibits A1 and R3 respectively.

(d) P in his oral evidence stated that the Contract, which is a lengthy document, went through a considerable number of drafts and was the subject of extensive negotiation. At a comparatively late stage in the negotiations, sales tax advice was sought by the Council from a well known firm of accountants (who will be referred to as the “Accountants”) and by the Contractor from a well known firm of solicitors (who will be referred to as the “Solicitors”). That tax advice is set out in annexures “B”, “C”, “D” and “E” to Exhibit A1. It may be noted in particular that all of the sales tax advices referred to in those annexures were given by the professional firms concerned prior to the delivery of the original Brambles decision. In this particular context:

(1) in Annexure “B” to Exhibit A1 which is a letter by the Accountants to the Council dated 29 March 1989 the Accountants recommended inter alia, at the foot of page 4 that the price payable to the Contractor consist of two elements, in the following terms:

“In our opinion, under the current terms of the tender, it would be difficult for the contractor to justify purchasing either the trucks or the mobile garbage bins free of sales tax. We say this because it would seem that the contractor, rather than the Council, has substantial use of the relevant goods. Under the current terms of the tender which stipulate an all up price per service, the Australian Taxation Office would, from our experience, consider that the contractor, rather than the Council, is the end user of the goods and therefore sales tax exemption would not be available.

To enable the goods to be purchased tax free by the contractor on the basis that they are to be substantially used by the Council, we recommend that the terms of the tender be altered such that the price quoted by the contractor consists of two elements. i.e.:
  1. a hire or lease charge in respect of the provision of the trucks and the bins; and
  2. a separate waste disposal service fee.”

(2) Annexure “C” which is a letter by the Accountants to the Council dated 4 July 1989 in effect repeats the advice previously given.

(3) Annexure “D” is a letter by the Accountants to the Council dated 1 September 1989 which read (in part):

“In our letter of 29th March, 1989, we recommended the terms of the tender be drafted such that the price quoted by the contractor consists of two elements, i.e.:
  1. a hire or lease charge in respect of the provision of the trucks and the bins; and
  2. a separate waste disposal service fee.
We believe that by hiring the capital plant and equipment to the Council, the contractor would be in the best position to argue that the goods are clearly dedicated to the use of the Council.

The draft contract does not provide for separate plant hire/disposal service fees and, accordingly, we believe the case for exemption is weakened.

Other factors such as affixing Council logo to the side of the vehicle or mobile garbage bins, although persuasive, are not conclusive that the goods are substantially applied to Council’s use.”

(4) Annexure “E” includes a fax by the Solicitors, to the Contractor dated 19 June 1989 and also a fax by the Solicitors to the Contractor dated 9 June 1989; it is not necessary to quote their advice which was generally to the effect that they had doubts as to whether a sales tax exemption for the Vehicles would be available.

(e) P in his evidence stated that he had been informed, (prior to execution of the Contract by the solicitors who represented the Council), that the Contract in its final form had, prior to its execution been considered by the Accountants who had confirmed that it was in a form approved by them; however, it would seem that no such confirmation may have been obtained. Exhibit R2 which is a letter by the Accountants to the Council dated 9 July 1990 issued shortly after the delivery of the original Brambles decision provides:

“As you are aware, we reviewed the draft contract for the provision of garbage services by the Contractor to the Council. We recommended in our letter dated 29th March, 1989 and restated in our letter of 1st September, 1989 that the terms of the tender be altered such that the contract consisted of two elements, i.e..:

- a hire or lease charge in respect of the provision of the trucks and the bins; and

- a separate waste disposal service fee.

We understand that the final contract does not provide for this breakup.

It may be that the Contractor, if it has claimed exemption on the bins and trucks, will seek to vary the contract price.

Consequently, we strongly recommend that we be consulted before Council has discussions with the Contractor regarding this matter.”

(f) While in general terms P’s credibility is not in doubt, the Contract, in its terms contradicts his assertion that the Council intended in respect of the Vehicles to achieve a lease or hiring arrangement equally and, for the same reasons the contentions set out in the refund application that the Vehicles were leased by the Contractor to the Council and then licensed or bailed back to the Contractor (T4 of the T Documents) are untenable. On the contrary, the Contract in its terms clearly relates to a supply of services and not a supply of vehicles. The reasons why the leasing contention is untenable are more fully set out in the next succeeding clause 7.

(d) There are other provisions in the Contract which indicate that it was not intended in respect of the Vehicles that a hiring arrangement be constituted; by way of example:

(1) the Council is not described as a hirer;

(2) there is no separation (contrary to the tax advice previously referred to) of a fee for the hiring of the Vehicles;

(3) the Contractor was nevertheless liable for all running expenses (clause 20(d) of the Contract), and damage caused (clause 13(a) of the Contract); in respect of the latter provision, there was no indemnity by the Council, and on the contrary the Contractor was required to indemnify the Council against damage (clause 17 of the Contract);

(e) Even more to the point is the fact that clause 2(kk) is a definitional provision only and not a substantive provision, it is not again referred to anywhere in the Contract, and in effect appears to serve no useful purpose. The Contract does not anywhere contain any provision which could be construed as a stand-alone leasing or hiring arrangement, which, if it were truly intended that the Contract should indeed constitute a leasing or hiring arrangement, might reasonably have anticipated.

(f) It seems likely that clause 2(kk) (which in accordance with P’s evidence was inserted only in later drafts of the Contract) was inserted following the receipt of the tax advice referred to previously in these Reasons, in the hope that it would achieve the desired sales tax exemption.

(g) T15 is a draft advice by Mr Sundberg QC (now Mr Justice Sundberg). While that advice, being a draft only and moreover not complete, must be treated with caution I am in agreement with, and thus adopt his reasoning, as set out, (amended slightly for the reasons set out previously) in relation to clause 8 entitled “Hire or lease?" as follows:



“8. Hire or Lease?

The question then is whether the definition of “Price per Service” alters the position, and in effect puts the vehicles in the same position as the bins in Brambles. Had the refuse trucks in that case been hired to the Council, they would have been held exempt. The Contractor contract has no distinct, stand-alone, hiring clause.

The reference to “hire and/or lease charges” appears only in a definition related to the calculation of the amount payable by the Council under clause 33(b). Clause 33(a) deals with payment during the Stage One Period. The payment is for “the Contractor rendering the garbage services to the Council”. The amount payable is an amount equal to “117.6% of the Contractor’s Direct Operating Costs of providing the garbage services”. Direct Operating Costs are “the Contractor’s reasonable and necessary costs of providing the services pursuant to this Agreement”, but excluding any costs or expenses involved in complying with clauses 24 or 20(f).

There is no mention of leasing or hiring in relation to Stage One. Payment of Stage Two is again expressed to be “for the Contractor rendering the services to the Council herein provided for”. The amount payable is the price per service as set out in clause 2. “Price per Service” is there defined in pars. (i) and (ii) in a manner that makes no reference to hire or lease. 9. It is convenient at this point to refer to the decision in the Brambles case, and including for this purpose the original Brambles decision.

(a) Davies J, at p4588 of the original Brambles decision set out the reasoning in respect of the "use" test set out in Stewart's case as follows:

“In D.F.C. of T. v. Stewart & Anor 84 ATC 4146; [1984] HCA 11; (1984) 154 C.L.R. 385, it was pointed out that the First Schedule to the Act describes exempted goods principally by reference to the nature or character of the goods themselves or by reference to the use to which the goods are to be put. As Gibbs C.J. said at ATC p.4149; C.L.R. p. 390:

“In many cases of the second kind the words ‘for use’ indicate the purpose to which it is intended the goods shall be put, rather than the use for which the goods were designed.”

The members of the Court pointed out that the prescribed test is to be examined as at the pint of time at which the relevant and applicable Sales Tax Assessment Act imposes liability for duty. Gibbs C.J., with whom Dawson J. agreed, Brennan and Deane JJ. each explained that exclusivity of use was not required. At ATC p.4149; C.L.R. pp. 390-391 Gibbs C.J. said:

“The word ‘use’ does not connote exclusive use. ...No doubt an article would not fall within Item 81 if the use to which it was to be put by the hospital or institution was transient or insubstantial; the suggestion in F.C. of T. v. Hamersley Iron Pty. Ltd. 81 ATC 4582 at p. 4590; (1981) 37 A.L.R. 595 at p. 605, that the goods must be for use ‘to a significant degree’, would appear to be correct. However if the goods are intended to be put to substantial use by a public benevolent institution, they will fall within Item 81 notwithstanding that they may be applied to the use of others as well.”

At ATC p. 4153; C.L.R. pp. 397-398 Brennan J. said:

“The use by a public benevolent institution referred to in Item 81 is not necessarily exclusive of the use of the goods by others. Indeed, the use of particular goods by others is often the use intended for them by public benevolent institutions - hospital beds, for example. But the proposed use by a public benevolent institution must be sufficiently substantial in extent and time that it is right to regard that proposed use of the goods as giving a character to the goods. That is a question of fact and degree. Among the material circumstances which reveal a characteristic use, regard may be had to the nature of the goods, the activity of the institution which is to be advanced by using the goods, the terms upon which the goods are to be acquired by the institution or upon which the institution is to be entitled to use them, the power of others to determine or qualify that use, and the likelihood of the use being changed by the decision of the institution, by the decision of another person having power to determine or qualify the use or by the decision of both the institution and that person. As the question whether goods were goods for use by a public benevolent institution is likely to arise for determination after the institution has begun to use them, evidence of the actual use to which the goods have been put will be relevant and admissible.”

At ATC p. 4155; C.L.R. pp. 401-402, Deane J. said:

“From this variety of references to ‘goods for use’, three presently relevant points emerge with tolerable clarity. The first is that, where it is intended that goods be for use exclusively or ‘primarily and principally’ before they come within a particular exemption, the First Schedule expressly says so. The second is that, where the relevant requirement is that goods be ‘for use by’ a particular person or organisation (eg. the Governor-General, the Governor of a State, certain Navy, Army and Air Force Institutions, a public benevolent institution: Items 73, 73A, 74E, 81(1)), the requirement is referring to projected actual use rather than the inherent quality or nature of the goods themselves. The third is that, in those items (such as Item 81) where it is required that goods be ‘for use’ by a particular category of person or organisation, there is no designation of any person, such as the manufacturer or the purchaser of the goods, by reference to whose purposes or intentions the question whether the relevant requirement is satisfied must be determined. ...

Item 81 does not require that the goods be used ‘exclusively’ or ‘primarily and principally’ by a public benevolent institution. It suffices that the goods come within the classification of goods for use by such an institution. That does not mean that any intended or planned or possible subsequent use of the goods by a public benevolent institution, however transient or insignificant or uncertain, would justify the goods being characterised as ‘goods for use’ by that institution. The projected use by the institution must be such as would warrant characterisation of the goods by reference to it. Ordinarily, that would involve definite commitment to a use by the institution as the main projected use of them. In other words, the question whether particular goods satisfy the description of being goods for use by a public benevolent institution will ordinarily fall to be answered by identifying the relevant projected use of the goods and by ascertaining whether that use answers the description of a use of the goods by a public benevolent institution.”

Therefore, to meet the description of goods “for use by” the council, the intended use by the council, if not exclusive, must be sufficient so to characterise the goods. In Stewart’s case, in the instance where goods “may be applied to the use of others as well”, Gibbs C.J. thought that “substantial use” by the designated authority would be sufficient. At ATC p. 4153; C.L.R. p. 397, Brennan J. thought that the use “must be sufficiently substantial in extent and time that it is right to regard that proposed use of the goods as giving a character to the goods”. At ATC p. 4155; C.L.R. p. 402, Deane J. thought that ordinarily the question was whether the “use answers the description of a use of the goods by a public benevolent institution”. .”

(b) Again in respect of the original Brambles decision, Davies J., (at page 4589) noted that the Council had no rights in respect of the Vehicles.

(d) Beaumont J. delivering the majority judgment of the Full Federal Court found that the trucks were not exempt goods; see in this context pages 4295, 4296 and 4297 from which I quote as follows:

“Were the trucks exempt goods?

On behalf of Brambles, it is submitted that an exemption such as Item 78 should be construed and applied liberally; that it would defeat the legislative purpose behind Item 78 if a narrow view were to be taken of the form of the transaction by which a council could secure the relevant “use” of the goods; that it is not necessary that a council have a contractual right to use the goods physically; and that it is sufficient if the Council can, as here, require that the goods be “used” entirely on its behalf in the provision of the relevant service.

Counsel for Brambles relies especially upon the reasoning of Gibbs J. in Max Factor & Co. (Inc. in U.S.A.) v. F.C. of T. 71 ATC 4136 at p. 4138; [1971] HCA 36; (1971) 124 C.L.R. 353 at pp. 361-362, to the effect that the phrase “goods applied to his own use” in s. 17(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No.1) 1930 is of broad import and is equivalent in meaning to “employed for his own purposes”; and that the meaning of the noun “use” is not confined to physical consumption: its ordinary meanings include “purpose served” and a “purpose, object or end, especially of a useful or advantageous nature”.

It was there held that a manufacturer may apply goods to his own use within the meaning of s. 17(1), notwithstanding that he gives away the goods to others, provided that the purpose for which the goods are given is a purpose of the manufacturer. But, in my opinion, this reasoning is not applicable here. For one thing, we are now concerned with the interpretation of an exempting provision and not with the distinct question of liability to tax. For another, the phrase “goods applied [by a manufacturer] for his own use” is, I think, substantially different, in terms, and in context, from the expression “goods for use ... by ... a ... council.”

On behalf of Brambles, reliance is also placed upon Stewart’s Case, above. There, a supplier of lottery tickets also manufactured machines which could be used by its customers to sell the tickets mechanically. It supplied a number of the machines to certain public benevolent institutions free of charge. The institutions used the machines to raise funds by conducting lotteries. The machines remained the property of the supplier, who serviced them. Item 81(1)(c) of the First Schedule exempts “Goods for use (whether as goods or in some other form), and not for sale, by ... a public benevolent institution”.

...

In this respect, the present case is, in my view, on all fours with the decision in Otto’s Case, above (see our reasons in the appeal in that matter [91 ATC 4305]). The primary Judge in Otto’s Case said (at p.4608):

“... the word ‘use’ in the context of Item 78 does not connote exclusive use; but this is not to the point in the present case because in my view there is no relevant use at all by the councils concerned. The use is the use of the bins by the applicant as contractor to the councils, though in one sense the use is shared by the local residents themselves who deposit their rubbish into the Otto carts. That fact of shared use would not, of course, destroy the exemption if it were otherwise available.”

The Judge said (at p. 4610) that the hiring of the rubbish containers in the present case was “the vital point of distinction” between the two cases.

Reverting to the popular or ordinary meaning of the words “for use ... by ... a ... council”, it can, I think, be accepted that the trucks were appropriate, or adapted, to being employed, put into service, or applied, for the purposes of the Council, for the reasons given in the case of the containers. That is to say, the trucks were for use for a Council purpose. But were the goods for use by the Council? That is to say, were they for use with the participation, or through the agency or efficacy of the Council?

I have come to the conclusion that there was no relevant user of the trucks by the Council.

As has been said, these goods were not entrusted to the Council’s possession. Nor did the Council conduct the day to day operation of the trucks. It is true that the Council arranged that Brambles supply to the Council waste collection services. But Brambles did not supply the trucks to the Council, by hire or otherwise. What Brambles supplied to the Council was its services, but not its goods. Council acquired no rights of any kind over the trucks.

In Arbuckle Smith & Co. Ltd. v. Greenock Corporation [1960] 1 All E.R. 568, Lord Radcliffe said at p. 574:

“‘Use’ is not a word of precise meaning, but in general it conveys the idea of enjoyment derived by the user from the corpus of the object enjoyed.”

In the present case, in my opinion, the Council did not enjoy the objects in question in this sense. Put differently, the Council used Brambles’ services; it did not use Brambles’ goods.

It is true that Council had a statutory function and duty to ensure that waste was properly collected and that Council could have discharged that function and duty by acquiring possession of, and access to, vehicles by hire or other methods. The Council did not do this. It is hardly necessary to say that Item 78 comes to an actual situation as it finds it; it is not possible, for present purposes, to reconstruct events which have in fact occurred.

It may be accepted that an exemption such as Item 78 should, ordinarily, receive a liberal interpretation so as to give effect to a policy to promote a particular activity, in this case, the use by councils of goods for council purposes and not for resale (see eg. F.C. of T. v. I.C.I. Australia Ltd 72 ATC 4213 at pp. 4216-4217, 4227-4228; [1972] HCA 75; (1971-1972) 127 C.L.R. 529 at pp. 563, 581). But where, as here, the facts disclose no intended or actual user with the participation, or through the agency, or efficacy, of a council, it would be contrary to the policy sought to be promoted by the Parliament to allow an exemption.

It is plain that the present items were “for use” by somebody. The question for determination is: For whose use? Use by whom? It is one thing to say, as is common ground here, that the goods were for use by a contractor in the provision of services to a council. But it is a different thing, I think, to say that the goods were for use by the Council. In my opinion, as a matter of the language of the provision and its evident policy, the latter falls within the description of the exemption, whereas the former does not. Unless it can be said that the contractor and the Council were jointly, or even concurrently, to use the goods (and this is not, in my view, open) the present case is one of use by the contractor, but not of use by the Council.”

The minority judgment by Burchett J. referred to the closeness of the Council’s involvement in the performance of the Contract. See in this context page 4301 of the Brambles case as follows:

“But in my opinion, the most significant thing about the contract, for present purposes, is the closeness of the Council’s involvement in its performance. This is not a case of a party engaging a contractor and remaining in the background, demanding only results. Here, the Council never ceased to be involved, in close and constant detail, with the carrying out, day by day, of the garbage collection work. Of course, it had a statutory duty, which it may well have regarded as both politically and legally incapable of entire delegation. The agreement to hire the MGBs is not tacked on to the rest of the arrangements as some addition. It is an integral part of them, consistent with the permanent marking of the MGBs with the Council’s name, the recording of each serial number and address by the Council, the terms of the inaugural brochure firmly attaching responsibility for the waste collection service to the Council itself and referring enquirers to its Waste Management Department, the daily supervision of the work, and the direct participation of the Council’s officers whenever the system faltered. Even if, technically, the MGBs were not bailed to the Council, the right described by the contract as a hiring reflected the real agreement of the parties that the MGBs would be obtained for the Council, delivered on its behalf, paid for on a weekly basis at its expense, and ultimately controlled by it.

The close involvement of the Council did not stop with delivery of the MGBs to the kerb for collection. The garbage trucks were marked with a K, for the City of Knox. They were dedicated to the waste collection task. They operated under the same daily supervision. Even their drivers - who, in a normal contract situation, would have been answerable only to their own employer - had to be satisfactory to the Council. It was the Council which dealt with complaints about the service they gave, and might direct reparation where it considered there had been some omission.

Except when exercising some of the specific powers I have mentioned, the Council did not directly carry out the waste collection work. But it could be said to have used a contractor to do so. At the same time, through the contractor, and through its own supervisory operations, it used the MGBs and the trucks. So far as it did so through the contractor, the maxim applies: “Qui facit per alium facit per se”. Of course, it used both the MGBs and the trucks to proclaim publicly (by its name on the MGBs and initial on the trucks) that the waste collection service was provided by the Council. That, in itself, might not be a very large use by comparison with the total work done by the MGBs and the trucks, but it is significant as an indication that in reality all the work is an activity carried on by the Council, though in a substantial measure through its contractor. Taken together with the Council’s direct role, it is an important use. So far as the MGBs were concerned, having obtained a contractual right in respect of them, the Council used them by making them available to the persons at whose premises they were placed: Tourapark Pty. Ltd. v. F.C. of T. 82 ATC 4105 at pp. 4106-4107, 4110; [1982] HCA 18; (1982) 149 C.L.R. 176 at pp. 181, 187; Max Factor & Co. (Inc. in USA) v. F.C. of T. 71 ATC 4136 at pp. 4137, 4138; [1971] HCA 36; (1971) 124 C.L.R. 353 at pp. 359, 362.”

(e) The Tribunal accepts that the Contract was in some respects draconian; moreover the Tribunal accepts that the Council had powers and remedies which exceeded those conferred on Knox Council under the Brambles Contract. But, and as the Contract and the evidence revealed, those powers were in essence powers entitling the Council to ensure that the Contractor carried out its obligations under the Contract or in default to take the necessary steps themselves. Under the Contract, (as with the Brambles Contract), it was the Contractor who performed the garbage service, notwithstanding that its performance was supervised and monitored.

(f) Davies J. in the original Brambles decision noted that the Knox Council in the Brambles case had no rights in the Vehicles; a similar sentence appears in the majority judgment in the Brambles case at page 4296: “Council acquired no rights of any kind over the trucks”. Clause 42(b) of the Contract contains a provision which does not appear to have a counterpart in the Brambles Contract. Clause 42(b)(i) provides:

“Upon termination of this instrument pursuant to paragraph (a) hereof the Contractor agrees that it shall offer to sell to the Council, and the Council agrees that it may, if it so desires, purchase from the Contractor all the Plant and Equipment owned or leased by the Contractor for the price and on the terms hereinafter appearing.”

In effect clause 42(b) of the Contract obliged the Council on termination to offer the Vehicles (inter alia) to the Council and the Council was entitled if it so desired to accept the offer; in such event and on acceptance the price was to be that agreed between them or failing agreement determined in accordance with a procedure specified in the Contract.

However, clause 42(b) of the Contract does not confer any rights on the Council in relation to the Vehicles prior to termination of the Contract; this is made clear by the definition of “Plant and Equipment” contained in clause 2(ff) which reads as follows:

“‘Plant and Equipment’ shall, for the purposes of Clause 42 hereof, mean all the plant, equipment and stock-in-trade used by or held ready for use by the Contractor as at the date of termination of this Agreement including without limiting the generality thereof all trucks, motor vehicles, compactors but excluding all Mobile Garbage Bins (other than those referred to specifically in Clause 24(j) and (k) of this Agreement), Bulk Containers or Other Approved Garbage Bins or Garbage Containers.”


(g) Grove J. in Transfield Kumagai Contracting Pty. Ltd. v. F.C. of T. 90 ATC 4960 distinguished the original Brambles decision because the Authority involved (the Roads & Traffic Authority) (“RTA”) retained the freehold in the relevant land and moreover had the right at any time to close down the tunnel. The limited rights conferred on the Council under clause 42(b) of the Contract do not in my view, constitute rights in any way analogous with or comparable to those vested in the RTA in the Transfield case. A right to buy such Vehicles as remain in use under the Contract at termination is a right of a sort, but hardly equitable with the rights retained by the RTA in the Transfield case. Assume that the Contractor sold vehicles prior to termination of the Contract; it does not seem that the Council would have been entitled to any injunctive or for that matter any other relief.

(h) S in his evidence said that it was always anticipated that the Vehicles would be purchased by the Council on termination; however, clause 42(b) of the Contract does not so provide, and in any event P said that prior to the execution of the agreement which is exhibit R1, there was a possibility of another contractor taking over the Contract.

10. The Tribunal considers, in respect of the Contract, that the Council had a “close involvement” comparable with that described by Burchett J. in his minority judgment in the Brambles case. However, the Tribunal is bound, unless it can properly distinguish the majority decision in the Brambles case, to follow that majority decision. The Tribunal does not consider that it can or should distinguish the majority decision in the Brambles case, and accordingly the control contention is also rejected.

11. Accordingly, the decision under review is affirmed.
