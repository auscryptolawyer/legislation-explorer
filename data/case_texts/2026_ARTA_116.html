Statement of Reasons

  1. Aus Loadshifting (ALS) Pty Ltd (Aus Loadshifting or Applicant) has sought a review in the Tribunal of the Commissioner of Taxation’s (Commissioner or Respondent) decision dated 10 March 2025, to refuse the Applicant’s application for an extension of time to lodge an objection.
  2. For reasons that are explained below, I am satisfied that although the Commissioner made a decision that is (in form), a reviewable decision enlivening the Tribunal’s jurisdiction, the Applicant’s purported taxation objection was not validly made. The substantive application therefore has no reasonable prospects of success as there is no valid objection to which an extension of time can attach. In those circumstances, it is appropriate to dismiss the application for review.
  3. If I am wrong about the validity of the Applicant’s taxation objection, I am nevertheless satisfied that the correct and preferable decision would be to affirm the Respondent’s objection decision.

In making this determination, I also had regard to the Applicant’s circumstances including the challenges associated to being unrepresented and the manner in which it presented the case with a focus on the substance of the dispute. In this context, I decided not to determine the dismissal application separately and instead directed that all issues be heard and determined together. The matter was listed for a hearing on 3 December 2025.

THE LEGAL PRINCIPLES

  1. Part IVC of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (TAA 53) refers to taxation objections, reviews, and appeals. The following sections in Part IVC are relevant in this proceeding.
  2. Section 14ZL of the TAA 53 sets out when Part IVC applies and the meaning of taxation objection, as follows:
    (1) This Part applies if a provision of an Act or of regulations (including the provision as applied by another Act) provides that a person who is dissatisfied with an assessment, determination, notice or decision, or with a failure to make a private ruling, may object against it in the manner set out in this Part.

    (2) Such an objection is in this Part called a taxation objection.

  3. Section 14ZU of the TAA 53 states out how taxation objections are to be made by taxpayers:
    A person making a taxation objection must:

    (a) make it in the approved form; and

    (b) lodge it with the Commissioner within the period set out in section 14ZW; and

    (c) state in it, fully and in detail, the grounds that the person relies on.

    Note: A person who objects against the Commissioner's failure to make a private ruling must lodge a draft private ruling with the objection: see subsection 359-50(4).

  4. Section 14ZY of the TAA 53 states that the Commissioner is to decide taxation objections. He must decide whether to allow the taxation objection, wholly or in part, or disallow it:[2] A decision of the Commissioner on a taxation objection is an objection decision.[3]
  5. If the person is dissatisfied with the Commissioner’s objection decision, the person may, if the decision is a reviewable objection decision, apply to the Tribunal for the review of the decision or appeal to the Federal Court against the decision.[4] A reviewable objection decision is defined in s 14ZQ of the TAA 53 to mean an objection decision that is not an ineligible income tax remission decision.
  6. It is generally accepted that an objection does not need to need not be in formal or legal language [5] but it must at least specify the taxation decision they are objecting to, and provide sufficiently detailed grounds for their objection.[6]
  7. A mere assertion that an assessment is wrong in fact or law, without more, does not amount to a statement of grounds ‘fully and in detail’ as required by the TAA 53. As the Court has observed 'Vague grounds such as that the assessment is excessive are not, in my opinion, a compliance with the Act.'[7]
  8. Having regard to the authorities, an objection will be valid where it identifies the assessment, determination, notice, or decision objected to and does so with such precision to enable the Commissioner to understand what aspects of that assessment, determination, notice or decision is said to be incorrect and why.
  9. Where the statutory period for lodging an objection has expired, a taxpayer may nevertheless lodge an objection, together with a written request that it be dealt with as if it had been lodged within time. In considering whether to exercise the discretion, the decision maker must have regard to the objection itself and any material put forward in support of the request.
  10. In Brown v the Commissioner of Taxation,[8] the Court identified some common relevant considerations for a decision maker:
    (a) The taxpayer's explanation for the delay and the circumstances attendant upon that delay;

    (b) Whether the objection is, on its face, frivolous, bound to fail or the taxpayer has no arguable case;

    (c) Any prejudice to the Commissioner;

    (d) Such other matters as the circumstances of the particular case make relevant.

  11. The court emphasised the discretion is to be exercised by reference to the particular circumstances of the case and what justice requires. While a stronger substantive case may favour the exercise of the discretion, the decision maker is not required to engage in any assessment of credit or reconcile the taxpayers evidence to other material in the Commissioner’s possession.
  12. If such a request is refused, the taxpayer may apply to the Tribunal for review of that decision[9].
  13. Section 101 of the ART Act provides that the Tribunal may, at any time, dismiss an application if satisfied in s 101(1)(b) that the application has no reasonable prospects of success.
  14. For the Tribunal to be satisfied of no reasonable prospects of success of a given application, there must exist a high degree of certainty about the ultimate outcome of the proceeding if it were allowed to go to proceed in the ordinary way.[10]
  15. Previous Court and Tribunal decisions that have considered section 42B of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth) , which is the predecessor to s 101 of the ART Act have emphasised that the dismissal power should be exercised cautiously and carefully.[11]

ISSUES

  1. The issues before the Tribunal for determination are as follows:
    (a) Whether the application for review should be dismissed pursuant to s101(1) of the ART Act on the basis that the substantive application has no reasonable prospects of success. This issue turns on whether the Applicant has valid taxation objection, by identifying an assessment, decision, determination or notice that is capable of being objected to and by satisfying the requirements in s 14ZU of the TAA 53.

    (b) To the extent that the Tribunal can identify a valid objection, whether the Tribunal ought to grant an extension of time for the Applicant to make any such objection, pursuant to ss 14ZW and 14ZX of the TAA 53.

THE APPLICANT’S CONTENTIONS

  1. At the hearing, Ms Irmisch argued:
    (a) The Applicant is objecting to the Assessment of net amounts following the revisions made to the quarterly BAS by the Tax Agent during the 2012 to 2016 income years.

    (b) Even though the PAYGW is only a component of the assessment, this does not render the entire assessment unobjectionable. The facts of this case are distinguishable from those in the Price v Commissioner of Taxation (Price).[14]

    (c) In circumstances where there is a valid objection, the Tribunal should exercise a discretion grant an extension of time having regard to:

    • the strength of the Applicant’s case. They have proven the revisions were unauthorised, included incorrect figures and they have made payments more than the amounts said to be owed; and
    • the Applicant has tried to resolve the matter with the ATO since 2017 as evidenced by the safe harbour application, complaints to various external bodies and discussions with the ATO about the debt.

THE RESPONDENT’S CONTENTIONS

  1. The Respondent contended that in the purported objection, the Applicant failed to identify an assessment, determination, notice or decision capable of being objected to. Where no valid taxation objection exists, there is no objection to which an extension of time can attach. In those circumstances, the discretion to extend the time within which to make an objection cannot be exercised in a manner favourable to the taxpayer because any such decision would have no legal effect.
  2. In the alternative, the Respondent contended that should the Tribunal find a valid objection has been lodged, his refusal to grant the extension of time should be affirmed because the facts of this case (and in particular the poor prospects of success) weigh against the exercise of this discretion.

CONSIDERATION OF THE ISSUES

  1. The Tribunal has jurisdiction to consider the application for review as the Commissioner made an objection decision. This is the case, notwithstanding the fact the taxation objection itself was invalid.[15]

Is there a valid taxation objection?

  1. A taxation objection must do more than merely express dissatisfaction with an aspect of the Commissioner’s business in general terms. While no particular form of words is required, it must identify with sufficient clarity:
    • the assessment, determination, notice, or decision objected to; and
    • what is said to be wrong with it, and why.
  2. The objection form lodged by the Applicant did not identify any assessment, determination, notice, or decision of the Commissioner. It referred instead only to an ‘Amended assessment’ and reference numbers which related to phone calls or other interactions with the Commissioner. There was no further identifying detail such as the specific tax liability, tax period, date of issue or other information capable of identifying the decision being challenged. Standing alone, it did not meet the minimum statutory requirement for a valid objection.
  3. Deficiencies in the objection form may, in some instances, be cured by the provision of further information. The Applicant’s email dated 7 February 2025 provided slightly more context, referring to unauthorised amended activity statements and discrepancies in figures. However, on a fair reading, that communication still failed to identify an objectionable decision with particularity or explain how any such decision was incorrect. The issue was framed as a concern as to how the amendments were made and the existence of a debt on the ICA, rather than an objection to a specific assessment.
  4. I accept that poor expression is not a bar to a valid objection. But, even allowing for the Applicant’s unrepresented status, the objection form and associated material does not, in substance, disclose the elements required by s 14ZU of the TAA 53.
  5. At the hearing, the Applicant characterised the taxation objection as relating to the assessment of the net amount following the revised BAS lodged by the Tax Agent. Those revisions were said to be unauthorised and incorrect.
  6. Ms Irmisch submitted the revisions were unauthorised because she did not review or approve the lodgement of the amended BAS. The Applicant, however, was aware from the meeting with Ms Graham, the BAS were going to be revised by the Tax Agent to reconcile the PAYGW position with figures already reported to the ATO. That is precisely what the Tax Agent did.
  7. Having regard to the surrounding circumstances, including the Ms Irmisch’s evidence about the matters discussed at the audit meeting, I am not satisfied the amendments were unauthorised. In any event, a complaint about the Tax Agent’s conduct, without more, does not identify an error in an assessment or other objectionable decision.
  8. The Applicant submitted the present case is distinguishable from the facts in Price which concerned whether an employee could dispute PAYGW credits applied to a personal income tax assessment. In that case, the Court observed that PAYGW is not a particular of an assessment and therefore cannot be challenged in a Part IVC proceeding. Here, the Applicant is an employer disputing amounts said to arise in connection with revised BAS liabilities.
  9. Although I accept the factual distinction, that distinction alone does not ultimately assist the Applicant. The question of whether the Aus Loadshifting’s factual circumstance gives rise to an objection right, turns on what the legislation treats as an assessment or whether there is any other determination, notice or decision to which Part IVC of the TAA 53 applies.
  10. The Applicant’s PAYGW obligations arose as a consequence of s 12-35 of Schedule 1 of the TAA 53 upon the making of payments to employees. Although PAYGW amounts are reported on a BAS and may inform the final amount be paid or refunded, the mere inclusion in the BAS does not convert those amounts into components of the assessment.
  11. The Commission can make an assessment of an amount, including a net amount[16] and those assessments are capable of being objected to[17] under Part IVC of the TAA 53. However, the net amount as defined by the applicable provision, does not include PAYGW.[18]
  12. Accordingly, even accepting Aus Loadshifting’s factual distinction from Price, the reporting or adjustment of PAYGW figures on a BAS does not give rise to an assessment capable of objection. The Applicant has not pointed to any separate determination of the PAYGW liability or other objectionable decision made by the Commissioner.[19]
  13. Regardless, the Applicant accepted at the hearing the revised PAYGW figures were likely to be correct and consistent with the amounts otherwise reported to the Commissioner. Even if the PAYGW amount were capable of objection, no error affecting liability has been identified.
  14. The only remaining issue raised by the Applicant concerned the wages reported in the revised BAS. Wage figures are not a particular in an assessment and were lower overall to those initially reported. Even if wage figures were capable of objection, no error effecting liability has been established.
  15. The Applicant’s evidence and submissions demonstrate the real dispute concerns the balance of the ICA, including allocations, reversals, transfers, and overall debt said to be due and payable, rather than any particular assessment, determination, notice of decision of the Commissioner.
  16. The ICA reflects multiple discrete liabilities. A general challenge to the balance of the account or a request that it be reconciled or reviewed does not attract objection rights under Part IVC TAA 53.
  17. The conclusion is reinforced by:
    • the application for review which refers to the debt on the ICA generically;
    • the ASFIC which argues that the debt as recorded in ICA is unreliable given the chaotic way in which the statement is presented, the debt amount continually changing and the relief sought, which is to reassess the debt amount;
    • the oral evidence of Ms Irmisch in which she repeated concerns about reconciliation and reliability of the ICA
  18. I am satisfied the taxation objection was invalid. The Applicant has failed to identify an assessment, determination, notice or decision or explained why it is incorrect. Even taking the Applicant’s case expressed at the hearing at its highest (that it disputes the PAYGW revisions made by the Tax Agent) the position does not improve. PAYGW does not attract objection rights in this instance and moreover no error capable of effecting liability has been expressed. Where no valid objection exists, the discretion cannot cure that defect. An objection that is invalid does not become valid merely because it is treated as having been lodged in time. Accordingly, even if the extension of time decision were to be set aside, there would be no valid objection for the Commissioner to determine.
  19. The Respondent purported to make an objection decision on a plainly invalid basis. Perhaps the Commissioner thought a response which sought to clarify the relevant events and the current status of the debt might assist the Applicant. Unfortunately, the purported objection decision has left the Applicant frustrated and confused as to the appropriate resolution pathway. It also led to the instigation of these proceedings, which in addition to the time and resources dedicated by both parties, have been particularly stressful on Ms Irmisch, the director of the Applicant. The adverse impact on the Applicant of the dispute generally, is a matter acknowledged by the Tribunal
  20. Notwithstanding this, in the absence of a properly articulated taxation objection, even if the Tribunal were to set aside the extension of time decision, there is no valid taxation objection to be considered by the Commissioner. In that context, the Applicant’s substantive application, has no reasonable prospects of success.
  21. The power to dismiss an application under s 101 of the ART Act is discretionary. I do not consider dismissing the application in this case would be prejudicial or otherwise unfair to the Applicant in light of the procedural history. The Applicant has been afforded the opportunity to put evidence and submissions in support of its case.
  22. I have decided to dismiss the proceeding rather than to affirm the Respondent’s decision, as the latter carries an implicit acceptance that there was a valid objection capable of determination. For the reasons set out above, that is not consistent with my findings. In circumstances where I am satisfied the Applicant’s has no reasonable prospects of success and the substantive application is futile, it is appropriate to dismiss the application under s101 of the ART Act.

Alternative Position - Extension of Time Application.

  1. Even if I am wrong in concluding that the Applicant did not make a valid taxation objection, I am satisfied that the correct and preferable decision would nevertheless be to affirm the Commissioner’s refusal to grant an extension of time to lodge an objection.
  2. The discretion to extend time is not to be exercised lightly. It requires the decision maker to consider the explanation for delay, the length, and circumstances of that delay, whether the objection has substantive merit or is arguable, and any prejudice to the Commissioner. These matters must be assessed having regard to the facts of the particular case, and what justice requires.
  3. The delay in this case is substantial. The revisions to the BAS the Applicant now seeks to challenge were made in 2017. The objection was not lodged until October 2024, which is well beyond the statutory timeframes contemplated by Parliament. This weighs against the exercise of the discretion.
  4. The Applicant's explanation for the delay was that Ms Irmisch did not understand how the debt arose until relatively recently, and there were several attempts to resolve the matter directly with the Commissioner before lodging an objection.
  5. The Commissioner’s records show that as early as August 2017, the Applicant considered the debt arose as a result of the Tax Agents conduct. That issue was raised multiple times thereafter and used as a basis to negotiate a payment arrangement. Those records are inconsistent with the proposition that the Applicant only recently became aware of the nature or cause of the debt.
  6. Although I accept the Applicant may not have fully understood the legal framework governing the liabilities or the operation of the ICA, a lack of understanding does not explain or justify the significant delay.
  7. The passage of time alone is such that prejudice can be inferred. The statutory regime, including the defined time limits in Part IVC of the TAA 53 are designed to promote certainty and finality in taxation matters. Allowing an objection to proceed after an extensive delay can undermine those objectives.
  8. The merits of the objection in this case weigh decisively against the exercise of discretion. For the reasons already explained, the objection does not disclose any substantive error affecting liability. The Applicant accepted that the revised PAYGW amounts were correct. The wage figures said to be incorrect are not assessed amounts and did not lead to the liability. No alternative calculation of liability has been advanced.
  9. Even if the objection were treated as valid, it would be one that is bound to fail or have no reasonable prospects of success. This factor alone strongly militates against granting an extension of time.
  10. Taking these matters together, the extensive delay, the absence of a satisfactory explanation, the lack of substantive merits and prejudice to the Commissioner, I am not satisfied that it would be appropriate to extend the time even if a valid objection had been made. The correct decision would be to affirm the Commission's decision.

CONCLUSION AND DECISION

  1. The Applicant has failed to identify an assessment, determination, notice or other decision capable of being objected to or any error affecting liability. Even if the Tribunal were to set aside the Commissioner’s decision to refuse the extension, there is no valid objection to be considered by the Commissioner. Accordingly, and in order to bring finality to the process, it is appropriate to dismiss the application under s101 of the ART Act.
