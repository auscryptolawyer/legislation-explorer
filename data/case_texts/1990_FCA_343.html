Re Griffin Coal Mining Company Limited v the Commissioner of Taxation [1990] FCA 343 (6 September 1990)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE GRIFFIN COAL MINING COMPANY LIMITED

And: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Nos. WA G100-101 of 1989

FED No. 496

Taxation

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

WESTERN AUSTRALIA DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Davies(1), Wilcox(2) and French(2) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - income tax - expenditure incurred by coal mining company in investigating the feasibility of establishing an aluminium smelter - Whether expenditure incurred by the taxpayer in gaining or producing assessable income or in carrying on a business for the gaining or producing of assessable income - Whether expenditure was of a capital nature.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s.51(1)

HEARING

PERTH

6:9:1990 Counsel for the Appellant: R A Conti, QC and W S Martin

Solicitors for the Appellant: Corrs Solicitors

Counsel for the Respondent: R J M Anderson, QC and R E

Birmingham

Solicitors for the Respondent: Australian Government

Solicitor

ORDER

The appeals be dismissed.

The appellant pay to the respondent his costs of the appeal.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

The issue in this case is similar to that considered in BP Australia Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1964] HCA 81; (1964) 110 CLR 387 in which McTiernan, Windeyer and Owen JJ., upholding the decision of Taylor J. at first instance, held that certain outgoings of BP Australia Ltd were not allowable deductions under s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) as they were outgoings of capital or of a capital nature. There was a dissent from Dixon C.J. and Kitto J. and the dissenting view was subsequently upheld by the Privy Council constituted by Lord Reid, Lord Morris, Lord Pearce, Lord Upjohn and Lord Wilberforce in a decision reported in [1965] UKPCHCA 2; (1965) 112 CLR 386. Another like case was Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Ampol Exploration Ltd (1986) 13 FCR 545 in which Lockhart and Burchett JJ., Beaumont J. dissenting, held that certain expenditure of Ampol Exploration Ltd in connection with exploration for oil was an allowable deduction under s.51(1) and was on revenue, not on capital account. In my opinion, the facts of the present case fall within the view which found favour with Dixon C.J. and Kitto J. and with the members of the Judicial Committee in the BP Australia case and with Lockhart and Burchett JJ. in the Ampol Exploration case.

2. The case is a marginal one. The expenditure was incurred in unusual circumstances and was a very substantial amount. There are strong arguments, arguments which prevailed with the trial Judge, that the expenditure was not sufficiently connected with the existing business of the taxpayer, Griffin Coal Mining Co. Ltd ("Griffin"), to be incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income of Griffin or necessarily incurred in carrying on its business for that purpose and, being devoted to the development of a new venture, was of a capital nature.

3. However, I have come to the view that the expenditure was essentially devoted to the improvement of Griffin's coal mining business, and was directed to attaining both tangible and intangible business objectives. The activity which gave rise to the expenditure improved Griffin's relationship with its major purchaser, the State Energy Commission of Western Australia ("SECWA"), and with the Government of Western Australia and it was directed primarily, though in the end unsuccessfully, to obtaining market for coal from mines which Griffin wished to develop. The expenditure arose out of and reflected Griffin's perceived need to obtain an enhanced market which would enable it to exploit its existing coal reserves and was a revenue type activity. The expenditure was not expenditure of capital and was not of a capital nature. What it achieved, in addition to the intangible benefits I have mentioned, was information about a possible venture which, had it come to fruition, would have provided the market which Griffin sought. The expenditure was not itself directed to or appropriate for the acquisition of a capital asset. The activity was too preliminary for that. The time for capital expenditure had not arisen and did not arise.

4. I do not propose to discuss separately the two issues, namely whether the expenditure was incurred in gaining or producing Griffin's assessable income or necessarily incurred in carrying on its business for that purpose on the one hand or whether it was of capital or of a capital nature on the other. The two questions are answered by the view taken of the facts, which in my view have many analogies to those considered in the BP Australia case, in which Dixon C.J. said at p 410:-

"I do not think it was acquiring a capital asset or doing any more

than so conducting its business on revenue account as to increase it

and make as certain as it could that its business was continuing and

also would continue, if possible, to expand. For my part I cannot

think that all the course adopted changed the character of the

transactions of the company from those of a continual attempt to

establish its product in a consumers' market and to meet all the

obstacles which arose in a long and rather troubled period to

obtaining a reputation for its product. There appears to me to be no

specific expenditure in increasing its plant, machinery or any other

element in the profit-earning instrument under its control. I do not

see any sufficient ground of a distinct or specific nature for

saying that the expenditure was of a capital nature."

At p 413, Kitto J.said:-

"The payment of the money was analogous to expenditure in sending a

commercial traveller on his rounds to secure orders; it was part and

parcel of the business of effecting sales."

5. In its income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1985, Griffin claimed $1,439,930 as a deduction for "Smelter Feasibility Study Costs" and $774,025 for "Smelter Coal Supply Study; Investigation re Possibility of Sales". In the following year, the claim for "Smelter Feasibility Study Costs" was $135,874. The Commissioner disallowed the deduction claimed for the "Smelter Feasibility Study Costs" but allowed the deduction claimed for the "Smelter Coal Supply Study; Investigation re Possibility of Sales". In my opinion, all items were of the same nature. The "Smelter Coal Supply Study" looked at the costs of producing coal and the price at which it could be supplied to SECWA; but the supply contemplated was supply to the aluminium smelter, if that project proceeded. The "Smelter Feasibility Study Costs" were designed to see if the smelter was feasible. Both sets of expenditure were designed to advance the same end, for the smelter would have provided the additional market which Griffin sought.

6. Since 1925, Griffin has carried on the business of coal mining. It has coal fields near Collie in Western Australia and has been one of two coal miners operating in that district. In recent years, its production has come mainly from the Muja leases and has been supplied principally to SECWA. In 1979, SECWA entered into a long term contract which I shall call "the Coal Sale Agreement". The execution of that agreement became the foundation of a major expansion of Griffin's operations. The Agreement was followed by the Collie Coal (Griffin) Agreement Act 1979 (WA). In accordance with that Act, Griffin almost trebled the coal mining leases under its control and obtained leases at Chicken Creek and Ewington. However, in the early 1980's Griffin had little prospect of developing its leases at Chicken Creek and Ewington for SECWA was bound to take gas from the fields of the northwest coast of Western Australia. It appeared that, for some years, there would be what was described as a "gas bubble".

7. During the early 1980's there were several disputes between SECWA and Griffin arising under the Coal Sale Agreement and, from time to time, SECWA threatened to terminate the Agreement. In September 1983, SECWA commenced proceedings against Griffin in the Supreme Court of Western Australia seeking a declaration of an entitlement to terminate the Coal Sale Agreement on the ground of Griffin's default. That dispute was finally compromised in June 1985, after most of the expenditure with which we are concerned had been incurred. Evidence was given at the trial, which there is no reason not to accept, that the directors of Griffin had in mind that involvement in the smelter project would assist Griffin in its relations with SECWA and with the Government of Western Australia and would assist Griffin to overcome its long standing dispute with SECWA. The trial Judge appears to have accepted that this was so for his Honour referred in the course of his judgment to "aspects such as the . . . . . effecting a reconciliation with SECWA may have been within the contemplation of the Board of Directors of Griffin Coal at the time the outgoings were incurred" and his Honour went on to say that the subject expenditure was not incurred "merely for the purpose of reducing the 'gas bubble' and improving its relationship with SECWA thereby securing the existing principal source of assessable income."

8. In 1983, when the litigation with SECWA was still on foot, it came to the attention of officers of Griffin, particularly Mr G.M. Strmotich, who subsequently became Managing Director of Griffin and was the principal witness for Griffin at the trial, that the construction of an aluminium smelter in the southwest of Western Australia was under discussion between the Government of the State of Western Australia and interested parties. Mr Strmotich considered that the project might provide a substantial additional market for Griffin's coal. The Government had established an Aluminium Smelter Task Force to endeavour to form a consortium of parties interested in establishing such a smelter. One such party was a Korean company, Kukje-ICC Limited ("ICC"). In March 1983, executive officers of Griffin initiated discussions with ICC. Officers of Griffin and consultants on behalf of Griffin became involved in discussions with respect to the smelter project.

9. At first it was thought that the proposed smelter would draw power from a dedicated power plant which would be constructed by and operated on behalf of the smelter and that no power would be sought from SECWA. It appeared to Griffin that, to make the aluminium smelter viable, coal might have to provided to the power plant at little or no profit and perhaps even at a loss. The officers of Griffin therefore turned their attention to the question whether, if it supplied coal at a low price, Griffin might nevertheless still make a profit if it took an equity interest in the smelter project. The trial Judge found the following facts, inter alia:-

"In August 1983 Griffin Coal was invited by the Aluminium Smelter

Task Force to provide its projected prices for the supply of coal

between 1990 and 2000.

. . .

Griffin Coal responded to the Aluminium Smelter Task Force request

by stating that it did not believe that coal could be supplied at

commercial rates because such rates would make the cost of

electricity too high for the smelter to operate profitably. Griffin

Coal therefore suggested that it supply coal to the project at an

appropriate price and take up a position as one of the parties in

the smelter project.

. . .

In February 1984 Griffin Coal supplied a letter of intent to ICC

setting out the basis on which it would be prepared to enter a

long-term coal supply arrangement with the proprietor of the power

station to be constructed as part of the aluminium smelter project.

The letter quoted a base price for the supply of coal of $19-22 per

tonne. The response from ICC was that the smelter project would need

coal to be supplied at a base price of $18 per tonne. Griffin Coal

indicated that it would be prepared to offer the delivery of coal at

such a price on condition that the company was invited to join the

smelter project as the holder of 25 per cent interest therein. By

April/May 1984 exchanges between ICC and Griffin Coal gave

encouragement to Griffin Coal that a coal supply agreement could be

established would 'provide Griffin with a modest but adequate margin

over cost' and that Griffin Coal would be regarded as an acceptable

member of an aluminium smelter consortium.

. . .

By May 1984 Griffin Coal was seeking to be included as a participant

in a feasibility study on the costs of construction and operation of

the proposed smelter to be carried out by parties interested in the

smelter project. As at late May 1984 Griffin Coal had abandoned the

pursuit of a long-term contract for the sale of coal at a reasonable

'arms-length' profit. The long-term sale of coal at a reduced profit

was considered to be a possibility provided the smelter could be

proved to be feasible. Griffin Coal was, therefore, looking at

participation in the operation of a smelter as a means of providing

an appropriate return upon any capital it applied to the mining of

coal and the construction and operation of the smelter." (the

emphasis is mine).

10. Further consideration of the project led the participants to conclude that SECWA should operate the power station and supply electricity to the station. The trial Judge found:-

"At about the time it had also become clear to Griffin Coal that the

proposal for a privately owned power station dedicated to the needs

of the aluminium smelter would not proceed and that SECWA would be

the supplier of power to the smelter.

. . .

In June 1984 Griffin Coal, at the request of SECWA, supplied a

proposal for a seventeen-year contract for the supply of coal

commencing in the year 1996, being the year in which SECWA

anticipated it would cease to use gas as a fuel source for the power

station generating the electricity supply for the smelter. Griffin

Coal quoted a base price for the supply of coal of $20 per tonne

which apparently provided a small measure of profit. The proposal

was made conditional upon Griffin Coal becoming a member of the

consortium to be formed to construct the aluminium smelter. Further

discussion with SECWA ensued resulting in minor modifications to the

proposal.

. . .

Griffin Coal's major expenditure on matters related to its proposed

participation in the smelter joint venture project commenced in

about July/August 1984. At the beginning of August 1984 a meeting

attended by representatives of Griffin Coal, ICC, Reynolds Metals,

BHP and the Government of Western Australia was held in Korea. At

that meeting BHP decided to withdraw from the venture. ICC,

Reynolds Metals and Griffin Coal agreed to form a consortium known

as the International Aluminium Consortium of Western Australia

("IACWA") to pursue the objective of constructing an aluminium

smelter and to conduct a feasibility study to determine the costs of

constructing and operating the smelter and to initiate such studies

as were necessary to assess the impact of the proposal on the

environment.

. . .

It was agreed by the consortium members that part of the feasibility

study which related to the costs of the smelter should be completed

by 30 November 1984 and that the environmental studies should be

completed by 31 January 1985. The costs of the study were to be

shared between ICC (50 per cent); Reynolds Metals (25 per cent); and

Griffin Coal (25 per cent)."

A memorandum of understanding executed by the then participants read:-

"KUKJE-ICC Corp., Reynolds Metals Co., and Griffin Coal have met in

Seoul for the purpose of establishing a consortium to pursue the

joint objective of creating a W.A. aluminum smelter in the form of

an unincorporated joint venture. Having done so, we have agreed

(subject to final approval by our respective Boards) to take the

following steps. However, none of these steps will be undertaken

without certain agreed upon additional assurance by the W.A.

Government which include Cabinet approval of the power tariff.

1. ORGANIZATION

* Establish a Consortium Project Management Committee

whose initial responsibility will be to oversee and manage

the feasibility study and propose a detailed plan of action.

* Authorize the above committee to undertake to negotiate a

technical assistance agreement with Reynolds Metals Co. for

the construction and operation of the smelter.

2. FEASIBILITY STUDY

* Subject to the immediate realization of the above, initiate an

appropriate feasibility study to determine capital and

operating costs to be completed by 31 November (sic), 1984

and initiate the required environmental studies which are to

be completed by January 31, 1985. The studies are based

upon an agreed cell technology and smelter site.

* The distribution of feasibility study costs will be based upon

the preliminary understanding of each party's share of the

project, but will ultimately be adjusted to reflect final equity

distribution. Each company's share will be paid directly to

the vendors.

3. EQUITY DISTRIBUTION

Having reached general accord on our individual equity participation

(for the purpose of the feasibility study) which is 50% KUKJE-ICC,

25% Reynolds and 25% Griffin Coal, it is the intention of the

consortium to seek additional Australian participation in the

venture prior to the completion of the feasibility study.

4. PUBLIC RELATIONS

No public announcements will be made by the consortium unless they are

mutually agreeable."

A more formal agreement was executed on 3 October 1984 between ICC, Reynolds and Griffin. It provided inter alia:-

"WHEREAS the Consortium desire to explore the feasibility of establishing an

aluminium reduction plant in the southwest of Western Australia (the

'Reduction Plant') and for this purpose to be known as International

Aluminium Consortium of Western Australia (IACWA).

IT IS HEREBY AGREED

1 A Consortium Committee ('Committee') is hereby established to

which the provisions of the Schedule 1 hereto shall apply.

2 The mandate of the Committee is to obtain and evaluate

feasibility and environmental studies and to propose a detailed

oversee, manage, evaluate and report on:

(a) A budget for carrying out the following matters;

(b) A feasibility study to be conducted by Raymond

Engineers Australia Pty Ltd to determine, inter alia,

the most desirable site for the Reduction Plant and

the capital and operating costs which will be incurred therefor;

(c) An environmental study to ascertain the parameters,

precautions, restrictions and conditions which must

be observed for the purposes of environmental

protection and the compliance with all laws,

regulations and requirements relating thereto, and the

preparation of a draft environmental Review and

Management Programme;

(d) The negotiation of any agreement or contract

necessary for the implementation of the Reduction

Plant project (including a technical assistance

agreement with Reynolds Metals Company).

(e) Studies relevant to and to the obtaining of necessary

Government or statutory approval; and

(f) All matters reasonably incidental to the foregoing.

. . .

3 The consortium agree to share the charges incurred after 27

August 1984 with Raymond Engineers Australia Pty Ltd, any

person engaged to conduct the environmental study and any other

persons engaged to perform any of the matters referred to in

Clause 2, in the following proportions (herein referred to as the

'Share' of a Member): 50% Kukje, 25% Reynolds, 25% Griffin Coal.

. . .

6 Each member will bear all costs and expenses incurred by it in its

own management, accounting, engineering, technical and other

functions that is, its own internal costs.

13 This Agreement shall terminate on 31 March 1985 or such other

date as may be mutually agreed by the Consortium but the

In the year ended 30 June 1985, the cost to Griffin of that feasibility study formed a major part of the $1,439,930 claimed as a deduction. The expenditure for the following year was a carryover from activity then initiated.

11. The trial Judge further held:-

"The IACWA feasibility study on the construction and operation of the

smelter had been completed by December 1984. The study indicated

that, in appropriate conditions, the operation of the smelter would

be viable and that it could be constructed by 1987. In January 1985

the State of Western Australia through the Western Australian

Development Corporation, joined the consortium. Its 20 per cent

interest in the consortium was obtained by reducing the interests of

ICC and Reynolds Metals. In February 1985 ICC was dissolved by

decree of the government of South Korea and it departed from the

consortium. The consortium continued, however, and in March 1985

selected contractors were invited to bid for contracts for phases of

construction of the smelter. In 20 June 1985, however, Reynolds

Metals gave notice of its withdrawal from the consortium and on 25

June 1985 the Government of the State announced that the aluminium

smelter project had been deferred indefinitely. After the collapse

of the project and the consortium in June 1985 Griffin Coal incurred

some further expenditure in respect of the smelter project by

discharging the fees for the completion of certain environmental

studies charged to Griffin Coal's account and fees for other work

which may be described as tailing-off expenses in respect of the

study of the smelter project undertaken by the consortium on the one

hand and by Griffin Coal on its own account on the other. Between

July 1984 and June 1985 Griffin Coal continued to conduct detailed

feasibility studies on its proposal for the long-term supply of coal

for power generation for the smelter and entered more detailed

negotiations with SECWA in respect of the necessary terms of a

contract to supply such coal including consideration of a draft

contract. Substantial costs were incurred in discharging the fees of

Jacia and other consultants in respect of the assessment of Griffin

Coal's ability to mine and supply such additional coal and in

settling the terms of the proposed contract. At all times the

accounting records of Griffin Coal maintained a separation between

the costs and expenses incurred in respect of the proposal for a

coal supply contract relating to the proposed aluminium smelter and

the costs incurred in assessing the feasibility of, and obligations

arising under, participation in a joint venture to construct the

smelter."

12. The trial Judge described Griffin's involvement with the smelter in this way:-

"Although Griffin Coal's preferred option may have been to further develop

its coalmining business by extracting coal from a mine to be

established on its Ewington leases and to sell that coal at

commercial rates to the operators of the proposed smelter, it was

very clear by July 1984 that no such sales could be negotiated.

Griffin Coal, therefore, determined that the path to be followed was

to accept a modest or minimal return over the cost of extraction of

the coal and to endeavour to secure a participating interest in the

operation of the smelter and receive a profit on that investment

sufficient to providean appropriate return on its investment in the

smelter and on the investment in the additional coalmining

operation. (the emphasis is mine).

. . .

Griffin Coal argued that its motivation was to obtain an appropriate
profit

from the sale of coal and to obtain part of that profit by using the

lever of its willingness to enter a contract for the sale of coal on

terms favourable to the smelter participants to obtain an interest

in the smelter project and access to the profit to be obtained by

such participation. The facts show that the activities and

objectives of Griffin Coal were not so limited. Firstly, it

perceived the leverage it could exert as supplier of the cheapest

fuel for power generation as opening up an opportunity for the

company to diversify its activities and move beyond the extraction

and sale of coal for which there was little or no export market and

for which Griffin Coal was dependent upon the goodwill and

bargaining strength of a single customer. Secondly, participation in

the smelter project itself opened up possible areas of further

diversification for Griffin Coal, or for the group of companies of

which it was part, as a fabricator or manufacturer of aluminium

products. Thirdly, Griffin Coal could negotiate for such profit as

it may have foregone on the sale of coal to be recognized by the

smelter participants and have that profit capitalized and treated as

part of Griffin Coal's capital contribution to the smelter project.

. . .

In participating in these activities, Griffin Coal was looking at

the reality of a new venture. . . . . The expenditure on those

activities was incurred with the knowledge that the company may have

been a participant in the smelter project but not the supplier of

coal to be used to generate electricity required by the smelter if,

in due course, SECWA accepted a more competitive tender for the

supply of coal submitted to Western Collieries Limited. Griffin

Coal's proposal to SECWA for the supply of coal at a price of $20

per tonne enable SECWA to obtain the approval of State Cabinet to

offer to the consortium to supply power at a nominated tariff, but

SECWA intended to seek competitive offers from both suppliers before

concluding a contract for the supply of coal. The efforts of Griffin

Coal had won for it a place in the consortium, but neither on its

part nor on the part of its co-venturers was Griffin Coal's place in

the consortium conditional upon it being the supplier of coal to be

used for the purpose of generating the electricity to be supplied to

the smelter."

13. Notwithstanding the last sentence in the above findings, it is clear that Griffin's principal interest was to develop its coal leases at Chicken Creek and Ewington and to sell its coal to the smelter, or more precisely to SECWA which was to operate the power station. To do this profitably, Griffin considered that it would have to take an equity interest in the smelter, for the sale of coal at an ordinary commercial price would make the smelter unprofitable. Griffin had no binding agreement with the co-venturers that it would be the coal supplier, nor did it have any contract with SECWA which bound SECWA to purchase from it. Nevertheless, Griffin was confident that it would be able to undercut the other colliery in the Collie district and would obtain the contract.

14. It is important that the venture was at a very early stage, at the stage of investigation as a feasible project. The changing composition of the co-venturers shows this. The structure for the venture and its details had not been determined. Griffin had formed a subsidiary, Griffin Aluminium Pty Limited, but that subsidiary took no action. The subject expenditure was incurred by Griffin, the coal mining company, not by a company whose activities were or were to be devoted to an aluminium venture. It is the characterisation of the expenditure by Griffin that is to be determined. Even the nature and extent of Griffin's contribution to the venture, if it went ahead, had not been discussed in any detail. The project had not proceeded to the stage where Griffin was clear as to what capital it would have to contribute to the venture, as to whether that capital would be contributed by way of development of its own mines or otherwise and as to what Griffin's overall total responsibility would be. Nor was any party committed to the smelter.

15. In my opinion, the expenditure at this early stage was relevant and incidental to Griffin's existing business and was not of a capital nature. It is clear that no capital was contributed to the venture. The time for the contribution of capital to the venture had not arrived. The expenditure was not directed to the acquisition of an asset of an enduring character. In relation to the aluminium project, no asset was acquired. The expenditure was not on items such as roads and drainage works necessary in the event the opening up of the new mines, or on the taking away of the overburden, or on the construction of buildings and the acquisition of plant and equipment. The subject expenditure was expenditure by a coal mining company made out of current revenue directed to ascertaining whether the development of an enhanced market for the coal was feasible, as well as attaining the intangible ends I have mentioned.

16. In GP International Pipecoaters Pty Ltd v. Commissioner of Taxation [1990] HCA 25; (1990) 64 ALJR 392, Brennan, Dawson, Toohey, Gaudron and McHugh JJ. said, at p 396:-

"The character of expenditure is ordinarily determined by reference to the

nature of the asset acquired or the liability discharged by the

making of the expenditure, for the character of the advantage sought

by the making of the expenditure is the chief, if not the critical,

factor in determining the character of what is paid: Sun Newspapers

Ltd and Associated newspapers Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation

[1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337 at 363; Colonial Mutual Life Assurance Society Ltd

v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1953] HCA 68; (1953) 89 CLR 428 at 445-447,

454; Cooper v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1957] HCA 72; (1957) 97 CLR 397 at

404 (affirmed on other grounds: [1958] HCA 46; 100 CLR 131); and see the differing

analyses of what the subject expenditure was for in Cliff's

International Inc v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1979) 142 CLR

140."

I therefore place considerable weight upon the nature of the investigation which was funded by the expenditure. The investigation sought to obtain information as to the capital required for an aluminium smelter, the operating costs of the smelter, the most desirable site for the plant, what would be the environmental factors to take into account and so on. The information resulting from these studies was to be a guide to a decision whether or not to undertake the smelter project. It did not constitute an asset of a capital nature.

17. It is worth noting that the subject expenditure, although considerable, was not out of proportion for revenue expenditure, having regard to Griffin's revenue from its sales of coal which, in the year ended 30 June 1985, totalled $69,278,012 and in the following year totalled $70,661,507.

18. The trial Judge placed weight upon the fact that Griffin had in mind that, if it were to make a profit from the sale of coal to the operator of the smelter's power station, Griffin would have to make that profit through the profitable sale of aluminium. In my opinion, that factor has little weight having regard to the revenue, the assessable income, which Griffin hoped to obtain from the Chicken Creek and Ewington leases. It is not necessary for the operation of s.51(1) that expenditure be directed to the gaining of profit, it is sufficient that it be devoted to the gaining of assessable income.

19. For these reasons, I would allow the appeals, would set aside his Honour's orders and would substitute therefor orders that the Commissioner's decisions on Griffin's objections to the income tax assessments be set aside and that the matter be remitted to the Commissioner with the direction that the subject sums of $1,439,930 and $135,874 were allowable deductions pursuant to s.51(1) of the Act. In my opinion, Griffin should have the costs of the appeals and of the proceedings below.

These are appeals by The Griffin Coal Mining Company Limited ("Griffin Coal") against decisions of Lee J dismissing with costs two appeals against the disallowance by the Commissioner of Taxation of objections to assessments of tax for the years of income ending 30 June 1985 and 30 June 1986. The point at issue in each case is the deductibility under s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 of expenditure incurred by Griffin Coal in the relevant years in connection with a proposed aluminium smelter. The respondent does not dispute that the claimed sums were actually spent. However, he says that they were not incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable income or necessarily incurred in carrying on business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income; and that, in any event, the expenditure was a capital outgoing. Lee J upheld both these contentions.

2. Section 51(1) provides:

"All losses and outgoing to the extent

to which they are incurred in gaining or

producing the assessable income, or are

necessarily incurred in carrying on a

business for the purpose of gaining or

producing such income, shall be allowable

deductions except to the extent to which

they are losses or outgoings of capital,

or of a capital, private or domestic

nature, or are incurred in relation to

the gaining or production of exempt income."

The background facts

3. The facts of the case are not in dispute. They may be taken from the reasons of the learned primary judge, subject to a few additional matters referred to in argument.

4. Griffin Coal was incorporated in 1925. Since then it has been engaged in the business of coalmining near Collie, Western Australia. At all material times the company has been one of only two coalmines operating in the area, the other being Western Collieries Limited. For many years Griffin Coal has supplied coal to instrumentalities of the Western Australian government, particularly those associated with the generation of electricity and the provision of rail services. Significant sales were made to private customers in the early years of the company's operation; but, by 1979, these sales had become a minor aspect of the company's business. Essentially, Griffin Coal had become a supplier to the State Energy Commission of Western Australia ("SECWA"). SECWA is responsible for providing electricity in Western Australia and, for that purpose, operates a number of coal-fired power stations.

5. In March 1979, Griffin Coal entered into a contract with SECWA ("the Coal Sale Agreement") under which Griffin Coal agreed to sell coal to SECWA for a period of 25 years from 1 July, 1978. The price to be paid for the coal was variable, being determined by adding a margin to the cost of mining the coal. Accordingly, SECWA sought to control those costs by subjecting Griffin Coal to a substantial degree of supervision in relation to such matters as the areas to be worked, mining methods, equipment, etc.

6. Lee J referred to the execution of the Coal Sale Agreement as a "significant event" for Griffin Coal. He explained:

"It became the foundation for a major expansion

of Griffin Coal's coalmining operations. It

provided a substantial long-term market with a

dependable profit and assisted Griffin Coal to

secure its hold on the various coalmining

leases that had been granted to it by the

State and to obtain the grant of further leases."

7. The further leases referred to by his Honour were granted pursuant to another agreement entered into by Griffin Coal; this time with the State of Western Australia. That agreement ("the Coal Leases Agreement") was made in November 1979 and ratified by State legislation of the same year. It provided for the State to grant to Griffin Coal additional coalmining leases. The effect of this grant was to treble the leases under Griffin Coal's control, although the company was bound by the Coal Leases Agreement to reserve substantial holdings to satisfy the present and future needs of SECWA The agreement required Griffin Coal to prepare an overall plan for the exploration and development of its coal resources and to submit to the government by 1 July 1980 detailed proposals for the exploration and development of the leases over the ensuing 15 years. Similar obligations arose at later stages of the operation of the agreement.

8. The primary judge found that, as at July 1980, "the prospect of Griffin Coal obtaining further coal sales and developing its coalmining operations beyond the level required to meet the Coal Sale Agreement was very limited". The reason for this was the existence of a circumstance described in evidence as the "gas bubble". It appears that, in November 1977, SECWA had undertaken an obligation to take or pay for vast quantities of natural gas to be produced by the North West Shelf project. At least in the short term, there was a gross oversupply of fuel suitable for use in the production of electricity.

9. During 1981 differences arose between Griffin Coal and SECWA concerning the operation of the Coal Supply Agreement. It is not necessary to set out the details of the various disputes, which are recounted in the reasons of Lee J. It is sufficient to say that the dispute involved litigation between the parties in the Supreme Court of Western Australia, in which Griffin Coal sought to enforce certain terms of the Coal Sales Agreement, and the service by SECWA on Griffin Coal of a notice of default containing a threat to terminate the agreement. These disputes were resolved early in 1982 but, later that year, the parties were again in dispute and further litigation was commenced. This new dispute was resolved by a compromise arrangement in August 1982.

10. In September 1983 a fresh dispute occurred, resulting in SECWA commencing proceedings against Griffin Coal in the Supreme Court seeking a declaration that it was entitled to terminate the Coal Sale Agreement on the ground of Griffin Coal's default. That dispute was not compromised until about June 1985.

11. Against this background, it is not surprising that, in 1983, Griffin Coal decided that it should endeavour to diversify its mining activities, in order to lessen its financial dependence upon SECWA. At that time the possibility of the construction of an aluminium smelter in the southern part of Western Australia was under discussion. The State government had established an Aluminium Smelter Task Force ("the Task Force") to discuss the proposal with interested parties. One interested party was a Korean company, Kukje-ICC Limited ("ICC").

12. In March 1983 executives of Griffin Coal initiated discussions with ICC. They learned that ICC contemplated that the electricity required for the smelter would be supplied by a dedicated power plant to be constructed by the consortium independently of SECWA. The Griffin Coal representatives realised that the viability of the project might depend upon the cost of the energy supplied to the smelter. Energy costs are a major component of the cost of running an aluminium smelter. In May 1983 Griffin advised the Task Force that it was very interested in the project and that it regarded itself as a prospective supplier of coal for the proposed power station, even though it recognised that coal might have to be provided at little or no profit, perhaps even at a loss. However, Griffin Coal indicated that it would be prepared to supply coal on such terms provided that it was given an equity interest in the project. The company was prepared to provide finance for its share of the project.

13. In August 1983 the Task Force invited Griffin Coal to provide prices for the supply of coal between 1990 and 2000, the deliveries ranging from 500,000 tonnes of coal in the first year to 1.7 million tonnes per year by the end of the ten-year period. The Task Force proposed that, until about 1990, the power station would be fuelled by natural gas drawn from the "gas bubble" and that it would be operated by SECWA, though privately constructed and owned. Griffin Coal responded by informing the Task Force that it did not believe that coal could be supplied at commercial rates because such rates would make the cost of electricity too great for commercial viability. Accordingly, the company suggested that it supply coal at an appropriate, lower price and that it take up a position as a partner in the project.

14. Shortly before this, Griffin Coal, through two consultant companies owned by it, Jacia Pty Ltd and Devereaux Holdings Pty Ltd, had commenced to investigate the world aluminium market and the economics of aluminium smelting. In mid-1983 Griffin Coal made a submission to ICC concerning its capacity to supply coal to the project. The submission presented various alternatives, including both an "arm's length" agreement for supply at commercial rates and an arrangement whereby Griffin Coal's obligation to supply coal would be capitalised and converted into an equity holding in the project.

15. Throughout the latter half of 1983 Griffin Coal maintained contact with ICC and supplied information to it, and to Reynolds Metals Limited ("Reynolds") and Broken Hill Proprietary Limited ("BHP"), both of which were prospective partners in the project. In November 1983, Mr G M Strmotich, a civil engineer who was then Assistant Managing Director of Jacia and who became Managing Director of Griffin Coal in September 1984, had discussions with representatives of ICC. At that meeting Mr Strmotich raised directly with ICC, for the first time, the possibility of Griffin taking a 25 percent interest in the smelter.

16. In February 1984 Griffin Coal supplied a letter of intent to ICC setting out the basis on which it would be prepared to enter a long-term coal supply arrangement with the proprietor of the power station to be constructed as part of the aluminium smelter project. The letter quoted a base price for the supply of coal of $19-22 per tonne. The response from ICC was that the smelter project would need coal to be supplied at a base price of $18 per tonne. Griffin Coal indicated that it would be prepared to offer the delivery of coal at such a price on condition that the company was invited to join the smelter project as the holder of a 25 percent interest. By April/May 1984 exchanges between ICC and Griffin Coal encouraged Griffin Coal to believe that a coal supply agreement could be established which, in Mr Strmotich's words, would "provide Griffin with a modest but adequate margin over cost" and that Griffin Coal would be regarded as an acceptable member of an aluminium smelter consortium.

17. The proposal was that each member of the consortium would be responsible for supplying its share of the alumina required by the smelter and would take and dispose of its own share of the aluminium ingot produced by the smelter. Accordingly, Griffin Coal entered into discussions with ICC regarding an "off-take" agreement whereby ICC would purchase Griffin Coal's share of the ingot at a price designed to provide for Griffin Coal "an internal rate of return on equity invested in the smelter of not less than 20 percent".

18. During May and June 1984 the outlines of an "off-take" agreement were agreed. But Griffin Coal was also actively investigating alternative methods of disposing of the ingot. The company had been advised that it should consider becoming involved in the fabrication of aluminium products, because value-added aluminium products were less susceptible to variation in price than raw metal ingots.

19. By May 1984 Griffin Coal had abandoned hope of a long-term contract for the sale of coal at an "arm's length" profit. But, the company's executives still envisaged the possibility of supplying coal to the project on the basis of an equity interest, provided that the feasibility of the smelter was established.

20. However, in that month there was a major change in the proposal. The Western Australian government decided that electricity for the smelter should be supplied by SECWA. The notion of a dedicated power plant owned and operated by the smelter consortium was abandoned. Mr Strmotich said in his affidavit that, by June 1984, "it had become apparent to me that the proposed private power station would not proceed". He said that it followed "that if Griffin were to participate further it would have to be on the basis of the coal supply to SECWA, and this was a less desirable concept from Griffin's point of view because of the then state of Griffin's relationship with SECWA". Nonetheless, there remained a prospect of Griffin Coal being indirectly involved in fuelling the smelter.

21. In June 1984, SECWA invited Griffin Coal to put up a proposal for a 17-year contract for the supply of coal. This supply was to commence in 1996, the year in which SECWA estimated that it would cease to use gas as a fuel source for the power station supplying the smelter. Griffin Coal quoted a base price of $20 per tonne, a price which apparently provided a small measure of profit. The quotation was made conditional upon Griffin Coal becoming a member of the consortium operating the smelter.

22. By early July 1984, SECWA was able to inform the Task Force of the likely tariff it would charge for power supplied to the smelter. This price was passed on to ICC, but the Task Force indicated that the actual cost would be determined by competitive offers for the supply of coal to be obtained by SECWA from coal suppliers. Apparently, SECWA was negotiating with Griffin Coal's competitor, Western Collieries, so as to obtain the best price for the supply of coal. During July, SECWA informed Griffin Coal that it would be seeking competitive tenders for the supply of coal to the smelter power station.

23. At the beginning of August 1984, representatives of Griffin Coal attended a meeting in Korea. Also present were representatives of ICC, Reynolds, BHP and the government of Western Australia. However, BHP announced its withdrawal from the project, whereupon ICC, Reynolds and Griffin Coal agreed to form a consortium known as the International Aluminium Consortium of Western Australia ("IACWA") to pursue the objective of constructing an aluminium smelter, to conduct a feasibility study to determine the costs of its construction and operation and to carry out all necessary environmental studies. The feasibility study was to be completed by 30 November 1984 and the environmental study by 31 January 1985. The partners agreed to share the costs of this work as follows: ICC, 50 percent; Reynolds, 25 percent, and Griffin Coal, 25 percent.

24. The IACWA feasibility study was completed by December 1984. It indicated that, under appropriate conditions, the smelter would be viable and that it could be constructed by 1987. In January 1985, the Western Australian government joined the consortium, through the Western Australian Development Corporation. Its 20 percent interest was obtained by reducing the shares of ICC and Reynolds. However, in February 1985, ICC was dissolved by decree of the government of South Korea and it withdrew from the consortium. Nonetheless, the consortium continued and, in March 1985, selected contractors were invited to bid for contracts for phases of the smelter construction.

25. On 20 June 1985, Reynolds also withdrew from the consortium. A few days later the Western Australian government announced that the project had been deferred indefinitely.

26. The expenditure which is the subject of the dispute between the present parties is identified in particulars supplied by the appellant to the respondent prior to the primary hearing. In the year ended 30 June 1985, a total of $902,493 was spent on consultants' services in connection with the economic feasibility study. A total of $271,721 was spent on travel and accommodation, publications, legal services, entertainment and smaller miscellaneous charges, all in connection with the proposed joint venture arrangements. $169,192 was paid to IACWA, being Griffin Coal's contribution to the cost of investigations undertaken by this body. Similar items of expenditure were incurred in 1985-1986, although for smaller amounts.

27. In its income tax return for the year ending 30 June 1985 Griffin Coal claimed the sum of $1,439,930 as an allowable deduction for "smelter feasibility study costs". A separate deduction was claimed for the sum of $774,025 which was described as having been expended upon "smelter coal supply study; investigation re possibility of sales".

28. The respondent assessed Griffin Coal's liability for income tax by disallowing the first claim and allowing the second, subject to a small adjustment in respect of some items included in the second category which the respondent thought ought to have been included in the first. In the result, the respondent disallowed a total sum of $1,446,900.16. A similar course was taken in the following year. The respondent disallowed the whole of the "smelter feasibility study costs" claimed by the appellant.

The parties' contentions

29. The case made on behalf of the appellant is that the relevant expenditure was incurred for the purposes of expanding and maintaing its coalmining business; these being purposes which are an affair of income rather than of capital. Counsel refer to Vacuum Oil Company v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1964] HCA 83; (1964) 110 CLR 419 at p 434, Maryborough Newspapers v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1929] HCA 16; (1929) 43 CLR 450 at pp 454-455, BP Australia Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1964] HCA 81; (1964) 110 CLR 387 at pp 409-410 and [1965] UKPCHCA 2; (1965) 112 CLR 386 at p 405, Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Snowden and Willson Pty Limited [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431 at p 437, Commissioner of Taxes Nchanga Consolidated Copper Mines Ltd (1964) AC 948 at p 964 and Walker v Cater Securities (1974) 3 All ER 63 at p 74 They submit that, in determining the relationship between particular expenditure and the carrying on of a business, or otherwise gaining assessable income, it is normally appropriate, if not necessary, to balance all considerations and to analyse the matter from a practical and business point of view: see Hallstroms Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1946] HCA 34; (1946) 72 CLR 634 at p 648. They refer to the observation of Deane and Fisher JJ., in Magna Alloys and Research Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1980] FCA 150; (1980) 33 ALR 213 at p 237 that whether voluntary outgoings have been incurred in carrying on a business will depend upon,

"whether the outgoing was reasonably capable

of being seen as desirable or appropriate from

the point of view of the pursuit of the

business ends of that business and, if so,

whether those responsible for carrying on the

business so saw it".

Accordingly, they say, the subjective purpose or motive of a taxpayer in incurring such outgoings becomes relevant to the question whether they were necessarily incurred in carrying on the relevant business, and so were deductible under the second limb of s.51(1). Counsel submit that there is no good reason why similar considerations should not apply to the first limb. Finally, in relation to matters of principle, counsel say that it does not matter that a subjective purpose or motive of the taxpayer in seeking to advance, maintain or protect its existing business is entwined with some other purpose or motive directed to a private, domestic or capital advantage.

30. Counsel for the respondent do not contest the legal propositions advanced on behalf of the appellant. But they dispute that the subject expenditure was incurred for the purpose of advancing or defending the appellant's existing coal mining business. They say that examination of the contemporanous documents leads inexorably to the conclusion that the expenditure was capital expenditure incurred in the hope of creating a new business, aluminium smelting. The case turns upon the issues of fact raised by these competing submissions.

31. The appellant puts its case upon two bases. First, it is said that the expenditures were incurred for the purpose of investigating the feasibility of profitably expanding its coal market and coal sales, by disposing of coal in circumstances that would yield profits in the form of aluminium ingot, which would desirably be presold pursuant to an off-take agreement. Secondly, counsel contend that the appellant had the secondary purpose of protecting its existing market outlet under the 1979 Coal Sale agreement with SECWA.

32. In the course of developing these submissions counsel for the appellant referred at some length to their client's relationship with SECWA. They pointed out that Griffin Coal's market and profitability was substantially dependent upon that relationship and that the relationship had been rendered more difficult because of the oversupply of fuel caused by the "gas bubble". SECWA's obligation to take or pay for natural gas, for which it had no ready market, gave it an interest and an incentive to escape from its contractual obligations to Griffin Coal; hence - according to Griffin Coal - the disputes of 1983 and 1984. Counsel suggested that Griffin Coal's interest in the aluminium smelter project was a direct response to its market vulnerability. At best it would achieve a new market which was independent of SECWA; at worst it would have played a part in creating a new industry whose appetite for energy would have a major impact on the "gas bubble", thereby bringing SECWA back to a more reasonable attitude.

33. There is ample evidence to support the proposition that Griffin Coal's initial interest in the aluminium project was sparked by considerations such as those mentioned by counsel. It is not necessary to refer to that evidence because counsel for the respondent do not dispute the matter. They accept that any expenditure incurred by Griffin Coal before May 1984 may properly be regarded as expenditure incurred in pursuing the prospect of a new market for its coal. Until that time, Griffin Coal entertained the hope that it would supply coal to a dedicated power station owned and operated by a consortium of which it would be a member. But counsel for the respondent say that the Western Australian government's decision in May 1984, that SECWA would supply electricity to the smelter, changed the whole situation. Thereafter, Griffin Coal recognised that it would not be a supplier of coal to the consortium; its prospect of selling the coal needed for the project was no longer linked with its participation as an equity partner. Counsel say that Griffin Coal was simply one of two possible suppliers to SECWA of the coal which would eventually be needed - after the natural gas ran out - to fire the generators supplying the smelter. As SECWA made clear, it was seeking quotations for coal from both coal companies so that it might make the best available bargain. Counsel say that there was no suggestion that SECWA's choice would be influenced by the fact that Griffin Coal was seeking to be, or had become, a member of the consortium. SECWA had no reason to take this into account. So far as appears, SECWA's decision would be made solely by reference to commercial considerations. If Western Collieries tendered a more favourable price, it was likely that SECWA would purchase its coal from that company, whether or not Griffin Coal was a member of the consortium.

34. Counsel for the respondent further submit that, just as Griffin Coal's role as the coal supplier was not dependent on its membership of the consortium, so also was the reverse true. Griffin Coal was admitted as a consortium member in August 1984; admittedly only for the purposes of the feasibility study and other investigations, but with an apparent general expectation that, if the investigations proved satisfactory and the proposal proceeded, it would become a partner in the project itself. At that time no decision had been made as to which company would supply the coal SECWA needed to provide electricity for the smelter. It was perfectly possible, say counsel, that Griffin Coal would find itself a partner in a consortium whose electricity was generated by coal supplied by Western Collieries.

35. As to the appellant's secondary argument, counsel for the respondent concede that, in a general way, it was in the interests of Griffin Coal that the "gas bubble" be reduced and eventually eliminated; an energy glut was obviously detrimental to the company's market position. But looking at the matter objectively, they say, the ultimate fate of the project was not linked to Griffin Coal's equity participation; and, looking at it subjectively, they say, there is no evidence to suggest that anyone at Griffin Coal thought it was. The truth of the matter, say counsel for the respondent, is that it was always the position, and understood to be the position, that the future of the project would depend upon economic factors, including costs and prospective markets. Counsel point out that the evidence shows that Griffin Coal was not invited to join the consortium; but, rather, becoming convinced that the project was a worthwhile investment in its own right, it hammered at the door to get in.

Findings: the first limb of s.51(1)

36. We accept the appellant's submission about its dependence upon SECWA and its concern about that situation, especially in the light of the energy oversupply which occurred in about 1978-1979. We also accept that it initially became interested in the smelter project because of the prospect of supplying the consortium with coal. No doubt that prospect was only inviting if coal could be supplied at a profit. But profit may be taken in various ways. If Griffin Coal was both a coal supplier to the consortium and a consortium member one possibility would be to barter coal for equity or aluminium ingot. The evidence indicates that, by May 1984, the executives of Griffin Coal, especially Mr Strmotich, had become strongly attracted to participation in the smelter project; but, nonetheless, it is possible, until that time, to see the major purpose of the company's participation as being the opening up of a new market for its coal.

37. However, the critical matter is the effect of the May 1984 decision that SECWA would supply the smelter's electricity. Did that decision change the situation? To determine that question it is necessary to refer both to the evidence of Mr Strmotich and the contemparaneous documents.

38. In the course of their submissions counsel for the appellant referred to a number of passages in the affidavit and oral evidence of Mr Strmotich in which he deals with the reasons for Griffin Coal's interest in the smelter project. These passages refer to Griffin Coal's desire to expand its coal sales or to see the "gas bubble" absorbed. But, in almost every one of these passages, Mr Strmotich was referring to the initial motivation of Griffin Coal, which is conceded. On no occasion did he address himself to the position after May 1984.

39. In two passages in his evidence, Mr Strmotich did deal with the company's position if it was not a supplier of coal to the project. He said that, in that case, Griffin Coal would not have gone ahead with equity participation in the project and he compared the risks of investment in an aluminium smelter with the Griffin group's investment in W R Carpenter Holdings Limited, an investment of similar magnitude but with the risk spread over some 30 different businesses.

40. By its very nature, this evidence could not be contradicted. It was an expression of opinion, made some five years later, about a hypothetical situation which did not in fact arise. The learned trial judge made no finding, one way or the other, about this evidence or about Mr Strmotich generally. No doubt Mr Strmotich set out to give an accurate account of Griffin Coal's involvement in the smelter project and, insofar as he dealt with matters of fact, his account would appear to be supported by the contemporaneous documents. But it does not follow that reliance should be placed upon his answer to this hypothetical question. Mr Strmotich did not suggest that the question had been considered at the time. His view may or may not have been shared by others. He was speculating, years later, about the decision which the company would have made had the question arisen. And he was doing so in the context of litigation wher it was important for the company to link the aluminium smelter expenditure to the company's coal mining activities. It seems to us that little weight can be put upon this evidence. The more reliable method of determining the purpose of the expenditure is to look at the overall facts, with particular reference to the contemporaneous documents.

41. It is important to note that the May 1984 decision severed the envisaged connection between membership of the consortium and the supply of the coal. So, if the project proceeded, there were three possibilities: Griffin Coal might be both a consortium member and the supplier of the coal to SECWA; it might be one of those, but not the other; or it might be neither. The appellant's participation as a consortium member or supplier would depend upon different decisions, to be made by different people and by reference to different criteria. The only suggested link between the two decisions is that Griffin Coal's price for the supply of coal to SECWA might be lower if it was also a consortium member. The argument was that it could then afford to quote a keener price because it would enjoy a prospect of earning profits from the operation of the smelter. Griffin Coal could extract a portion of the profit which it would seek in respect of its coal supply from its receipt of aluminium ingot produced by the smelter.

42. This view of the matter derives some support from the form of the quotation submitted by Griffin Coal to SECWA in June 1984. The prices there stated were made conditional upon Griffin Coal's equity participation. But Mr Strmotich said that those prices provided a modest profit to Griffin Coal and there is no reason to doubt that Griffin Coal would have been prepared to deal with SECWA over the supply of coal even if it had been excluded from the consortium. The condition is readily understandable. Griffin Coal had not then been admitted to the consortium and was doing everything it could to secure admission. This is evident from a memorandum dated 18 June 1984 from a Griffin Coal negotiator, Mr Dan Magasania, to his superior, Mr John McLean, reporting on discussions he had had with Mr Frank Yans, an employee of consultants retained by ICC. Mr Magasania said, amongst other things:

"Main points emerging from meeting with Frank Yans:

1. If satisfactory tariff proposal will be

forthcoming from SECWA then both BHP and

CSR are expected to be interested in

smelter. Chances of satisfactory tariff

better than even.

2. If CSR and BHP each want minimum of 20

pct, then it will be difficult to find a

share for Griffin.

3. I doubt that Griffin will be given

opportunity to participate unless its

coal price is the lowest and Griffin

makes participation a condition re coal supply.

4. Yans does not consider that Griffin met

the condition of offering 18 dollar coal

to be a member of the smelter consortium

but ICC and Reynolds could live with the

price which was offered and are

favourably inclined towards Griffin.

Point 3 stands nonetheless.

5. Yans expects SECWA to make a firm power

offer and carry the risk of securing

appropriately priced coal."

43. There is no reason to doubt Mr Strmotich's assessment that participation in the smelter project would give Griffin Coal an edge over Western Collieries in quoting prices to SECWA, but this would be an advantage purchased at some cost. Griffin Coal contemplated that a one-quarter share would involve an outlay exceeding $200 million and that no coal would be required for the power station during its first ten years of operation. Plainly, the smelter had to be an attractive proposition in its own right and the evidence strongly suggests that, by August 1984, Griffin Coal regarded participation in the smelter project as a worthwhile activity for its own sake. As early as September 1983, John W Curtis, Manager - Resource Finance of B A Australia Limited, a company retained to advise Griffin Coal, had outlined his understanding of Griffin Coal's objectives in these terms:

"a) to expand its existing coal mining

operations with a view to creating value

through both the added business and

economies of scale

b) to gain a secure outlet for the coal

thereby produced by taking an equity

position in the coal-fired power station

and aluminium smelter complex

c) at the same time, to diversify profitably

into the aluminium business with a view

to perhaps ultimately producing

fabricated products. An interest of up to 20%

in the smelter is considered to be achievable"

44. On 5 July 1984 Mr McLean wrote to Mr J B Kirkwood, Commissioner of SECWA, modifying Griffin Coal's terms for supplying coal to SECWA for the project. In that condition he said:

"Griffin remains dedicated to its

objective of realising an equity position

in the smelter and the condition

precedent as expressed remains pending a

formal acknowledgement of the company's

participation. However we give you an

undertaking that this condition precedent

need not form a part of the final

documentation between us."

45. Griffin Coal's commitment to its partners was also unconditional. The Memorandum of Understanding signed by ICC, Reynolds and Griffin Coal in Korea in August 1984 referred to the signatories' "purpose of establishing a consortium to pursue the joint objective of creating a W A aluminium smelter in the form of an unicorporated joint venture". The document specified certain steps to be taken, but the partners agreed that "none of these steps will be undertaken without certain agreed upon additional assurances by the W A Government which include Cabinet approval of the power tariff". The particular matters to be resolved with the Western Australian government included the elimination of a particular SECWA charge and "agreement that any coal price improvement below A$20/tonne (July 83 basis) will be shared with the consortium on a 50/50 basis". It is clear that the consortium, as a group, contemplated arm's-length negotiations with the Western Australian government and SECWA as to electricity prices; SECWA having been left to negotiate its own coal prices.

46. On 3 September 1984 the Chairman of Griffin Coal, Mr R F Stowe, sent a circular letter to shareholders announcing the company's participation in the consortium "to develop an $800 million aluminium smelter in Western Australia". Mr Stowe referred to the benefits of participation:

"Unique Opportunities for your Company

Your Directors believe that the Smelter

presents a unique opportunity for your

Company to achieve, in the one

investment, diversification and the

enhancement and protection of its coal

interests which comprise its principal

asset. Moreover, the Smelter will

benefit not only Griffin Coal but the

people of Western Australia generally in

providing an outlet for the State's over

commitment to energy, for which they would

otherwise have to pay. Industrial development

and employment opportunities will also be

created, particularly in the south-west region

where the Smelter is to be located.

Benefits of Investment to Griffin Coal Shareholders

The establishment of the Smelter is much

in Griffin Coal's interests in creating a

new demand for energy in Western

Australia. This will not only protect

Griffin Coal's present supply commitments

but will provide an opportunity for it to

develop another mine at Collie and

significantly increase production and

sales. This could ensure a market for

the Company's coal beyond the year 2004

when its present contract with the State

Energy Commission of Western Australia

(SECWA) expires.

Independently of the above consideration,

your Directors believe that the Smelter

will be successful and profitable and

provide worthwhile returns to its

consortium members who have the necessary

experience and skills to ensure its

success." (emphasis added)

47. Under the heading "Funding of Investment" Mr Stowe announced that the company intended to dispose of certain assets including its recently acquired interest in W R Carpenter Holdings Limited. The evidence shows that this take-over had occurred in the previous year pursuant to a policy of diversification then being pursued by the Griffin group.

48. The organisational arrangements made within Griffin Coal at the time suggest that the aluminium venture was seen by the relevant officers of Griffin Coal as a new and separate activity of the company. A new company was formed, Griffin Aluminium Pty Limited. Griffin Coal's accounting records separated the expenses incurred in respect of the proposed contract to supply coal for the smelter and the costs relating to participation in the smelter consortium itself. The latter costs include the costs which are the subject of the present dispute.

49. The view which we take of the matter accords with that taken by the learned trial judge, a view formed with the advantage of hearing the detailed explanations of Mr Strmotich. In his reasons for judgment his Honour said:

"Griffin Coal argued that its motivation was to

obtain an appropriate profit from the sale of

coal and to obtain part of that profit by

using the lever of its willingness to enter a

contract for the sale of coal on terms

favourable to the smelter participants to

obtain an interest in the smelter project and

access to the profit to be obtained by such

participation. The facts show that the

activities and objectives of Griffin Coal were

not so limited. Firstly, it perceived the

leverage it could exert as supplier of the

cheapest fuel for power generation as opening

up an opportunity for the company to diversify

its activities and move beyond the extraction

and sale of coal for which there was little or

no export market and for which Griffin Coal

was dependent upon the goodwill and bargaining

strength of a single customer. Secondly,

participation in the smelter project itself

opened up possible areas of further

diversification for Griffin Coal, or for the

group of companies of which it was part, as a

fabricator or manufacturer of aluminium

products. Thirdly, Griffin Coal could

negotiate for such profit as it may have

foregone on the sale of coal to be recognized

by the smelter participants and have that

profit capitalized and treated as part of

Griffin Coal's capital contribution to the

smelter project.

The securing of an off-take agreement or

tolling agreements to fix a rate of return and

eliminate risks that may be inherent in

attempting to sell aluminium ingots on the

world market would be prudent steps to be

expected of any operator of a business of

aluminium production. It cannot be said that

the proposal under consideration by Griffin

Coal, and on which outgoings were incurred,

was the consideration of some scheme for the

receipt of a deferred profit on the sale of

coal. The outgoings were incurred for the

purpose of assessing and understanding the

range of its obligations and liabilities as a

joint venturer in the construction and

operation of an aluminium smelter. The

proposal entailed entirely new activities for

the company necessitating an understanding of

the business of an aluminium producer and

vendor. Although the eventual target of the

expenditure on attempts to obtain a contract

to supply coal to the smelter operation and on

the study of the feasibility of participation

in the smelter project may have been the same,

namely the return of a profit on whatever

investment was required to be committed, part

of that target was to be reached by the use of

a new arrow in the quiver, namely the

operation of an aluminium smelter.

Furthermore, any profit foregone on the sale

of coal and capitalised as equity in the joint

venture project for the production of

aluminium could not serve to stamp the profits

of the investment in the smelter project joint

venture as profits from the existing business

activity of extracting and selling coal.

The argument that the pursuit of a contract

for the sale of coal was so inextricably mixed

with the pursuit of participation in the

aluminium smelter joint venture that all

outgoings were necessarily incurred by Griffin

Coal in carrying on its business for the

purpose of gain or, alternatively, incurred in

producing assessable income, does not reflect

the actual circumstances. There was a clear

demarcation between the activities undertaken

by Griffin Coal in seeking a further long-term

contract for the sale of coal and the

activities undertaken by it to assess the

feasibility of the construction and operation

of an aluminium smelter and of Griffin Coal's

participation in that venture.

At the time Griffin Coal undertook the latter

expenditure the broad outline of the extent of

Griffin Coal's possible commitments to the

venture were known and horizons had been set

as targets for the construction and completion

of the smelter. The project was well

advanced. The feasibility studies were not

mere assessments of whether a project could be

undertaken, they were also ground-work

activities for the project involving the

preparation of tender documents and draft

contracts leading to the distribution of

invitations to contractors in February 1985 to

bid for project construction contracts. There

were no discrete stages. The feasibility

studies were flowing into the selection of a

site, settlement of environmental questions,

negotiation of contracts and firm commitments.

It would not be correct to say that the

expenditure incurred in the years ending 30

June 1985 and 30 June 1986 was merely of a

preliminary nature made under the umbrella of

the conduct of the existing business. By July

1984 Griffin Coal had moved well beyond

seeking general information. It was engaged

in precise activities directed at assessing

the prospects of, and preparing the ground

for, acquisition of an asset, namely a right

of participation in a joint venture for the

construction and operation of an aluminium

smelter. It was not to the point that

essential contracts for the venture had not

been concluded. Understandings had been

reached and the activities undertaken on the

foundation of those understandings were not

mere explorations of feasibility but were also

the erection of framework necessary for the

commencement of a project to construct and

operate an aluminium smelter. All of those

activities were clearly directed at bringing

about the formation of an asset.

In participating in these activities, Griffin

Coal was looking at the reality of a new

venture. It had moved well beyond an incident

occurring in the course of the business of

coal extraction and sale. The expenditure on

those activities was incurred with the

knowledge that the company may have been a

participant in the smelter project but not the

supplier of coal to be used to generate

electricity required by the smelter if, in due

course, SECWA accepted a more competitive

tender for the supply of coal submitted by

Western Collieries Limited. Griffin Coal's

proposal to SECWA for the supply of coal at a

price of $20 per tonne enabled SECWA to obtain

the approval of State Cabinet to offer to the

consortium to supply power at a nominated

tariff, but SECWA intended to seek competitive

contract for the supply of coal. The efforts

of Griffin Coal had won for it a place in the

consortium, but neither on its part nor on the

part of its co-venturers was Griffin Coal's

place in the consortium conditional upon it

being the supplier of coal to be used for the

purpose of generating the electricity to be

supplied to the smelter.

It must be concluded that the subject

expenditure incurred by Griffin Coal was part

of the cost of formation of a new source of

income and that the expenditure was not an

aggregation of outgoings necessarily incurred

in carrying on the existing business for the

purpose of producing assessable income from

the conduct of that business."

We respectfully adopt the whole of this passage.

Findings: the second limb of s.5(1)

50. Lee J went on to consider the second limb of s.51(1). He held that the relevant expenditure was not necessarily incurred in carrying on the business of extraction and sale of coal; but, rather, in seeking to acquire an asset to be used in an expanded business of the company or in a business to be conducted by another member of the group. He considered that the outgoings were not expenses necessarily incurred in carrying on the existing business of Griffin Coal. We agree with this characterisation, which seems to us to follow inexorably from the conclusion reached by Lee J, and by us, in connection with the first limb.

51. Lee J dealt separately with what he called "the second line of submission": that is, the argument that the outgoings were deductible because they were made for the purpose of increasing the demand for energy, requiring SECWA to retain or expand its supply contracts with Griffin Coal and to adopt a more conciliatory attitude to the company. Lee J pointed out that the assessable income gained by Griffin Coal pursuant to that contract was gained irrespective of the subject outgoings. He said:

"At best it could be said that the outgoings

had the object of creating a situation that

would protect the source of assessable income,

the Coal Sale Agreement, or would remove the

risk of possible destruction of the existing

business by removing the threat of termination

of that Agreement. Outgoings upon such an

object would be capital in nature, but in any

case the expenditures in question were too

removed from the gaining or producing of the

assessable income to be able to be described

as either incidental or relevant thereto or

incurred in the actual course of production of it."

52. As to the second limb of s.51(1), Lee J pointed out that it is not sufficient that outgoings have a temporal connection with the conduct of a business:

"They must be necessarily incurred in carrying

on the business for the purpose of gaining

assessable income. Expenditure must be

clearly appropriate for the conduct of the

business, the exigencies of the business

making the expenditure imperative in the

conduct of the business. (See Snowden and

Willson Proprietary Limited v. Federal

Commissioner of Taxation [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431

Per Fullagar J. at p 444.)"

53. We respectfully agree with Lee J's comments on this argument. We merely add that there is no basis in the evidence for the conclusion that Griffin Coal's participation in the smelter venture would be critical, or indeed very relevant, to the smelter's prospects of proceeding. The other interested parties did not come to Griffin Coal with an invitation to participate; Griffin Coal sought them out. The price at which Griffin Coal, or Western Colleries, could supply coal to SECWA was certainly fundamental to the viability of the project; but that had nothing to do with incurring the relevant expenditure.

54. We are of the opinion that the subject expenditure does not fall within either limb of the opening words of s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. Consequently, it is not necessary to deal with the respondent's further submission that the expenditure is excluded from the operation of the subsection because it is capital expenditure. The appeals should be dismissed with costs.
