Re Irena Lander and Commissioner of Superannuation [1993] AATA 65; (1993) 29 ALD 870 (26 February 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: IRENA LANDER

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A92/69

AAT No. 8553

Number of pages - 26

Superannuation

[1993] AATA 65; (1993) 29 ALD 870 (extract)

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

B.J. McMahon(1) (Deputy President), Dr D.B. Travers(1) (Member) and H.G. Julian DSC(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - deferred benefit - election outside prescribed period - discretion to recognise election - whether discretion should be exercised - whether failure to inform employee of rights - long delay - whether acceptable explanation for delay - prejudice to respondent - decision affirmed

Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) ss 135, 136, 137, 157

Bond Corporation Holdings Ltd and Ors v Australian Broadcasting Tribunal [1988] FCA 433; 84 ALR 669

Re Dudley Engineering Pty Ltd (1968) 1 NSWR 483

Hall v Nominal Defendant [1966] HCA 36; 117 CLR 423

Hickey and Ors v Australian Telecommunications Commission [1984] FCA 176; 47 ALR 517

Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd and Ors v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; 7 ALD 315

Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456

Re Mulheron and Australian Telecommunications Corporation 14 AAR 42

University of Newcastle v Chopra [1989] HCA 27; 63 ALJR 397

HEARING

SYDNEY, 3-4 February 1993

26:2:1993

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

This is an application to review a decision of 1 May 1992 by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation. The decision, made under sub-section 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"), was that the Commissioner declined to direct that an election made by the applicant on 26 March 1990 under sub-section 137(1) should be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that sub-section of the Act.

2. The relevant parts of s-137 are as follows -

"s137(1)A person who -

(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which

paragraph (b) does not apply and, upon so ceasing, is not

entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in

accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or

(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue

of which the person is to be deemed, under subsection 58(3), to

have retired involuntarily;

may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an

eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the

Commissioner that this Division apply in relation to the person.

(2) if a person makes an election for the purposes of this

section within 1 month before he ceases to be an eligible

employee, the election has effect as if it had been made on the

day after he ceased to be an eligible employee.

(3) Subject to subsection (4) an election under this section is

of no effect unless the person who made the election gives

notice in writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within

such further period as the Commissioner allows, after expiration

of the period that is the prescribed period in relation to him -

(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of

the prescribed period and, if so, the name and address of

the employer; and

(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the

prescribed period a member of the superannuation scheme

applicable in relation to that employment and, if so,

specifying the scheme concerned."

3. The effect of making an election under this section where a person ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement, is that Division 3 of Part IX of the Act will then apply. This provides for various benefits which can be chosen by a retiring employee, having regard to that employee's particular circumstances. Section 135 (for example) provides for a transfer value to be paid. The benefit which the applicant seeks is that provided by s 136, entitled deferred benefits, but often referred to as preservation of superannuation rights. Various time limits are prescribed by sections in Division 3. A transfer value, for example, is payable if an election is made within 3 months. The deferred benefit, which the applicant now seeks, is dependent upon an election being made within 21 days of the applicant ceasing to be an eligible employee or within one month before that event. Other benefits are applicable if an employee resumes full time employment as a public servant within 5 years of retirement. The actual election which Mrs Lander made was contained in a letter dated 26 March 1990, some 11 years after her earlier resignation.

4. Sub-section 157(1) contains a power to recognise an election made after the prescribed time in certain circumstances. It is in these terms -

"s157(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an

election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the

period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and

the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case

it is desirable that the election should be recognized, the

Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been

made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect

accordingly."

5. The applicant was born on 22 February 1947 and was educated at Wallsend High School, Newcastle, where she obtained her Leaving Certificate. A Commonwealth Scholarship took her to the University of Newcastle, where she obtained a BA in Geography. Immediately after graduating in 1969, she found employment as a researcher at Parliament House in Canberra where she remained for 2 years. During this time, she completed her library studies part time. She took only 2 years to finish a 3-year course.

6. Upon becoming a qualified librarian, she was appointed to the ANU Library in the Department of Social Studies. At that time, she was a member of a superannuation fund but could not remember what fund that was.

7. She resigned from ANU to go to Melbourne, where her intended husband was taking a course. She found employment at Melbourne University as a librarian where she worked for one year. When she came back to the ACT, she found employment with the National Library as a librarian on 5 March 1973.

8. Her appointment was confirmed on 4 May 1973 and she became a permanent employee in November 1973, thus becoming eligible for membership of the superannuation fund. She has no recollection of what happened to her previous superannuation.

9. She was later promoted to librarian class 2. She believes that she signed some papers concerning membership of the superannuation scheme, although she can not recall exactly what they were. They were in fact tendered in evidence by the respondent. At any event, deductions were made from her salary.

10. She now has 4 children. The first was born on 8 October 1974 and the second on 14 August 1976. In the case of each birth she took maternity leave. Although entitled to 52 weeks leave, she said that she returned a little earlier than she was required to.

11. After the birth of the second child, she returned on 24 June 1977 to her old job in the films section of the National Library. Immediately relations were unsatisfactory with her supervisor, Mr Edmonson. He too had been a librarian class 2 when she left. He had been promoted while she was away and was now her immediate superior. Shortly after her return, he handed her a document consisting of several typed pages, which she no longer has. It referred to the fact that he was unhappy with her. He regarded her as unsuitable and he considered that she was causing problems. As the relationship worsened, she said that she sought help from the personnel department and went to see the personnel officer, George Clark, shortly thereafter.

12. He told her there was nothing he could do and that she should continue working. She had the feeling she was being let down and later this led to a loss of confidence in the support that she felt she was entitled to expect from the personnel department.

13. Mr Edmonson was amazed that she had taken this attitude and that she had been to see Mr Clark. Mrs Lander had 20 staff under her at that stage, but (she said) she was treated as if she were of a much lower rank. Mr Edmonson made life difficult for her. He wanted her out of the films area. He was harassing her and undermining her authority by going directly to her junior staff. He did not consult with her and continually checked her on small matters. He would not tell her when he was going on leave. He disapproved of the way she signed her letters. There were other petty matters that led to disagreements between them. Other staff complained about him to her. Eventually, she decided that she could not work with him and on 1 August 1977 she applied for a transfer to any other section of the National Library.

14. Between then and November 1977 when the transfer came through, she continued to work in a manner that she considered was conscientious. By arrangement with her husband, she obtained an appointment with the Director-General of the National Library in the presence of Mr Clark. Her husband accompanied her. The Director-General, while saying that he would help her, did nothing more than refer the matter back to Mr Clark. She considered that this was an unhelpful attitude.

15. On 24 November 1977, she was transferred to maps at the same level of seniority. She was happy to get out of films but was conscious that she had a cloud over her and that the new supervisor might have known her background. She said he was very distant and not at all friendly.

16. In 1978, she again became pregnant. It was an unplanned pregnancy and she was not aware of it until it was advanced to 4 months. At that time she was still breast feeding the younger child. She went on maternity leave for the third time, but continued to be self-conscious about this. In late 1978, she returned to the maps section and to a busy schedule domestically. By then she had 3 children under the age of 4 and was still breast feeding 2 of them. She was pre-occupied with the job and with her family.

17. After returning in 1978, she had no occasion to see George Clark again. She had no particular problems with her work, except that she had no extra privileges because she was breast feeding and was managing other young children at the same time. It was necessary for her to express breast milk at lunch time and she found this difficult.

18. Having discussed the matter with her husband, she said that she decided to resign. On 12 February 1979 she wrote a letter in these terms -

"Mrs. R. Kazakow,

Personnel Officer,

National Library of Australia.

I have decided to resign from the Library as from the

close of business, Monday 5 March.

Please credit all payments due to me to my account, and

advise of superannuation refund arrangements.

Thank you.

(signed)

Mrs. I. Lander

Maps.

Accept

(Signed) G Hughes.

13/2/79"

19. She had spoken with no one in authority concerning her intended resignation, and no one had asked her why she intended to resign. She said that she requested a refund of her superannuation contributions because she thought that was the only thing to do. She said that she was not aware that there was any other option. Her husband at that time was first assistant secretary in the Department of Capital Territory and had been a second division officer since May 1975.

20. She put the letter in a tray. She did not approach personnel as she had no confidence in them. She continued working until the day she left on 5 March 1979. She said that she is the sort of person who takes her work seriously.

21. She signed the application form for refund, T5/26, on 23 February 1979. She gave evidence however that she could not recall signing it or the circumstances under which it was signed. Her signature is said to be witnessed by a Ms Reed, but Mrs Lander said that she could not recall her, except that she remembered on one occasion, she came to the films branch to show everybody her baby. From later evidence of Ms Reed, it is probable that this recollection was incorrect and confused her with another employee. She agrees that she filled out the form, putting her own name and address in block printing and signed it. She does not recall whether she crossed out the options and made the crossing which now appears on the form. In the original form the signature appears in different ink from the opening writing. She could not explain the difference. The form, on its face, confirms an application for refund of accumulated contributions and rejects a possible election "for preservation of superannuation rights".

22. During the whole of the time that she was a member of the scheme, she does not recall being given any information on superannuation. She thought that applying for a refund of contribution was routine. She also thought that superannuation was a routine matter. She knew only that she was paying a percentage of her salary. She said that she was not aware of the new scheme that came out in 1976 or 1977 and that if it did have any publicity she was not conscious of it. She said that she had never heard of the Superannuation Act 1976. She later added that she was not a "money person" and had not taken much notice of these matters.

23. She had married on 18 December 1971. Although her husband was also a member of the same superannuation scheme, she said they never discussed its provisions. She said : "we were very young then and did not worry about retirement. It was just another routine deduction from our salaries".

24. She did not recall receiving the lump sum of $2998-15, but did not dispute that it was paid into her bank account. She said that she did not invest it and did not even use it to pay off an existing mortgage. It simply merged with her current finances.

25. Some months after she retired, she took employment as a casual teacher. She then went to Melbourne with her husband late in 1981 where her fourth child was born in 1982.

26. In 1983 they came back to Canberra. She then started work as a full time teacher librarian after accepting an offer made to her by the Education Department. Following her acceptance, she went to the school at Chifley and interviewed the principal about school affairs, but there was no discussion concerning her eligibility to resume superannuation payments. She received certain documents, including documents from the Department of Education which were tendered in evidence, but could not remember the circumstances under which she received them. Each of the documents touched only briefly on the subject of superannuation.

27. She commenced paying superannuation again and continues to do so. She agrees that she must have signed some documents but can not recall what they were. She does recall going for her medical examination on this occasion.

28. In recent times, yet another new superannuation scheme has been introduced, and Commonwealth public servants are being induced to change from the old to the new system. She said that she has been made aware of the new scheme. For the first time, she has become aware of publicity being given to superannuation matters. She has received a lot of information and has been to meetings where she has been addressed on the choices available to her. As part of a program, sponsored by her Department, she went to a seminar at which she was addressed by a consultant. During question time she became aware, for the first time, that it was open to her to make a late election to have her former membership of the fund count as part of her present membership. She discussed this matter with her husband who then wrote document T10/32 a few days after the seminar. This is the document that has been treated as a late election.

29. In cross-examination, she agreed that the principal reason she resigned was because of the conflict between the 2 demanding roles that she had at the time. On the one hand, she had pressing family considerations with 3 young children. On the other hand she had a demanding job. She had thought that she could carry out both roles, but found that she could not. She agreed that she did not make any enquiry about any other superannuation options.

30. She agreed that she would have read the application for refund at the time she signed it. However, she did not remember striking out the options. She denied that Ms Reid had alerted her to those options. She said that she did not tell her, Mrs Lander, that there was a choice available to her. She agrees however that she did not ask any person whether or not any choice was available to her.

31. She agreed that when she went on leave to have her third child, she had no specific problem at work. When she came back there were still no work problems, except those associated with breast feeding. She said that she was self conscious about having to take leave for the third time in 5 years and she was determined to do well.

32. She was shown certain pro forma information statements that were sent out by the respondent in 1987, 88 and 89. She recognised the first page but not the second page. It was on this page that information is contained concerning preservation of superannuation rights. Even if she had read it, she said, she would not realise that it applied to her as she would take it to refer to people with current needs not to people with situations that occurred 10 years previously.

33. Evidence was given by Mr Fielder, a financial consultant, which was quite unhelpful. Mr Fielder's knowledge of superannuation in the late 1970s was admittedly poor. He thought that regular statements of information given by the Commissioner to members ceased at some time but he could not remember when the gap started or finished. Indeed it is difficult to understand why his evidence was tendered.

34. Mr Lander gave general corroborative evidence of what his wife had said. More important, however, was evidence concerning his own career.

35. From 1963 to 1966 he was with the Reserve Bank. In 1967 he transferred to the Public Service Board as an administration trainee. Between 1968 and 1970, he was employed at Treasury moving from class 4 to class 8. Between 1970 and 1972 he studied for his MBA at Melbourne University and was employed by the Post Office on secondment in the meantime. He returned to Canberra in 1973 where he was employed until 1975 in the Treasury. By this time, he was a Class 11 officer. Between 1975 and 1980, he was employed in the Department of the Australian Capital Territory, finishing up as first assistant secretary. Prior to that he was secretary to the City Services branch, then secretary to the Urban Affairs branch of the same Department. Throughout all his time in that department, he was involved with industrial matters dealing with employees on buses and in the Commonwealth car fleet. He said however that he had no responsibility for payment of superannuation for employees.

36. In 1980, he joined the Immigration Department as first assistant secretary. In 1981, whilst in that position, he worked on exchange with John Holland Constructions for 17 months in the field of industrial relations. He returned to Immigration in 1983, where he stayed until 1988 as head of the review branch. During that time, he took a law degree at Macquarie University between 1986 and 1989. When he left the Public Service in 1988, he worked for solicitors in Canberra as an employed solicitor and later established his own practice which he continues to carry on.

37. He agreed in cross-examination that he had been appointed to the second division as a young man and it would be fair to call him "switched on" (to use counsel's term). Nevertheless, he said that he did not ever see any circular, manual, or pamphlet from the Superannuation Office. Superannuation rights were never explained as part of any induction course. He never had occasion to deal with the problem of superannuation while he was in management.

38. He said that his wife was of an independent turn of mind. She did not consult him concerning her resignation letter. He did not see it until he saw the T-documents. She did not consult him concerning her application for refund of contributions or concerning any of the forms that she signed. He said that he did not discuss superannuation with her when she retired as she did not seek his advice.

39. Documentary evidence was tendered by the respondent and various witnesses gave oral evidence on his behalf. The Tribunal derived much assistance from the evidence of Mrs Reed, which was given with quiet confidence and fairness.

40. Mrs Reed started at the National Library in 1969 in the personnel section and later moved to management in 1983. In 1979, at the time of Mrs Lander's retirement, she was staff clerk with 2 cells of leave clerks and their teams in her charge. It was her responsibility to process resignations and to witness signatures as an adult contributor to the fund. It was also her function to explain their entitlements to persons retiring if the leave clerk did not.

41. She had no specific recollection of Mrs Lander's retirement. She was clear, however, that the procedure was uniformly followed. When a person was to retire, the employee was given the form to claim superannuation benefits and the employee's entitlement was explained to him or her. If there were any complications in the employee's entitlement, the employee was referred with the form to the Superannuation Board to obtain more expert advice and to bring the form back later, filled out in accordance with that advice.

42. Mrs Reed recognised her signature on the form and said that she signed it in the presence of Mrs Lander, who had also signed it in her presence. She said quite emphatically that when she signed as a witness, the form was never signed in her absence.

43. The bottom half of the form containing calculations of entitlements was done later, but the signatories on the top half of the form, signed the document at the same time.

44. Mrs Reed, who was aged 29 at the time and a Class 4 officer, said that she knew as much as anyone in staff about superannuation, as in 1979 she had been in her job for 10 years. In fact she had personally produced the staff manual which included information on superannuation.

45. It was her responsibility to respond to queries on superannuation from members of the staff. If the queries were too complicated she would have referred the staff member to the Superannuation Board. She confirmed that she was well aware of the nature of preservation of rights.

46. She denied that dealing with dismissals was a mechanical function which might have been carried out in an absent-minded way. She said that the machinery for resignations was "well-oiled". She did not know the members of the staff personally, but as she was dealing with a pay team in alphabetical order, she knew the names quite well. She took a personal pride in what she did even if it might be said to be boring. She affirmed that whatever she did would have been well done.

47. Since 21 May 1964, Mr Donda has worked for the National Library. He is now an assistant director of the personnel section. He has worked in personnel management since he joined the Library and has been trained in superannuation. He gave oral evidence on behalf of the respondent.

48. He identified the superannuation circular of 2 August 1978 as one that had been circulated amongst employees. This summarises rights brought about by the change in legislation in 1976.

49. In 1979 when Mrs Lander retired, he was senior personnel officer. He had prepared a check list of matters to go over with employees when they left. These matters included checking with them on their superannuation rights. He shared the actual interface work with separating employees with Mrs Reid and actually advised employees on their rights.

50. He confirmed that in 1979 the turnover rate at the Library was about 120 per year. He denied that this was unusual, or that it could result in any carelessness in handling any person's entitlements on separation.

51. Mr Whitton works for the Human Resources Branch of Corporate Services Bureau, a recently formed organisation which offers its services in personnel recruitment and management to other government agencies. At all times he has been in the Education area. He too gave oral evidence on behalf of the respondent.

52. He was appointed to the personnel unit in the ACT Department of Education on 17 November 1975. In July 1983 he was still in that job. In 1985 he had direct responsibility for recruitment and management of school teachers in all ACT public schools.

53. He confirmed that for new permanent officers (teachers) there was an induction course which included general advice on superannuation. Mrs Lander had been inducted on 25 January 1985 by a person named Leigh Connors. Mr Whitton agreed that this would have been directed principally to her then present superannuation rights and would not have raised the question of her previous service for superannuation purposes, unless the employee raised it.

54. The discretion given to the Commissioner under s 157(1) is at large subject to any limitations that may be seen in its terms. A wide discretion is not controlled by any express criteria but only by the general purpose and policy for which the discretion is granted (Hall v Nominal Defendant [1966] HCA 36; 117 CLR 423 at 445). More recently, Gummow J restated the principles to be applied in such cases in Bond Corporation Holdings Limited v Australian Broadcasting Tribunal [1988] FCA 433; 84 ALR 669 at 680. In Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456, all 3 members of the Tribunal agreed that the discretion conferred by s 157 was, to some extent, limited by the use of the word "desirable". We agree with the conclusions of the Tribunal in Liddle. The majority, whilst accepting the legal principles described in that case, disagreed with the minority in the application of those principles to the particular facts of the case. That disagreement does not, in our view, detract from the value of the exposition of those principles. In our view, however, there are other matters of principle which should also be addressed in cases of this nature and to which we will now turn our attention.

55. Firstly, it should be made clear that a person who seeks the exercise of the Commissioner's discretion in his or her favour must satisfy the Commissioner (and this Tribunal) that the discretion should be so exercised. It is not for the respondent to establish that the applicant does not have a case for the beneficial exercise of discretion. There is in this Tribunal no formal onus of proof. There is however an evidentiary onus which an applicant must bear if the result of this administrative enquiry is to be to the applicant's satisfaction. The principles discussed in Hickey and Ors v Australian Telecommunications Commission [1984] FCA 176; 47 ALR 517 are useful guides to appropriate considerations and circumstances such as the present ones.

56. Counsel for the applicant paid considerable attention to authorities dealing with legislative intent and drew our attention, in particular, to a judgment of the Full High Court in University of Newcastle v Chopra [1989] HCA 27; 63 ALJR 397. In our view. however, the circumstances considered in that case are quite different from those in the present case. What the courts were called upon to decide was whether the member of the fund under consideration in that case had in fact made an election. Here it is not contended that the events of 1979 amounted to an election of the type that the applicant now seeks to have accepted out of time. The decisions to which counsel referred us, dealing with the necessity for the elements of full information and understanding before an election was a valid election, are therefore in our view inappropriate in considering the issues to be resolved in the present case.

57. Counsel for the applicant also referred us to a number of authorities, affirming the propositions that effect should be given to legislative intent and that legislation conferring benefits should, wherever possible, be interpreted in favour of those likely to benefit from the legislation. There can be no contention with these propositions. However the intention of the legislature is to be determined by reference to the words it uses. In all the choices provided by Division 3 of Part IX, a time limit is included. Elections must be made within the stipulated period. In the case of the benefit to which the applicant now aspires, quite a short period is provided. It is either one month before resignation or 21 days after resignation.

58. One can not ignore these time limits. They are in the statute. If they are to be bypassed regularly to give effect to the benign intentions of the legislature, then in our view legislative amendment to the terms of s 157(1) would be necessary to spell this out. What s 157(1) does is to give an applicant a right which is tantamount to a right to apply for an extension of time. In considering whether an extension should be granted, the principles considered by the courts when dealing with applications under the Defence Forces Death and Retirement Benefits Act and the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act are useful. In Liddle at 467 the Tribunal noted that both parties agreed that this was the case. The authorities were gathered and analysed by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Limited and Ors v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; 7 ALD 315 at pages 319-320. These principles have been applied by this Tribunal in a number of contexts. A good example is to be found in the decision of the President in Re Mulheron and Australian Telecommunications Corporation 14 AAR 42 at 48.

59. The passage in Wilcox J's judgment is well known. We propose to repeat it, however, so that it may be considered in its application to the requirements of s 157(1) -

"Section 11 of the Administrative Decisions (Judicial

Review) Act does not set out any criteria by reference to which

the court's decision to extend time for an application for

review under s 5 is to be exercised. Already there have been a

number of decisions of judges of this court, all sitting at

first instance, dealing with the approach proper to be taken.

They differ a little, both in language and in emphasis, but I

venture to suggest that from them may be distilled the following

principles to guide, not in any exhaustive manner, the exercise

of the court's discretion -

(a) Although the section does not, in terms, place any

onus of proof upon an applicant for extension an

application has to be made. Special circumstances need

not be shown but the court will not grant the application

unless positively satisfied that it is proper so to do.

The "prescribed period" of 28 days is not to be ignored

Ralkon v Aboriginal Development Commission (1982) 43 ALR

535 at 550). Indeed it is the prima facie rule that

proceedings commenced outside that period will not be

entertained (Lucic v Nolan (1982) 45 ALR 411 at 416). It

is a pre-condition to the exercise of discretion in his

favour that the applicant for extension show an

"acceptable explanation of the delay" and that it is "fair

and equitable in the circumstances" to extend time (Duff

at 485: Chapman v Reilly (unreported), Neaves J, 9

December 1983, at 7).

(b) Action taken by the applicant, other than by making an

application for review under the Act, is relevant to the

consideration of the question whether an acceptable

explanation for the delay has been furnished. A

distinction is to be made between the case of a person

who, by non-curial means, has continued to make the

decision-maker aware that he contests the finality of the

decision (who has not "rested on his rights": per Fisher J

in Doyle v Chief of Staff) (1982) 42 ACR 283 at 287 and a

case where the decision-maker was allowed to believe that

the matter was finally concluded. Compare Doyle, Chapman,

Ralkon and Douglas v Allen unreported, (Morling J, 3 April

1984, at 18 with Lucic at 414-15 and Hickey v Australian

Telecommunications Commission (1983) 48 ALR 517 at 519.

The reasons for this distinction are not only the "need

for finality in disputes" (see Lucic at 410) but also the

"fading from memory" problem referred to in Wedesweiller v

Cole [1983] FCA 94; (1983) 47 ALR 528.

(c) Any prejudice to the respondent including any

prejudice in defending the proceedings occasioned by the

delay is a material factor militating against the grant of

an extension : see Doyle at 287 Duff at 484-5; Hickey

at 525-7 and Wedesweiller at 533-4.

(d) However, the mere absence of prejudice is not enough

to justify the grant of an extension: Douglas at 18: Lucic

at 416; Hickey at 523. In this context, public

considerations often intrude (Lucic, Hickey). A delay

which may result, if the application is successful, in the

unsettling of other people (Ralkon at 550, Becerra at

12-13) or of established practices (Douglas at 19) is likely

to prove fatal to the applicant.

(e) The merits of the substantial application are properly

to be taken into account in considering whether an

extension of time should be granted: (Lucic at 417);

Chapman at 6).

(f) Considerations of fairness as between the applicants

and other persons otherwise in a like position are

relevant to the manner of exercise of the court's

discretion (Wedesweiller at 534-5)."

60. We consider it helpful to apply these principles to applications seeking a favourable exercise of discretion under s 157(1). His Honour's first principle is that the prescribed period is not to be ignored. In our view, this should be regarded as the paramount principle. The fact that 21 days is prescribed as an appropriate time within which to make an election indicates that discretion to extend this time is to be limited by reference to the relatively short time period prescribed. The longer the interval between ceasing to be an eligible employee and making a late election, the heavier the onus the applicant bears.

61. His Honour noted that there must be an acceptable explanation of the delay. In the circumstances of late election cases, one should ask firstly whether the applicant in fact knew of his or her rights to preserve superannuation benefits. One should ask whether one is satisfied that the employee was in fact appraised of the situation and whether, through the passage of time, or through the supersedence of other interests at the time, that information has simply been forgotten. Secondly, if one is not satisfied that specific information was made available to the applicant, one should then ask whether any enquiries were made of the applicant's personnel officer or at the office of the Commissioner. It is also relevant to take into account whether there was any superannuation manual available. Thirdly, one should consider the opportunities given to the applicant to enquire. One should look at the employee's education level, his or her health, any language difficulty or impediment to information sharing, any family or personal circumstances that might impinge upon the employee's opportunity to enquire, the location of the employee and any general publicity available at the time. In Liddle's case the majority considered that the remote location of the applicant and his state of health precluded him from making full enquiries. If however, an educated person who has no difficulties with the language (unlike, for example, a migrant hospital cleaner) who would normally be expected to read newspaper accounts of radical changes in public service superannuation, who is located in Canberra, who has access through her family connections and her work connections to sources of information which are, or should be available, then a different result might ensue.

62. His Honour considered that one should take account of any action taken by the applicant other than making an application for review under the Act. In the circumstances of a s 157 application, one may ask what happened to the lump sum that was paid to the applicant. In looking at the applicant's actions, one may also ask whether the application for refund is the only record of an election or whether separate correspondence also amounts to an election to take accumulated contributions. One should also enquire about the actions of the employee when he or she again entered the public sector and became eligible to renew contributions.

63. His Honour considered that any prejudice to the respondent should be taken into account. The principle prejudice in the s 157 context is that arising from delay - what His Honour referred to as the "fading from memory" factor. Again, the length of the delay will be material in considering the extent of the prejudice. The longer the interval between ceasing to be an eligible employee and exercising a late election, the more difficult it will be to establish the facts and the degree of knowledge available to the applicant on resignation.

64. His Honour also considered that public considerations should be taken into account. These were identified in paragraph 26 of the respondent's s 37 statement. There is a small financial prejudice to the fund. There is however a larger public consideration in that if the discretion is freely exercised in favour of late election applicants, then the stability and structure of the fund could be affected and furthermore those who fail to make an election because of their perception of time limits under the Act would be unfairly prejudiced.

65. The principles referred to by Wilcox J dealt with removal of procedural barriers thus enabling applicants to bring proceedings outside the time limited by the statute under which the reviewable decision was made. In the present case, a favourable exercise of discretion would not merely remove procedural barriers. It would confer a substantive right. Principles of the kind described by His Honour have assisted courts in a number of cases, where an extension of time resulted in the granting of substantive rights. An example may be seen in applications to register company charges later than the prescribed time, thus giving the chargee priority over other creditors by the mere granting of the extension of time. These principles were discussed by Street J in Re Dudley Engineering Pty Limited (1968) 1 NSWR 483. In our view, the Commissioner (and this Tribunal) should be at least as reluctant to exercise discretion favourably under s 157(1) as the courts have been to grant extensions of time where mere procedural barriers only have been under consideration.

66. In the light of these principles, we have reviewed the evidence described earlier. We accept Mrs Reed's evidence that the applicant would have been told of her rights. In saying this, we do not, in any way, wish to impute a lack of credit to Mrs Lander. At the time she had 3 very young children who, understandably, dominated her life. She was a young woman. It is common experience that superannuation does not concern young people so much as those of middle or older age. On the balance of probabilities, we find that her rights were fully explained to Mrs Lander on separation but that the significance of these rights did not impinge greatly on her consciousness at the time.

67. In any event, we consider that Mrs Lander had ample opportunities to make enquiries. It is not to the point to say (as she implied) that her hand was not held. There are matters in which a person in her position is expected to exercise some faculty of enquiry and judgment. She was an educated woman, clearly a hard and conscientious worker, a person without apparent health or language problems, a person located in Canberra, a person married to a man with an unusual degree of seniority in the public service for his age. We accept that Mrs Lander is, to use her husband's words, an independent woman. Nevertheless, she gave evidence that she did discuss her impending resignation with Mr Lander. That being so, it is relevant to take into account the knowledge, or the capacity for finding out knowledge, which he must have brought to their discussions.

68. The documents themselves are clear. Both the letter and the form reject the election for preservation of rights and stipulate for a refund of accumulated contributions. The Commissioner is entitled, in our view, to accept these documents, having regard to the background from which they emerged, as a conscious decision on the part of the applicant to seek and accept what was the entitlement she ultimately obtained. We do not consider that an "acceptable explanation of the delay" has been put forward. A particularly convincing explanation would have been necessary in our view, having regard to the 11 year interval. Such an interval hardly seems to have been contemplated by s 137. Furthermore such an interval has created considerable prejudice for the respondent. It may well be that in regard to s 137, the interval to be considered under s 157 should be no more than a few days over the 21 days. It is not necessary to decide that here. What does emerge, however, is that the history of the applicant's association with the fund has not provided any grounds for an exercise of the Commissioner's discretion in the applicant's favour.

69. The decision under review is therefore affirmed.
