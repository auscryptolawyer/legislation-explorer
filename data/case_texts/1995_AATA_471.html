Gibbins and Commissioner for Superannuation [1995] AATA 471 (17 March 1995)

Last Updated: 23 September 2008

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



SUPERANNUATION - late election - discretion as to whether to direct that election be treated as made within time - objective judgement having regard to all the circumstances - knowledge at time of election - justice as between the parties



Superannuation Act 1976 ss.137, 157



Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 19 AAR 450

Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239

Decision : The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and remits the matter to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the election made by the applicant on 14 June 1990 under subsection 137(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed under that subsection.



(Sgd) Mrs H. E. Hallowes

Senior Member



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No V93/1197

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )



Re: BRIAN DOUGLAS GIBBINS

Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent



REASONS OF MAJORITY FOR DECISION

17 March 1995 Mrs H. E. Hallowes, Senior Member

Mr J. T. C. Brassil AM, Member



  1. This is an application for review of a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation (“the respondent”) made under subsection 154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (“the Act”) on 22 November 1993. The delegate confirmed a reviewable decision of another delegate of the respondent made on 28 January 1992 that he was “. . . not satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election made under subsection 137(1) made by Mr Gibbins on 14 June 1990 should be recognised”.
  2. Subsection 137(1) of the Act provided at the time the applicant ceased to be an eligible employee in July 1978.

“137(1) Where a person ceases to be an eligible employee and, upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73, he may, not later than 21 days after he so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner, that this Division apply in relation to him.”

The section has been amended since then but the time limits within which to make an election have remained the same. The Division to which the section applies is Division 3 of Part IX of the Act which preserves rights of persons ceasing to be eligible employees. Subsection 157(1) of the Act now provides:

“157(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly.”

The Tribunal’s jurisdiction arises under subsection 154(6) of the Act which provides that the Tribunal may review reviewable decisions that have been confirmed or varied under subsection 154(4).

  1. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the documents”) together with further material lodged by both parties at the hearing. The applicant represented himself at the hearing. The respondent was represented by Mr A Merrilees, an officer within the respondent’s office.
  2. The documents disclosed that the applicant was born on 16 November 1944. Following his appointment to the then Postmaster General’s Department he became a contributor to the superannuation scheme established under the Superannuation Act 1922 (“the superseded Act”) on 11 September 1961. He became an “eligible employee” for the purposes of the Act on its commencement on 1 July 1976. By letter dated 16 May 1978 the applicant advised:

“I hereby submit my resignation, to take effect from the conclusion of shift on July 5, 1978.

I am giving early notice in order to give Personnel sufficient time to finalise moneys owing as I leave Australia for overseas on July 16, 1978 and wish to settle my financial affairs prior to my departure.

Any correspondence or moneys owing may be forwarded to me, C/o Noel Jones and Associates, 1741 Malvern Rd., Glen Iris. 3146.”

  1. All Retirement Benefit Office papers relating to the applicant have been destroyed. A copy of a Form S2A “APPLICATION FOR REFUND OF ACCUMULATED CONTRIBUTIONS/LUMP SUM BENEFIT OR ELECTION FOR PRESERVATION OF SUPERANNUATION RIGHTS” was amongst the documents. Under the above heading the following note appears:

“Note: This application form should be completed by a contributor who resigns or a contributor who retires and who elects to exercise an option to take a Lump Sum Benefit in lieu of a pension entitlement.

This form should not be completed by a contributor who is applying for a pension and a refund of accumulated contributions or supplementary contributions.”

The form invites an applicant to either:

“ ‘ apply for a refund of accumulated contributions

apply for a payment of a lump sum benefit under the provisions of the Superannuation Act

elect for preservation of superannuation rights

* Delete whichever inapplicable’ ”

  1. There is no dispute between the parties that the applicant applied for refund of his accumulated contributions as a file note from the applicant’s supervising engineer dated 4 July 1987 was also amongst the documents. It records that the applicant had tendered his resignation and sought calculation of his final payment as he was leaving for overseas on 14 July 1978. It further notes “A copy of the resignation, Forms 28C and S2A are attached for your action.” A payment of accumulated contributions of $2826.89 was paid to the applicant as directed on 11 August 1978 and a further payment of $866.40 was made to the applicant on 25 March 1979 being interest and the applicant’s share of the surplus distributed following the closure of the fund under the superseded Act which closed on 30 June 1976. Seven months after receiving the payment of interest and surplus, 25 September 1979, the applicant again became an eligible employee under the Act having been appointed to Telecom (now Telstra Corporation Ltd).
  2. On 14 June 1990 the applicant wrote to the respondent as follows:

“I hereby apply for a review of my decision not to preserve my Superannuation Benefits on resignation.

I am currently employed by Telecom Australia and contributing to the CSS Fund.

For a period of approximately 17 years from 1961 until my resignation in 1978 I was contributing to the CSS Fund.

At the time of my resignation I was not informed of the option to preserve my Superannuation benefits in lieu of being refunded approximately $3,600.

I am anxious to be credited with my period of service prior to 1978 and will reimburse any money required to do so.”

The applicant’s letter was treated by the respondent as a late election under subsection 137(1) of the Act and the applicant’s attention was drawn to subsection 157(1). The applicant was invited to provide any further material to the delegate before a decision was made. On 8 November 1990 the applicant advised the respondent:

“As it is many years since I signed the form, I don’t recall the exact form I signed, however I was of the opinion at the time of signing, that when resigning from Telecom one severed all ties with the Superannuation Scheme. I obviously deleted certain options when I signed in the belief that the only option available to me was that of refund of contributions. As your letter states, preservation of superannuation rights appears in two places on the application form. I would have assumed at the time of filling out the form that this option was available for a person in a different situation from myself.

In the work situation within Telecom there has always been a lack of information about the options available to Superannuation Scheme members. It has only been brought to my attention this year, in a series of lectures explaining both the Commonwealth and new Telecom Super schemes, that it was possible to preserve benefits in the Commonwealth scheme.

I would like to restate that at no stage during the process of tendering my resignation did anyone with a knowledge of the scheme point out to me that the option of preservation was available to me.”

  1. The applicant gave evidence that he is still employed by Telecom as a Principal Technical Officer. He said that he first heard about preserving his superannuation rights when he read an article in a union newsletter in May 1990. He immediately wrote to the respondent (see paragraph 7 above). The applicant said that when he resigned in July 1978 after approximately 17 years of service he knew nothing about preserving his rights. He had separated from his wife and he had made arrangements to go overseas on a holiday. Before departing overseas he had already paid for his trip and he had met his financial obligations in advance with respect to maintenance of his daughter. As he had not received all moneys owing to him before his departure he made arrangements for the moneys to be paid into a bank account operated for him by a real estate agent, a personal friend, who was also responsible for the letting of a house he owned. The applicant said that he made no drawings on his account while he was overseas and he has no recollection of how he spent his accumulated contributions on his return to Australia. On resigning he had given no thought to retirement nor to future employment.
  2. In 1978 the applicant carried out his duties at the City West Exchange in Melbourne. All officers at the exchange were technical staff apart from one clerical assistant through whom contact could be made with a staff clerk in another building of undisclosed location if an officer had an issue with respect to pay or leave. The applicant had no contact with any personnel officer. The Tribunal notes that the applicant’s letter of resignation (paragraph 4 above) was directed to his supervising engineer. The applicant said that he discussed his resignation with his supervisor and he was asked about taking leave without pay but as he had had enough of shift work he “wanted out” as clerical work in Telecom was the only alternative. At that time he believed that he had severed all his ties with Telecom. The applicant has no recollection of completing the S2A form.
  3. On returning from overseas the applicant met a friend who worked in the section of Telecom in which the applicant said that he had always wanted to work. There was a position available in the section and the applicant applied for it and commenced work on 9 May 1979. When he rejoined Telecom the applicant was provided with a booklet printed in 1978 with respect to his conditions of employment and advice with respect to superannuation contributions but he said that the booklet contained no information about preservation. On being shown a pro forma of an information statement for contributors to the superannuation fund by Mr Merrilees the applicant said that the document looked familiar and that he was provided with a statement of his contributions each year. The statement includes advice with respect to “Preservation” indicating that contributors who resign may elect to preserve their benefits in the Fund until retirement however the applicant could not recall reading this section of the statement. On being shown an article in a union publication, “Teletechnician” of August - September 1987 headed “Don’t throw away your super! PRESERVE IT!” (exhibit 1) the applicant said that he could not recall the article and he was not a regular reader of “Teletechnician”.
  4. The applicant is not aware of the financial benefit to him if his election is treated as having been made within the period allowed but he understands that the benefit would be substantial however under cross-examination by Mr Merrilees he said that with the knowledge he now has he could not say positively that he would have preserved his rights in 1978.
  5. Mr J Barnett, an administrative officer with Telecom gave evidence on behalf of the applicant. A copy of information he provided to the respondent on 11 December 1990 was amongst the documents. Mr Barnett commenced with Telecom in 1974 as an assistant staff clerk in personnel and he remained in this area for six to eight years. He gave evidence that superannuation was not a big issue during that period and that there were few resignations amongst staff. Part of his duties included advice with respect to superannuation but he said that it was not until 1990 that the preservation of rights was highlighted. Most officers who resigned from the service during the period were going into business or travelling overseas and they all wanted a refund of their contributions. S2A Forms were sent out on request. If an officer had sought further information with respect to the preservation of rights, Mr Barnett said that he would have “referred the question on”. He could not recall the options on the S2A Form being discussed nor any training that he was given with respect to the options available.
  6. Mr F Herbstreit worked in Telecom’s personnel area from 1962 until 1988. He gave evidence on behalf of the respondent. He outlined the procedure with respect to resignations of staff. He was responsible for answering queries with respect to superannuation schemes under the Act and the superseded Act. He said one and a half hours training was given to a staff clerk with respect to superannuation entitlements. He said that the S2A Forms were held by staff sections and on resignation an officer would be provided with a form to fill in which was then sent to the personnel section. The section stressed that preservation was complicated and that enquires should be referred to the personnel section and not be dealt with in the local regions. It was his experience that most officers wanted their money on resignation and he could recall only two people who preserved their rights. Mr Merrilees drew Mr Herbstreit’s attention to a Superannuation Board circular No. 8 of 1976 with respect to the new superannuation scheme for Commonwealth employees effective from 1 July 1976. Mr Herbstreit recalled receiving the circular and said that it was circulated to branches for reading and filing. In 1978 he had handled enquires with respect to pensions and benefits on retirement. In response to a question from the Tribunal Mr Herbstreit said that the training had not included counselling an officer with respect to the options available to an officer on resignation concerning election for preservation of superannuation rights.
  7. The matter for the Tribunal to decide is whether, in the terms set out in subsection 157(1), the Commissioner could be “satisfied in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election be recognised”. This issue has been considered by the Tribunal in a number of applications and there are two Federal Court judgments which were properly drawn to the attention of the Tribunal during these proceedings. These are a Full Court judgment of Black CJ, Davies and Cooper JJ, Chalk v Commissioner of Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 19 AAR 450, and a following judgment of Von Doussa J in Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239.
  8. Since then an unreported Tribunal decision Re Ward and Commissioner for Superannuation AAT 9987 has been handed down on 6 February 1995. In that decision of B J MacMahon, Deputy President, N Attwood and H Julian, Members, there is a consideration of the Chalk and Boardman judgments and an erudite summary of the views expressed in the Federal Court judgments as they relate to the recurring issues for consideration in the exercise of the subsection 157(1) discretion. Their summary follows:

“A General considerations setting the context within which the discretion is to be exercised

(1) The discretion given by s 157 should be exercised by reference to the words of the statute and the contents within which the discretion is conferred. Section 157 uses simple words and the issues which arise under it are invariably issues of fact, the ultimate issue being whether it is desirable that a late election should be recognised (Chalk at 455).

(2) On the one hand an election recognised under s 137(1) will extend to a person who has periods of broken service, benefits which fully reflect the total period of service as an eligible employee. That would be in accordance with the general policy of the Act. On the other hand the orderly administration of the superannuation scheme requires that time limits be applied, generally speaking, so that decisions which impinge on investment and accounting procedures are made as soon as possible after an event such as resignation occurs. Matters of this kind set the context within which the discretion under s 157(1) is conferred and the context in which the discretion must be exercised (Boardman at 246 and 247).

(3) In s 157(1) ‘desirable’ is not referable to a state of mind - whether of the elector or of the Commissioner. Whether a late election is ‘desirable’ is to be judged objectively having regard to all the circumstances of the case...whether the recognition of a late election is ‘something worthy of achievement’ must depend on what is fair in order to do justice between the person who seeks to make the late election and the requirements of the administration of the fund (Boardman at 247).

  1. Observations on whether extension of time principles apply --

(1) Wilcox J referred to the general principles on which time for the performance of an act may be extended but His Honour did not limit the factors which might arise from the context in which a particular statutory discretion to extend time arises (Chalk at 455).

(2) It is understandable, indeed inevitable, that a decision-maker considering an application under s 157(1) will have close regard to the assistance which decision such as Hunter Valley Developments and A’hern offer (Boardman at 247 and 248).

(3) There is a public interest in enforcing procedural time limits which extends beyond the immediate parties (Boardman at 248).

(4) The time limit specified in the statute gives a prima facie rule as to when the application should have been made. Before an exercise of discretion in the applicant’s favour can occur, it must be made to appear that it is fair and just in all the circumstances to extend time. One of the relevant considerations will be whether there was a reasonable explanation for the delay, although the absence of a reasonable explanation is not necessarily fatal: Comcare v A’hern at 88. There is therefore an evidentiary burden on the applicant to place information before the decision-maker sufficient to satisfy the decision-maker that in all the circumstances of the case, it is desirable that the late election should be recognised (Boardman at 249).

(5) The time limit has its purpose, but the Act recognises that there will be deserving cases where fairness to the applicant requires that the time limit be relaxed. In such cases it is the policy of the Act, and the legal right of the applicant, to a favourable exercise of discretion under s 157(1). If the proper application of s 157(1) to individual cases, results in many people obtaining recognition of late election, so be it, that is the law (Boardman at 252).

  1. Application of extension of time principles may not always give the correct answer --

(1) Most provision which authorise an extension of time are instances of beneficial legislation which, accordingly should be applied beneficially (Chalk at 455).

(2) In the exercise of the discretion under s 157 it may be more important to consider the consequences of extending or refusing to extend time, than to debate the reasons why the act was not done in time (Chalk at 456).

(3) Extension of a procedural time limit gives a party an opportunity of establishing rights but to recognise a belated election immediately confers rights. However in each case the question is whether it is fair and just that the party who is out of time should by the exercise of the discretion be extended the rights which would have been enjoyed had the act in question been done within time (Boardman at 248).

  1. The existence or non existence of fault on the part of the respondent or its agent will have little bearing on desirability --

(1) The operation of s 157 is not dependent upon the existence of fault on the part of officers of the employer or of the scheme, although matters such as lack of advice may well be relevant (Chalk at 455).

(2) Whether or not there is a fiduciary duty by the Commissioner, is not relevant. The conduct of the Commissioner is only relevant to the exercise of the discretion to treat the election as within time under s 157 if it helps to explain why the appellant did or did not make a timely decision in a particular way if the conduct of the appellant is itself a relevant consideration (per Cooper J in Chalk at 458).

(3) Section 157 does not require that the Commissioner determine whether there exists attributable fault on the part of the appellant or any other person giving rise to the need to extend time; nor that the discretion be exercised on the basis of the existence or absence of attributable fault...the section merely requires that the Commissioner ‘be satisfied that in all the circumstances it is desirable that the election should be recognised’ (per Cooper J in Chalk at 458).

(4) It is unnecessary to decide whether the Commissioner was under a legal or equitable duty to provide to contributors to the scheme information as to choices available to them under it. The relevant fact may be that the commissioner did not provide the information (per Cooper J in Chalk at 459).

  1. What obligation is there on an employee to make enquiries?

(1) In the application of s 157 it may be of little consequence that the employee did not enquire as to his or her right to elect under s 137 or even that the employee positively decided not to elect. Section 157 confers a discretion which operates in either case (Chalk at 456).

(2) Wherever it is necessary to have regard to factual circumstances as they existed at a date in the past, delay in making the application which initiates that consideration is likely to add to the difficulties of ascertaining those circumstances. This difficulty is likely to be a consideration in almost every application to extend time no matter what the context (Boardman at 249).

(3) Depending on the circumstances, the decision-maker might proceed on the footing that there is no reasonable explanation for delay beyond the point when lines of enquiry so suggested would have revealed to the applicant the right to apply for an extension. But where the evidence establishes that the suggested lines of enquiry would have produced no useful information, so as to leave the applicant in the same state of ignorance or misinformation, the absence of enquiry becomes irrelevant (Boardman at 251).

  1. Does delay of the applicant create prejudice to the respondent?

(1) Frequently the discretion is exercised favourably because the facts which the employee took into account when he or she decided not to elect, subsequently turned out differently. Often times the discretion is favourably exercised when the employee has encountered health problems of which he or she was unaware at the time of retirement. One of the objects of s 157 is to alleviate the consequences of such a change in circumstances (Chalk at 456).

(2) It is difficult to see prejudice to the fund in a late election. A favourable exercise of the discretion would extend to the respondent the benefit of additional years of service, but this liability would have arisen in any event had the election been exercised in time. To grant the respondent the benefit of years of service actually performed is consistent with the policy and objects of the Act (Boardman at 252).

(3) In saying that the issue before the Tribunal involved a ‘determination as to what is fair in the circumstances of this case, when the need of the applicant for the extension of time to be granted is weighed against the prejudice to the respondent if that extension is granted’ the Tribunal did not apply a wrong test (Boardman at 247).

  1. Should the decision-maker have regard to the totality of service?

(1) A decision-maker exercising the s 157 discretion could think it fair to the employer, to the employee, and to the scheme that the benefit should be calculated by reference to the totality of the employee’s service and to the totality of his contributions, including any sums which must be refunded (Chalk at 456).

  1. What significance should attach to the form of investment of the lump sum?

(1) Where the accumulated contributions were immediately invested in a business enterprise such a use of the money would in itself often indicate that a deliberate decision had been made to reject the option of preservation in favour of receiving the immediate benefit of the accumulated contributions (Boardman at 250).”

  1. The applicant has only worked for one employer, previously the Postmaster General’s Department, then Telecom. He has been a member of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme all his working life. The exercise of the discretion he seeks would restore to him substantially what he could have expected when he joined this employer as a trainee upon leaving school. In Chalk at 456 Davies J notes in dicta, that:

“The Superannuation Scheme operates in the context of Commonwealth employment. The rights which the Act confers are statutory rights. But the entitlement to participate in the Scheme and to receive the benefits of the Scheme do not merely have a statutory base. They form an incident of the employment. Thus, a decision-maker exercising the s 157 discretion could think it fair to the employer, to the employee, and the Scheme that the benefit should be calculated by reference to the totality of the employee’s service and to the totality of his contributions, including any sums which must be refunded.”

  1. In the circumstances of this case the majority of the Tribunal, upon due consideration, finds that the applicant was not in full knowledge of the implications of the preservation option at the time of his resignation; that there was not in place at that time a reasonable procedure that would ensure that he became aware of potential benefits of preservation, and that he did take responsible action to seek preservation as soon as he became aware that it could be beneficial to him and there was a procedure available to him. Although the respondent put to the Tribunal that rights to preservation were set out in the annual information statement of the Superannuation Fund Investment Trust, implying that the applicant would have thus been placed on notice, the Tribunal notes that there was no reference therein to the possibility of retrospectively seeking to have those rights restored.
  2. The majority of the Tribunal finds that the applicant acted reasonably and responsibly at the time of his resignation even to the extent of paying, from funds then available to him, in advance for the period he intended to be overseas, his commitment to the maintenance of his young daughter. While it is not possible to say what he may or may not have done if he had known about the preservation option, we find that in the circumstances it is not unreasonable to accept his explanation of delay in seeking preservation.
  3. As the Tribunal has concluded that it is desirable in all the circumstances that the applicants’ request for a late election be recognised, the decision under review will be set aside and the matter will be remitted to the respondent with the direction that the election made by the respondent on 14 June 1990 under subsection 137(1) be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by the subsection of the Act.

REASONS OF MINORITY FOR DECISION



17 March 1995 Mr C. G. Woodard, Member



  1. I am conscious that the circumstances in this matter happen to be particularly those to which E(2) in Re Ward cited in the majority judgment applies. I do not arrive lightly at a dissenting view from that majority judgment, and further at one which would deprive a long-serving Commonwealth employee of benefit under beneficial legislation (which is now taking on an added dimension of communitarian or national benefit as well as individual). Nevertheless, my perception of the facts and the inferences which I draw from them differ from those of my colleagues in material particulars.
  2. The applicant has worked as a technician for Telecom and its predecessor for all or almost the whole of his working life. On 5 July 1978, after some 17 years’ service, he resigned, to take an overseas holiday. When he resigned he had no intention of returning to Telecom. This is common ground.
  3. It is also clear that in 1978 Telecom was not proactive in its approach to staff superannuation. This was no doubt widely true in the 1970s. Telecom’s personnel administration and training courses were shaped to be reactive to employees’ demands and the work-load. The recollection of a witness for the respondent, Mr Herbstreit, is that in eight years in the 1970s and 1980s only two staff in Victoria opted to preserve their benefits, out of possibly over 6000 who resigned. Telecom technicians enjoyed mobility in the work-place and a significant number resigned annually, including for the purpose of joining the private sector. Superannuation seems to have preoccupied Telecom staff to much the same degree as Serjeant Sullivan sardonically observed that the doctrine of mens rea was discussed amongst the denizens of the peat bogs of Ireland. Nevertheless there were mechanisms in place to react to enquiries, though primarily the task of administration in handling superannuation was to process applications on resignation and retirement expeditiously. These mechanisms were readily available to the applicant. He is not one of that class of applicants who worked alone or in isolated areas. He worked in the central business district of Melbourne. He had access to a clerical assistant who in turn worked to the staffing officer, at a different location, who was responsible for answering employees’ questions about superannuation as well as processing papers in connection with superannuation. The clerical officer knew he should direct employees with enquiries about superannuation to the staffing officer. I find myself in disagreement with the majority opinion that “there was not in place at that time a reasonable procedure that would ensure that he (Mr Gibbins) became aware of potential benefits of preservation”. I base my different view on the fact that there was at that time a readily available mechanism for reference on superannuation which in the circumstances then obtaining in Telecom could be considered to be reasonable. There were good reasons why the overwhelming majority of those resigning from Telecom at that time would not be interested in preservation.
  4. Did the applicant fall into that category, or can he be classified as more likely to fall into the minority (no doubt potentially larger than the two in eight years who actually opted for preservation) who would have exercised their rights to preservation if they had been in the position to give the option mature consideration? The applicant said that he just could not say how he would have decided. My colleagues conclude that “it is not possible to say what he may or may not have done if he had known about the preservation option”, and it is asking too much on the limited and conflicting facts available, and after the effluxion of time, to be any more specific.
  5. The applicant was not one of that category of Telecom employees who resigned to go into business and applied his superannuation for that purpose. It went into a bank account into which rent was paid and from which mortgage payments less than the rent were paid out. No doubt he would have drawn on these funds had he needed to, e.g. in the event of ill-health or had he substantially extended his ten month holiday. But he did not have to do so. There were no financial reasons why he should not have opted to preserve his benefit, had he been aware of the option.
  6. The applicant by payment in advance arranged to meet his maintenance obligations while overseas. Generally speaking, however, the applicant did not give the impression of a man who plans carefully. There is no evidence that he had a “commitment to superannuation” or a “saving strategy” similar to the various steps taken by the applicant in Re Ward.
  7. It was by chance that the applicant resumed working in Telecom, after meeting a friend, and while he was looking “in the employment pages to see what was available in the technical side, and there was nothing at the stage”. He had been working for Telecom for a further eleven years before he became aware of, and then acted reasonably expeditiously, on preservation.
  8. Taking into account the public interest in enforcing time limits, I do not consider that the applicant has met the

“. . .evidentiary burden to place information before the decision-maker sufficient to satisfy the decision-maker that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the late election should be recognised (Boardman at 249).”
