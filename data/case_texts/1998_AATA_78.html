Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

DECISION

CATCHWORDS

SALES TAX - application for refund - whether catalogues exempt - whether donated to schools - whether established and maintained principally for the advancement of agriculture - whether dairy products agriculture

Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1992 Item 193, Item 104 of Schedule 1

McPhail v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1968] HCA 13; (1968) 15 ATD 16

Leary v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 80 ATC 4438

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Coppleson 81 ATC 4550

The Producers Co-operative Distributing Society v Commissioner of Taxation (1944)

[1944] HCA 39; 69 CLR 523, (1947) 75 CLR 134

G Gramp and Sons Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 53; (1965) 115 CLR 170

Proserpine Co-operative Sugar Milling Association v Federal Commissioner of Taxation

95 ATC 431, 96 ATC 5016

REASONS FOR DECISION

12 February 1998 Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member



  1. This is an application to review a decision of the respondent to disallow an objection against the disallowance of a sales tax refund claim in respect to the period 23 February 1993 to 23 February 1996. The applicant, Australian Dairy Corporation (“ADC”), made application for refund of sales tax paid on catalogues, posters and stickers. The application was made on the basis that certain catalogues, posters and stickers were exempt under Item 193(1) of Schedule 1 of the Sales Tax (Exemption and Classification) Act 1992 (“the Act”). Prior to and at the hearing, the applicant sought leave to add to the grounds of objection the ground that, alternatively, the goods were exempt under Item 104 of Schedule 1 of the Act. As the respondent had no objection, the Tribunal ordered, pursuant to section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953, that the applicant was not limited to the grounds stated in the objection to which the decision relates but may add the further ground sought.
  2. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by Mr P. Senserrick, an accountant, and the respondent by Ms A. Richards of counsel. Evidence was given by Mr Langdon, Accounting Manager of ADC.
  3. The major part of the claim was for a proportion of the sales tax paid on the purchase of catalogues distributed to primary and secondary schools. The catalogues were entitled “Health and Nutrition Resources Catalogue” with separate publications for primary school teachers and secondary school teachers. The 1995 and 1996 catalogues were glossy colour printed booklets of 14 pages plus order forms. There was said to have been no substantial change from those of earlier years. The catalogues listed and described booklets, leaflets, posters, computer discs and videos for sale by ADC. These products in general related to the production of dairy foods, dietary information or health issues in which dairy foods may play a part. The sales tax said to be applicable to the catalogues distributed was $19,735. A further refund claim of $848.05 related to posters and stickers promoting “Healthy Bones Week”.
  4. Item 193 of Schedule 1 of the Act provides an exemption in the following terms:
“Item 193:

(1) Goods for use by a person:
(a) for donation to an always-exempt person or the government of a foreign country; or

(b) for lending free of charge to an always-exempt person, or to the government of a foreign country, for the whole of the statutory period.
(2) Subitem (1) does not apply:
(a) in any case –– unless the goods will be assessable goods when donated or lent; and

(b) in the case of a donation or loan to the government of a foreign country –– if the goods will be used by:
  • (i) the country’s diplomatic mission in Australia; or
  • (ii) a consular post in Australia of the country; or
  • (iii) a Trade Commissioner in Australia of the country.”

“An always exempt person” is defined in section 5 of the Sales Tax Assessment Act 1992 (“the Assessment Act”) to mean “a person whose use of goods of whatever kind is always covered by an exemption Item, regardless of the way in which the goods are used by the person”. Item 109 of Schedule 1 of the Act exempts “goods for use by a university, or school, that is conducted by a non-profit body”. The situations in which a person is entitled to claim credit for sales tax are set out in Table 3 of Schedule 1 of the Assessment Act. Credit Ground No. CR5A applies where “Claimant has borne tax on goods and has applied the goods to own use while still assessable goods. The AOU satisfies exemption Item 192, 193 or 194.” The definition of application to own use (“AOU”) in section 5 of the Assessment Act includes:

“(b) giving the goods away, or transferring property in the goods under a contract that is not a contract of sale.”
  1. There appeared to be no dispute that the goods were applied to the ADC own use when distributed to the schools. Apart from the clear words of the definition, decisions under the former legislation such as Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Taubmans [1966] HCA 18; (1966) 115 CLR 570 and Max Factor & Co Inc v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1971] HCA 36; (1971) 124 CLR 353 demonstrate that the free provision of catalogues to promote the sale of a taxpayer’s products are an application for own use. There was no dispute that the schools who received the catalogues satisfied the definition of always exempt person. The key issue was whether the catalogues were used by ADC for “donation” to the schools.
  2. For the respondent it was submitted that the provision of catalogues could not be considered a “donation”. It was said that the purpose of the catalogues was to promote the ADC’s products and their use was a business purpose of the ADC and served that end. For the applicant, it was contended that the question of whether the ADC derived any material benefit from the free provision of the catalogues was irrelevant. It was said to be sufficient that they were provided without charge for the use of schools. Mr Senserrick instanced the gift of a motor vehicle for a charity raffle which provided the donor with a benefit by way of advertising but was, nevertheless, clearly a donation. He argued that the ADC was established to promote the marketing of dairy produce. It did not itself sell dairy products and the catalogues were not for the purpose of selling such products.
  3. The word “donation” as used in Item 193 is not used in any technical sense and its meaning is that which is understood in ordinary parlance. The Oxford English Dictionary 2nd Edition defines “donation” as: “The action or facility of giving or presenting; presentation, bestowal, grant.” The New Shorter Oxford Dictionary provides the meaning as:
“1. The action or an act of donating, the transfer of ownership as a free gift.
  1. The right of bestowing or conferring a benefice; gift.”

In The Macquarie Dictionary 2nd edition, “donation” is defined as:

“1. the act of presenting something as a gift.
  1. a gift, as to a fund, a contribution.”

In McPhail v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1968] HCA 13; (1968) 15 ATD 16, which involved a contribution to a school building fund and a consequent reduction in school fees, Owen J. (at page 20) stated:

“But it is, I think, clear that to constitute a ‘gift’, it must appear that the property transferred was transferred voluntarily and not as the result of a contractual obligation to transfer it and that no advantage of a material character was received by the transferor by way of return.”

In Leary v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 80 ATC 4438 involving a purported gift to the Order of St John, all members of the Full Court of the Federal Court were of the opinion (inter alia) that benefaction was an essential element of a gift and this was lacking in that case. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Coppleson 81 ATC 4550 the Full Court of the Federal Court cited with approval the observations of Bowen C. J. in Leary’s case (supra) where he said (at page 4441):

“It ... seems relevant in applying para. 78(1)(A) to consider whether benefaction is intended towards and conferred upon the prescribed body when considering whether there has been a ‘gift’ within the meaning of the provision. Intention may generally be gathered best from objective circumstances.”

but their Honours went on to say (at pages 4551 and 4552):

“...Thus, it will frequently not be benefaction alone which motivates such a gift. While the judgments in Leary’s case emphasis the need for there to be a gift according to ordinary understanding, proceeding from motives of benefaction, they do not require this to be the sole motive.”

The analogy of a gift of a motor vehicle for a charity raffle used by Mr Senserrick is an example where benefaction is not the sole motive. However, in that instance, the element of benefaction exists with a material and real benefit being derived by the recipient charity. In this case, I have great difficulty in seeing that the schools which received the ADC catalogues would consider themselves as being the recipients of any benefaction from the ADC. The catalogues were provided in the same way as any vendor of products, information or services will provide a catalogue setting out what is available for purchase and the price. The ADC was pursuing its objective stated in section 6 of the Dairy Produce Act 1986 “to enhance the profitable production and marketing of Australian dairy produce”. At page 28 of the 1996 Annual Report of the ADC, where the Corporation outlined its achievements and under the heading “Schools” it stated:

“ADC materials continued to be developed for and offered to schools around the nation. A major new primary school resource, in the form of a video, teacher guide, activity sheets and promotional items was developed and issued during the year. Called, Rattle Chatter and Chew, it was based on the formal National Curriculum and was offered to all primary schools nationally through a direct marketing campaign.”

It is clear that the provision of catalogues was an integral part of that marketing campaign. I am unable to see that the catalogues themselves constituted a school resource. The booklets, videos, etc advertised in the catalogues may well have constituted a valuable resource for schools but the catalogues themselves were simply a marketing tool for use for the benefit of the ADC to enable sales of these materials.

  1. I am unable to see that the provision of the subject catalogues can in any ordinary understanding of the word be regarded as a “donation”. There was no element of benefaction to the recipient schools. Whilst they were provided free of charge, the only value in them resided with the ADC and were provided by way of an advertisement for the material available for purchase from the ADC. As such I find that the goods in question were not exempt under Item 193.
  2. The alternative ground relied upon by the applicant was Item 104 of Schedule 1 of the Act. This Item exempts:
“Printed matter for use by a non-profit body established and maintained principally for the advancement of agriculture. In the Item, ‘printed matter’ includes printed award ribbons and printed badges made of cloth or felt.”

Section 3(2) of the Act defines “agriculture” in the following terms:

“‘agriculture’ includes:

(a) viticulture, horticulture, pasturage, apiculture, poultry farming and dairy farming;

(b) other operations connected with the cultivation of the soil, the gathering in of crops and the rearing of livestock; ”

It was submitted for the ADC that it is a body established and maintained for the advancement of agriculture. It was said that the Corporation promotes the sale and consumption of dairy products which advances the interests of dairy farmers. The survival of that segment of the agricultural industry was said to rely on the maintenance and increase of consumption of products emanating from dairy farming. It was argued that the use of the word “advancement” in the Item connotes a wider activity than one related solely to within farm production. Mr Senserrick maintained that this exemption Item has been applied in the case of the Royal Agricultural Society to the awards, ribbons, certificates, etc issued at its annual show to producers of processed products such as cheese, cakes, butter, yoghurt, etc. He argued that the ADC did not sell products but promoted the sale and consumption of products arising from agricultural activities and, in so doing, had the purpose of advancing agriculture.

  1. For the respondent, it was submitted that the ADC was established to enhance the profitable production and marketing of Australian dairy produce which is not agriculture, agricultural produce or an agricultural pursuit. Reliance was placed on the decision in The Producers Co-operative Distributing Society Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1944] HCA 39; (1944) 69 CLR 523 which was concerned with the question of whether the principal business of the appellant was the manufacture, treatment or disposal of the agricultural products or livestock of its members. The business of the Society was the sale on commission of butter, bacon, cheese, honey, eggs, poultry, fruit and vegetables and similar commodities. Latham J. said (at page 529):
“...If a farmer grazes cows, produces milk and makes butter, there is, I should think, no doubt that he is engaged in a rural industry within the meaning of the Act, because he is plainly using land for a dairying purpose. The evidence, however, shows that to-day the making of butter has become a factory process, separated from the farm. Formerly the farmer made butter on the farm, but now the farmer milks his cows, separates the cream and disposes of the cream to a butter factory, either co-operative or proprietary. In the present case the cream which becomes the butter which the appellant company sells is sold to the co-operative societies which conduct factories. Those factories manufacture the cream into butter. It cannot be said that the factories cultivate or use land for a dairying purpose, because they do not cultivate or use land for that purpose. The butter which a factory produces is not the product of the land which the factory occupies. The factory is not, in my opinion, engaged in a rural industry within the meaning of section 5 of the Co-operation Act. If this be so, the butter made by the factory is not ‘a product of any rural industry’ and therefore it is not an agricultural product as defined in section 5.”

On appeal [1947] UKPCHCA 2; (1947) 75 CLR 134, the Privy Council said (at page 139):

“There are two industries, the farming industry and the butter-making industry. The industries are indeed closely related, but they are independent and the product of the latter industry is not in any real sense the product of the former industry. The appellant’s first contention in their Lordships’ opinion therefore fails.”
  1. The ADC came into existence by virtue of section 4 of the Dairy Produce Act 1924. Section 5 of the Dairy Produce Act 1986 continued its existence as a body corporate, under and subject to that Act, under the same name. The objective of the ADC is set out in section 6 (see paragraph 7 above). Section 7 sets out the functions of the ADC as:
“(a) to improve the marketability of Australian dairy produce;

(b) to promote the consumption of Australian dairy produce, both in Australia and foreign counties;

(c) to assist in the marketing of Australian dairy produce for export and for trade and commerce among the States, between States and Territories and within the Territories;

(d) to control the export from Australia of dairy produce;

(e) to control the marketing of dairy produce that has been exported from Australia;

(f) to advise the Minister on matter relating to the marketing of dairy produce including matters related to the export from Australia of dairy produce;

(g) to provide assistance by way of loans to manufacturers of dairy produce in Australia;

(h) to deal in dairy produce; and

(j) such other functions as are conferred on the Corporation by or under an Act.”

Section 3 defines “dairy produce” in the following terms:

“‘dairy produce’ means:

(a) milk;

(b) cream; and

(c) such other products made from or containing milk or a constituent part of milk as are declared by the Corporation by notice published in the Gazette to be dairy produce for the purposes of this Act;

...”

The question to be dealt with in this case is whether the objective and functions of the ADC are for the advancement of agriculture.

  1. The definition of agriculture in the Act is an inclusive definition to ensure that certain operations which may not be otherwise regarded as agriculture are included. The New Shorter Oxford Dictionary defines “agriculture” to mean: “the science or practice of cultivating the soil and rearing animals; farming.” Other dictionary meanings also make it clear that, in ordinary parlance, agriculture is limited to the cultivation of land, raising of crops, rearing of animals, etc. Decisions of the Courts and these generally understood meanings of the word, have led to the acceptance that agriculture, in the colloquial sense, stops at the farm gate. Decisions such as those in The Producers Co-operative Distributing Society (supra), G Gramp & Sons Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 53; (1965) 115 CLR 170 and Proserpine Co-operative Sugar Milling Association v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 95 ATC 431 and 96 ATC 5016 distinguished the processing of agricultural products from the production of such products as being separate and distinct industries.
  2. It is clear that a major part of the ADC’s activities involve the promotion of sale and consumption of processed dairy products including ice-cream, yoghurt, butter, milk powder etc. These are not agricultural products. It was put by Ms Richards that milk as purchased and consumed is equally no longer an agricultural product but a processed product from a milk factory. Mr Senserrick did not seek to argue that the promotion by the ADC was of agricultural products but that the promotion of dairy products advanced agriculture. One difficulty with this argument is the delineation of a cut-off point. For example, would an organisation promoting sale and consumption of beer, or cigarettes, or flour, or meat pies, or cotton clothing be regarded as advancing agriculture because the raw materials in the products are produced by agricultural activities. In my view, the ADC was established and maintained principally for the advancement of dairy products, the bulk, if not all, of which are manufactured or processed products. Whilst this promotion may indirectly advance agriculture, that is not the principal purpose of the ADC.
  3. Mr Senserrick sought to equate the ADC with the Royal Agricultural Society which was said to have been granted exemption under Item 104. No evidence of the objectives or activities of that body was provided. It may well be that the principal purpose of that body is the promotion of agriculture with a minor activity related to products processed from agricultural products and that the requirements of Item 104 are satisfied. It may be possible that the exemption should not have been accepted. I am in no position to judge and it is not to the point whether another body qualifies or does not qualify for the exemption. The sole issue here is whether the ADC satisfies the requirements of Item 104. In my view it does not.
  4. It follows that the decision under review should be affirmed.
