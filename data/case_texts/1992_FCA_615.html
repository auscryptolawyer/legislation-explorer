ORDER

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

1. In respect of each of the notices of referral in these matters, the Commissioner of Taxation's decision on the objection under review be set aside, with costs.

2. In lieu thereof, in each of those matters, there be substituted a decision allowing the taxpayer's objection.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

INTRODUCTION

BEAUMONT J. Pursuant to s.187(b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"), Citibank Limited requested the Commissioner of Taxation to refer to the Court the Commissioner's decision to disallow the taxpayer's objection to an income tax assessment for the year of income ended 31 December 1988. In its objection, the taxpayer claimed that the Commissioner's amended assessment dated 7 March 1990 should be further amended by excluding from the taxpayer's taxable income the amount of $15,001,316 assessed by the Commissioner as "net leasing income".

2. Similar objections relating to four earlier years of income involving companies in the Citibank group were disallowed by the Commissioner:

Year Company Amount involved

1987 Citibank Limited $16,446,588

1986 Citibank Limited 6,471,108

1987 Citicorp Wholesale Pty. Limited 873,861

1986 Citicorp Finance Pty. Limited 1,292,131

3. Oral argument and written submissions covered all five referrals to this Court. This judgment will focus on Citibank Limited's objection to the 1988 assessment, as a representative year. However, as the issues and reasoning relevant to that year and company are applicable to the other years and companies, they should be taken as applying mutatis mutandis to those referrals.

THE TAXPAYER'S CONTENTIONS

4. In its statement of facts, issues and contentions, the taxpayer argued in essence:

(1) The principal business of the taxpayer, a trading bank, was the borrowing and lending of money. In the course of this business in 1988, the taxpayer purchased certain chattels, namely 772 "prestige" motor vehicles ("the vehicles") for the sole purpose of leasing them to its customers ("the leases").

(2) The value of the vehicles exceeded the amount ascertained in accordance with the provisions of s.57AF of the Act. (Section 57AF, inserted by Act No. 57 of 1980, imposes limits on the cost price of motor vehicles for the purpose of calculating depreciation allowable under the Act. Provision is made for the ascertainment of an "indexation factor". In the income years 1986/7, 1987/8 and 1988/9, the limits were $29,646, $34,775 and $39,331 respectively).

(3) The leases were "finance leases" within two official standards, namely: (a) Australian Accounting Standard 17 ("AAS 17") prepared by the Accounting Standards Board and the Public Sector Accounting Standards Board of the Australian Accounting Research Foundation and issued by the National Councils of the Institute of Chartered Accountants in Australia and the Australian Society of Accountants; and (b) Approved Accounting Standard ASRB 1008, "Accounting for Leases", approved by the Accounting Standards Review Board for the purposes of Part VI of the Companies Code. The taxpayer employed the method of accounting for the leases described in AAS 17 and ASRB 1008.

(4) The taxpayer did not, in calculating its profit or taxable income, depreciate the vehicles. Specifically, it did not seek to apply s.54, and related provisions of the Act dealing with depreciation, to the vehicles.

(5) The Commissioner made an assessment in accordance with his ruling "IT 2105".

(6) The sum assessed was not made income, or assessable income, of the taxpayer under s.57AF, s.25, or any other provision of the Act.

THE COMMISSIONER'S CONTENTIONS

5. In its essence, the Commissioner's statement of facts, issues and contentions was as follows:

(1) The Commissioner accepts that the taxpayer entered into the leases, as a banker, for the purpose contended for by the taxpayer.

(2) Legal title to the vehicles remained with the taxpayer throughout the currency of the leases. The lessees were obliged to pay rental to the taxpayer in accordance with the leases. Further, the leases expressly did not confer on the lessees any option to purchase the vehicles at their expiration.

(3) The leases were "finance leases" within AAS 17 and the taxpayer employed the method there specified in accounting for the leases.

(4) It is common ground that the amount added to the taxpayer's taxable income by the Commissioner was calculated as the difference between: (a) the amount of gross rentals (hire or lease fees) received or receivable in respect of the leases; and, (b) the deductions properly allowable to the taxpayer for depreciation of the vehicles in accordance with ss.54 - 62 of the Act.

(5) The amount of the gross rentals in 4(a), above, was income within s.25(1) or some other provision of the Act; and it was inappropriate, as the taxpayer had purported to do, to bring to account as revenue only an amount calculated in accordance with para. 83 of AAS 17. (Para. 83 of AAS 17 states that the finance revenue attributable to a "direct financing lease" should be brought to account progressively by the lessor, in its profit and loss or other operating statement, over the lease term and in accordance with the actuarial method, so as to achieve a constant periodic rate of return on the investment in the lease outstanding at the beginning of each lease payment period.)

THE TAXPAYER'S SUPPLEMENTARY CONTENTION

6. The taxpayer further contended that, because the Commissioner had issued an assessment on the basis that an item constituted assessable income, it was not open on appeal for the Commissioner to justify the inclusion of that item by, instead, increasing both assessable income for the year and allowable deductions in order to get the same result.

THE ACCOUNTING STANDARDS

7. Reference should be made to the relevant details of the accounting standards mentioned.

(a) Accounting Standard AAS 17

8. The objective of this standard, issued in March 1984, and re-issued in June 1987, is to ensure that reporting entities disclose sufficient information regarding lease transactions to enable users of financial statements to assess the effect of such transactions upon those statements where such entities act as lessees and/or lessors. AAS 17 sets standards for both lessees and lessors, regarding the accounting for and disclosure of leases involving land and/or depreciable assets.

9. The standard requires leases to be classified as either "operating" or "finance". The basic distinction underlying this classification is that where substantially all the risks and benefits incidental to ownership of the property effectively remain with the lessor, the lease is an operating lease; however, where the risks and benefits effectively pass to the lessee, a finance lease exists. Risks of property ownership include those associated with unsatisfactory performance, obsolescence, idle capacity, losses in realisable value and uninsured damage or condemnation of the property; benefits include those obtainable from the use of the property and gains in realisable value.

10. As a guideline, the effective passing, from lessor to lessee, of substantially all of the risks and benefits incidental to ownership could normally be assumed where:

(a) the lease is non-cancellable; and

(b) either of the following tests is met:

(i) the lease covers 75% or more of the useful life of the

property; or

(ii) the present value, at the beginning of the lease term, of

the minimum lease payments equals or exceeds 90% of the fair

value of the property to the lessor at the inception of the

lease. (The discount rate to be used in calculating the

present value being the interest rate "implicit" in the

lease.)

11. Leases classified as finance leases by lessors are further sub-classified into "direct financing", "sales-type" or "leveraged" leases, and the accounting treatment of each is different. The taxpayer contends that we are concerned here with direct financing leases. (A sales-type lease is a finance lease where the fair value of the property at the inception of the lease differs from its cost (or carrying amount) to the lessor.)

12. A direct finance lease establishes the lessor's right to receive a series of payments from the lessee in return for effectively conveying to the lessee substantially all the risks and benefits incidental to ownership of the property. Accordingly, the lessor records an asset equal to its investment in the lease as at the beginning of the lease term, and apportions minimum payments received over the lease term between reduction in the investment and recognition of finance revenue. The lessor's investment in the lease, which is essentially a receivable, may be recorded as the total of the present value of the minimum lease payments receivable and the present value of the unguaranteed residual value, if any, expected to accrue to the lessor at the end of the lease term. ("Minimum lease payments" are the rental payments over the term of the lease, including any bargain purchase option, premium and guaranteed residual value, but excluding any rental relating to reimbursement of executory costs to be met by the lessor and any contingent rentals.) The discount rate to be used in determining the present value is the interest rate implicit in the lease. The difference between (a) the sum of the total minimum lease payments receivable and the amount of any unguaranteed residual value expected to accrue to the benefit of the lessor, and (b) the fair value of the property at the inception of the lease, represents the finance revenue to be brought to account progressively over the term of the lease, with the interest component determinable on an actuarial basis.

13. Alternatively, the lessor's investment in the lease may be recorded on a "gross" basis. This involves recording a lease receivable as at the beginning of the term of the lease, equal to the total minimum lease payments receivable and the amount of any unguaranteed residual value expected to accrue to the benefit of the lessor. Unearned finance revenue is recorded separately and shown as a deduction from the lessor's gross investment in the lease. As each periodic rental falls due, its interest component is transferred from unearned revenue to revenue for the period. The periodic rental is accounted for as a reduction of the lease receivable.

14. Unguaranteed residual values are periodically to be re-assessed, to confirm that they remain realistic estimates of realisable value at the expiration of the lease.

15. Accounting standards are specified for the treatment of the lessor's investment in a direct finance lease in accordance with the provisions previously described.

16. As mentioned, para. 83 provides accounting standards for a lessor under a direct finance lease:

"The finance revenue attributable to the lease shall be

brought to account progressively by the lessor in the profit

and loss or other operating statement over the lease term in

accordance with the actuarial method so as to achieve a

constant periodic rate of return on the investment in the

lease outstanding at the beginning of each lease payment

period."

(b) Approved Accounting Standard ASRB 1008

17. As noted, ASRB 1008 was approved by the Accounting Standards Review Board ("ASRB") and its background is as follows.

18. The ASRB was established by the Ministerial Council. Section 266B of the Companies Act 1981 (inserted by s.79 of the Companies and Securities Legislation (Miscellaneous Amendments) Act 1983) empowered the ASRB to publish in the Commonwealth Gazette approved accounting standards for the purposes of Part VI of the Companies Act (see R.H. Parker, C.G. Peirson and A.L. Ramsay, "Australian Accounting Standards and the Law" (1987) 5 Company and Securities Law Journal 231 at 235-6; QBE Insurance Group Limited v Australian Securities Commission, (1992) 8 ACSR 631).

19. Section 269(8A) of the Companies Act required company directors to ensure that company accounts complied with such accounting standards. This requirement was relaxed where the accounts would not give a true and fair view of the company's state of affairs and profit and loss if prepared in accordance with the standards (s.269 (8B)). (This provision was repealed in 1991; now all financial statements must both comply with accounting standards and present a true and fair view.)

20. ASRB 1008 was notified in the Commonwealth Gazette dated 31 July 1986. It was replaced by another version gazetted on 20 November 1987, which applied to the first financial year of a company ending on or after January 1988.

21. Relevantly, the provisions of ASRB 1008 were similar to those of AAS 17 (see ASRB 1008, cl. .55 and cl. .56 and the commentary thereon).

THE COMMISSIONER'S RULINGS

22. IT 2105 and other rulings, or expressions of opinion by the Commissioner, were in evidence and should be mentioned at this stage.

(a) Ruling IT 2105

("Motor Vehicle Depreciation Limit - Lease finance companies -

Finance - Actuarial method of accounting for lease income")

23. This ruling first referred to s.57AF(2) of the Act, and to observations in the Explanatory Memorandum circulated with the Income Tax Assessment Amendment Bill (No. 2) 1980 introducing this provision.

24. (Section 57AF(2) is as follows:

"Where the cost of a unit of property to which this section

applies for the purpose of calculating the depreciation

allowable to a taxpayer under this Act in relation to a year

of income would, apart from this subsection, exceed the

motor vehicle depreciation limit in relation to the year of

income (in this subsection referred to as the 'year of first

use') in which the unit of property was first used by the

taxpayer...then, for the purpose of calculating the

depreciation allowable under this Act to the taxpayer in

relation to a year of income, the cost of that unit of

property shall be deemed to be the amount of the motor

vehicle depreciation limit in relation to the year of first

use."

25. The Explanatory Memorandum commented on s.57AF(2):

"The motor vehicle depreciation limit will apply for the

purpose of section 56(1)(b) of (the Act) in calculating

depreciation under the prime cost method. It will apply

also for the purposes of section 62(1) of (the Act) in

calculating the 'depreciated value' of property. The

'depreciated value' of property is used in the calculation

of depreciation under the diminishing value method of

depreciation and for the purpose of calculating any

balancing adjustments on disposal, loss or destruction of

the property in accordance with section 59.

The limit will apply in relation to all cars and station

wagons which are not 'excluded units of property' and in

respect of which depreciation is allowable. It will thus

apply to vehicles owned and let out on lease by finance

companies or other lessors. Where finance companies use a

financial or actuarial method of calculating the amount of

taxable income - in which case an allowance for depreciation

is reflected in the calculations - an appropriate adjustment

will be made in calculating the taxable income of the

finance company to reflect the depreciation cost limit that

applies to the vehicles concerned.")

26. The ruling then proceeded as follows:

"Ruling

3. The Explanatory Memorandum of Income Tax Assessment

Amendment Act (No. 2) 1980, the Act which introduced

sec.57AF into the Principal Act, states that the limit in

subsec. 57AF(2) will apply in relation to all cars and

station wagons which are not excluded units of property and

in respect of which depreciation is allowable. The

statement continues:

'It, sub-section 57AF(2), will thus apply to vehicles

owned and let out on lease by finance companies or

other lessors. Where finance companies use a finance

or actuarial method of calculating the amount of

taxable income - in which case an allowance for

depreciation is reflected in the calculations - an

appropriate adjustment will be made in calculating the

taxable income of the finance company to reflect the

depreciation cost limit that applies to the vehicles

concerned.'

4. The adjustment to reflect the depreciation limit

through the finance method is determined by use of the

formula -

(C - V) (C - X)

C where:

C = cost of the motor vehicle,

V = residual value under the lease agreement, and

X = the relevant depreciation limit applicable to

the vehicle in the hands of the lessor.

5. The amount resulting from the application of this

formula is to be included in assessable income over the

period of the lease to reflect the depreciation limit."

(b) Ruling IT 2162

("Income Tax: Basis of Assessment for income from Chattel Leasing

Transactions - Finance or actuarial method - Gross rents less

depreciation method")

27. On 20 May 1985, the Commissioner gave ruling 2162:

"Ruling

Head Office memorandum J146/569 of 28 August 1974 stated

that, for income tax purposes, finance and leasing companies

may be allowed to use the same method of accounting for

income from chattel leasing transactions as they use in

their published accounts. The method so used is known as

the financial or actuarial method. Returning income on this

basis was accepted in place of the traditional method, viz.,

gross rentals less deductions by way of depreciation plus

adjustment at the time of disposal of the leased property on

the understanding that income from all chattel lease

transactions entered into by the companies would be returned

on the new basis.

2. In more recent years a number of the companies who had

opted for the financial or actuarial method of returning

income from chattel leasing transactions sought to revert to

the traditional gross rentals less depreciation method.

This has been readily accepted on the understanding that it

would be applied to all chattel leasing transactions entered

into from the commencement of the year of change, and that

the companies would not in future seek to again vary the

basis of accounting for chattel leasing transactions unless

there is some variation in the income tax law at the time.

3. Some companies, however, have sought approval to return

income from a number of their chattel leasing transactions

by the rentals less depreciation method while returning

income from other transactions on the financial-actuarial

method. The option has been sought depending upon the tax

benefits that would apply to particular leases, e.g., taking

advantage of high depreciation rates. Approval to do so has

not been given.

4. Where a company or group seeks to avail itself of the

better of both methods by the device of placing

financial-actuarial transactions in a separate subsidiary while

maintaining the rentals less depreciation transactions in a

parent or another subsidiary, the group should be required

to return income from all chattel lease transactions entered

into by the various companies in the group on the same

basis. Generally this will be the rentals less depreciation

method."

(c) The opinions expressed in the Commissioner's letter dated 20th May 1988

28. By letter dated 20 May 1988, the Commissioner wrote to the taxpayer:

"INCOME TAX : MOTOR VEHICLE DEPRECIATION LIMIT

I refer to your letter of 15 February 1988 concerning the

calculation of taxable income under the finance method where

the cost of motor vehicles which are leased by your company

exceed the motor vehicle depreciation limit imposed under

section 57AF of the Income Tax Assessment Act.

The Commissioner acknowledges that for the purposes of

ascertaining an accounting profit for your company return on

funds are measured against cost of funds. Essentially

leasing companies which use either the finance method or the

asset method of accounting are concerned with their net

return on funds invested. For income tax purposes, the

overall profit under either method of accounting must (be)

the same.

Consequently, with the enactment of Section 57AF taxpayers

are required to adjust their deductions for depreciation or

returns on leases if there is any excess over the motor

vehicle depreciation limit set by the section. Should this

adjustment not be made by lessors which adopt the finance

method the law would operate to their advantage. That is,

lease payments charged by those lessors may be reduced to

reflect the non-compliance of section 57AF thus providing

them with a competitive edge over those lessors using the

asset method of calculating taxable income.

The long established view of this office has been that a

departure from the practice of not including the excess in a

finance lessor's assessable income over the term of the

lease may result in the determination of the lessor's income

by the strict application of the law. That is, taxable

income would be calculated under the asset method of

deducting depreciation as limited by section 57AF from gross

rentals.

I note Mr Wilmot's comments in his letter of 31 August 1987

to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation, Sydney that there is

no statutory authority for the Commissioner to make the

adjustment referred to in Taxation Ruling No. IT 2105.

Correspondingly, there is no statutory provision which

requires the Commissioner to accept the finance method of

accounting for lessors. Nevertheless the Commissioner is

prepared to accept the finance method for income tax

purposes to avoid the maintenance (of) dual accounting

systems by lessors. This acceptance has been on the basis

mentioned in the second paragraph above.

You are advised therefore that Citibank in adopting the

finance method of returning income for income tax purposes

must comply with Taxation Ruling No. IT 2105.

Finally, I have sent a copy of this letter to the Deputy

Commissioner of Taxation, Sydney to ensure that the section

57AF adjustment of $6,471,108 is made to your company's

income tax return for the year ended 31 December 1986."

THE PRIMARY ARGUMENT ADVANCED ON BEHALF OF THE TAXPAYER

41. The taxpayer submits that, unless the Act otherwise requires, an accounting treatment of the subject transactions is appropriate. Reliance is placed, for this purpose, upon reasoning in Scott v Commissioner of Taxation [1935] NSWStRp 9; (1935) 35 SR (NSW) 215, Commissioner of Taxes (S.A.) v Executor Trustee and Agency Co. of South Australia Ltd. (Carden's case) [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108 and Arthur Murray (N.S.W.) Pty. Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 58; (1965) 114 CLR 314.

42. In Scott, Jordan C.J., in considering whether a sum recovered as a statutory compensation for the abolition of a public office was income, said (at 219):

"The word 'income' is not a term of art, and what forms of

receipts are comprehended within it, and what principles are

to be applied to ascertain how much of those receipts ought

to be treated as income, must be determined in accordance

with the ordinary concepts and usages of mankind, except in

so far as the statute states or indicates an intention that

receipts which are not income in ordinary parlance are to be

treated as income, or that special rules are to be applied

for arriving at the taxable amount of such receipts."

43. Carden's case raised the question of whether, in ascertaining the income of a medical practitioner, it was appropriate to adopt an earnings, rather than a receipts, basis. Dixon J. said (at 152):

"Income, profits and gains are conceptions of the world of

affairs and particularly of business. They are conceptions

which cover an almost infinite variety of activities. It

may be said that every recurrent accrual of advantages

capable of expression in terms of money is susceptible of

inclusion under these conceptions. No single formula could

be devised which would effectually reduce to the just

expression of a net money sum the annual result of every

kind of pursuit or activity by which the members of a

community seek livelihood or wealth. But in nearly every

department of enterprise and employment the course of

affairs and the practice of business have developed methods

of estimating or computing in terms of money the result over

an interval of time produced by the operations of business,

by the work of the individual, or by the use of capital.

The practice of these methods of computation and the general

recognition of the principles upon which they proceed are

responsible in a great measure for the conceptions of

income, profit and gain and, therefore, may be said to enter

into the determination or definition of the subject which

the legislature has undertaken to tax. The courts have

always regarded the ascertainment of income as governed by

the principles recognized or followed in business and

commerce, unless the legislature has itself made some

specific provision affecting a particular matter or

question."

44. Dixon J. added (at 153) that in the ascertainment of taxable income, the tendency of judicial decision has been to place increasing reliance upon "the conceptions of business and the principles and practices of commercial accountancy."

45. Dixon J. went on to say, in a passage frequently relied on, (at 154):

"In the present case we are concerned with rival methods of

accounting directed to the same purpose, namely, the purpose

of ascertaining the true income. Unless in the statute

itself some definite direction is discoverable, I think that

the admissibility of the method which in fact has been

pursued must depend upon its actual appropriateness. In

other words, the inquiry should be whether in the

circumstances of the case it is calculated to give a

substantially correct reflex of the taxpayer's true income."

46. In Arthur Murray, the question was whether fees received for future tuition were assessable income in advance of the tuition. Barwick C.J., Kitto and Taylor JJ. said (at 318-9):

"The ultimate inquiry in either kind of case, of course,

must be whether that which has taken place, be it the

earning or the receipt, is enough by itself to satisfy the

general understanding among practical business people of

what constitutes a derivation of income. A conclusion as to

what that understanding is may be assisted by considering

standard accountancy methods, for they have been evolved in

the business community for the very purpose of reflecting

received opinions as to the sound view to take of particular

kinds of items.

...

"What ultimately matters is the concept; book-keeping

methods are but evidence of the concept.

...

It is true that in a case like the present the circumstances

of the receipt do not prevent the amount received from

becoming immediately the beneficial property of the company;

for the fact that it has been paid in advance is not enough

to affect it with any trust or charge, or to place any legal

impediment in the way of the recipient's dealing with it as

he will. But those circumstances nevertheless make it

surely necessary, as a matter of business good sense, that

the recipient should treat each amount of fees received but

not yet earned as subject to the contingency that the whole

or some part of it may have in effect to be paid back, even

if only as damages, should the agreed quid pro quo not be

rendered in due course. The possibility of having to make

such a payment back (we speak, of course, in practical

terms) is an inherent characteristic of the receipt itself.

In our opinion it would be out of accord with the realities

of the situation to hold, while the possibility remains,

that the amount received has the quality of income derived

by the company. For that reason it is not surprising to

find, as the parties in the present case agree is the fact,

that according to established accountancy and commercial

principles in the community the books of a business either

selling goods or providing services are so kept with respect

to amounts received in advance of the goods being sold or of

the services being provided that the amounts are not entered

to the credit of any revenue account until the sale takes

place or the services are rendered..."

47. Their Honours went on to say (at 320):

"The paragraph of the case stated in which the established

principles are described does not leave to inference why it

is that books are kept in this manner. It is there

specifically stated, as an agreed fact, that according to

established accounting and commercial principles, in the

case of a business either selling goods or supplying

services, amounts received in advance of the goods being

delivered or the services being supplied are not regarded as

income. We have not been able to see any reason which

should lead the courts to differ from accountants and

commercial men on the point. Neither, apparently, has the

Taxation Department seen any reason in principle, for we are

told that the Department was accustomed to take the view we

have expressed until an opinion grew up that to do so was in

some way inconsistent with the judgment of this Court in the

case of Federal Commissioner of Taxation v JAmes Flood Pty.

Ltd. ... The Court there held that, while commercial and

accountancy practice may assist in ascertaining the true

nature and incidence of an item as a step towards

determining whether the item answers the test laid down in

the Act for allowable deductions, it cannot be substituted

for the test. In so far as the Act lays down a test for the

inclusion of particular kinds of receipts in assessable

income it is likewise true that commercial and accountancy

practice cannot be substituted for the test. But the Act

lays down no test for such a case as the present. The word

'income', being used without relevant definition, is left to

be understood in the sense which it has in the vocabulary of

business affairs. To apply the concept which the word in

that sense expresses is not to substitute some other test

for the one prescribed in the Act; it is to give effect to

the Act as it stands."

CONCLUSIONS

63. I have concluded that the taxpayer's primary argument should be accepted for the reasons advanced on its behalf. In these circumstances, I need not deal with the taxpayer's supplementary contention. My reasons for accepting the taxpayer's primary argument are as follows:

(a) What was the nature of the relevant business activity of the taxpayer?

64. The material business activity of the taxpayer, as a bank, was to provide finance to clients through the medium, or vehicle of a leasing arrangement. In ordinary usage, the leases were, in truth, a "financing device" (to borrow Hutley JA's description in Austin v United Dominions Corp Ltd (1984) 2 NSWLR 612 at 614). This was so, regardless of how such dealings might be characterised for other special statutory purposes (cf. Turner v Morlend Finance Corp. (Vic.) Pty. Ltd. (1990) ASC 56-006).

(b) What was the appropriate accounting treatment, in the case of the taxpayer, of the subject transactions?

65. The unchallenged expert evidence indicated that the taxpayer was justified in treating the subject transactions, as it did, in accordance with the provisions of the accounting standards. That is to say, these dealings were "direct finance" leases within the meaning of AAS 17 and ASRB 1008 because, in reality, the transactions established the taxpayer's right to receive a series of payments from the lessees for effectively conveying to the lessee substantially all of the risks and benefits incident to ownership of the property. As the standards state, the difference between the sum of the total minimum lease payments receivable and the amount of any unguaranteed residual value expected to accrue to the benefit of the taxpayer, and the fair value of the property at the inception of the lease, represents the finance revenue to be brought to account progressively over the term of the lease, the interest component to be determined on an actuarial basis. In my opinion, especially given the absence of any expert evidence to the contrary, this was an appropriate accounting treatment of these transactions.

(c) Did the treatment of the transactions in accordance with the accounting standards disclose "income" as understood in ordinary usage?

66. In my opinion, the interest component, determined on the actuarial basis stated in the accounting standards, was "income" of the taxpayer within the meaning of s.25 of the Act. As Davies J. said in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Dunn (1989) 85 ALR 244 at 252 (a passage cited in Barratt v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1992) 107 ALR 385 at 388):

"Although ordinary accounting principles and practice are

not determinative of the issue, they are relevant and may be

influential..."

67. In the present context, it has been observed by one commentator that:

"...accounting rules cannot, per se, represent an

appropriate basis for taxing. However, so long as profit

remains the basis for assessing income tax then it is

administratively appropriate that tax rules and accounting

rules should maintain a degree of similarity."

(Chris N. Westworth, "Accounting Standards - A Framework for Tax Assessment" (1985) 2 Australian Tax Forum (at 243).

68. As Bowen C.J., Fisher and Lockhart JJ. have pointed out (in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v National Commercial Banking Corporation of Australia Ltd. [1983] FCA 302; (1983) 50 ALR 322 at 326):

"Receipts of interest, but not receipts on account of the

principal amount of a loan, are assessable income in the

case of a taxpayer carrying on the business of money

lending."

69. These observations are applicable in the present context. The relevant inquiry is to determine what, for our purposes, is the interest component.

70. It is true that, in discussing the "profit emerging" basis of assessment, Menzies J. said, in J. Rowe and Son Pty. Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1970] HCA 57; (1971) 124 CLR 421 at 451:

"...the fundamental scheme of the Act is inconsistent with

attempts to calculate the profit element in each transaction

undertaken by a taxpayer in the course of its business and

to aggregate those profits to arrive at taxable income..."

71. However, as one commentator has noted in this connection:

"Nevertheless, running parallel with these observations (and

to some extent inconsistent with them) are cases which,

independent of the operation of any specific provision of

the Act, require that only a profit is to be included in

assessable income. Where only a profit is income, the

individual receipts and expenses which were the basis of the

calculation of that profit, are not accounted for separately

and are not individually regarded as assessable income or

allowable deductions. In those cases which adopt it, the

calculation of profit as income proceeds almost without

notice. In Commercial and General Acceptance Ltd v FCT

Mason J. stated ((1977) 137 CLR 373 at 382-3):

The expression 'gross income' in relation to a

taxpayer conveys the sense of entire income of a

taxpayer. No doubt in the context of the Act that

income is to be ascertained in the first instance by

reference to the gross income receipts of the

taxpayer, but in my view it also includes a net amount

which is income according to the ordinary concepts and

usages of mankind, when the net amount alone has that

character, not being derived from gross receipts that

are revenue receipts.

This position can be found expressed in several distinct

lines of cases dealing with the activities of banks,

insurance companies, some investment companies, land

developers and with long-term construction projects. The

cases on exchange gains and losses, such as the CAGA case,

may also be examples of this idea."

(Graeme S. Cooper, "Tax Accounting for Deductions" (1985) 5 Australian Tax Forum 24 at 87-8.)

(d) Is this tax accounting treatment made impermissible by any express provision of the Act?

72. The Commissioner contends that, properly construed, the depreciation provisions of the Act (ss.52-64, particularly s.57AF) preclude the taxpayer adopting the tax accounting treatment described above. It is said, for the Commissioner, that as a matter of purposive construction, the taxpayer's primary argument should be rejected. The legislature, in enacting s.57AF, is said to have specifically sought to increase the tax burden on luxury motor cars by reducing the depreciation allowed in respect of those vehicles. The taxpayer, it is claimed, is seeking to "subvert" that legislative intention by an "accounting manoeuvre".

73. I have difficulty in accepting this contention. In my view, all that can be inferred from the legislation is that the true object of s.57AF is to limit a taxpayer's claim for depreciation in certain circumstances. But where, as here, the depreciation provisions themselves have no application at all, no question of subversion or frustration of the legislative purpose can arise. In my opinion, there is nothing in the provisions of the Act which would render inappropriate the tax accounting treatment provided for in the accounting standards which were adopted by the taxpayer. It follows that s.57AF(2) has no application here.

RELIEF

74. The decisions of the Commissioner on the objections under review should be set aside, with costs. In each case there will be substituted a decision allowing the objection.
