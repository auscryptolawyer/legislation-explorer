Allen and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 654 (30 September 1993)

Last Updated: 27 August 2008



Administrative Appeals Tribunal





DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



SUPERANNUATION - deferred benefit - election outside prescribed time -discretion to recognise election - whether discretion should be exercised - whether acceptable explanation for delay



Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) sections 136(1), 157(1)



Hunter Valley Development Pty Ltd v Minister for Home Affairs and Environment

58 ALR 305

REASONS FOR DECISION



Tribunal : Dr D J Grimes, Senior Member

Mr N J Attwood, Member

Dr D B Travers, Member



1. This is an application to review a decision of 4 December 1991, by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner"). The decision, made under sub-section 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"), affirmed on reconsideration a decision, dated 23 January 1990, that an election made by the applicant on 18 January 1989 under sub-section 137(1) should not be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that sub-section of the Act.



2. The benefit which the applicant seeks is that provided by s 136, entitled deferred benefits, but often referred to as preservation of superannuation rights. Sub-section 137(1) of the Act prescribes the period in which it was open to the applicant to make an election to preserve his superannuation rights; that being 21 days immediately after he ceased to be an eligible employee. The relevant parts of s137 are as follows:

"s137(1) A person who -



(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which paragraph (b) does not apply, and upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or



(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue of which the person is to be deemed, under subsection 58(3), to have retired involuntarily;



may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect by notice in writing to the Commissioner that this Division apply in relation to the person.



(2) If a person makes an election for the purposes of this section within 1 month before he ceases to be an eligible employee, the election has effect as if it had been made on the day after he ceased to be an eligible employee.



(3) Subject to subsection (4) an election under this section is of no effect unless the person who made the election gives notice in writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, after expiration of the period that is the prescribed period in relation to him -



(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of the prescribed period and, if so, the name and address of the employer; and



(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the prescribed period a member of the superannuation scheme applicable in relation to that employment and, if so, specifying the scheme concerned."



3. Section 157 sub-section (1) contains a discretionary power, vested in the Commissioner for Superannuation, to recognise an election made after the prescribed time in certain circumstances. These circumstances are as follows:

"s157(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly."

The Tribunal will now turn to consider the evidence, and decide whether, on the evidence before it, it is desirable that the discretion provided in S157(1) should be exercised. The issue for the Tribunal to consider is; whether it is desirable, in all the circumstances of the present case, pursuant to section 157(1) of the Act, that the applicant be permitted to elect for preservation of his superannuation rights, notwithstanding that the time prescribed by section 137(1) of the Act has passed?



4. At the hearing the applicant represented himself and the respondent was represented by Ms T Munoz. The Tribunal had before it documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and admitted into evidence exhibits tendered throughout the proceedings. The Tribunal heard evidence from the applicant and Mr G Cupitt.



5. The applicant was born on 20 November 1936, joined the public service as a junior clerk with the Australian Taxation Office in January 1953 and became a contributor to the Commonwealth superannuation scheme in December 1953. He completed an accountancy degree in 1962 and graduated in law in 1972.



6. The applicant resigned from Commonwealth Employment, and the Taxation Office, on 15 June 1979. At the time of resignation the applicant was an executive officer with the International Tax Branch, National Office Canberra. On resignation the applicant completed a form entitled "Application for Refund for Accumulated Contributions/Lump Sum Benefit or Election for Preservation of Superannuation Rights" (form S2A). It was his evidence that he opted for a refund of contributions as he was unaware at the time of his option to preserve his superannuation contributions. Around the time of the applicant's resignation he recalls having discussed the resignation with a staff clerk at the Taxation Office and has a clear recollection that the option of preservation was not mentioned. The applicant conceded that, at the time of resignation, he had no intention to return to the Commonwealth public service and he had expressed this to the staff clerk.



7. The applicant, in a letter to the Commissioner and dated 18 July 1979, inquired as to the whereabouts of the refund of his contributions and requested;

"As the cheque is required to help finance me buying into an accountancy practice I would appreciate your advice as to whether the cheque has yet issued and, if not, if its issue could be expedited".



In evidence to the Tribunal the applicant denied that he wanted the payment expedited for the reasons he had stated, as he had the money for the practice from the proceeds of the sale of his Canberra residence. Rather, he informed the Tribunal that he was at a loss as to why he wrote that and could only assume that his intention at the time was to expedite payment.



8. The applicant moved to Queensland after resigning in 1979 and obtained employment with a private accountancy practice. When he bought into the practice twelve months later he financed this through a loan in order to maximise a tax advantage and left his lump sum refund as an investment. The applicant informed the Tribunal that he invested his refund of contributions as the interest rate for investments with private lending institutions was higher than the interest his contributions would attract if left in the superannuation fund.



9. In 1984 the applicant returned to Canberra and the Australian Taxation Office (ATO) and has been employed continuously with the ATO since that time. Upon rejoining the public service the applicant contacted the Retirements Benefits Office and inquired about his status for the purposes of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme. He was informed that as he had elected for a refund of contributions in 1979 he would rejoin as a new contributor.



10. When the applicant re-entered the public service he did not undergo formal induction training, instead he spoke to someone in the personnel section and signed the relevant forms. He agreed that he was given a leaflet entitled "Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme New Contributors Leaflet 1981".



11. It was the applicant's evidence that, whilst employed at the ATO he did not deal with superannuation matters, therefore despite his accountancy and legal expertise he was not very familiar with superannuation. An article appearing in a public service journal " Private Eye" alerted the applicant to the existence of the option to preserve superannuation rights and the right to apply to the Commissioner for a late election to preserve benefits pursuant to section 157(1) of the Act. The applicant took this advice and wrote to the Commissioner on 18 January 1989, and this letter was treated as a late election for preservation of superannuation rights.



12. The applicant was shown certain pro forma information statements that were sent out by the respondent in 1987 and 1988. He recognised the statements and agreed that they included information on preservation of contributions on resignation. The applicant had no recollection of reading that specific information and is of the belief that had he read it, it would not have put him on notice to inquire further as he would not have thought the information was relevant to his situation.



13. In essence the applicant submitted that although, at the time, he made a conscious and deliberate decision to request a refund of his contributions this decision did not amount to an election. The applicant argued that a valid election is predicated upon him being fully informed of the options available to him, and, it was submitted, that he was not at the relevant time aware of his ability to preserve his contributions. It was his contention that the Commissioner owes a duty of care to resigning contributors to fully advise them of what their legal rights and options are concerning their superannuation entitlements. Further, the applicant submitted that this duty was breached both at the time of his resignation and upon rejoining the public service. In evidence to the Tribunal he summarised this alleged breach as follows:

"(a) I was not counselled or informed at the time of my resignation of the various legal rights and options available other than the refund of contributions option;

(b) this incomplete advice amounted to incorrect advice which mislead me into believing that a refund of contributions was the only option suitable to my circumstances; and

(c) I was not counselled or informed at the time of my re-employment of the facility provided under the Act for acceptance of late elections for preservation of rights notwithstanding that I contacted the Retirement Benefits Office then concerning my superannuation position on re-employment. ..."



14. In cross examination the applicant conceded that, considering his high level of education and expertise, he ought to have made further inquiries about his superannuation options, rather than being satisfied with the information provided by the staff clerk. However the applicant was resolute that as it was his belief that, at the relevant time, refund of contributions was the only option available he was therefore satisfied that the staff clerks information was correct and complete. It was also put to the applicant that, given the economic climate at the time and the relatively high interest rate that private investment attracted, would he have elected to preserve his contributions, had he known of that option. It was his belief that he would have as death insurance cover continued if his contributions were preserved.



15. The applicant referred to two other cases which he considered to be factually similar to his and in which the late election was recognised. The reasons for the decision in both cases were not available to the Tribunal at the time of hearing and therefore the Tribunal was not in a position to consider the alleged inconsistency. The Tribunal is mindful that the exercise of the discretion pursuant to s 157(1) warrants that each case be considered in light of its own circumstances.



16. The Tribunal heard evidence from Mr G Cupitt, who was a class 5 officer with approximately six years in the personnel section of the ATO at the time of the applicant's resignation. Mr Cupitt's duties included advising retiring or resigning employees of their rights and responsibilities with regard to superannuation, and it was his recollection that he was aware of the option to preserve superannuation contributions and that it was available to resigning employees.



17. He said that most of the staff in personnel were trained informally, and that information about superannuation was exchanged during these sessions. He was familiar with the superannuation circular and said that it would have been distributed to all staff clerks and inserted into the superannuation manual that was available to all officers and held in the personnel section. In all, Mr Cupitt's evidence was vague and of little assistance to the Tribunal, suffice that he informed the Tribunal of the general practice of the ATO in relation to resigning employees. It was Mr Cupitt's impression that during the late 1970's many employees were leaving the public service and opting for a refund of their contributions, in his words they opted to "take the money and run."



18. It was the respondent's contention that the applicant, having signed the form S2A, and received superannuation circulars in 1979, 1981, 1987 and 1988, as well as having discussed his resignation at an exit interview with a staff clerk, was sufficiently put on notice to make further inquiries regarding his options. Further the applicant, being a highly skilled and educated person, was in a position to make such inquiries and afforded himself of this opportunity when he contacted the Retirements Benefits Office in 1984.



19. When deciding the question of whether or not to exercise the discretion under s 157(1) this Tribunal has recourse to the principles laid down in a number of recent Administrative Appeals Tribunal decisions. (See Re Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT 8553, 26 February, 1993) and Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (14 AAR 456)) The Tribunal considers that the power is not unrestricted in its terms as it specifies that the Commissioner must be "satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised. ...", The Tribunal is also mindful that a wide discretion is curtailed by the general purpose and policy for which the discretion is granted. The Tribunal in Re Lander considered that the discretion conferred by s 157(1) was, to some extent, limited by the use of the word "desirable". In Liddle it was said that the word "desirable" strongly suggests a positive connotation, that is something "worthy of achievement" rather then "merely advisable" and that "objectively good reasons" must exist which justify non-compliance with a statutory time limit. The applicant submitted that the facts of his case amounted to objectively good reasons sufficient to warrant the exercise of the discretion.



20. The statute is explicit that elections made under the superannuation fund must be made within the time limit stipulated. In the case of a superannuation fund preservation election, the time period is relatively short, being 1 month before resignation or 21 days after resignation. The Tribunal in Re Lander found that, prima facie, time limits are not to be ignored and therefore the discretion to grant a late election, pursuant to s 157(1), was tantamount to a right to apply for an extension of time. Proceeding on that understanding, the Tribunal in Re Lander drew upon the principles considered by the courts when dealing with applications under the Defence Forces Death and Retirement Benefits Act and Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act, in particular, the principles enunciated by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Limited & Ors v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; 7 ALD 315 at pages 319-320. It was made clear in Re Lander that a person who seeks the exercise of the Commissioner's discretion in their favour must satisfy the Commissioner that the discretion should be so exercised.



21. The applicant submitted that the respondent bears a similar evidentiary burden, that being; a duty to positively inform the applicant of all options available to him upon resignation in regards to his superannuation benefits. The Tribunal has sympathy with the reasoning of this submission and is mindful that the commissioner has a duty to provide information to all contributors to a compulsory superannuation scheme. However the burden of satisfying the Tribunal that the merits of the applicant's case warrant a favourable exercise of the discretion lies firmly with the applicant.



22. The Tribunal will rely on the principles applied by Wilcox J. in Hunter Valley and the Tribunal in Re Lander as tools helpful to the exercise of the discretion. These principles are summarised as follows:

"... (i) prima facie, proceedings commenced outside the prescribed period will not be entertained. An extension of time will be granted, however, if it is proper to do so;



(ii) it is relevant whether the applicant rested on his rights or took action to make the decision-maker aware that the decision was being contested;



(iii) any prejudice to the respondent that would be caused by granting the extension of time is relevant;



(iv) any wider prejudice to the general public in terms of disruption to established practices is relevant;



(v) the merits of the substantial application are relevant; and



(vi) fairness of granting the extension of time as between the applicant and other persons in a like position is relevant ..."



23. These principles offer the Tribunal a guide to the exercise of the discretion, enabling the Tribunal to make a decision which is just and equitable between the parties. In considering these principles and their relevance to this application the Tribunal recognises in recent cases a broader approach to the exercise of the discretion to grant an extension of time. In A'Hearn v Comcare (unreported,16 April 1993), Hill J. said:

"... I should say that Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd was at pains to make clear that he was not seeking to set out principles of law governing the exercise of discretion to extend time. Care must be taken by the Tribunal to ensure that there is not a slavish adherence to the matters which are referred to in that judgement, which are listed merely as matters for guidance ..."



He noted later that:

"... [A]t the end of the day the discretion of a court or tribunal given power to extend time will be a wide one, to be exercised as the justice of the case requires ..."



24. The Tribunal in Re Lander considered each of the principles enunciated by Wilcox J. in Hunter Valley and found that it was not desirable to recognise Mrs Lander's late election. The Tribunal in that instance were particularly influenced by the fact that Mrs Lander was located in Canberra, that her rights had been carefully explained to her by a personnel officer and that she had the personal ability and the opportunity to make inquiries. In this application, the Tribunal will consider the evidence before it having regard to those principles enunciated by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley in determining how to exercise its discretionary power, and will consider whether the applicant has shown an acceptable explanation for delay and that it is fair and equitable in the circumstances to extend time. Action taken by the applicant, prejudice to the respondent, and considerations of fairness as between the applicant and other persons in like positions are all relevant to the consideration of the exercise of the discretion.



25. The applicant's explanation of the delay was essentially that, at the time of his resignation, he was unaware of the availability of the preservation of benefits option. It was his submission that the respondent did nothing to disabuse him of this position and that the general mores in the public service at the time of his resignation was that resigning employees were entitled to a refund of contributions alone.



26. On the evidence before it the Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant received circulars regarding superannuation at the time of his resignation and upon re-entering the public service and that the applicant completed and signed the form S2A. Notwithstanding the ambiguity of the form and the applicant's contention that the circulars did not inform him of the existence of the preservation option, the Tribunal is of the opinion that sufficient information was available to the applicant regarding superannuation options at the time of his resignation. Although the Tribunal accepts that this information was not specifically presented to the applicant the information that was available was sufficient to alert the applicant to opportunities available to him.



27. The applicant's opportunity to make further inquiries was not restricted by his health, level of education nor location of employment, considerations that led the Tribunal in Liddle's case to find that the applicant was precluded from making full inquiries. Moreover, the Tribunal finds that the applicant's level of education and his position in the ATO would have put him in a position to make full inquiries regarding superannuation.



28. The Tribunal heard evidence of the standard procedures regarding dissemination of superannuation information at the time of the applicant's resignation. The position of the personnel section of the ATO was by no means proactive, however information was available and staff were aware of the preservation option. The particular characteristics of the applicant lead the Tribunal to the opinion that he was in a position to inquire further and indeed ought to have done so.



29. The applicant's letter requesting a refund of superannuation and the form S2A are clear. Both stipulate a refund of accumulated contributions, and the Tribunal is of the opinion that they reflect a conscious decision on the part of the applicant to seek and accept what he obtained, that is a refund. The Tribunal, in light of the applicant's evidence, is not hesitant to conclude that knowledge of the option to preserve would not have altered the actions of the applicant. He made a conscious decision to leave the public service, based on sound economic judgement. He could, and did, invest his refund at a high rate of interest, and certainly had the necessary skills to manage his financial affairs to his best advantage.



30. The Tribunal considers that some prejudice to the respondent is occasioned by the delay. The length of delay is therefore a material consideration as the longer the interval between the applicant's resignation and application for late election, the more difficult it will be to establish the facts and degree of knowledge available to the applicant on resignation. In this matter there was a significant gap in the evidence as all persons involved in the applicant's resignation and relevant to the proceedings did not appear before the Tribunal. Notwithstanding the considerable financial benefit to the applicant if his late election were accepted, the Tribunal does not consider that an "acceptable explanation of delay" has been put forward sufficient to warrant a favourable exercise of the discretion pursuant to s 157(1).



31. For the reasons given the Tribunal affirms the decision under review.
