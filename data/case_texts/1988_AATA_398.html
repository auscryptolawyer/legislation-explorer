Superannuation - issue of benefit classification certificate -s. 184(5) - whether applicant failed to furnish or give information or furnished or gave false or misleading information - nature of requirement discussed - Fry followed -applicant answering questions truthfully to best of his knowledge

REASONS FOR DECISION

1. From April 1970 until he retired in 1983 the applicant was employed by the Australian Broadcasting Commission as a technical officer and contributed first to the Superannuation Fund established under the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act") and then, after that Act had been superseded by the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act"), to the Superannuation Fund established by that Act. On 15 August 1983 a Benefit Classification Certificate in respect to the applicant was issued by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") pursuant to section 184(5) of the 1976 Act specifying the condition "neurasthenia". The delegate also decided that the incapacity which was the ground of the applicant's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by that condition and that accordingly his benefit entitlement under the 1976 Act fell to be determined under section 66(2) of that Act. The effect of that was that the applicant was entitled to be paid invalidity benefit under Division 4 of the 1976 Act at a lower rate than would have been payable to him if a Benefit Classification Certificate had not been issued or if the incapacity which was the ground of his retirement had not been caused or substantially contributed to by the condition specified in the certificate.

  1. The applicant applied for reconsideration of the decision by the Commissioner. In due course another delegate of the Commissioner carried out that reconsideration in accordance of the provisions of section 154(4) of the 1976 Act. He confirmed the decision to issue the Benefit Classification Certificate but varied the condition specified in it to "History of Anxiety State". He also confirmed the decision that the applicant's benefit entitlement fell to be determined under section 66(2) of the 1976 Act. The applicant then applied to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal, pursuant to section 154(6) of that Act, for review of the decision as so varied.
  2. At the time when the decision under review was made, section 184(5) of the 1976 Act, was as follows:-

"184. (5) Where -

(a) a person to whom this section applies (whether or not he was, immediately before the commencing day, a contributor to the Provident Account) ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of death or retirement on the ground of invalidity;

(b) his period of contributory service is less than 20 years and, on the day on which he ceases to be an eligible employee, he has not attained his maximum retiring age;

(c) the person was, immediately before the commencing day, a contributor to the Fund or, if the person was, immediately before the commencing day, a contributor to the Provident Account, the Commissioner is not of the opinion that the death of the person or the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the person was caused, or was substantially contributed to –

(i) by the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, by those conditions or one or more of those conditions; or

(ii) by a physical or mental condition or conditions of the person connected with the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, with those conditions or one or more of those conditions; and

(d) the Commissioner is satisfied -

(i) that, at or in connexion with a medical examination which the person underwent for the purposes of the superseded Act, the person failed to furnish any information required to be furnished by him or furnished false information; and

(ii) that, if the person had not failed to furnish that information or had not furnished that false information and, in a case where the person would not have been a contributor to the Provident Account or a contributor to the Fund immediately before the commencing day but for the failure to furnish that information or the furnishing of that false information, the person had been a contributor to the Provident Account immediately before the commencing day, there would have been deemed to be in force in respect of the person, immediately before his death or retirement, a benefit classification certificate in which there would have been specified the physical or mental condition or conditions, which caused, or substantially contributed to, the death or retirement, or a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the person a benefit classification certificate in which there is or are specified the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, would have been the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person specified in the benefit classification certificate that the Commissioner would have issued, or, but for sub-section (4), would have issued, in respect of the person under sub-section (2) if the person had not failed to furnish that information or had not furnished that false information and, in a case where the person would not have been a contributor to the Provident Account or a contributor to the Fund immediately before the commencing day but for the failure to furnish that information or the furnishing of that false information, the person had been a contributor to the Provident Account immediately before the commencing day and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(11) and to have been in force in respect of the person immediately before his death or retirement."

4. On 18 December 1986 section 184 of the 1976 Act was amended by section 76 of the Superannuation Legislation Amendment (No. 2) Act 1986. Sub-section (5) was omitted and the following new sub-sections (5), (5A), (5B) and (5C) were substituted for it:-

" (5) Where the Commissioner is satisfied, in respect of a person who is a relevant person -

(a) that, at or in connection with a medical examination that the person underwent for the purpose of the superseded Act, the person failed to give information that the person was required to give or gave false or misleading information; and

(b) that a physical or mental condition of the person or physical or mental conditions of the person (in this sub-section referred to as an 'unrecorded condition' or as 'unrecorded conditions') that was not or were not relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act, would have been relevant for the purposes of that Act if the person had not failed to give the information the person was required to give or had not given that false or misleading information,

the Commissioner shall -

(c) if there is a benefit classification certificate in force in respect of the relevant person - revoke that certificate and issue a new benefit classification certificate in respect of the person in which the unrecorded condition or the unrecorded conditions is or are specified either in addition to or in substitution for the physical or mental condition that was, or any or all of the physical or mental conditions that were, specified in the first-mentioned certificate;

(d) if there is no benefit classification certificate in force in respect of the person but a physical or mental condition of the person or physical or mental conditions of the person was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act - issue a benefit classification certificate in respect of the person in which the unrecorded condition or the unrecorded conditions is or are specified either in addition to or in substitution for the physical or mental condition that was, or any or all of the physical or mental conditions that were, relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act; or

(e) if there is no benefit classification certificate in force in respect of the person and no physical or mental condition of the person was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act - issue a benefit classification certificate in respect of the person in which the unrecorded condition is or the unrecorded conditions are specified.

(5A) In sub-section (5), a reference to a relevant person shall be read as a reference to a person to whom this section applies (whether or not the person was, immediately before the commencing day, a contributor to the Provident Account) -

(a) who is an eligible employee, has a period of contributory service of less than 20 years and has not attained his or her maximum retiring age;

(b) who is or was an eligible employee to whom partial invalidity pension is or was payable and who, at the time when partial invalidity pension became so payable, had a period of contributory service of less than 20 years; or

(c) who has ceased (whether before or after the commencement of this sub-section) to be an eligible employee because of invalidity or death and who, at the time when he or she so ceased, had a period of contributory service of less than 20 years and had not attained his or her maximum retiring age.

(5B) Where the Commissioner, in pursuance of paragraph (5)(c), revokes a benefit classification certificate and issues a new benefit classification certificate in substitution for that certificate, the first-mentioned certificate shall be deemed never to have been issued and the certificate issued by the Commissioner in substitution for the first mentioned certificate shall be deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(4) on the commencing day.

(5C) Where the Commissioner, in pursuance of paragraph (5) (d) or (e), issues a benefit classification certificate, the certificate shall be deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(4) on the commencing day."

5. At the hearing of the application the applicant was represented by Mr G. B. Wicks, of Counsel; the respondent was represented by Mr R. Whithear, an Assistant Commissioner for Superannuation. The case was listed to be heard on three consecutive days. As not all the witnesses were available to give evidence during those three days, it was agreed by the parties, at the suggestion of the Tribunal, that during those days the only evidence to be presented should be that relevant to the issues:-

(a) whether at or in connection with the medical examination which he underwent in 1969 for the purpose of assessing the applicant's eligibility for admission under the 1922 Act as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund he had failed to furnish or give any information required to be furnished or given by him or had furnished or given false information; and

(b) if so, what would have happened if he had furnished or given the information required or had not furnished or given the false information.

Mr Whithear informed us that in his view the legislation which we were required to apply was that which was in force when the decision under review was made but that, in any event, in the circumstances of the present case it made no difference whether that legislation or the legislation by which it had been superseded on 18 December 1986 had to be applied. Mr Wicks did not demur and we are satisfied that Mr Whithear correctly stated the situation. It was noted that, if the Tribunal decided the two issues adversely to the respondent, it would not be necessary to resume the hearing and receive further evidence. If, on the other hand, the issues were decided adversely to the applicant, the hearing would be resumed in March and further evidence received.

6. In consequence of the agreement to narrow the scope of the evidence presented at that initial hearing the only witnesses who gave evidence were the applicant, his brother Dr J. G. Lyle, Dr G. E. Doery and Dr B. J. Hackett. Dr Lyle is not a medical practitioner but holds a doctorate of philosophy and is a consultant psychologist. Dr Doery is a general practitioner who practised in partnership with other general practitioners between 1958 and 1969; the applicant was a patient of Dr Doery and one of his partners. Dr Hackett is a Senior Medical Officer employed by the Commonwealth in the Department of Health. In addition to the oral evidence the Tribunal received documentary evidence including a copy of a form relating to the medical examination which the applicant underwent on 1 September 1969 for the purpose of ascertaining whether or not he was eligible to be admitted under the 1922 Act as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund. The documents also included a number of medical reports relating to the applicant; two were written by Dr Doery in 1983 and 1986 in response to enquiries received by him from the respondent. The medical records also included medical records relating to the applicant during the period of his service in the Royal Australian Air Force from 1944 to 1947, a record of sick leave taken by the applicant between July 1969 and December 1982 while he was in the employment of the Australian Broadcasting Commission and letters written about the applicant by Dr Lyle to the applicant's solicitors.

7. In Fry v Commissioner for Superannuation [1984] FCA 211; (1984) 6 ALD 229 the Full Court of the Federal Court of Australia considered the provisions of section 16 of the 1976 Act, as it was in force at that time. In particular, it was concerned with section 16(11)(d)(i), which was in precisely the same terms as section 184(5)(d)(i), as in force at that time. Consequently, its statement of the effect of the provisions of section 16(11)(d)(i) is applicable also to section 184(5)(d)(i) as in force when the decision under review in these proceedings was made and, as we understood Mr Whithear to concede, also to the present section 184(5)(a). The decision of the Federal Court in Fry was made on appeal from the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. The Tribunal, when considering whether Mr Fry had failed to furnish information which he should have furnished or had furnished false information, had applied a test which involved first ascertaining what were the facts and matters of which he had been aware at the time when he was required to furnish the information and then deciding what opinions a reasonable man having an awareness of those facts would have held, how he would have answered the questions seeking the information and what he would have disclosed. The Federal Court decided that that was not the correct test. When the correct test was applied, the first matter to be ascertained was what information Mr Fry had been obliged to furnish; that, the Court said, depended upon the questions asked of him and the actual state of his knowledge in relation to them. He was to be taken not to have had information as to any matter unless he had been apprised or told of it. He was not required to make any enquiries in order to meet his obligation to furnish information in respect of the matter. He had complied with the section if he answered the questions truthfully "according to his actual knowledge and state of mind". The reference in the legislation to "false information" did not mean simply information that was factually untrue; it had to be factually untrue but the employee would not have breached his obligation if he had answered the questions truthfully "according to his actual knowledge and state of mind".

8. Mr Whithear drew to the Tribunal's attention the fact that the Federal Court had not stated precisely what it meant by the expression "his state of mind". He referred us to Re Sosnowski and Commissioner for Superannuation (Decision No. 3515: 29 May 1987), in which the Tribunal had considered that question. In that case the majority said:-

"Mr Whithear asked us to decide the meaning of 'state of mind'. As we pointed out at the hearing, the phrase is not part of the language of the statute; it was used by Sweeney J. to state his understanding of what the statute required. We think that no useful purpose would be served by exploring its precise meaning. On the other hand we consider that it is significant that His Honour referred not only to 'actual knowledge' but to the employee's 'state of mind', thereby indicating that something more than mere knowledge of the facts in respect of which information was required had to be taken into account.

We are conscious of the fact that a protestation of misunderstanding of a question in Form S, or its current replacement, may be a mere pretext for failure to answer it truthfully or to furnish the information required. We are not to be taken to encourage such deceit. However, where an employee, having given to a question the requisite degree of attention that the completion of the form properly requires, genuinely misunderstands it and that misunderstanding is the reason why the answer to the question is not what it should have been, the employee ought not to be held to have failed to furnish the information or to have furnished false information (or to have given false or misleading information); we consider that this accords with the principles which underlie the decision in Fry's case and Sweeney J.'s reference to 'state of mind'."

  1. We understand that the decision in Re Sosnowski is under appeal in the Federal Court at present. We have no doubt that we are bound by the decision of that Court in Fry. We have come to the conclusion that, whatever was meant by the phrase "his state of mind", as used in Sweeney J.'s statement of the reasons for the decision in that case, the applicant in these present proceedings did not fail to furnish or give information which he was required to give and that he did not furnish or give false information. It was not suggested to us that he gave misleading information; we are satisfied that he did not do so.
  2. The form which had to be completed in respect of the applicant's medical examination for the purpose of ascertaining his eligibility to be accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund under the provisions of the 1922 Act had to be completed in part by the applicant himself and in part by the Commonwealth Medical Officer who conducted the medical examination. The part which the applicant was required to complete consisted of details relating to himself and his family, including answers to a number of questions about his health. He gave evidence, which Mr Whithear did not suggest was not true and which we accept, that the Commonwealth Medical Officer first conducted the medical examination and then gave him the form for him to complete his part of it. The Commonwealth Medical Officer did not give him any explanation of the meaning of any of the questions relating to his health that he was required to answer. He was left to answer them on his own as he thought fit. The only question in respect to which he did not write the answer was number (4) which read:-

"(4) Have you ever had an accident or surgical treatment? If so, give full details."

The answer to that was inserted by the Commonwealth Medical Officer as the result of information given to him orally by the applicant.

11. The form of the questions which a person undergoing a medical examination for the purpose of his entry into the Commonwealth's Superannuation Scheme is required to answer has been changed at least twice since the date when the applicant completed his part of it. In a number of previous cases the Tribunal has commented on the unsatisfactory wording of questions in the earlier forms. The questions which the applicant was required to answer would have been reasonably satisfactory if they had been put to him by the Commonwealth Medical Officer with an appropriate explanation of their meaning. But they were far from satisfactory where the person undergoing the examination was not himself medically qualified and was required to answer without any explanation of them being given to him by the Commonwealth Medical Officer.

  1. Mr Whithear submitted that the applicant had answered incorrectly several of the questions and that by doing so either he had failed to furnish or give information required to be furnished or given by him or he had furnished or given false information. It is necessary for us to deal in turn with each of those questions and to state why we are satisfied that, in answering it as he did, he did not fail to furnish or give information required to be furnished or given by him and did not furnish or give false information.
  2. The first question which he was required to answer was as follows:-

"(1) Has there been any tuberculosis, rheumatism, diabetes, gout, asthma, recurring bronchitis, cancer, diseases of blood, bones or joints, insanity or any other nervous condition in yourself or any other member of the family? If so, give particulars.

The applicant was required to answer that question and all the others in respect of himself and his family. He answered "No" in respect of himself and "No" in respect of his family. Mr Whithear submitted that he should have answered "Yes" in respect to himself because he had suffered from an anxiety state for a number of years before 1969 and must have been aware of the fact. If he had given proper attention to the question, he would have understood that the anxiety state was an "other nervous condition", so that he was required to answer "Yes".

  1. Dr Doery gave evidence of the occasions on which the applicant had consulted him and his partner, Dr Hare. He said that he had no personal recollection whatsoever of the applicant and had to rely entirely on the clinical notes which he and Dr Hare had made at the time. The first occasion on which the applicant had come to him or Dr Hare was on 1 December 1958, when he was 32 years old; the last occasion on which he had come had been on 10 April 1969. Altogether in that period of just over 10 years the applicant had consulted him and Dr Hare on a total of 13 occasions. He himself had seen the applicant on only three occasions, in 1958, in 1959 and on 10 April 1969. The applicant had come to see him on 1 December 1958 complaining of being excessively tired and also of having passed blood after an act of sexual intercourse. He had examined the applicant thoroughly and found that he had no problems with his sleep, his digestion, his bowels or his micturition. He had no cough, but complained of headaches and being tired all the time. He had told Dr Doery he had lost his libido, Dr Doery had made no diagnosis and had prescribed vitamin capsules. He had made no note of any conclusions reached by him as to the causes of the headaches and the tiredness.
  2. The next occasion on which Dr Doery had seen the applicant was on 28 July 1959 when he had come because of a sore throat. Dr Doery had found that he had tonsillitis and pharyngitis and prescribed antibiotic medication and vitamins. On his next visit to the surgery, the applicant had been seen by Dr Hare; that was on either 20 August 1959 or 20 October 1959 (Dr Doery could not read what Dr Hare had written). He had been suffering with an abscess on the root of one of his teeth. Dr Hare had prescribed penicillin. Dr Doery had next seen the applicant on 23 October 1959 when he had come for varicose veins in his right leg to be treated by injection. At the same time Dr Doery had prescribed hormone tablets for his impotence. Again, Dr Doery had made no diagnosis of the cause of the impotence. From the manner in which the clinical note relating to 23 October 1959 was recorded it appears that the applicant may not have complained of impotence but have given Dr Doery information about it in answer to questions put by the doctor.

16. The applicant did not go to the surgery again until 27 June 1963. He gave evidence to us that during the whole period when he was attending the surgery he did not consult any other doctors. Dr Lyle gave evidence that the applicant had enjoyed such good health that he had envied him for it. We accept the applicant's evidence that he attended no other doctor. Consequently there was a gap of over three and a half years when he had not found it necessary to seek medical attention. Dr Doery gave evidence that on 27 June 1963 the applicant was seen by Dr Hare. Dr Hare had recorded that the applicant had complained to him that he had been suffering sharp pains over the heart region for the previous two or three months, that the pains had been brought on by exertion and that they had been relieved by rest. Dr Hare had recorded also that the applicant was "moderately short of breath", that his blood pressure was 130/80 and that his heart, lungs and abdomen were normal on clinical examination. He had arranged for an ECG examination and had received the cardiograph. He had recorded that he had certified the applicant unfit to work for one week and had advised him to rest. He had later recorded that at the end of the week he had again examined the applicant and had received another cardiograph in respect to an examination made on that day, and that the applicant was still feeling tired and listless. However, he had examined him again a week later and had recorded that he was feeling better for the rest, that his blood pressure was 150/80 and his cardiograph unchanged and he had certified him as fit to return to work. The details of what was shown on the cardiograph had been recorded; Dr Doery gave evidence that they were within the normal range of heart function.

17. The applicant gave evidence that during the two or three months before he went to the surgery on 27 June 1963 he had been doing a lot of heavy work building an extension to his house without any assistance. There is no record of his having told Dr Hare of that; when giving evidence in these proceedings he did not suggest that he had done so. However, Dr Lyle gave evidence that the applicant did all the building work over many years on his own, often doing very heavy work in the process.

18. The next occasion on which the applicant had gone to the surgery, Dr Doery said, was on 28 August 1963. Again he had been seen by Dr Hare, who had recorded that he was feeling tired and lacked energy but was not suffering any chest pains, that his blood pressure was 120/80 and that his heart was not enlarged. He had recorded a systolic murmur of the apex and that he had encouraged the applicant to do 5BX exercises. Dr Doery said that those were strenuous exercises and that Dr Hare would not have encouraged the applicant to do them if he had considered that there was anything seriously wrong with his heart.

  1. The applicant had not gone to the surgery again until more than 11 months later, when he had again had a sore throat which Dr Hare had treated with penicillin. He had suffered an allergic reaction to the penicillin and had been treated with adrenaline by injection and antihistamine by tablets. A period of more than 15 months had then passed before he had again gone to the surgery. On that occasion, according to the clinical notes made by Dr Hare, he had complained of difficulty in sleeping. He had had a cough for two weeks which was non-productive. On examination Dr Hare had found that his chest was clinically clear. He prescribed Doriden for the difficulty in sleeping. Three months later the applicant had gone to the surgery and had again been seen by Dr Hare. Dr Hare had recorded that he had again complained of poor sleep and had said that the Doriden was not helping him. Dr Hare had recorded that he had prescribed a barbiturate, Carbrital. Dr Doery gave evidence that Carbrital is a much stronger medication than Doriden.
  2. The applicant gave evidence that the reason why he was having difficulty with sleeping was that his third wife, whom he had married in 1963, had brought into the marriage with her a French poodle, which had been accustomed to sleeping on her bed. He had tried to make it sleep elsewhere without success. At the time when he had complained of difficulty with his sleeping the dog had been disturbing him. He said that subsequently the dog had died. Before his problems with the dog, he had had no trouble sleeping and after the dog had died he had again had no trouble sleeping. Dr Doery gave evidence that Dr Hare was a cautious doctor and would not have prescribed more than 25 Carbrital tablets and that he had prescribed them only once; that evidence tends to support the applicant's evidence that afterwards he had had no trouble with his sleep.
  3. Almost three years elapsed before the applicant had again gone to the surgery, on 10 April 1969. Dr Doery had made clinical notes recording that the applicant had told him that he had knocked his shin a week earlier and had hurt it. He had recorded that there were signs of inflammation present and that he had recommended that it should be treated with rest in bed and had prescribed antibiotic medication.
  4. On 19 May 1986 the respondent wrote to Dr Doery informing him of the answers the applicant had given in 1969 to three of the questions on his medical examination form and asking him to give details of the medication prescribed to the applicant on 17 January 1966 and 27 April 1966, to state for how long it was prescribed and whether the applicant was aware that it had been prescribed for him and would have known that it was related to a nervous condition, and to express an opinion, on the basis of the applicant's recorded medical history whether he considered that it would have been reasonable to expect the applicant to answer "Yes" to any of the three questions. On 10 July 1986 Dr Doery replied giving details of the medication and stating that he would have expected the applicant to have been aware that it had been prescribed for insomnia and to conclude that there would be a relationship between insomnia and a nervous condition. He considered that the applicant would have remembered the episode of the chest pains. He suggested that the questions that the applicant "was asked in respect to neurosis trouble including anxiety state" might have been ill interpreted to him "unless those questions were enlarged upon by the examiner who was with him at that time". He commented that, from the records of his practice, it should have been apparent to the applicant that he had been suffering at various times from an anxiety state, but that some patients who reported with symptoms that were interpreted by doctors as evidence of nervous troubles or anxiety state could be quite unaware that those symptoms were due to that underlying neurosis or nervous trouble. He suggested that that might have been so in Mr Lyle's case, but that "his complaint of insomnia was a persistent complaint as was his complaint of fatigue and tiredness".
  5. As he was leaving the witness box, Dr Doery volunteered the information that he had had great difficulty answering in that letter the questions which had been asked of him. His letter gives the distinct impression that his answers were influenced by information, which had been supplied to him by the respondent in the letter of request and of which he had not previously been aware, that the applicant had been retired from his job in December 1982 on the ground of invalidity due to the effects of a psychiatric illness, two manifestations of which were difficulty in sleeping and excessive tiredness. We have considerable sympathy with him; it must have been very difficult in the circumstances to be sure what his state of knowledge and his opinion about the applicant had been between 1958 and 1969. He acknowledged in his oral evidence that he no longer had any personal recollection of the applicant. He had seen him on only four of the occasions on which he had come to the surgery. Dr Hare, who had seen him on all the other occasions, had died and was not available to assist him with any recollection of the events which he had recorded in the clinical notes. In these proceedings we are concerned, of course, to find out what was the applicant's state of knowledge in September 1969 and what would have happened then if the applicant had answered "Yes" to any of the questions. So far as Dr Doery is concerned, we are essentially concerned with what his opinion of the applicant's medical problems was at that time and with answers which he would have given then to any questions asked of him. When he gave oral evidence, he admitted that it was very difficult to be sure what he would have answered but he was of the view, having regard to the contents of the clinical notes, that, if he had been asked whether the applicant had suffered from a nervous condition or if he had been asked about his general health, he would have raised the possibility that he might have been suffering from an anxiety state and would have recommended that the opinion of a psychiatrist be obtained. He conceded that he had not at any time recorded a diagnosis of anxiety state or of any other nervous condition.
  6. In view of our findings in respect of the answers given by the applicant to the questions on the form, it has become unnecessary for us to consider how Dr Doery would have replied to an enquiry made to him in 1969. However, his evidence of how he believes that he would have replied is relevant to the question of whether the applicant knew that he had been suffering from an anxiety state at various times between 1958 and 1969. If Dr Doery had not himself formed such an opinion, it is unlikely that the applicant would have done so. If he had formed that opinion, the question then arises whether he had told the applicant that he had done so or had otherwise given him reason to believe that he was suffering from a nervous condition. The applicant gave evidence that Dr Doery did not at any time tell him he was suffering from an anxiety state or any other nervous condition. Medication of the various types well known to people as treatment for nervous conditions was never prescribed; so he would have received no indication in that manner.
  7. Giving evidence-in-chief, Dr Doery said that he could not recall what he had told the applicant at the time when he had been treating him for impotence. At present his invariable practice was to tell patients fully what he considered the cause to be. He thought that the fact that the applicant had not returned for repeated visits might be significant; he might have intended to explain the cause to him when he came back. He repeated that his present practice was to discuss possible causes in detail but said that in 1959 he might not have done so and that in any event he would have been expecting the applicant to come back after a week. He thought that he would have discussed the cause with him during the first consultation; but he could not be sure. He would expect a patient to think about what was the diagnosis of his problem and have an insight into it; but he admitted that patients usually went to a doctor for the doctor to make the diagnosis and that any insight which a patient might have otherwise than as a result of what the doctor told him might be valid or invalid. He admitted also that a patient would not necessarily diagnose himself as suffering from anxiety state because he was suffering from insomnia.

26. On re-examination he said that it was more likely that he discussed with the applicant the causes of his impotence. However, at no time did he say expressly in what terms he would have done so, that is to say whether he would have expressed himself in terms of "a nervous condition", "an anxiety state", "being under some strain" or "being run down" or in some other way. The ability of a patient without medical qualifications to perceive that the cause of his impotence might be categorised as "a nervous condition" would depend to a great extent on the terms in which it was explained to him. Although the applicant was not an entirely satisfactory witness, because his psychiatric problems and the treatment he has received for them have had an adverse effect on his memory, nevertheless we are far from satisfied on a balance of probabilities that Dr Doery ever explained to him the causes of his impotence in terms which could have been expected to make him aware that Dr Doery regarded him as suffering from a nervous condition.

27. He gave evidence that throughout the period from 1958 to 1969 he was building extensions to his house without assistance and that consequently he sometimes had to lift heavy objects by himself. His brother gave evidence corroborating that, in particular giving examples of the extremely heavy nature of some of the work on the house which the applicant did. Before he joined the Australian Broadcasting Commission he was employed as a travelling salesman selling an encyclopaedia from door to door. In that job, he said, he had worked very long hours, travelling considerable distances. We consider that he might have been justified in thinking that his excessive tiredness was due in considerable part to the nature of his job and to his building activities. He has given us an explanation of the problem he was having with his sleep in January 1966 and April 1966. His brother gave evidence, which we accept, that the applicant's third wife brought the poodle with her into the marriage and treated it like a child. That gives some support to the applicant's evidence that before the marriage his wife had allowed it to sleep on her bed and that after the marriage it had slept on the bed of his wife and himself. His inability to sleep on each of the two occasions when he went to see Dr Hare about it could, therefore, well have been due to reasons other than a nervous condition. In that event the applicant, knowing those reasons, would not have had any reason at all to believe that it was related to a nervous condition. Consequently we have come to the conclusion that, when the applicant answered the first question on the form in September 1969, he probably was not aware either that he had suffered from a nervous condition or that Dr Doery had thought it possible that he might have done so. That being so, it is unnecessary for us to consider whether, if he had had any particular knowledge apposite to the first question, it was expressed in terms which would have enabled him to realise that, having that knowledge, he was required to answer "Yes" to it.

28. The second question which Mr Whithear says should have been answered "Yes" but which was answered "No" in respect of the applicant, was:-

"(2) Have you ever suffered from (if so give details and dates) -

(a) Sunstroke, spitting of blood, pleurisy, influenza, or a persistent cough or pains in chest? If influenza, state frequency."

The applicant gave evidence that he did not consider that he had ever suffered persistent pains in the chest and that was why he had answered "No". Mr Whithear conceded that, because the word "or" had been inserted between "influenza" and "a persistent cough or pains in chest", the latter words had to be read together as a phrase and that it was reasonable for a person so reading them to believe that he was being asked whether he had ever suffered from persistent pains in the chest. We accept that that was so. There is no evidence that the applicant suffered pains in the chest at any time except during the two to three months before he went to see Dr Hare in June 1966, or that he ever suffered such pains after they had cleared up on that occasion. He undoubtedly had knowledge that he had suffered those pains during that period in 1966; he did not deny that.

29. A medical practitioner required to answer the question might well have understood that he was required to answer "Yes" if he had ever suffered pains in the chest which had persisted for any considerable period, even though he had done so only during one isolated period. But the situation was very different where the person who was required to answer the question had no expertise in medicine and no experience of the use of medical phraseology. The applicant gave evidence that he answered "No" because, in effect, the pains in the chest were not persisting in 1969. In our view it was not unreasonable for him, as a person without medical expertise or experience of the use of medical phraseology, to understand the question in that way. We are satisfied that he answered truthfully and honestly the question as he understood it. That being so, he did not fail to furnish or give any information required to be furnished or given by him, and by answering it in that way he did not furnish or give false information. We should add that, even if we had been satisfied that he was under an obligation to answer "Yes" to the question, the evidence given at the hearing satisfies us that, as he properly answered "No" to the other questions, so that no doubts would have been raised about his general health, the enquiries which would have been made if he had answered "Yes" would have related only to the chest pains. We are satisfied that they would not have elicited from Dr Doery or Dr Hare any information which might have caused a Commonwealth Medical Officer to give an opinion that the applicant was not fit to be accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund, and in particular that they would not have elicited information which would have resulted eventually in a Benefit Classification Certificate being issued in terms of any psychiatric, mental or nervous illness, disorder or condition.

30. The third question which Mr Whithear says should have been answered "Yes" was as follows:-

"(2) Have you ever suffered from (if so give details and dates) –

.........

(b) Rheumatic Fever, St. Vitus's Dance, growing pains, palpitation, fainting, breathlessness, disease of the heart or lungs, any affection of or stone in the kidneys or bladder stricture?"

He submitted that the applicant should have answered "Yes" because he was aware that he had suffered from breathlessness. The only occasion on which Dr Doery and Dr Hare referred to breathlessness in their clinical notes was in relation to the pains in the chest of which the applicant complained in June 1963. The impression given by the clinical notes is that the information about breathlessness was elicited by Dr Hare during his examination of the applicant in June 1963. When the applicant gave evidence, he stated in cross-examination that he had always been a heavy smoker and sometimes had been breathless with smoking but could not recall any specific incident of breathlessness. It had never affected him so as to prevent him from working. Again the evidence of his brother as to the extremely heavy work which he undertook over many years building the extensions to his house without help, except occasionally from his brother, indicates to us that any breathlessness that he may have suffered was not of such a degree as to cause him any concern or any problem. We doubt very much whether it was of such a degree that an answer "Yes" to the question was required, even if the applicant was aware that he was suffering from breathlessness. We are satisfied that it had caused him no problems and that he did not regard it as "breathlessness". We find, therefore, that he answered that question truthfully and honestly; he did not fail to furnish or give information required to be furnished or given by him and he did not furnish or give false information by answering the question as he did.

31. The fourth question which Mr Whithear says the applicant should have answered "Yes" was as follows:-

"(2) Have you ever suffered from (if so give details and dates) –

.......

(d) Any affection or ulcer of stomach, liver or bowels, indigestion, diarrhoea, constipation, appendicitis, piles, rupture or gall bladder trouble?"

Mr Whithear referred us to the medical records maintained by the RAAF which showed that on 1 April 1946 and again on 9 April 1946 the applicant attended sick parade complaining on the first occasion of "flatulence, pain under the right costal region (tenderness only) and bowels constipated" and on the second occasion without pain but with constipation. The applicant gave evidence that he now had no recollection whatsoever of having suffered constipation on those occasions. We are not the least surprised by that. He suffered it for a very short period nearly 42 years ago. Even by September 1969 24 years had elapsed since he had suffered it. We accept his evidence that, when he completed the form, he had forgotten about the events of 1946; we regard it as entirely reasonable that he should have done so. This is not a case of what might be called "convenient forgetfulness" of a condition which a person had good cause to remember. In our view it was genuine forgetfulness as the result of which he was no longer aware of having suffered the constipation. We make no comment about the form of the question, as it is no longer in use. However, there must be very few people who have not at some time in their lives suffered indigestion, diarrhoea or constipation, or all of them. Did those who devised the form seriously expect that a person who had suffered indigestion on one occasion after over-eating, or diarrhoea on one occasion after consuming tainted food, or constipation on one occasion only, would answer "Yes" to the question. Possibly they did; but, if so, we regard it as a most unreasonable expectation. We are satisfied that in answering "No" to the question the applicant did not fail to furnish or give any information required to be furnished or given by him and did not furnish or give false information.

32. The next question which Mr Whithear submitted should have been answered "Yes", was:-

"(2) Have you ever suffered from (if so give details and dates) –

......

(e) Epilepsy, fits, headaches, migraine or turns of any kind?"

The applicant gave evidence that he was not aware that he had ever suffered from headaches. The only evidence that he had ever done so was Dr Doery's clinical note made on the one occasion in 1958. It appears from the form of the note that he complained only of over-tiredness and that the fact that he had also experienced headaches was elicited by Dr Doery's questioning. There is no evidence that he had suffered any headaches before then or that he did so between then and September 1969. We are satisfied that, when he answered "No" to the question, that was an honest and truthful answer, as he was not aware that he had suffered headaches. Accordingly we find that, in answering "No" to that question, he did not fail to furnish or give any information required to be furnished or given by him and that he did not furnish or give false information. Again, we note the quite unreasonable breadth of the question. Most people, but by no means all, have suffered at least one headache, if only after indulging in an excess of achohol. Was an answer "Yes" really expected from people who had never suffered from persistent or frequently recurring headaches? However, as the question is no longer asked in that form, there is no point in our commenting further on it.

33. The next question which Mr Whithear originally submitted that the applicant should have answered "Yes" read:-

"(2) Have you ever suffered from (if so give details and dates) –

......

(f) Frequent coughs, colds, sore throat, catarrh, nasal, antrum or sinus trouble?"

The applicant injured his nose in 1946 or 1947 when he was in the RAAF; while he was dismantling fittings from a Dornier bomber, a metal post fell onto his nose and caused the septum to be displaced to one side. As a result he was unable to breathe through one of his nostrils. Within about a year he underwent an operation to remedy the problem. There is no evidence that he has ever suffered any other nasal trouble; it was on that one injury and its consequences that Mr Whithear relied. However, after it had been suggested to him by the Tribunal that the adjective "frequent" qualified all the words following it, as we are satisfied it does, he conceded that there was no evidence of frequent nasal trouble. We find, therefore that, in answering "No" to the question, the applicant did not fail to furnish or give any information required to be furnished or given by him and did not furnish or give false information.

  1. Initially Mr Whithear suggested to the applicant that he should have answered "No" to the question "Do you sleep well?" However, subsequently he conceded correctly that there was no evidence that in September 1969 or for some considerable time before then the applicant had had any problems with his sleep, and that the question as phrased sought information only as to whether he was sleeping well at the time when he completed the form or, possibly, whether he had been sleeping well during the period immediately preceding that. We are satisfied that the applicant, in answering "Yes" to the question did not fail to furnish or give any information required to be furnished or given by him and did not furnish or give false information.
  2. Finally Mr Whithear submitted that, in answering the question whether he had ever had an accident or surgical treatment, the applicant should have referred to the accident to his nose and the surgical treatment which he received to remedy the effects of it. The applicant did not himself write an answer to the question but apparently told the Commonwealth Medical Officer of surgery for an impacted wisdom tooth in 1958 and the Commonwealth Medical Officer wrote in that detail. The applicant, giving evidence, admitted that he did not tell the Commonwealth Medical Officer of the accident to his nose. He said that he had forgotten about it. Mr Whithear submitted that he could not reasonably have forgotten it, as surgical treatment was not an every day experience. However, on a balance of probabilities we find that he did forget it and that, as it had all happened more than 20 years before, it was not unreasonable for him to have done so. We add, however, that, even if he had informed the Commonwealth Medical Officer of that accident and the surgical operation to his nose, and even if the Commonwealth Medical Officer had considered it of any significance, and even if the question relating to pains in the chest had been answered "Yes", enquiries would not have been made of Dr Doery and Dr Hare as to the applicants general health. The only enquiry which might have been made would have been as to whether the effects of the injury still persisted and, if so, whether they were causing any continuing problem. There is no evidence that the problem was not remedied by the surgical treatment or that the applicant had experienced any further problems after the surgical treatment. We are not sure whether Mr Whithear also wished to rely on the applicant's failure to refer to another accident which had occurred more recently; he had struck his cheek against an object but had suffered no lasting injury. Some accidents are so trivial that it would not have been appropriate for a Commonwealth Medical Officer to have recorded them. Indeed, if the applicant had told him about hitting his shin in April 1969, we can well imagine that the Commonwealth Medical Officer might have expressed some not unreasonable irritation at having his time wasted by the recital of such trivialities. We have no doubt that, by failing to tell the Commonwealth Medical Officer of the accident to and the surgical treatment of his nose, the accident to his cheek and the accident to his shin, the applicant did not fail to furnish or give any information required to be furnished or given by him.

36. Having found that the applicant did not fail to comply with the requirements of section 184(5) in any way, it is unnecessary for us to consider what would have been the effect if he had answered any question in another way. We are satisfied that, because he did not fail to furnish or give any information that he was required to give and did not furnish or give any false or misleading information, neither the Commissioner nor any of his delegates had power to issue a Benefit Classification Certificate in respect of him. That being so, none of them had power to decide that his benefit entitlement was to be determined under section 66(2) of the 1976 Act. Accordingly, the decision under review must be set aside and the appropriate decision made in substitution for it.
