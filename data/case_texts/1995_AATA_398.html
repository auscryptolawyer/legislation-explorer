Thomas Jones Trimnell and Commissioner of Superannuation [1995] AATA 398 (22 December 1995)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

THOMAS JONES TRIMNELL v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V94/1200

AAT No. 10637

Number of pages - 11

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

B.M. FORREST (Deputy President), E.A. SHANAHAN (Member) AND C.G. WOODARD

(Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - stress and anxiety condition and skin disorder - subsequent resignation - whether incapacitated to perform duties at date of resignation - whether permanently incapacitated - exercise of discretion to deem retirement on grounds of incapacity - whether special circumstances exist to justify refusal to exercise discretion - decision set aside.

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 25 July 1995

22:12:1995

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr McInnis

Solicitor for the Applicant: Melville Orton Lewis

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr Ginnane

Solicitor for the Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor,

Canberra

ORDER

The Tribunal decides that when the applicant ceased to be an eligible employee he was unfit to perform the duties of a Clerical Assistant Grade 3.

The decision under review is set aside and the matter remitted to the respondent with a direction that the applicant is to be deemed for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1976 to have been retired on the ground of mental incapacity.

DECISION

B.M. FORREST, E.A. SHANAHAN AND C.G. WOODARD This matter comes before the Tribunal following the dismissal of an appeal to the Full Federal Court reported at [1994] FCA 1234; (1994) 123 ALR 22 from a decision of a judge of the Court, Olney J reported at (1993) 19 AAR 51.

2. His Honour (Olney J) decided that on the proper construction of s. 7(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 "the Act" at the relevant time, the term "his duties" in the phrase "unfit to perform his duties", refers to the duties Mr Trimnell had been required to perform immediately prior to his resignation and not the duties of the substantive office he held at that time.

3. Section 7(2) provides:

"7(2) Where a person has, before attaining his maximum

retiring age, ceased to be an eligible employee otherwise than

by reason of death or by reason of his having been retired, or

his services having been otherwise terminated, on the ground of

physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties, but the

Commissioner is satisfied that, at the time he ceased to be an

eligible employee, he was, by reason of physical or mental

incapacity, unfit to perform his duties, the Commissioner may

direct that the person shall, for the purposes of this Act, be

deemed to have been retired on the ground of that physical or

mental incapacity."

4. Olney J stated that:

"In my opinion, a proper reading of the reasons in Hastings

required the Tribunal in the present case to direct its

attention to the duties the respondent had been required to

perform immediately before his resignation. To the extent that

the Tribunal construed s. 7(2) as not requiring such an inquiry

it erred in law.

By reason of the approach it adopted, the Tribunal

deliberately refrained him making any finding as to the

respondent's capacity to perform the duties of a clerical

assistant class 3. This is not a case in which it can be said

that on the evidence adduced before the Tribunal only one

finding is open on that question. The question needs to be

considered by the fact finding tribunal and accordingly it is

appropriate that the matter be remitted to the Tribunal to be

heard and decided again. In view of the different basis upon

which the case will have to be decided, the Tribunal's approach

to the exercise of the Commissioner's discretion may have to be

different in the event that it is found that the respondent was

unfit to perform the duties to which he had been assigned

immediately before his resignation."

5. The matter therefore proceeded on the basis that the Tribunal consider whether, at the time Mr Trimnell ceased to be an eligible employee, he was unfit to perform the duties of a Clerical Assistant Grade 3 ("CA3") by reason of physical or mental incapacity.

6. The Tribunal heard further evidence from Mr Trimnell and Drs Taylor and Kornan, both psychiatrists. Evidence was also given by another psychiatrist, Dr Cole and by Mr Robert Maguire, a former Commonwealth Public Servant who at the relevant time was Assistant Inspector Staffing and Recruitment, at the Victorian head office of the Department of Social Security.

7. Mr Trimnell gave further evidence which in substance did not expand on that given earlier. Initially when he returned to work in May 1980 he was coping reasonably well but he gradually, from about July onwards, declined and was having difficulty coping. At first he had no difficulties with other employees but increasingly he found he was not fitting in with other staff, who were all much younger than he. He said that when he returned to work there was no discussion with anybody about rehabilitation/redeployment nor were there any options discussed regarding his future. Specifically he had no contact with Mr Maguire. He agreed that for a while he performed quite well in the role of a CA3 and that there had been no complaints about his work. In August 1980 his skin complaint erupted, for which he had treatment from early September. He had 3 weeks recreation leave in August, having been refused a longer period of accrued leave. After he returned to work he was on sick leave from 22 September for the skin disorder and for stress and anxiety. Medical certificates were provided for the absences on sick leave stating he was suffering from nerves, skin disorder, anxiety and depression.

8. In further evidence as to the events surrounding his resignation Mr Trimnell said the consultation with the dermatologist Dr Bekhor on 1 October 1980 was the lynch pin that prompted his resignation, although his assertion that Dr Bekhor had said the skin condition was work related was disputed by Dr Bekhor.

MR TRIMNELL'S 'DUTIES'

9. The Public Service Board, when asking the Department of Social Security on 7 May 1980 to make arrangements for Mr Trimnell to return to work on appropriate light duties, the nature of which might be discussed with the Senior Medical Officer if necessary, forwarded a report from Dr Freed, a psychiatrist. It stated that Mr Trimnell's redeployment should be made subject to him accepting psychiatric treatment and to recommendations being obtained from the treating psychiatrist 'regarding possible effects upon his health of continued employment in any sort of work capacity with the Public Service'.

10. In affidavit and oral evidence Mr Maguire outlined the practice and procedure for redeployment of staff in the Department of Social Security in 1980. Mr Maguire noted the failure of the Department to get a report on Mr Trimnell's capability before ordering his return to work and the absence of any planned work program, and commented that if an officer had been off work with a stress-related condition for 15 months it would be poor practice not to keep him fully informed on his return to work.

11. Mr Maguire gave evidence about consideration at the Melbourne office of Mr Trimnell's duties after his return to work, at the direction of the Personnel Officer in the Department of Social Security in May 1980. Following a satisfactory report of Mr Trimnell's performance of the duties of a CA3 in the Hamilton registry in July 1980, it was decided that he should be redeployed on trial at a more senior level. Mr Maguire recalled visiting Hamilton in connection with this and another staffing matter but could not recall any discussion with Mr Trimnell. There is no reason to doubt Mr Trimnell's evidence that he was never at any time consulted after 14 May about assessment of his performance or possible redeployment.

12. Mr Maguire was the author of a file note in the following terms:

"Mr Trimnell to undergo trial as Field Officer commencing

1/9/80. In the event this does not work out he is to undergo

trial as CA4-either counter officer or new position in Registry.

Reversion to CA3 is considered too drastic at this stage, in any

event, he should probably get preference over Regnier.

Any action to please be discussed with AI(S and R).

RJM

AI(S and R)"

13. The Personnel Officer advised the Regional Manager by memo dated 19 September 1980 that 'Further to our discussion on 18/9/80 Mr Trimnell will occupy a Field Officer Grade 2 position on a trial basis until 31/10/80'. Mr Maguire, who stated in his affidavit that his 'recollection of the situation with respect to Mr Trimnell is limited', was unable to explain the discrepancy in dates between his file note and the memo to Hamilton. He hypothesised that his note should perhaps have read '1/10/80'. A further difficulty is that his affidavit states that 'it was my view at the time that a reasonable length of time to trial any option would have to be at least three months'.

14. There is also no dispute that Mr Trimnell was not informed of the trial redeployment decision. According to his sick leave records, Mr Trimnell was away from 22 September 1980 to 2 October 1980, and from 3 October 1980 to 9 October 1980. As Mr Trimnell did not resume duty after 22 September and was not advised of the decision notified to the Regional Manager at Hamilton by memo of 19 September (and possibly orally on 18 September, or earlier), his 'duties' or the 'duties of his office' (s 67 of the Public Service Act) at the date of his resignation are, in accordance with the Federal Court judgments, those of a CA3 performing the duties to which he was informally redeployed on 14 May 1980. It remains only to add that his duties were full-time.

MR TRIMNELL'S MEDICAL CONDITION

15. Although the Tribunal had the benefit of some further medical evidence, we have also given attention to the previous medical evidence, particularly that arising from direct knowledge of Mr Trimnell's condition in 1980, which was before the earlier hearing, although we then noted its' inadequacies.

16. The preponderance of the medical evidence concludes that Mr Trimnell was not suffering from a physical incapacity which rendered him unfit to perform his duties. The skin condition for which he received recurring medical treatment, including from 25 September 1980, was severe in August/September 1980, when he had an open weeping sore as well as rashes; however the skin condition's relevance lies in its relationship to the underlying stress and psychiatric problems which had necessitated Mr Trimnell's absence from the workplace for 15 months in 1979-80. Mr Trimnell saw the skin condition as associated with stress in the workplace. Dr Fergin, a dermatologist whom he saw on 1 October 1980, associated his eczematous and lichenified conditions with his psychological problems and with his having been on a prolonged course of Phenergan, an anti-depressant. Dr Bekhor, whom he next saw, associated it with stress and the same psychological problems.

17. We accept that Mr Trimnell believed at the time of his resignation that he could not continue in his employment with the Department of Social Security because of stress and associated conditions. His medical certificates for his final periods of sick leave cite nerves, anxiety and depression, as well as skin disorder. The Commissioner argues that Mr Trimnell's mental incapacity was an inability to tolerate stress arising from investigative work and that 'any broadening of Mr Trimnell's incapacity would be purely speculative'. We cannot accept this. Mr Trimnell had not been required to perform investigative work, or work involving duties with the public, for nearly two years before he went on sick leave with stress-related complaints in September. For some time he had appeared to cope well with his duties, which were essentially mundane or mechanical. In August he sought eight weeks' recreation leave and was granted only three, without discussion with him of this decision or of the reasons behind his lengthy leave application. Shortly after returning from recreation leave he was afflicted with a painful skin complaint. Mr Trimnell was suffering from a stressful condition not related to investigative work and which he associated, and continues to associate, with the workplace.

18. As we noted in 1992, the medical reports as to Mr Trimnell's mental state at the time of resignation suffer from a lack of contemporaneity. The skin specialists who did see him in October each saw him once and were locums for the specialist to whom he was referred. The Commissioner submitted that the evidence of the second skin specialist seen, Dr Bekhor, was 'very important', but we adhere to our earlier view that this evidence is not directed toward Mr Trimnell's mental condition. It is not simply a case of a person suffering from a skin complaint but the associated stress and anxiety which we accept Mr Trimnell was suffering from at the time of his resignation. The fact that the skin complaint was making good progress is not necessarily determinative of Mr Trimnell's capacity for work. We therefore do not accept the Commissioner's submission that Dr Bekhor's evidence:

'is quite opposed to any suggestion that Mr Trimnell was

incapacitated for his duties or could not cope with them...It is

inconceivable that in such a medical interview Mr Trimnell would

not have indicated to Dr Bekhor that he was unable to cope with

his duties had there been a solid foundation for that suggestion

at 8 October 1980'.

19. Dr Kornan initially (in 1981) diagnosed Mr Trimnell as being sensitised to the workplace; he envisaged him working again, 'with the exception of perhaps working again for the Department of Social Security'. At the re-hearing he was inclined to resile from this diagnosis, though for reasons which were not convincing, related as they were to Mr Trimnell's later achievements in local government, which if anything seem to prove the point about sensitisation to the particular workplace. He strongly adhered to the view that the subsequent work record showed that Mr Trimnell could have gone back to doing clerical-type duties in the department, to being a field officer. Dr Kornan dated a possible return to work as two to three years after the date of resignation, or at the latest 1985.

20. Dr Taylor saw Mr Trimnell on 20 July 1983 and concluded that:

'at the present time Mr Trimnell is not significantly

incapacitated by his nervous symptoms. He is capable of

undertaking full-time employment but would probably not cope

with work involving severe stress and responsibility or

prolonged contact and confidential dealings with the public'.

21. She noted that because of personality attributes he had difficulty in dealing with hostility expressed toward him. In evidence at the re-hearing she stated that Mr Trimnell's statement that he could not continue was not necessarily indicative of any underlying illness; this statement, though qualified, goes somewhat further than the other psychiatric evidence.

22. Dr Jamieson saw Mr Trimnell on 26 June 1989 and wrote to Mr Trimnell's solicitors:

'Mr Trimnell has a constitutional skin problem of acne and

Seborrhoeic Dermatitis, plus a tendency under stress conditions

to develop Lichen Simplex, (reported by Dr. Fergin) and Flexural

Eczema which is now present.

In answer to your specific questions;

1) There is a definite evidence from Dr Fergins' (sic) report,

and the present examination, that Mr Trimnells' (sic) skin

problem was aggravated by stress. There was enough stress in

his work situation to account for such aggravation.

2) It is difficult for me to state at this time, whether the

skin disease was sufficient to prevent continuation of his work

as Field Officer with the Department of Social Security.

However, the uncomfortable skin complaint, and the emotional

problems evolving from his employment, together, would

constitute a disability - the inability to face up to conflict

stress, which would prevent him from returning to his original

work.'

23. Dr Cole who was asked for an opinion on behalf of Mr Trimnell "on the papers" came to the conclusion that Mr Trimnell was unable to cope with the duties of a CA3.

"Had he been able to do so he would surely have elected to

remain at work rather than resign and forego financial

advantages that he might otherwise have enjoyed. His condition

had obviously improved by the time he was elected to Council,

but this only goes to reinforce the view that his nervous

disorder was work related.

In my experience when a person develops a chronic nervous

disorder in response to long-standing work related stress he

seldom recovers to a stage where he is able to return to his

former duties if only because of past unpleasant associations

with work. Attempts to employ the person within the same

organisation in a less responsible position are often also

doomed to failure because of these associations and because the

person is unable to accept what he perceives to be a demotion or

loss of status which further damages his self esteem."

24. Dr Stephen saw Mr Trimnell more often than any other psychiatrist, though he did not begin doing so until some years after he resigned (1985, when he saw him twice) and he did not treat him. Dr Stephen diagnosed Mr Trimnell in 1985 as having a work-related chronic anxiety state with depressive features, and added:

'If the skin condition is indeed accepted as work related, I

think there is not the slightest doubt that he was and is

unemployable as a clerical officer and even without that, my

opinion is that he is unable to work in that capacity. Indeed,

it is fairly evident that he has a diminished capacity for work

even in employment outside the Department and it would appear

that he is actually capable of no more than about 20 hours a

week in a menial job without responsibility'.

25. When asked at the first hearing to clarify this, Dr Stephen said he was accepting another doctor's view that the skin condition was work-related and said that if that is the case, added to a pretty definite history of the psychiatric element, he is unemployable. Asked by the Commissioner of Superannuation to comment on this report in 1989, Dr Stephen said that 'in my view, the true disability in Mr Trimnell's case which has been recognised by compensation for years, has been his incapacity to withstand conflict stresses'. Dr Stephens concluded that he had no doubt that Mr Trimnell's condition was indeed long term and concluded:

'I assume that his long standing disability leaves no room for

argument that he is never going to return to employment with the

department and his performance more recently has shown he has

very poor tolerance of stress even in work of his own choosing.

I regard him therefore as being in all probability virtually

totally unemployable though there remains the theoretical

possibility that he would be able to manage casual work from

time to time but even there probably only for brief periods'.

26. Dr Stephens re-examined Mr Trimnell in September 1990 and found his health and employment prospects basically unchanged:

'he is virtually unemployable and the only prospect of his

working is part-time and by his account even that cannot be too

strenuous or pressured'.

27. At the time of this last report Dr Stephens noted that Mr Trimnell was Mayor of Hamilton. Dr Stephens' reports are significant because he was the only psychiatrist to examine Mr Trimnell in 1988, 1989, and 1990; years in which, after being elected as a councillor in 1987, he progressed to becoming Deputy Mayor and Mayor of Hamilton. His contemporaneous diagnoses tend in our view to nullify the reasoning by others, Dr Kornan in particular, that the advantage of hindsight is conclusive in pointing to Mr Trimnell as not being permanently incapacitated.

28. The Tribunal has no difficulty in finding on the preponderance of the medical evidence (only Dr Taylor and the CMO dissenting), that Mr Trimnell was incapacitated, by reason of mental incapacity, for work as a CA3 in the Department of Social Security in Hamilton, at the time of his resignation.

29. It is a more difficult matter to decide whether Mr Trimnell was permanently incapacitated. As subsequent events transpired, Mr Trimnell has demonstrated an ability to function at a level which enabled him to pursue other employment and achieve a successful subsequent record of voluntary public service, which on the face of it could be argued to be more demanding than the menial duties of a CA3. Against this we have Mr Trimnell's own evidence of the nature and relative demands and stresses on him of the different duties, which supports the medical hypotheses that he was sensitised to the workplace, being the Department of Social Security in a country centre. Overall, the medical evidence is not particularly persuasive either way but in our view greater weight should be attached to the first-hand evidence of Dr Stephen from 1985; the year in which Dr Kornan would have expected Mr Trimnell to be fit to return to his CA3 duties.

30. Hypothetical though it must necessarily be, we consider that we should also note the sensitisation to the workplace, which again came to a head in 1980, (even when Mr Trimnell was performing mechanical duties) would likely to have been exacerbated by the work program planned for him had he taken up duty at any time after 19 September. All doctors agree that Mr Trimnell had a vulnerable personality and Dr Stephen gave particular emphasis to his sense of aloneness in the bureaucratic environment, illustrated by the inappropriateness of the decision to return him to field officer duties and the unacceptability of those duties.

31. We find on balance that Mr Trimnell was permanently incapacitated to perform the duties of a CA3 in the Department of Social Security from the date of his resignation.

EXERCISE OF DISCRETION

32. We now turn to consider whether the Commissioner should exercise the discretion under s. 7(2) to direct Mr Trimnell is to be deemed to have been retired on the ground of his incapacity, notwithstanding his resignation. As the Federal Court pointed out in Scott v Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 8 AAR 20 the existence of special circumstances may justify a refusal to exercise the discretion given by s. 7(2). The following matters we consider to be relevant.

33. Firstly, the finding of incapacity to perform the duties of a CA3 and the nature of that incapacity.

34. Secondly, the totality of his service from 1959 to 1980 in employment by the Commonwealth in which the superannuation scheme operates.

35. Thirdly, a direction that Mr Trimnell be deemed to have been retired on the ground of invalidity would not adversely affect other eligible employees. In Commissioner for Superannuation v Hastings (1986) 6 AAR 152 the Federal Court said (at 158) that the interests of other contributors to the superannuation fund was also a factor to be considered. We make the observation that given the length of Mr Trimnell's service, being a contributor since 1959, we do not consider the interests of other contributors operate against the exercise of discretion in his favour.

36. Fourthly, at the time Mr Trimnell ceased to be an eligible employee while it could not be said that he did not have some awareness of rights under the Act (as was found to be the case in Scott), as in 1979 he had discussions about retirement on invalidity grounds and signed a consent to be retired, equally, he felt he was unable to continue and considered his rights to obtain retirement on the ground of invalidity had been exhausted, when retirement did not eventuate as he had expected in 1979-80. This is indicated by the terms of his letter of resignation.

37. Fifthly, there is the question of whether there has been an unreasonable delay in seeking invalidity benefits. Mr Trimnell gave evidence that he first discussed compensation and superannuation claims with his solicitor in January 1981. A workers compensation claim was made in 1981 and medical reports were obtained for the purpose of that claim. Determinations in favour of Mr Trimnell were made on 6 March 1984 for aggravation of anxiety and depressive syndrome and on 21 March 1986 for aggravation of a pre-existing skin condition. In evidence, Mr Trimnell said that he had no idea why a claim under s. 7(2) was not made until 1986. The question of Mr Trimnell's entitlements was left in the hands of his solicitors and it was reasonable to do so. The material before the Tribunal would suggest that the superannuation issue was addressed upon termination of incapacity for work payments. Medical reports relevant to the workers compensation claim were made available to the Commissioner. Notwithstanding the delay in the making of the claim we are satisfied the Commissioner has not suffered any prejudice by the delay.

38. We take the view that special circumstances do not exist as would justify a refusal to exercise the discretion in s. 7(2) of the Act.

39. Accordingly, we would direct that for the purposes of the Act Mr Trimnell is to be deemed to have been retired on the ground of mental incapacity.

40. Accordingly, the decision under review is set aside and the matter remitted to the Commissioner with a direction that Mr Trimnell is to be deemed for the purposes of the Act to have been retired on the ground of mental incapacity.
