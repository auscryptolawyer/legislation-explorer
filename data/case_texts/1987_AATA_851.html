Income Tax - housekeeper rebate - meaning of housekeeper whether wholly engaged in keeping house.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss. 159J, 159L

Case R98 84 ATC 642

Case M69 80 ATC 486

REASONS FOR DECISION

Date: 25 November 1987 Mrs H E Hallowes, Senior Member

1. The applicant has objected to his assessment of personal taxation for the year of income ended 30 June, 1984. In his taxation return he claimed a rebate in respect of his spouse for the sum of $1,030 (full year) and a housekeeper $1,030 (engaged 15 July, 1983 to 16 March, 1934). He supported his claims with a medical certificate and declared that he had:

"[W]holly contributed to the support of .......... (mother in law) residing with me for the purposes of full time pre and post natal care of my wife and baby, assisting with children care, and housekeeping during the period 15.7.83 to 16.3.84."

2. The applicant told the Tribunal that his wife does not speak English. In 1983 she was pregnant with their third child. The two older children were born on 16 December, 1977 and 4 October, 1979. His wife was incapacitated by her pregnancy. She became depressed and unable to cope with her responsibilities. The applicant's parents were their only relatives in Australia and they were unable to assist the family. The applicant approached the local council for assistance but was told they would not assist the family "at present". He therefore paid for his mother-in-law aged fifty nine to come to Australia from Poland. She was not allowed to bring any money with her from Poland and the applicant fully supported her for the duration of her stay in Australia. After her arrival the applicant's mother-in-law shared the duties of the household with his wife. She assisted by taking the two older children to school and kindergarten. When. his wife was admitted to hospital for the birth of their third child on 3 September, 1983 his mother-in-law carried out all the duties of the household. The applicant's wife is not in receipt of an invalid pension.

3. The relevant provision of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") is Section 159L which provides:

"159L (1) [Entitlement] Where, during the year of income a person (in this section referred to as a 'housekeeper') is wholly engaged in keeping house in Australia for a taxpayer and in caring for-

(a) a child of the taxpayer less than 16 years of age;

(b) a dependant included in class 3, or a dependant less than 16 years of age included in class 4, in the table in sub-section 159(2) in respect of whom the taxpayer would be entitled to a rebate under section 159J in his assessment in respect of income but for sub-section 159J(1A);

(ba) a dependant included in class 5 in the table in sub-section 159J(2) in respect of whom the taxpayer is entitled to a rebate under section 159J in his assessment in respect of income of the year of income; or

(c) the spouse of the taxpayer, being a spouse in receipt of an invalid pension under the Social Security Act 1947, the taxpayer is entitled, in his assessment in respect of income of that year of income, to a rebate of tax ascertained in accordance with this section.

159L (3) [Part year - not wholly engaged] Where, by reason of the fact that during a part of the year of income, the taxpayer contributes to the maintenance of a dependant included in class 1 or class 2 in the table in sub-section 159J(2), not being a dependant specified in paragraph (1)(c) of this section, the taxpayer is entitled to a rebate of tax under section 159J in his assessment in respect of income of the year of income, a housekeeper shall be deemed not to have been wholly engaged in keeping house for the taxpayer during that part of the year of income.

The Guide provided by the Commissioner for taxpayers completing a S Salary and Wages Taxation Return gives advice to tax payers that :

ITEM 31 : HOUSEKEEPER

"You may claim a rebate of 3830 (or $1030 if you have maintained a dependent child or student during the year) if, for the whole of the year, you had a housekeeper and:

you were single, widowed or divorced; or if married, your spouse was an invalid pensioner; and the housekeeper was wholly engaged in keeping house for you and in caring fort

your children under 16 your spouse who is in receipt of an

invalid pension; an invalid relative for whom you are entitled to a rebate at Item 29; or a child under 16 dependent on you and whose separate net income was less than the appropriate amount set out in the notes under the heading 'ITEM 27: CHILD UNDER 16 OR STUDENT UNDER 25' above.

If the above conditions applied for only part of the year, you must claim only a proportion of the maximum rebate otherwise allowable. The rebate does not apply for any period during which you maintained either a spouse not receiving an invalid pension or a daughter-housekeeper, unless special circumstances exist."

4. The applicant told the Tribunal that he sought advice from an officer of the respondent and was advised that in special circumstances a rebate may apply. It was the respondent's contention that during a relevant year of income a taxpayer is entitled to a rebate for a spouse or a housekeeper but that:

"[T]he section [159L (3)] is mandatory and it deems that that housekeeper is not to be considered wholly engaged for the period that the taxpayer is entitled to, either a spouse or a daughter - housekeeper rebate."

The taxpayer received a rebate pursuant to Section 159J of the Act in respect of the full year at the maximum amount allowable. Section 159J provides:

159J. (1) Where, during the year of income, a taxpayer contributes to the maintenance of a person (in this section referred to as a 'dependant') specified in column 2 of the table set out in sub-section (2) and that person is a resident, the taxpayer is entitled, in his assessment in respect of income of that year of income, to a rebate of tax ascertained in accordance with this section.

(1A) A taxpayer is not entitled in his assessment in respect of income of a year of income after the year of income that ends on 30 June 1976 to a rebate under this section in respect of a person by reason that the person is included in class 3 or class 4 in the table set out in sub-section (2).

(1B) Where, but for sub-section (1A), a taxpayer would be entitled in his assessment in respect of income of a year of income to a rebate under this section in respect of a dependant included in class 3 or 4 in the table in sub-section (2), the entitlement of the taxpayer to a rebate under this section in that assessment in respect of any dependant included in class 1 or 2 of that table shall

be calculated as if the reference in that table of $830 were references to $1,030.

(2) Subject to this section, the amount of the rebate allowable in the assessment of the taxpayer in respect of a dependant under this section is the relevant amount specified in column 3 of the following table:

Column 1 Column 2 Column 3

Class Dependant Amounts of Rebate

1 Spouse of the taxpayer. $830

2 Daughter-housekeeper. $830

3 Child less than 16 years or age

(not being a student) In respect of 1 such child

In respect of each other

such child - $282

4 Student........................... $376

5 Invalid relative. $376

  1. Parent of the taxpayer or of his spouse. $749"

5. Sub-section 159L (4) of the Act provides:

"159L(4) [Housekeeper not caring for invalid spouse] Where a taxpayer is married and the housekeeper is not, during the year of income, engaged in caring for the spouse of the taxpayer, being a spouse in receipt of an invalid pension under the Social Security Act 1947-

(a) he is not entitled to a rebate under this section in his assessment in respect of income of the year of income unless the Commissioner is of the opinion that, because of special circumstances, it is just to allow a rebate; and

(b) the rebate (if any) shall be of such amount, not exceeding the amount in sub-section (2) in relation to the taxpayer as, in the opinion of the Commissioner, is reasonable in the circumstances. -

Mr Zosars, appearing for the respondent, put to the Tribunal that sub -section 159L(3) overrides sub-section 159L(4) of the Act so that :

"[I]n any case where a taxpayer receives either a spouse rebate or a housekeeper rebate during a year of income then for any period of that year that that rebate entitlement exists irrespective of whether a housekeeper is considered to be wholly engaged, the Act specifically excludes the entitlement to a tax payer of receiving another rebate for the housekeeper at that same time. It is either a matter of: you take the spouse rebate or you take the housekeeper rebate. You cannot have both money concurrently.''

6. In Case R98 (1984) ATC 642 a taxpayer similarly engaged a housekeeper to look after his children and do general housekeeping duties while his wife was in hospital. No relatives were available. The Board of Review disallowed the claim as the housekeeper was not "wholly engaged" in keeping house for the taxpayer within the meaning of sub-section 159L(1) of the Act because she worked limited days and hours. It was therefore unnecessary for the Board to consider whether because of special circumstances in the terms of sub-section 159L (4) it was just to allow a rebate. Mr H P Stevens, Chairman did indicate however that he adhered to the tentative view that:

"[T]he section read as a whole provides for a 'housekeeper' rebate in addition to a 'spouse' rebate only where the spouse is in receipt of an invalid pension. As far as I am aware this is the first case where a housekeeper rebate has been claimed in circumstances where a spouse rebate has been allowed and the spouse is not in receipt of an invalid pension. To my mind subsec, (3) operates in such circumstances according to its terms, i.e. despite the satisfaction of the 'wholly engaged' requirement of sec. 159L(1) such 'housekeeper shall be deemed not to have been wholly engaged in keeping house for the taxpayer'. The Commissioner argued that subsec. (4) must be read subject to subsec. (1) so as to incorporate the 'wholly engaged' requirement and I can see no reason why subsec. (3), which qualifies subsec. (1) and which deems such requirement not to have been satisfied should be ignored."

In deciding the question whether a person is "wholly engaged in keeping house" Mr TJ McCarthy (Member) said:

"It has been said that the relevant person should in fact control the household affairs, such as ordering supplies, settling accounts etc., as distinct from merely being a. servant who performs domestic duties: Gunn's Commonwealth Income Tax Law and Practice, 4th ed., 1954, at p 671."

He went on to say that:

"In previous times there may have been a number of domestic servants in the household under the control of a housekeeper who in fact carried out such functions as ordering supplies and paying accounts. However, in a smaller household this kind of housekeeper might fulfil the requirement of 'keeping house' by being involved only a few hours a week, and caring for a dependant of the taxpayer such as a child who attends school might only occupy a few hours a day, so that this perhaps explains the draftman's use of the words 'wholly engaged'.

Thus the words 'wholly engaged' suggest to me a full-time presence or commitment (within the time scale of a normal working week) but not necessarily a person who lives in.

The requirement that the person must keep house 'for a taxpayer' suggests, in the context of sec. 159L and surrounding provisions, that the person 'be wholly engaged in a household the primary responsibility for the support and maintenance of which has fallen upon [or continued with] the taxpayer Case No. E41 (1954) 5 TBRD 246."

Mr McCarthy formed the view that the housekeeper was "wholly engaged in keeping house" for the tax-payer. In reversing in part the Commissioner's decision upon the objection, Mr McCarthy noted that the Commissioner in that application had conceded that the terms of sub-section 159L(3) did not override the terms of sub-section 159L(4). Mr PM Roach, Member (now a Senior Member of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal) in considering sub-sections (3) and (4) of section 159L said:

"It is a matter of particular note that there is nothing in the section or in the Division to indicate whether subsec. (3) or subsec. (4) prevails over the other of them."

He pointed out that the material provisions of sub-section (4) resulted in a tax-payer being disqualified from claiming the rebate unless he could establish special circumstances and that because of those circumstances it was "just" that the rebate be allowed. He went on to say that as a consequence of sub-section (3), if considered alone, the tax-payer could not claim the rebate in relation to a spouse who was not in receipt of an invalid pension and also claim a rebate in respect of a housekeeper in relation to any common period. He said that:

"[B]y its terms subsec. (3) operates whenever the taxpayer contributes to the maintenance of his spouse and in consequence is entitled to a rebate, whether the rebate is claimed or not. Therefore, I conclude that if subsec (3) prevails over subs (4) the taxpayer's claim must fail.

If subsec (3) prevails over (4), then the latter subsection is limited in its operation to married taxpayers who do not contribute to the maintenance of a spouse or whose spouse is not resident in Australia. If subsec (4) prevails over subsec (3), then subsec (3) stands in full force and effect subject only to the exception that the rebate may be allowed if there are special circumstances it would be just to allow the rebate."

Mr Roach adopted the following construction:

"(a) that a taxpayer who contributes to the maintenance of his spouse resident in Australia is entitled to a rebate pursuant to sec 159J;

(b) that a taxpayer who has no such spouse is entitled to a rebate in respect of a housekeeper if the housekeeper is wholly engaged in keeping house for the taxpayer and a dependant child under 16 years of age (sec 159L(1)); and

(c) that a taxpayer who contributes to the maintenance of his spouse and also retains a housekeeper to keep house for him and care for a child shall be entitled to sec. 159H rebates in respect of both the spouse and the child, but shall not be entitled to a sec 159L(1) rebate for the housekeeper (sec 159L(3) unless either-

(i) the spouse is an invalid pensioner (as defined) (sec 159L(1)(c); or

(ii) special circumstances exist, such that it would be just to allow a further rebate (sec 159(4)).

However, the applicant did not succeed in that application as Mr Roach felt constrained to follow the construction given to the words "wholly engaged" by earlier Boards.

7. Mr Zosars traced the history of the relevant sections and made reference to explanatory notes and memorandum circulated by the then Treasurer, the Right Honourable A W Fadden with the Income Tax and Social Services Contribution Assessment Bill 1950 where in reference to sections 82B and 82D(4) of the Act, the precursor of sub-section 159L(4), they stated:

"As a general rule. a taxpayer who is married will be entitled to the spouse allowance and not to a housekeeper allowance. Where however, a married taxpayer is not entitled to the spouse allowance and special circumstances exist, the Commissioner is empowered to allow the taxpayer a deduction for a housekeeper of B101 or such lesser amount as is reasonable. An example of this class of case is where a husband is deserted by his wife and he is obliged to engage a housekeeper to care for his children under sixteen years of age.

The provisions in the present law for a partial allowance in appropriate cases are being retained in the proposed new section. The effect of sub-section (2.1 of section 82B for example is that where a taxpayer is entitled to a partial deduction for a spouse or daughter-housekeeper by reason of maintaining such a dependant for part only of the year, he will be eligible for a housekeeper allowance during the balance of that year."

8. The report on Income Tax Concessional Allowances for Dependants presented to the Parliament of the Commonwealth of Australia by the Commonwealth Committee on Taxation on 10 October, 1951 in making recommendations regarding qualifying conditions for concessional deductions said:

"22. Housekeeper - The deduction should be allowed where the housekeeper is wholly engaged in keeping house in Australia for a resident taxpayer and has the care of any child under sixteen or any invalid person over sixteen in respect of whom the taxpayer is entitled to a dependant's allowance.

  1. As such a provision would allow a widowed taxpayer a deduction for a female relative keeping house for him and caring for any of his children or stepchildren under sixteen, specific provisions relating to a "female relative" are not necessary.
  2. The deduction should not be allowed to a married taxpayer unless the Commissioner considers there exist special circumstances justifying such allowance, or where the taxpayer has been deserted by his wife.

25. A taxpayer should not, however, be allowed a deduction for a spouse and a deduction for a housekeeper."

9. The Tribunal must decide whether the applicant's mother-in-law fails within the definition of "housekeeper" for the purposes of section 159L of the Act and if so, whether the applicant is entitled to a rebate in respect of her during the same financial year in which he is entitled to a rebate for his spouse. Mr Zosars put to the Tribunal that the applicant's mother-in-law was not wholly engaged in keeping house. The provisions of sub-section 159L (1) were the subject of Case M69 80 ATC 486 where the No 3 Board of Review said:

"In other words, 'wholly' is not so much descriptive of the nature of the work but its quantum. It conjures up the notion of an entirety, to the exclusion of everything else. It follows that part-time domestic help can never qualify for a rebate - in whole or in part - in reliance on a section which affords relief only to the engagement of a person who 'is wholly engaged in keeping house';"

In Case N19 81 ATC 98 the No 2 Board in deciding whether a housekeeper was wholly engaged in keeping house, had no difficulty in reaching the conclusion that she was so engaged.

"The housekeeper worked no less number of hours than if she was engaged elsewhere in full time employment, and indeed probably worked more."

10. For the applicant to succeed in his objection the Tribunal must be satisfied that the mother-in-law was wholly engaged in keeping house and caring for a child, dependant, or invalid spouse as provided in section 159L of the Act. On the evidence before me I am unable so to find. The mother-in-law took the children to school and engaged in domestic activities. She also provided emotional support for her daughter. The section requires a person to be wholly engaged in keeping house. Even if the person is wholly engaged in domestic duties such as cleaning, washing, ironing and the preparation of meals, "keeping house" denotes more than this. Embodied in the concept is responsibility for and control over decision making which affects the day to day running of the household thus relieving the taxpayer of this burden. Added to this is the responsibility of caring for a child of less than 16 years of age or a dependant. Each application depends on the individual circumstances of the taxpayer. I am satisfied that during her time in Australia, the taxpayer's mother-in-law was caring for two of the taxpayer's children less than 16 years of age but I am not satisfied that she was "keeping house". She did not carry the responsibility of decision making for the household. She assisted with domestic tasks but this is not sufficient to satisfy the requirements of the legislation. She had a full-time presence in the household but the primary responsibility for the household was not the tax payer's and delegated by him to his mother-in-law, rather his wife retained the responsibility for the household.

  1. For the above reasons the Tribunal will affirm the objection decision under review. It is unnecessary for the Tribunal to decided whether sub-section 153L (3) overrides sub-section 159L (4) of the Act.
  2. The taxpayer relied on the advice of an officer of the respondent in submitting his claim and on the respondent's Guide to the taxpayers. The wording of Item 31 may be misleading to some taxpayers.
