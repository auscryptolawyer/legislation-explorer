ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

INCOME TAX - Assessable Income – Default Assessment following investigation - Failure to allow for cost of known class of expenses - Whether assessments excessive

INCOME TAX - Practice and Procedure - Objection to default assessment - Objection failing to expressly challenge exercise of discretion as to remission of additional tax for incorrect return - Whether exercise of discretion reviewable

INCOME TAX - Additional tax for incorrect return - Reviews of exercise of discretion as to remissions

) TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION ) .

First Applicant

Nos NT87/3885-3887

And:

Second Applicant

Nos NT87/3891-93

DECISION

Tribunal : Mr P M Roach (Senior Member)

Date : 20 October 1989

Decision : The decision of the Tribunal is that the determinations of the Commissioner under review shall be varied to reduce the taxable income of each applicant by $14,100; $14,800 and $15,600, and additional tax for each applicant to $2,100; $1,750 and $1,400 for the years of income ended 30 June 1982, 1983 and 1984 respectively.

P M Roach

(Senior Member)

) TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

First Applicant

REASONS FOR DECISION

20 October 1989 Mr P M Roach (Senior Member)

1. The Commissioner has alleged that in the three-year period ended 30 June 1984 the Applicants - two men who conducted a small suburban shop - understated their taxable incomes. Both have turned 60 years of age. They migrated to Australia from the area of the Mediterranean in the early 1950's. English is not their native language. Each worked as an employee for many years before becoming engaged in shop keeping. Neither ever married. In 1970, together with the sister of one of them, they bought a suburban freehold which they now jointly own. It is unencumbered. It comprises at ground level a hop facing street frontage with some living accommodation to the rear, and at the upper level there Is bedroom accommodation for each of the three owners. The two men conduct the shop as partners. The lady from time to time worked in the shop for the partnership as an employee.

2. As shop-keepers they conducted a "cash" business. By that I mean a business in which the vast bulk of sales was effected upon the exchange of goods for notes and coin of the realm. As the business of selling was only carried out by the proprietors or their close friend, it was not necessary for them to issue receipts in their own interest. It was also a "cash" business in the sense that most purchases of stock were effected under similar conditions. All purchases were paid for in cash but with payment commonly, but not invariably, being made against some documentation by way of an Invoice, delivery docket or statement of account. Bread and milk were delivered to the shop on a daily basis and purchases of fruit and vegetables were made at the markets ordinarily twice, and sometimes three times, per week. Milk and bread were paid for weekly in cash on the basis of dockets provided by suppliers. Market purchases for the shop, whether of fruits, vegetables, cigarettes, or other items, were effected as simply as sales were effected from the shop, but with dockets on some occasions being provided: goods were simply exchanged for cash. There was no need, as between vendor and purchaser, for invoices or accounting statements.

3. But it was also a "cash" business in the sense that the partnership operated no bank account. Each partner had his own small savings account, but they had no joint accounts. Surplus moneys were simply held in cash at the premises. So it was that funds were available from time to time to enable routine purchases to be made: frequently at the markets; for items such as bread, milk and the like weekly; for accounts, such as municipal rates, to be paid periodically; and for the personal assessments to income tax against the Applicants to be satisfied annually.

4. Unusual though it may have been, the partners claimed to have maintained a very simple and basic record of their transactions. It was a system which was quite capable of producing an accurate record of their financial activities. Notes were made of purchases of stock as they occurred; and daily of takings. These, it is said, were transposed into books recording sales and purchases. It was also the practice of the partners to preserve such accounts, receipts and similar documents as were delivered by suppliers. The method of collation of the latter documents was neither neat nor orderly. The records which they maintained certainly lacked the format of neat records maintained on computer or by a diligent clerk. But it did not thereby follow that what was recorded was inaccurate. Errors neatly preserved on computer remain errors. To the extent to which the system described was completely and accurately maintained, it constituted a primary record which - with difficulty - could be re-presented to verify at least a large part ol the records of the purchases and sales.

5. As to the taxpayers themselves it is appropriate to say that Peter, now aged in his 65th year, arrived in Australia in 1951; and Paul, now in his 61st year, arrived in 1952. I find that Paul was the dominant partner and principal spokesman for the partners in its business dealings and in its dealings with officers of the Commissioner.

  1. Peter was principally concerned in conducting the shop. It opened Monday to Friday at 7-30 a.m. and at 6-00 a.m. on Saturdays. After the doors were closed of an evening at 7-30 p.m. (6 p.m. Saturday), Peter would clean up the premises and prepare for the next day. He also completed the financial entries for the day and passed the records to Paul. His evidence was that he never learned to drive a car and rarely left the premises. When he did so, it was to travel with Paul and they would go to visit friends. His only assets apart from his interest in the freehold in the business (including a half interest in a 1975 station wagon) is some $27,700 which he has accumulated in a savings account.
  2. Paul was dominant. He did the outside buying. On two to three mornings a week he would go to the markets in the early hours, returning late in the morning. He took the records of the day and then added them up. I am satisfied that, if there were understatements of income, they were occasioned by Paul rather than by Peter. His evidence was that each lived a very quiet life, smoking and drinking only occasionally and never gambling. His evidence was that his only asset part from an interest in the freehold and in the business and motor vehicle was a savings account of some $8,000.
  3. The assessments in question arose following a field audit carried out by a young investigator. The investigator initially made contact with Paul by phone and arranged to visit the premises. Both partners were in attendance when he called. Paul was the principal spokesman. Paul handed to the investigator some summary figures and advised the investigator that there were no business bank accounts. Of his own initiative but supported by the consent of the Applicants, the investigator spoke with the accountant to the taxpayers. According to the evidence of the investigator, the accountant did nothing to protect the interests of his clients. Even though stating -correctly - that he had not prepared the relevant income tax returns, he volunteered to the investigator that he had no confidence in the accuracy of any figures which the partnership would be likely to present. I place no reliance whatever on the statements attributed to the accountant.

9. I accept that the interviewer returned to the shop on other occasions seeking to clarify problems arising from his examination of documents initially produced to him and also further information later presented to him. I find that his visits were brief and that, in view of the information presented to him and the manner in which it was presented, his doubts were completely understandable. However, I also find that on one occasion he called with a view to collecting original documentation which had been collated for him at his request. A box of material was made available to him, but he did not take it.

10. Paul's evidence as to the production and the availability of relevant original documents was most unsatisfactory. It varied during the hearing. Some documents were produced in a large green plastic garbage bag. It was said to contain the documents the interviewer had failed to take possession of. It was acknowledged that the investigator had not removed them and that he had not examined them prior to the commencement of the hearing. Then there was another aspect of the evidence. It was contended by Paul that relevant documents were available for inspection at the shop. An adjournment was allowed to enable the documents to be produced, but they were not produced. It was said that the documents which had remained at the shop had proved on examination not to be as described in evidence.

  1. Although the investigator had not examined the original records made available to him when they were produced, he did examine them during the hearing. But that was only after Paul had given evidence. When in due course the investigator came to give evidence, he described the contents of the bag and stated that the documents have been collated into groups. The groups comprised, inter alia, sets of documents the original dates of which have been altered; and other sets as to which the documents have been mutilated to remove the dates. An examination of the items received as exhibits confirmed the accuracy of that evidence.
  2. Because that examination only took place following the departure of Peter and of Paul from the witness box, Counsel for the Commissioner had had no opportunity to put to Paul who had presented the documents, or to Peter, in the course of cross-examination the assertions to be later made by the investigator. That being so the Applicants were given the opportunity to present further evidence in rebuttal, either to controvert or to explain away the evidence of the investigator. The opportunity was not availed of.
  3. I am satisfied that the difficulties the investigator had encountered in his investigation went beyond difficulties occasioned by problems of communication or as to presentation of documents. I am satisfied that his suspicions as to the possibility of understatement were only confirmed by his examinations. The result was that the investigator reported to his superiors his inability to obtain explanations from the Applicants refuting the possibility of an under-statement perceived by him of the order of $50,000 in the year of income ended 30 June 1984. That possibility was based on an examination of such records as had been made available to him. But in the making of it no allowance was made for the possibility of cash outlays on purchases from the markets.
  4. One result was that after no more than three quite brief interviews of the Applicants and the examination of obviously incomplete records, a decision was taken to raise assessments against the taxpayers, but without any allowance being made for cash purchases. The assessments so made were as follows -

15. One other matter needs to be mentioned. Paul was cross-examined and it was thereby established that the shop premises had been raided by police and goods removed. That was in December 1986 - well after the period under review. It was also established that there had been proceedings by way of prosecution against Paul which did not result in a conviction; and proceedings by Paul seeking recovery of the goods which had been seized - proceedings which did not result in their recovery. I found those matters to be of no great significance. But what is significant in relation to the general issue is that it is not disputed that Paul had possession at the shop of, inter alia, some 23 cordless drills - a circumstance not to be explained by reference to any ordinary feature of trade as conducted from the shop premises and not acceptedly explained by reference to any "hobby". Other items included large quantities of batteries and of dead-locks.

16. The conclusion is inescapable that Paul endeavoured to persuade me that the assessments were excessive by (inter alia) presenting documents which upon close examination proved to have been falsified. But it does not automatically or necessarily follow that the basic contentions of the Applicants are untrue. However, the significance of that generalisation is diminished when it is found that Paul did not take any opportunity to explain the falsified documents and did not submit himself to cross-examination upon them. If the dishonesty was an attempt to persuade of the truth of a true story, in the absence of relevant evidence from the taxpayers, it failed of its purpose. On the other hand, there are a number of factors which suggest that the assessments are in fact excessive. First, the unchallenged evidence of both Applicants is that their assets apart from the freehold are very few. Unless they enjoy an extravagant standard of living, or they have been generous donors, or have suffered substantial capital losses their asset position is hardly consistent with them having derived substantial levels of income over even three years, let alone over a much a longer period. If the Commissioner is correct in his contentions it is hardly likely that the practice of evasion only commenced on 1 July 1981. I am satisfied that there have been no substantial losses of capital and that neither Applicant has been an exceptionally generous donor. As to their standard of living, although it may well be that the incidence of gambling may have been under-played and the losses therefrom under-stated, I am satisfied that they have not enjoyed an extravagant lifestyle. The evidence, such as it is, suggests a very simple lifestyle.

  1. Another concern is the approach adopted by the Commissioner in fixing upon the figures for which the assessments issued. The investigator was troubled, and reasonably so, by the absence of any partnership bank account; the absence of conventionally kept records; and the apparent inaccuracies in, and incompleteness of the records presented to him. I accept that his perceptions were accurately recorded in minutes he maintained as a record of the interviews he had conducted. On his initial analysis of documents he found $51953 of the claimed expenses not accounted for. Paul advised him that the amount must have represented purchases in cash from the markets. That was in October 1984.
  2. The subject of the short-fall was again discussed at the following month and the investigator recorded the substance of that dialogue with Paul. That Interview took place on Thursday, 14 November 1984. The minute read as follows -

"Further discussion on shortfall between invoices and expenses claimed.

Tpr explained the difference relates to fruit and vegetables purchased from the "markets" for cash. The Tpr argued that the difference of $51953 was reasonable as he went to the markets twice a week and spent approximately $500 on each occasion. To support his argument, he produced a piece of paper listing produce purchased on the Monday, (11-11-84) totalling $429. The Tpr stated he would be going to the markets again on Friday. From observing the turnover of the shop it appeared the Tpr had purchased in excess of normal requirements for our benefit and it was not feasible that the Tpr would have to replenish his stocks for at least another week. Therefore, the claim for frequent vegetables of approximately $1000 per week is excessive and would be more realistically around the $300 mark. –

.....”

I note in passing that even if there had been a specially heavy buying that would not seem to avoid the need to purchase fresh stock within the week.

The final record to which I refer was a record of 16 April 1985, prepared shortly before the issue of the amended assessments as a "Supplementary Report". It outlined the history of the investigation; of the Commissioner's queries; and of the reliance by the Applicants on alleged market purchases. The minute recorded, inter alia, that the examination of invoices for the 1984 year -

"revealed that the invoices totalled approximately $50,000 less than the amount claimed for purchases"

and recorded the view that -

"it is unrealistic that the Tprs would be purchasing approximately $1,000 worth of fruit/veg each week".

The minute went on to record that, after attempts by a superior to communicate with the accountant had failed,

"it was then decided the only option was to raise the amended assessments based on the apparent over-statement of purchases. In accordance with the instruction by Mr ... the partnership net incomes are to be increased by following amounts -

  1. - $45,000
  2. - $47,500
  3. - $50,000"

The amended assessments duly issued on that basis to Peter on 11 June 1985 and to Paul on 1 July 1985.

  1. As a result of the decision minuted in April 1985, no allowance at all was made for cash purchases at the markets even though it seems to be undisputed that there were some such purchases. That is a matter of concern when the record shows that the first of two purchases in one particular week was in fact $492 and that the investigator, although thinking that to be atypical, had thought that such purchases would be "more realistically around the $300 (per week) mark".
  2. The Tribunal in some respects is not in as good a position as the investigator to make such judgments: it did not see the shop by physical inspection or in photographs, or observe its stock, or watch its trading. But such evidence as there is suggests that it was not a thriving store. Despite the long trading hours, frequently only Peter was there and the lady's hours of work were limited. Her wage for the 1984 year was $7,098. Further at 30 June 1984 the depreciated value of all plant and fittings was only $798 and of the car $352.
  3. Over and above that, although the investigator had formed the view that $300 per week was the more realistic rate of cash purchases, no provision at all was made to bring such outlays to account. Had the 1984 income of the partnership been increased by $34,400 rather than by $50,000 I might not have been persuaded that the assessments were excessive. But, no provision having been made for such cash purchases, I am persuaded that the assessments are excessive and that it is my responsibility to form a judgment as to the extent of that and to give effect to it.
  4. Doing the best I can, and weighing all of the factors which have been brought to my attention, I consider that the partnership income has been determined at an excessive figure and that it is excessive in sums of $28,200, $29,600 and $31,200 in the years of income ended 30 June 1982 to 1984 respectively.

ADDITIONAL TAX

23. The Applicants ask that the Tribunal should review the Commissioner's exercise of discretion in remitting additional tax for incorrect returns; and that it should direct further remissions than have been allowed. The Commissioner opposes the application. First, that opposition is based upon a contention that the Commissioner's exercise of discretion in that regard was not the subject of any challenge by any ground of objection expressed within the notice of objection. Secondly, it is contended that that deficiency is not curable by having resort to the provisions of s.190 of the Act which as amended now provides that (immaterial words are omitted) -

"In proceedings under this Part on a review before the Tribunal ...

(a) the taxpayer shall, unless the Tribunal ... otherwise orders, be limited to the grounds stated in his objection; and"

If the Commissioner is correct in those contentions not only will the harshness and injustice of tax litigation procedures pre-July 1986 be preserved in one significant area of contention, but all who assume responsibility for drafting objections will need to again constantly remind themselves of the dangers of that undertaking and of the risk of liability for negligence should their drafting skills be found wanting.

  1. The scope of the Tribunal's power to grant relief on grounds not specified in notices of objection has been considered on several occasions. I refer to my own Decisions (Cf. Case V102 (88 A.T.C. 657) and Case V169 (88 A.T.C. 1126) and to what is now the most recent statement on the issue: the decision of the President in Case W65 (89 A.T.C. 590).
  2. Before applying the principles expressed in those decisions it is appropriate to observe that it is accepted by the Commissioner that one consequence of any decision to reduce taxable income will be a reduction in additional tax for incorrect return. Fur her, it seems to be accepted by the Commissioner that such a reduction would follow even though additional tax levied by the assessments under review would still be less than 200 per cent of tax avoided as re-calculated following a decision to reduce taxable income. The result in a very practical sense is that additional tax for incorrect return is accepted as being a matter before the Tribunal.

26. In the circumstances of this case, in my view, it is within the power of the Tribunal to grant the relief sought by the Applicants. The Applicants in each instance have objected to "the assessment of income tax" as raised by each of the relevant notices of amended assessments. Those assessments were made by the Commissioner so as to involve not only to the determination of taxable income but also "of the tax payable thereon" (s 166). The tax payable thereon falls into two parts: the amount of tax (the primary or principal tax) leviable against taxpayers such as the present Applicants calculated in accordance with the appropriate provisions of the relevant Rating Act; and the amount of tax ("accessory" to the "principal tax" in the words of Dixon J in Richardson v F.C.T. [1932] HCA 67; (1932) 48 C.L.R. 192 at 204) leviable by force of the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act") such as s 223. That section, as did its predecessor s 226, imposes liability to tax additional to the primary tax first mentioned - but for all that it is additional and it is "tax". That is not to say that is for all purposes "tax". As the High Court of Australia held in Re Dymond [1959] HCA 22; (1959) 101 C.L.R. 11), for some purposes it is appropriate to say that the statutory provisions creating the liability

"impose not a tax but a penalty".

such a distinction was also made by Davies J in Healey v F.C.T. (89 ATC 4,152) in construing the provisions of Division 7 of the Act. But such distinctions for those purposes are not relevant in the present circumstances. The applicants have clearly protested the assessment of all that they became liable to pay on the assessment.

27. So it is that, just as a challenge to primary tax may be based on a proposition that taxable income is excessive, or that the primary tax has been incorrectly calculated mathematically, or that the incorrect rate of tax has been applied, so too there may be a challenge to an assessment of tax which involves not only a challenge to the primary tax but also to any other liability in tax imposed by the assessment, whether referred to as "additional tax" or not. It follows that a challenge to the imposition of additional tax for incorrect return is reviewable in the circumstances of this case. But the more particular question is whether the review may extend to a review of the exercise of discretion as to remissions. In my view, the answer is "yes". The imposition of "additional tax" may be erroneous because, taxable income having been found to be excessive, the calculation of tax avoided or, more particularly, 200% of tax avoided was excessive, or because in calculating the additional tax to be levied after remissions, calculations have been based upon an excessive amount of tax avoided. Alternatively, the imposition of additional tax may be excessive because of the exercise of discretion as to remissions. I am satisfied that provided the imposition of "additional tax" has been challenged, the Tribunal may grant relief on any of those grounds.

REMISSIONS

  1. Counsel for the Commissioner has conceded that if additional tax is to be reviewed, the compensation factor should be reduced from 20% per annum to 14.026% per annum in accordance with the Commissioner's Ruling No 2517. I consider the concession to be proper and appropriate. It remains to consider the question of a "culpability factor". A "culpability factor" has been taken to account at 55%. That was done on the basis that, at the time the assessments were raised, it was normative to apply a "culpability factor" of 40% across an extraordinarily broad range of cases; and to increase it where "aggravating factors" were considered to exist. In relation to these taxpayers the figure determined upon was 55%.
  2. In reviewing that decision I take substantial account of the criteria expressed in Income Tax Ruling No 2517. In fixing penalties, I do not take into account the "cash" nature of the business as an aggravating factor. It may be easier for persons in cash businesses to evade tax. But it is just as easy for such persons to be honest: as honest as anyone else. Secondly, I have regard to the unsophisticated character of the Applicants. In my judgment that goes a long way towards explaining their record keeping and the inadequacy of their records. But those inadequacies are not such as to be explained away as simple errors occasioned by carelessness. The magnitude of the under-statement is such that they should have been well aware of it. To that extent I am satisfied that the under-statements were deliberate. I exclude from account any conduct before me and indeed in this instance all conduct subsequent to the assessments. I find that the Applicants were in fact uncooperative during the course of the investigation, but I am not persuaded that they are uncooperative to such a degree as is deserving of any special censure or sanction.

30. All in all I consider that a "culpability factor" of the order of 40% of tax avoided would be more appropriate than 55% of tax avoided.

  1. In applying the foregoing principles I have not acted with mathematical exactitude in calculating figures or in applying percentages. Nor have I had regard to the slight differences in penalties to be applied on each taxpayer which would be appropriate if mathematical precision was the principal objective. Instead, to reflect the discretionary nature of the exercise, I have adopted the same figures for each Applicant and fixed on lump sums.
  2. The order of the Tribunal will be that for each Applicant in relation to the years of income ended 30 June 1982, 1983, and 1984 taxable income shall be reduced by $14,100, $14,800 and $15,600; and additional tax to $2,100, $1,750 and $1,400 respectively.
