the Taxpayers and the Commissioner of Taxation [1997] AATA 178 (27 May 1997)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

THE TAXPAYERS v. THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. NT95/109-111 and

NT95/112-114

AAT No. YYYY

Number of pages - 6

Evidence



COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

J. BLOCK (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

HEARING

SYDNEY, 1 April 1997 (hearing), 27 May 1997 (decision)

27:5:1997

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr BR Pape

Solicitor for Applicant: Mr G McElvenny

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr D McGovern

Solicitor for the Respondent: Ms P Hannigan of the Australian

Government Solicitor

ORDER

That of the documents summonsed, legal professional privilege applied, excepting only in relation to that document or that part of it in respect of which a witness for the Taxpayers refreshed his memory, and in respect of which privilege was waived.

DECISION

J. BLOCK

A Summons to Produce Documents dated 14 May 1997 addressed to Mr Graham McElvenny, was issued at the request of the Respondent. Mr McElvenny is the solicitor for the Applicants. The Summons required the production of "The ledgers and related documents of Mr V (or copies thereof) which were provided to Mr Pape as referred to on pages 113-114 of the transcript of the proceedings on 18 April 1997".

The documents sought by the Summons appear to relate to the fact that Mr V, in the course of cross-examination by the Respondent's Counsel, Mr McGovern, on 18 April 1997, stated that he had given certain ledgers to Mr Pape, Counsel for the Applicants.

Mr McElvenny, by an affidavit sworn on 20 May 1997, deposed that three documents identified in Schedule B of his affidavit were the subject of a claim for legal professional privilege on the basis that they were confidential communications made between a third party and his or her legal advisers for the purpose of these proceedings.

. Respondent's submission

In his written submissions, Mr McGovern relied upon two grounds in support of the argument that production of the documents was required. The first was that the ledgers were not privileged. Mr McGovern's argument in this regard was that unless it could be shown that an entry in a trust account ledger disclosed the contents of communications between a solicitor and client then the ledger could not attract the protection of legal professional privilege. Mr McGovern submitted that a ledger report will not of itself reveal any communication between a client or third party and a solicitor and cannot be said to be obviously brought into existence for the purpose of giving or receiving legal advice or for use in existing or anticipated litigation.

The second of Mr McGovern's arguments is that assuming the documents are protected from production on the grounds of legal professional privilege, that privilege has been waived. Mr McGovern submitted that there had been an implied waiver arising from the conduct of the witness, Mr V, in cross examination which was such as to reveal what Mr V alleged was the contents and identity of particular documents. On this basis it is alleged that fairness requires that as a witness for the Applicants had disclosed information up to a certain point the Applicants should not be allowed to withhold the remainder.

. Status of the ledgers

At common law, legal professional privilege attaches to communications, confidential in character, which (relevantly for present purposes) are brought into existence for the sole purpose of either enabling the client to obtain legal advice or the legal adviser to give legal advice or for use in litigation either actual or within the reasonable contemplation of the client: see generally Grant v Downs [1976] HCA 63; (1976) 135 CLR 674. A claim for legal professional privilege, by its nature, prevents disclosure of the contents of privileged communication, even if it appears that such disclosure will be conducive to justice in a particular case. The exclusion is not based on grounds of relevance, but rather on the policy that it is necessary for the administration of justice for persons to be able to obtain legal advice and assistance without being prejudiced by such communications being liable to compulsory disclosure to third parties: see particularly the discussion by the High Court in Baker v Campbell [1983] HCA 39; (1983) 153 CLR 52 and Carter v Northmore Hale Davy & Leake (1995) 183 CLR 121.

Mr McGovern's argument is essentially that a ledger of the type being sought in the Summons for Production is not, nor would it disclose the contents of, a confidential communication between a client and his or her lawyer for the purposes of legal advice or use in litigation.

I agree that the original ledgers themselves, as the evidence currently stands, are prima facie not privileged from production on the basis of legal professional privilege.

However, and in respect of Mr McGovern's submission there are two salient facts. The first is that the Summons to Produce was served on Mr McElvenny and required the production of documents by him. The second is that it requires the production, not of the ledgers themselves, but the ledgers and related documents (or copies of them) which were provided to Mr Pape. Mr McElvenny in his affidavit of 20 May 1997 lists in Schedule B documents in respect of which the privilege is claimed, all being copies of communications between Mr V and the legal advisers.

Accepting that these documents listed in Schedule B were provided to Mr Pape, there is no evidence to contradict the statements in Mr McElvenny's affidavit that they were confidential communications brought into existence for the sole purpose of use in these proceedings.

There is no evidence as to whether the documents referred to in Schedule B contain or annex copies of the ledgers. Accepting that they do it does not follow that such copies of the ledgers are not privileged. Copies of such documents may stand in a different position to the originals. The High Court has recently considered the issue in Australian Federal Police, Commissioner v Propend Finance Pty Ltd (1997) 71 ALJR 327. By a majority (Gaudron, McHugh, Gummow and Kirby JJ; Brennan CJ agreeing in part) the Court held that a copy of a non-privileged document could itself attract the protection of legal professional privilege where the copy was brought into existence for the sole purpose of giving or receiving legal advice or for use in litigation.

On the basis that copies of ledgers were provided to Mr Pape, Counsel for the Applicants, the inference is open to be drawn (and I draw that inference), that the purpose of their provision was for use in these proceedings. Accordingly, I hold that the documents referred to in Schedule B of Mr McElvenny's affidavit are privileged from production.

. Waiver

Having held that the documents are prima facie privileged, it is necessary to consider the question of whether there has been a waiver of the privilege that subsists in the documents. Where as here, there has been no intentional disclosure of privileged material, the question becomes whether there has been an implied (or more correctly, an imputed) waiver. The test for an imputed waiver is as stated by the High Court in Attorney-General (NT) v Maurice [1986] HCA 80; (1986) 161 CLR 475. The criteria adopted by the High Court as to whether a waiver of legal professional privilege should be imputed is based on the notion of fairness. As Gibbs CJ stated (at 481), the question is one of:

"Whether it would be unfair or misleading to allow a party to refer to or use material and yet assert that that material, or material associated with it, is privileged for production."

The High Court again considered the question in Goldberg v Ng [1995] HCA 39; (1995) 185 CLR 83. There the majority (at 95-96) held that the basis of an imputed waiver will be some act or omission of persons entitled to the benefit of the privilege and ordinarily that act or omission will involve or relate to limited actual or purported disclosure of the contents or the privileged material.

The rationale for the application of the fairness test where, as here, privileged material is sought to be used at a hearing addresses the concern that the disclosure of some privileged material and the simultaneous retention of privilege in relation to documents or material concerning the same subject matter may lead to an inaccurate and misleading impression of the evidence in relation to that subject matters: see for example Great Atlantic Insurance Co v Home Insurance Co [1984] 1 WLR 529.

In this context, it is clear that legal professional privilege will not be waived by merely referring to a privileged document by, for example, using it to draft pleadings or an affidavit: see Buttes Gas & Oil Co v Hammer (No. 3) [1981] QB 223; Maurice at 481, 489. Similarly, privilege will not be waived merely because a party to litigation adduces from a witness evidence which could be said to have ultimately arisen from privileged communications: see Ritz Hotel Ltd v Charles of the Ritz Ltd (No. 22) (1988) 14 NSWLR 132. However, where a witness uses a document to refresh his or her memory with a view to giving evidence, privilege may be waived: Trade Practices Commission v TNT Management Pty Ltd & Ors (1984) 56 ALR 647 at 687; Mancorp v Boulderstone (1991) 57 SASR 87; Instant Colour Pty Ltd v Cannon Australia Pty Ltd (unreported) Federal Court of Australia, 30 October 1995, Nicholson J.

In the present instance the disclosure of a reference to the ledgers occurred in this way:

"How much do you have tied up in him at the moment? ---- As far as I can think its about 900,000 - odd. What is the precise figure; surely you would know that? ---- I'd have to refer to a document if I may? Sure, you refer to a document, and just tell us what document you are referring to? ------ It's 914,056. What document is it that you are just referring to? ----- I was referring to a set of questions that I discussed with Mr Pape. I have spoken to Mr Pape on several occasions now to discuss this matter. Yes. Mr Pape told you that the figure was 914,056, did he? ----- No, Mr Paper [sic - Pape] didn't tell me that. I told Mr Pape that was the figure. Is it Australian dollars, or other currency? ------ That's Australian dollars. What is the break up of that 914,056? What is it comprised of? ---- I would have to get the detail for you. I don't have that with me at the moment. Where would you get that detail? ----- I would get that from our ledgers. Has anyone asked you for those ledgers before tonight or today? ----- Yes, they have. Who asked you for them? ----- Mr Pape. Did you provide them? ------ Yes, I did. When did you provide them? ------ When did I provide them? Was that the question? Yes? ------ This week." (Transcript 18 April 1997 at 113.12-114.3)

It is clear from the passage set out above that Mr V refreshed his memory by referring to a "set of questions" that he discussed with Mr Pape. It seems likely, although this is not clear, that the document from which Mr V refreshed his memory, namely the "set of questions" that he discussed with Mr Pape, is a document that falls within the scope of the Summons.

I have not read the documents which were produced in answer to the Summons in respect of which the claim for privilege has been made; and the sealed envelope in which they were handed to me, has not been opened.

In my view, there has been a waiver of privilege in respect of the document referred to by Mr V. The authorities show that fairness requires that legal professional privilege be treated as lost where a witness has refreshed his or her memory for the purpose of giving evidence by reading a document to which the privilege attaches and subsequently referring to that document in the course of examination or cross examination: see particularly Mancorp Pty Ltd v Boulderstone Pty Ltd (1991) 57 SASR 87 at 94. The reason is that an important aspect of the fair treatment of the interests of the other party is that that party have adequate opportunity to test the witness' evidence by comparing with the document the evidence which the witness gives: see MGICA (1992) Ltd v Kenny & Good Pty Limited (No. 2) (1996) 61 FCR 236 at 237 E - G.

The fact that a witness is unaware of the legal consequences of refreshing his or her memory from a document is not a sufficient reason for holding that there has not been a waiver of privilege: see Instant Colour Pty Ltd v Cannon Australia Pty Ltd.

If there has been a waiver, the question then is the extent of the waiver. In discussing this, Debelle J in Mancorp stated that the importance of upholding the sanctity of a privileged document might in some circumstances require that the Court exercise discretion in deciding whether to require a privileged document to be produced for inspection. His Honour there stated that the discretion would be more readily exercised when the document was very recently used for the purpose of refreshing memory.

Having regard to the rationale of legal professional privilege referred to above, and the limited reference to the document made by Mr V in the course of cross examination, I find, as the matter currently stands, that the waiver should be limited to that part of the document to which Mr V referred in giving the answers to the questions which appear on page 113 of the transcript and which are set out above.

Accordingly I return the sealed envelope containing the documents to counsel for the Applicants and order that if a document was relied upon by Mr V in giving his answers, and if that document was produced in answer to the Summons to Produce, that part of the document (which is referred to in subclause (k)) must be produced to Counsel for the Respondent.

5. Representation

(a) The Applicant was represented by Mr B.R. Pape of Counsel instructed by Mr G. McElvenny, and the Respondent was represented by Mr D. McGovern instructed by Ms P. Hannigan of the Australian Government Solicitor.
