Cormick and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 721 (29 November 1993)

Last Updated: 27 August 2008



Administrative Appeals Tribunal



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



SUPERANNUATION - Whether resignation should be treated as invalidity retirement. Did Applicant have a "moral" entitlement to the exercise of discretion. Mental capacity to understand availability of invalidity retirement but not effect of same.



Superannuation Act 1976 - S7 and S75



Commissioner for Superannuation v Hastings 70 ALR 62

Scott v Commissioner for Superannuation 8 AAR 20



AAT Decision No 9141



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL ) A93/2

)

GENERAL ADMINISTRATE DIVISION )



Re: JOHN PETER JULIAN CORMICK

Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal : Senior Member M.D. Allen

Mr N.J. Attwood AO, Member

Dr D.B. Travers, Member



Date : 29 November 1993



Place : Canberra



Decision : The decision under review is set aside and the Tribunal substitutes in lieu thereof its decision, namely:



THAT the Applicant John Peter Julian Cormick shall, for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1976, be deemed to have been retired on the ground of mental incapacity to perform his duties.



(Sgd) M.D. ALLEN

..................................................

Presiding Member

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL ) No. A93/2

)

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE )



Re: JOHN PETER JULIAN CORMICK

Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION



29 November 1993 Senior Member M.D. Allen

Mr N.J. Attwood AO, Member

Dr D.B. Travers, Member



1. The Applicant on 22 May 1986 submitted his resignation from the Commonwealth Public Service. That resignation was to take effect as and from 23 May 1986. On 2 June 1986 he completed an application for a refund of superannuation contributions and on 7 July 1986 was paid the sum of $20 721.66 being the refund of his accumulated contributions.



2. On 28 August 1986 the Applicant wrote to the Public Service Board seeking revocation of his resignation of 23 May 1986 and requesting invalidity retirement from that date.



3. By letter dated 20 October 1986 the Respondent advised the Applicant of the provisions of Ss7(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 and on 23 October 1986 the Applicant sought consideration of his case pursuant to Ss7(2).



4. Section 7 of the Superannuation Act 1976 states:



"(1) A reference in this Act to the retirement of an eligible employee on the ground of invalidity shall be read as a reference to his having been retired, or his services having been otherwise terminated, on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties.



(2) Where a person has, before attaining his maximum retiring age, ceased to be an eligible employee otherwise than by reason of death or by reason of his having been retired, or his services having been otherwise terminated, on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties, but the Commissioner is satisfied that, at the time he ceased to be an eligible employee, he was, by reason of physical or mental incapacity, unfit to perform his duties, the Commissioner may direct that the person shall, for the purposes of this Act, be deemed to have been retired on the ground of that physical or mental incapacity."



5. On 8 July 1988 a Delegate of the Respondent rejected the Applicant's claim. By letter dated 8 August 1988 the Applicant sought reconsideration of that decision.



6. The original decision was reconsidered pursuant to Ss154(4) of the Superannuation Act 1976 and on 9 December 1992, that is to say over four years after the Applicant's request for reconsideration, another Delegate of the Respondent determined that he would not direct that the Applicant be deemed to have retired on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties.



7. It must be stated that to take over four years to rule on the Applicant's request for reconsideration is totally unconscionable and a grave reflection on the professionalism and standards of the Retirement Benefits Office.



8. By notice lodged 6 January 1993 the Applicant sought further review by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal.



9. The said application for review came on for hearing before this Tribunal in Canberra on 2 November 1993, the Applicant representing himself and the Respondent being represented by an officer of the Retirement Benefits Office, Ms Munoz.



10. Exhibit 1 in the proceedings is the Respondent's Statement of Facts and Contentions. In that document the Respondent sets out certain concessions which were not resiled from at the hearing. Those concessions are:

That at the time the Applicant resigned in May 1986:

(i) the Applicant had a physical or mental incapacity;



(ii) the Applicant was unfit to perform his duties,



(iii) the Applicant's unfitness to perform his duties was by reason of his physical or mental incapacity, and



(iv) the Applicant could not be redeployed



11. The Respondent opposed the Applicant's case on two bases. The first being that any impairment of his mental or physical capacity at the time of his resignation was not permanent and, secondly, that he lacked any moral entitlement to the exercise of the discretion.



12. Section 7 does not refer to permanent incapacity but simply to retirement on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform duties.



13. Section 75 of the Superannuation Act clearly envisages the case where an employee may have been retired on the grounds of invalidity but that that physical or mental incapacity is not permanent, it states:

"(1) Where the Commissioner is satisfied, after receiving the report or reports of a medical practitioner or medical practitioners with respect to the health of a person to whom invalidity pension is payable, that the health of the person has become so restored as to enable him to perform duties of a kind that are, in the opinion of the Commissioner, suitable to be performed by him (having regard to the duties performed by him immediately before his retirement on the ground of invalidity and to such other matters as the Commissioner considers relevant), the Commissioner shall so inform such person or authority as the Commissioner considers appropriate with a view to that person or authority finding suitable employment for the pensioner.



(2) If -



(a) the pensioner is offered by the Commonwealth or by an approved authority employment (not being employment on a part-time basis) that involves the performance of duties that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, are suitable to be performed by him (having regard to the duties performed by him immediately before his retirement on the ground of invalidity and to such other matters as the Commissioner considers relevant); and



(b) the person unreasonably refuses or fails to accept the offer within 14 days after the receipt by him of the offer, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows,



the Commissioner may cancel the person's entitlement to invalidity pension."



14. The Respondent's reference to a moral entitlement refers to the judgment of the Full Court of the Federal Court in Commissioner for Superannuation v Hastings 70 ALR 625. At p630 the Court said:

"In our opinion, it is too simplistic to describe s 7(2) as creating a power intended to be used for the benefit of persons who comply with its pre-conditions. Rather, we see it as a provision designed to avoid injustice in cases where an employee might be thought to be morally entitled to a pension, because effectively forced out of his or her employment for health reasons, but is not within sub-s (1)."



The Court did, however, go on to say:

Because the circumstances of individual cases must differ widely, it is impossible to cast such a provision in a rigid form; a discretion must be vested in a suitable person, to be exercised upon the merits of the individual case. The industrial and medical history of the applicant for a pension is undoubtedly material to the decision to be made. Otherwise, as the relevant matters are unspecified in the legislation, it is for the Commissioner to determine what matters are to be taken into account and the weight to be given to them 'unconfined except in so far as the subject matter and the scope and purpose' of s 7(2) enables a court to say that 'given reasons are definitely extraneous to any objects the legislature could have had in view ' ... The ultimate question for the Commissioner is whether it would be reasonable, having regard to the interests not only of the applicant for a pension but of other contributors to the superannuation fund, to treat the applicant as being entitled to the same benefits as if he or she had retired on the ground of invalidity. The Commissioner's decision is, of course, itself subject to review by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal."



15. The reference to the taking into account the interests of other contributors to the fund was queried in Scott v Commissioner for Superannuation 8 AAR 20 at 34.



16. In this matter the moral disentitlement was said to result from the Applicant being aware at the time of his resignation of the availability of invalidity retirement and his decision to reject that option. Further it was stated that the evidence revealed that prior to his resignation the Applicant had investigated what entitlements he would receive and, weighing all options, had decided for resignation as opposed to invalidity retirement.



17. The Applicant has always contended since requesting reconsideration pursuant to Ss7(2), that at the time of his resignation he understood the options regarding invalidity retirement but, because of his mental state, he was incapable of making a rational decision regarding those options.



18. Certainly it is true to say that the Applicant, by opting for resignation as opposed to invalidity retirement, acted to his financial detriment. When an otherwise intelligent man makes an irrational decision the Tribunal is entitled to ask why and query what other factors may have affected his judgment.



19. On 9 November 1988 the Retirement Benefits Office sought an opinion from the Applicant's treating psychiatrist, Dr Knox, as to what physical and/or mental conditions the Applicant was suffering from at the time of his resignation and whether they were permanent and sufficiently incapacitating to have warranted his retirement on medical grounds as at the time of his resignation.



20. Dr Knox replied to the Commissioner for Superannuation by way of a report dated 28 November 1988 (ie some four years before a decision was made on the Applicant's case). In that report Dr Knox states inter alia:

"Mr Cormick was suffering from a combination of physical and psychiatric problems at the time of his resignation on 23 May 1986. I believe Mr Cormick appropriately describes the situation in paragraph 1 of his claim for compensation recorded on page 6 of your medical evidence summary; thus Mr Cormick's pre-existing Obsessional Personality Disorder and Irritable Bowel Syndrome were both aggravated by the accident of July 1982. Due to concussion Mr Cormick suffered higher levels of anxiety than he previously had done, and he also had neck pain and a degree of physical disability to contend with. Given the high standards he demanded of himself due to his obsessional personality, Mr Cormick found himself unable to work satisfactorily in his position as a Finance Officer. He saw his Public Service career jeopardised and lost interest in his work.



All the above-mentioned conditions continued to disturb Mr Cormick's physical and psychiatric health compounding his problems as time passed."



Dr Knox added:

"Although I consider Mr Cormick's condition long-term and relatively permanent, there is the possibility that if Mr Cormick is to find satisfactory work for himself, with duties that he finds agreeable and challenging, there could be a significant improvement in his health to the point where he may be capable of part-time or even full-time work.



It is very difficult for me to suggest what type of work Mr Cormick would find suitable for his health, and I can only offer a more definite answer following consultation with Mr Cormick, and also no doubt following his own attitude towards any available employment.



I believe that the combination of health problems suffered by Mr Cormick in May of 1986 did justify his retirement on medical grounds."



21. A further report dated 28 June 1993 was obtained from Dr Knox by the Respondent. That report states inter alia:

"While I believe that Mr Cormick did have the mental capacity at the time of his resignation to 'understand the procedure for invalidity retirement' I doubt very much whether this option was realistically available to him at that time. On the one hand I believe that he felt the Commonwealth Medical Officer had not determined that he was sufficiently unwell to warrant invalidity retirement, on the other hand I believe he feared that such a retirement path for him would have cut off his umbilical cord to the security of the public service and inhibited severely his prospects for retraining and future work. (I note from Dr Mickleburgh's report of 19 January 1989 that Mr Cormick had indeed made considerable progress with retraining in surveying and cartography at the Canberra College of Advanced Education.)



It is my opinion that due to his frustration and depression at the time of his resignation - although I don't believe that he was mentally incapacitated to the point where he was not able to appreciate his actions - and due to the fact that invalidity retirement was no real option at that time Mr Cormick felt forced by circumstances to retire as he did. He saw his health continuing to decline and felt that he was unable to go on. His circumstances had literally painted him into a corner at this point, and I think that he did act wisely to escape from the ongoing negative effects of his work environment.



I believe that If Mr Cormick had been realistically offered the alternative of invalidity retirement, and saw that this had certain advantages for him, then he could well have opted for this course of action at the time. For the reasons I have mentioned above I don't believe that he saw invalidity retirement as any option at all at the time of his resignation."



22. On 16 June 1993 Dr Mickleburgh, Psychiatrist, reported to the Respondent:

"In your letter dated 3rd June 1993 there was a request for a report on Mr Peter Cormick to address the specific issue:



'Was Mr Cormick's mental state such that at the time of his resignation he was capable of understanding the procedure for invalidity retirement?'



Mr Cormick's resignation was dated 22nd May 1986 and I did not see Mr Cormick until 12th August 1986 when he was seeking assistance with retraining for vocational rehabilitation, so my opinion was based on a retrospective account of the circumstances.



I wrote on 3rd February 1987:



'From the history given to me by Mr Cormick it appears that he was under severe emotional tension, related to work stress, at the time he tendered his resignation. This probably affected his judgement adversely. When he was able to discuss the matter in a more relaxed state of mind he regretted his resignation and would have preferred to seek early retirement on the grounds of invalidity'.



Subsequent interviews with Mr Cormick gave no cause for me to change this opinion."



23. As well as the mental state indicated by these reports the actions of the Applicant at the time of his resignation evidence an impairment of his critical facilities. He applied for Long Service Leave on 18 March 1986, stating he felt the need for a long break. The leave was to commence on 5 May 1986. On 21 May 1986 the Applicant sought advice as to the amount of any refund of superannuation contributions should he resign and on 22 May 1986 he submitted his resignation from the Public Service, that resignation to take effect on 23 May 1986. It would not appear from the material before the Tribunal that any counselling was given to the Applicant at this time by his employer, the Department of Finance.



24. Having regard to all the material before it, the Tribunal is satisfied that at the time the Applicant resigned from the Commonwealth Public Service he was not thinking or acting logically because of mental incapacity and that he was thus incapable of exercising a proper judgment regarding whether to seek invalidity retirement or not. Given contemporaneous medical reports and the later medical reports by his treating doctors the Tribunal is further satisfied that had the Applicant applied for invalidity retirement at the time of his resignation it would have been granted to him.



25. The Applicant did not rest on his rights. Upon being advised by the Commonwealth Rehabilitation Service in August 1986 he sought reconsideration of his case. That it has taken this long to resolve is not the fault of the Applicant.



26. In the opinion of this Tribunal, taking into account the interpretation Ss7(2) has received in Scott's case supra and Hastings supra, the decision under review should be set aside and the discretion in Ss7(2) exercised in the Applicant's favour.



27. The only other matter that requires comment is that the Applicant has managed to rehabilitate himself and has taken the final exams to be admitted to practice as a licensed Surveyor. It may well be that S75 of the Superannuation Act 1976 is now applicable to his case.
