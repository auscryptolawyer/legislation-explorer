SALES TAX - Two Products manufactured, sold and applied by taxpayer company for use in association with the pouring of concrete - Product CF prevents concrete from bonding to formwork - Product CS is applied to concrete to slow the set - exemption from Sales Tax - whether the Products fall within the relevant exemption provisions, particularly Item 89(4) and/or Item 85A(1) - meaning to be ascribed to words used in items - ambiguity or uncertainty

THE APPLICATION AND THE ASSESSMENT

This is an application for review of an objection decision made by the respondent referable to the imposition of tax pursuant to the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 1) 1930 as amended, and particularly Subsection 26 AA(1). The relevant assessment issued on 16 December 1986 and was in respect of two products "manufactured by, sold by or applied to the use of, the taxpayer or treated as stock for sale by retail by it".

THE EXEMPTION AND CLASSIFICATION

The taxpayer company claimed that no tax was payable in respect of the products in that the same were "unconditionally exempt from sales tax" pursuant to Division XII of the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act and, in particular, item 89(4) or "any other item of the division". The only other item relied upon at the hearing was item 85A(1).

The stipulated items under which exemption from sales tax was then sought to be obtained provided:

"Item 89(4):

Materials of a kind used exclusively or primarily and principally as ingredients of concrete or of cement, mortar or plaster mixtures."

"Item 85A(1):

Paints and other coatings in liquid, paste or powder form (including stains, creosote and other preservatives, waterproofing liquids and compounds and petrifying liquid but not including polishes or mop oils) of a kind marketed exclusively, or primarily and principally, for application to buildings or other fixtures."

THE PRODUCTS - THEIR ANALYSIS AMD THEIR USES

For the purpose of these reasons, the products will be designated as "Product CF" and "Product CS".

PRODUCT CF

The Technical Director of the taxpayer company gave evidence at the hearing. He said that the company manufactured and sold the two products. Product CF is known as a reactive form release agent. It prevents concrete from bonding to the formwork into which it is placed. It is applied by brush or spray onto the formwork itself before the concrete is placed. It is applied at a rate of one litre per 13-20 square metres, depending on the texture of the formwork, the application equipment and other factors. It is liberally applied to the surface of the formwork and at least four hours prior to concreting. The rate of application controls the amount of retardation achieved. The material contains a thickening agent. It forms like a coating on the surface of the formwork to be absorbed in due course by the concrete. Even a minute quantity will however prevent concrete adhesion. It is marketed and used principally and primarily for application as abovementioned to buildings or other fixtures to achieve a smooth, unblemished concrete finish during construction. It is also used for subsidiary purposes of preventing concrete from adhering to cylinder moulds, wheelbarrows and other equipment which may be used to carry or handle concrete. Its active components react with the alkali in the cement to form a water repellent film that prevents the concrete from bonding. Its components are dissolved in dieselene which acts as an inert carrier.

Product CF cannot be applied directly to buildings or other fixtures which are yet to be constructed of concrete or cement, mortar or plaster mixtures, nor made an ingredient or component part or element in the concrete, cement, mortar or plaster mixtures which may be being used for construction of buildings or other fixtures. It is a product which belongs to a class of products which are marketed and used on most precast or cast in situ concrete constructions throughout Australia.

A very high proportion of the product CF is ordinary dieselene. The additional ingredient identified as "Calcon P13B" comprises approximately three-and-a-half per cent of the total product. "Calcon P13B" is the proprietary name of a substance which itself contains five or six different other substances, the one group of which is comprised largely of fatty acids such as Oleic. The other significant component is what is described as a detraining agent, which agent tends to have the effect of removing air from "the system". "If you are in contact with the formworX and you have this material, the bubbles that would normally collect air tend not to do so or tend to be reduced down to smaller sizes". The component, Calcon P13B, "is a very important surface active component". It causes waterproofing and reacts with the cement regardless of the petroleum hydrocarbon which is basically the carrier. The P13B, "if dissolved and emulsified in water, would still act very effectively to perform its function of keeping a surface smooth and unblemished".

Dieselene itself can be used as a form release agent. The Technical Director had not however heard of it being used to produce a high quality blemish-free concrete surface. "That is one of the reasons we sell our product", he said. "If you use diesel for form release, you get oil staining, you can get quite a rough, quite a bad concrete finish. So someone does not care about his concrete, he uses diesel; if someone cares about his concrete, he uses a technical product". In fact, Calcon P13B itself can be used in an emulsion in water with no dieselene being present and produce the same kind of effect as the Product CF. Dieselene acts as a type of barrier and can be used on the surfaces of forms. The Calcon P13B reacts with the cement and prevents the concrete from coming in contact almost with the dieselene surface, thus preventing the staining process from occurring.

Generally, the reason that dieselene is chosen both for itself and with additives as a formwork release agent is that it is sufficiently volatile to evaporate from the surface after the formwork has been removed. If a heavier oil was used, a residue of oil might be left on the finished surface or on the formwork itself after use.

It is not a part of the function of the Product CF in the form of Calcon P13B or the dieselene to remain on or in the surface of the concrete after its function has been performed. Some of it, the reactive material, does remain on the surface of the concrete in the form of a whitish dust but it is removed in a matter of days.

Dr Patten, a senior lecturer in the Department of Geotechnical Engineering, School of Civil Engineering, University of New South Wales, said that the use of ordinary dieselene or other fractions of oil refining as a releasing agent does not give "quite as good a finish" as some of the proprietary compounds, but they can be used as release agents for formwork. There are, he said, quite a number of form release agents available:

"of which the formation, the Oleic materials which form soap on the surface, or soap like materials on the surface are one. They react with the calcium hydroxide which is released from the setting Portland cement, but they are only one of the possible release agents and petroleum hydrocarbons are certainly quite efficient at releasing concrete from formwork".

Dr Patten was of the view that when there exists a solution of fatty acids in dieselene, the dieselene itself has a function to fulfill in relation to the releasing of the formwork from the concrete surface. "It forms a very major component of the releasing", he said. "The formation of the fatty acids probably enhances the surface. It reacts with the alkali present in the cement to form a microscopic water-repellant film between concrete and formwork. By this action, formwork is protected against continuous wetting and drying action".

The Product CF is either lightly painted or sprayed onto the surface to form an oiling layer which prevents concrete or mortar adhering to the object. It is basically a petroleum hydrocarbon with a very small percentage of the designated "Calcon P13B" imported by the taxpayer from its parent company overseas which "presumably improves the ability of the basic hydrocarbon to prevent adhesion between the concrete and the formwork or other items coated with the product". Dr Patten continued by saying that "the manufacturer's claim that the reaction between their product (presumably the specific compound "Calcon P13B"), and the alkalis present in the Portland cement concrete takes place, which produces a microscopic water-repellant film or layer between the fresh concrete and the formwork, thus protecting the formwork from water penetration. As the compound in question is of unknown chemical composition, there is no way that this claim can be confirmed or refuted. However, it would seem that the petroleum hydrocarbon (which is basically hydrophobic) would contribute most to the waterproofing and corrosion protecting action exhibited by this product".

PRODUCT CS

Product CS is known as a surface retardant agent. It exposes aggregate in buildings or other fixtures so as to provide a keyed surface for subsequent rendering or concreting or a particular aesthetic effect. It is generally applied by brush to the formwork before the concrete is placed. Product CS can only be applied directly to concrete prior to initial set such as for horizontal construction joints. The product is applied at the rate of about 1 litre per 6 square metres depending on the texture of the formwork and the depth of aggregate exposure which may be required. The rate of application controls depth of penetration and retardation. It forms a tough semi-glazed coating on formwork which very slowly dissolves in the concrete surface to prevent set.

The Technical Director said that the dosage of the material "will influence how much reaction there is with certain components of the concrete". "However, generally, when we add more of the material, what happens is that we get a greater depth of reaction. So if you get very big stones and you want to expose to a depth of three-quarters of an inch to a half an inch, say you have the stones jutting out of the concrete, you add a lot more material so that permeates with the concrete and you have a soft layer inside the concrete". The rate of application controls more the depth of retardation than the amount of retardation.

The product is marketed and used exclusively for application to buildings and other fixtures to so expose aggregate for subsequent rendering or concreting or to provide a particular architectural appearance. It eliminates expensive and inefficient scabbling or brush hammering of the hardened concrete.

The product contains both chemicals which retard the hydration process or setting of the concrete, together with modifiers which enable its application to the formwork and slow dissolution into the plastic concrete. In this way the concrete surface becomes impregnated with the product and is kept soft until the surface mortar is washed away to expose the aggregate. In this sense, according to the technical director of the applicant, "it becomes an ingredient of and is a component part or element in the concrete or the cement, mortar or plaster mixture to at least its depth of impregnation and retardation, albeit temporarily".

Like Product CF, it is, according to the director, rarely applied directly to buildings or other fixtures which are yet to be constructed, but even when applied indirectly, it does become an ingredient of and component part of or an element in concrete or of cement, mortar or plaster fixtures being used for construction of buildings or other fixtures which are yet to be constructed.

Product CS is more restricted in its use than Product CF in that "it is marketed and used for a more limited and specialized purpose. It is a product which belongs to a broader class of products marketed as retarding add mixtures, which group differs from surface retarders only according to their application, distribution and dosage".

Product CS is a water-based product in which three compounds are dissolved. The three compounds are identified as "Wafex powder", "Diacell 1130" and "J5". Diacell 1130 and the J5 are imported by the taxpayer from its parent company; the Wafex powder is acquired locally.

Diacell 1130 is a cellulose product used to thicken the solution of other chemicals in the water, allowing the material to be thickened enough to apply but retain its solid nature so that when the wet concrete is applied it does not get "drained away", otherwise retardation would develop. It has to be held in a "slowly dissolvable film on the surface of the formwork rather than just making the material look thicker for some particular reason". The "Wafex powder" is composed of a chemical substance known as calcium ligno-sulphanate. This material exhibits a marked ability to retard normal chemical reactions which take place when water and Portland cement are mixed together in a concrete, thus slowing or even preventing the normal setting process. This retarding effect can be utilized in a number of ways and for a variety of purposes.

The other product, J5, is probably the most important of the retarding agents. Wafex is used in general concreting work. J5 is a very strong retarder "because we do not want the concrete to ever go hard; we want it to remain soft for as long as possible after the formwork is removed". The J5 is largely a boraheptomate.

THE PRODUCTS - FURTHER FEATURES

In terms of function, it is no part of the purpose or function of Product CS or any of its constituent parts to remain on the surface after the concrete has been hosed off. Any material that has impregnated the concrete will however remain so, but to no effect.

Product CF produces a hydrophobic material which reacts with the components of the cement to form a kind of wax, a water-repellant waxing material. Product CS reacts on one of the components of the cement called Tricalcium Aluminate stopping it from hardening. It reacts with the fresh concrete at the concrete formwork interface to produce a zone of concrete several millimetres deep which has a weakened cementitious binder which can be readily removed in order to roughen the surface for the better "keying or bonding of cement render for tiles to that surface".

Approximately 99 per cent of sales of Product CF and Product CS are made directly by the applicant to builders, formwork contractors and concrete contractors, otherwise sales are made through hardware stores. Most of the sales of the products are as a consequence of their being specified by architects and engineers in their project specifications.

THE ISSUES AND THE MEANING TO BE ASCRIBED TO WORDS USED IN THE EXEMPTIONS

The issue raised by the above recited factual situation is as to whether either or both of the Products CF and CS fall within either of the relevant exemptions, that is, item 89(4) or item 85A(1).

Item 89(4) talks about "materials" which are not articularized, and both items 89(4) and 85A(1) use the phrase "of a kind". Each of the items also use a formula which seems to focus upon either one or other of an exclusive use, a primary use or an exclusive and/or a primary and principal. Whether the word "principal" gives to the word "primary" in item 85A an additional meaning is debatable, but in any event, in both items the use has either got to be exclusive or primarily and principal; the marketing has got to be exclusive or primarily and principal. Item 89(4) is concerned as to the use of the material or materials "of a kind", item 85A(1) as to the marketing of the product to which the exclusivity or primary and principal characteristic is significant.

Item 89(4) talks about ingredients of concrete or of other kinds of mixtures. The distinction is between whatever it is that is concrete and whatever it is that comprises mixtures, the ingredients of which are cement, mortar or plaster. So if the product is an ingredient of concrete and the quality of the use is satisfied - that is to say, the exclusivity or primary and principal use - then it can either be as an ingredient of concrete or relevantly, for present purposes, as an ingredient of a cement mixture.

In order to determine whether either product falls within one or other of the exemptions, the Tribunal of necessity has to look at the evidence as to the use made of the products and as to their market. The words appearing in items 89(4) and 85A(1) are to be understood in their respective ordinary dictionary meanings to which is then to be related the evidence. The words used are ordinary common words having no special trade meaning, thus the word "materials" means articles of any kind requisite for making or doing something. Both items contain the words "of a kind", the one directed to use, the other to marketing. In Hygienic Lily Limited v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 87 ATC 4327 Gummow J. had cause to consider the meaning to be ascribed to the phrase "goods of a kind", be it in the context of a somewhat different product to that being presently considered. However, at 4330, his Honour said:

" .... the setting in which the phrase "goods of a kind" appears suggests it is directed not to the use for which the particular goods in question were designed or manufactured, nor to the purpose to which it is intended that those particular goods shall be put, but rather to the nature, quality and adaptation of goods in the class of genus in question. Thus goods are "of a kind ordinarily used for household purposes" if they are to be recognized as members of a class or genus which commonly or regularly (albeit not necessarily exclusively or principally) was used for household purposes: (see Customs and Excise Commissioner v Mechanical Servicing (Trailer Engineers) Limited (1979) 1 WLR 305, 312)."

The word "ingredients" in item 89(4) means the things that enter as an element into a mixture, or the constituent elements of anything. It was submitted that whether a particular ingredient enters as an element into a mixture directly or indirectly, or whether it enters as an element in a mixture at the same time as other elements of that mixture enter into the mixture or later matters not in the determination of whether something is an ingredient. I consider this to be so.

"Concrete" is an artificial stone-like material used for foundations etcetera made by mixing cement, sand and broken stones etcetera with water, and allowing the mixture to harden. "Cement" means a material which is soft when first prepared, but later becomes hard or stone-like commonly made by burning a mixture of clay and limestone, and used for making concrete for foundations or the like. The word "mixtures" when associated with the words "concrete, cement, mortar or plaster" simply means added elements or ingredients of the same. It was submitted that a material is an ingredient of cement mixtures, albeit that it may not be an ingredient of concrete, then it will fall within the class or genus referred to in item 89(4). I consider this to be so. Thus it was submitted, and it would seem to follow that the phrase "cement, mortar or plaster mixtures" would include inter alia concrete at a stage when cement, sand and broken stones have been mixed with water, and the mixture has not yet hardened, as well as concrete which has been poured but has not yet set.

The word "coatings" means a layer of any substance spread over a surface. The word "application" means the act of applying or the act of putting to a special use or purpose. The specific mention in Item 85A of "other coatings" in liquid, paste or powder form leads one to conclude that inference is there being made to coatings which are to be applied directly to a surface to enhance, finish or confer some attribute or quality to it.

It was submitted that in order to qualify for the exemption, it is sufficient if the relevant coating has a special use or purpose related to buildings or other fixtures. This, however, is not what the item says. It is the act of "applying to" or the act of "putting to" the buildings or other fixtures that is relevant. Alternatively, it was submitted that provided the product is marketed to enable an act of application to buildings or other fixtures, whether directly or indirectly, this would suffice to confer the benefit of the exemption. The "applying to" or "putting to" is still directed to the building or fixtures. The word "buildings" in this context does not necesssarily imply that the process of construction is complete. It may be that only a minimal state of progress towards the finished work has been achieved. A product falling within the purview of the item could still be so applied or put.

Item 85A(1) does not expressly stipulate a method of application. It is, it was submitted, sufficient if the relevant coating has a special use or purpose relating to buildings or fixtures. This again is not what the item says. The item is quite specific in that there must be a "putting to" or "applying to" both in a physical sense to a building. It is not sufficient that the product be marketed to provide an act of application in a physical sense to buildings, either directly or indirectly, in order that on this account only, a product be entitled to the benefit of the exemption.

THE APPLICATION OF THE PRODUCTS TO THE CLASSIFICATION ITEMS

The subject products are sought to be brought within the exemption provisions of the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935, particularly Schedule 1 thereto, and so far as it is here relevant, within Division XII - "Building Materials". The words "building materials", the heading to the relevant division of the first schedule, are by reason of Section 3(2) of the Act itself not to be read as affecting the interpretation of any item in that division of the schedule. though it may be said that because it is an exemption schedule it should be read in favour of a taxpayer (see Burt v F.C. of T [1912] HCA 74; (1912) 15 CLR 469, 482), nevertheless the structure of the schedule is such as to make it apparent, looking at it as a whole, that one really has to be able to say that a particular good or item falls squarely within one of the categories in order to reach the conclusion that it is exempt.

The Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935 provides by Section 5(1) that "notwithstanding anything contained in any Sales Tax Assessment Act, sales tax shall not, subject to this section, be payable upon the sale value of any goods covered by any item or sub-item in the first column of the first schedule to this Act under any act specified in the second column opposite that item or sub-item." The relevant items for consideration in this matter are item 85A(1) and item 89(4). Item 89, in its entirety, reads as follows:

"89 (1) Concrete

(2) Cement

(3) Lime
  • (4) Materials of a kind used exclusively or primarily and principally as ingredients of concrete or of cement, mortar or plaster mixtures."

It was submitted that the Product CF belongs to the class or genus of reactive form release agents, its primary and principal use being as a commodity which enters as an element into either the concrete or alternatively, the cement mixture; in either case, not directly but indirectly, and by application to the formwork before the concrete or mixture is placed, thereby preventing it from bonding. It should be viewed either as an ingredient of concrete, in that anything added to the concrete is an ingredient of it, or as part of the cement mixture because of the way in which it interacts with the mixture once it has been placed on the formwork. The product has got to be an "exclusive or primary and principal use" as an ingredient. It was said that the consequence of the formwork being able to be removed or reused is really a consequence of the ingredient being used. Its primary use is as an element which is entered into the mixture preventing the bonding from taking place, and ensuring that the end product, that is to say, the hardened concrete, is not damaged or interfered with by reason of the removal of the framework so that a surface is obtained which is reasonably consistent and does not require scabbling or any other process to remove the formwork itself. This was sufficient for the product to fall within item 89(4).

It was further submitted that the Product CF - applied a it is as a liquid layer over the surface of formwork before the concrete or cement mixture is poured - is marketed primarily and principally to enhance finish or confer a particular attribute or quality to a building, namely, to achieve a smooth, unblemished finish to its surface. The product is thus put to a special use or purpose relating to buildings. Alternatively, it is marketed primarily and principally to enable an act of application to buildings, which act is effected almost always by indirect means, that is, application to the framework. The Product CF is painted onto the timber and the contact with tzmber of the concrete once poured produces a chemical reaction enabling the timber to be removed later without damage to the concrete. It is analogous, it was submitted, to a paint being applied to a structure. One does not mix the paint with the wall; the paint is put on a brush and is then applied to a wall. The method of application to the building, that is, indirect application does not matter for the purposes of the item. The Product CF is put to a special use or purpose relating to a building. Thus it was said the product falls within Item 85A(1).

It was submitted that the Product CS belongs to the class or genus of retarding admixtures, and to the sub-class or sub-genus of surface retarding agents. The exclusive use is to retard the hydration process or setting of concrete by impregnating the concrete surface and keeping it soft until the surface mortar is washed away to expose aggregate. Generally it is applied indirectly by application to the formwork, but may be applied directly prior to the initial set. It was submitted that it enters as an element into the concrete, or alternatively, the cement mixture, but only to a limited surface depth.

The use of the product is in aid of producing an uneven surface to concrete, exposing aggregate, blue metal or whatever might have been contained in the mixture. It further enables a different surface to be constructed such as a further concrete surface or one compound of another kind of material. It has the opposite effect to the CF product, CF being used to ensure a smooth surface, CS to ensure the availability of a roughened surface.

The product is marketed exclusively for application directly or indirectly to buildings to enhance finish or confer a particular attribute or quality mainly to exposed aggregate of buildings for subsequent rendering or concreting or to provide a particular architectural appearance. Thus the product, it was submitted, falls within item 85A(1).

It was submitted on behalf of the respondent that neither of the Products CF or CS, for the purpose of inclusion within Item 85A(1), contain the two charactcristics which are essential to something being properly described by the use of the word "paint" or "coating". Neither product is applied to a building Itself but to formwork. Neither of the substances is intended to remain on the surface of the concrete after its unction has been fulfilled. Although it may be that some residue is left, the presence of that residue is not in any way n intended function of the usage of the product. Support for this view can be obtained by considering the other words used in item 85A(1) namely, stains, creosote and other wood preservatives, waterproofing liquids and compounds and petrifying liquids but not including polishes or mop oils". The expression pertrifying liquids" is defined to mean a substance which is used as a coating on porous surfaces to enable the application of oil paint" (Macquarle Dictionary), that is, a type of undercoat, something painted on to porous surfaces to enable a coat of paint be put over them. It was submitted that all of the items in the abovementioned description are ones which are plainly intended upon application to remain on the substance to which are they applied, and perform some function, either a decorative function like a paint, or a protective function, as might be the function of creosote or wood preservative or waterproofing liquids or compounds. There is, in my opinion, merit in this submission.

It was conceded by the applicant that the substance was not a paint in the ordinary usage of that term, but it was submitted that it clearly fell within the words "other coatings in liquid form". The bracketed words, in the item, are inclusory words. There was nothing in the structure of the item to suggest that the other coatings in liquid form already referred to, should be limited in their enumeration or identification by the words "are expressly included".

Item 85A(1), commencing as it does with the word "paints", is disparate from the Products CF and CS and it would be to unduly extend the meaning of the words "other coatings in liquid paste or powder form" to encompass the relevant Products which have their own particular function to perform. It would be a misnomer to treat the products in question as a "paint" or "other coating in liquid paste or powder form". Neither product comes within what would be a normal usage of these words whether looked at individually, as a whole or with the aid of the words in parenthesis.

Neither product is analogous to a paint. Insofar as the products are said to perform a function on the surface of the concrete when it is poured, it may be said that the application to the formwork is simply a method of applying it to the concrete. However, the other functions abovementioned in my view take it out of the category of a paint. In addition, the primary or principal use is not "for application to buildings or other fixtures".

As to Item 89(4), it was submitted in relation to both products that neither of them could properly be described as "ingredients" of concrete or of cement, mortar or plaster mixtures. "Ingredient" is defined as something ;

" that enters in; entering into a thing or place one who or that which enters in something that enters into the formation of a compound or element; a component part constituent element "

(The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary)

The Macquarie Dictionary defines the word as meaning "something that enters as an element into a mixture: the ingredients of a cake ..... a constituent element of anything ". Neither Product is a substance which enters into a mixture in the sense that they are not substances which, like sand, aggregate or cement enter into the mixture in the course of mixing a batch of concrete.

The products under consideration in this matter are substances which are applied to the surface of setting concrete for the principal purpose of ensuring that framework does not adhere to the concrete, for the further purpose of protecting the formwork and for the purpose of producing a particular effect on the face of the concrete.

So far as the Product CF is concerned, the function of enabling the formwork to be easily removed from concrete when it has set can be performed by using dieselene. What is required is an oily substance which will be sufficiently viscose to remain on the formwork and on the setting surface as the concrete is poured which will lie between the setting concrete and the formwork, and which will be sufficiently volatile to leave the surface at least apart from some small residue after the work has been performed. The Product has the substance described as P13B within it, which substance is said to be a fatty or soapy acid itself reacting to some extent with the surface of the concrete and removing air from such surface, this in order to improve the surface effect. The reaction which takes place on the surface of the concrete is as the result of a chemical reaction which occurs between some of the ingredients of the Product CF and some of the ingredients of the concrete. This is not sufficient to enable it to be said that the substance itself becomes an ingredient of the concrete. Ordinary usage denies this conclusion.

The product is described in the applicant's specification as a releasing agent and as such, not an ingredient. Item 89(4) speaks of materials of a kind used exclusively or primarily and principally as ingredients of concrete. So even if one were to accept that the fatty acids in reacting with some of the constituents of the concrete at the surface perform a function and this part of the characteristics of the Product CF, nevertheless the dieselene itself, in forming a barrier between the setting concrete and the formwork, performs a principal if not a primary part of the function of the product. As the technical director of the applicant said in his evidence, Product CF "cannot of necessity be applied directly to buildings or other fixtures which are yet to be constructed of concrete or of cement, mortar or plaster mixtures nor made an ingredient or component part or element in the concrete, cement, mortar or plaster mixtures which may be being used for construction of buildings or other fixtures, but it is marketed and used for applying it indirectly to buildings or other fixtures by the means which I have described".

Product CS performs a similar function to Product CF in the sense that it provides an inert layer between the setting concrete and the formwork to enable it to be easily removed. This reflects upon its capacity to be an ingredient of the concrete in the same way as the oily layer formed by the Product CF can not be regarded as an ingredient.

Product CS contains a cellulose material which brings it to the required consistency not only so that it will stay on the formwork for the time that it is usually there, but also so that it will not be washed, as it were, from the surface of the concrete as the mixture is being poured.

In relation to item 89(4), it was submitted on behalf of the applicant that having regard to the meaning ascribed to the word "ingredients", that it matters not how the Product enters as long as it does enter in some way, shape or form. It is not an element of the mixture known as concrete, but that does not mean to say that it is not an ingredient of the concrete. It will suffice, it was submitted, if it enters into the mixture at some stage or other. The Products become ingredients of the mixture, because they operate in the way they do and become component parts of the mixture, albeit to a limited extent. This operation is more stark and obvious, it was said, in the case of the Product CS where the purpose of its use is to penetrate to a certain level to enable a particular finish to be achieved.

The application of the Product CS to a concrete mix in order to keep the surface of the mix fluid for the desired time does not result in the Product becoming an ingredient of the concrete. Indeed, the presence of the substance described as J5 leads one to think that the Product CS is an antithesis to concrete: the J5 causes concrete not to set. The combination of J5 and Wafex powder causes the concrete not to set. Its function insofar as it reacts with the concrete surface is to prevent the concrete setting and to enable it to be washed off or scraped afterwards with greater ease than would occur if some abrasive process was necessary to achieve the same result. Bearing in mind the disparate functions which the product performs and the way in which it performs them, it is not appropriate to speak of it as an ingredient, something that enters as an element of a mixture. The Product CS does not fall within the Item 89(4).

DECISION

Accordingly, and for the above reasons, it is the view of the Tribunal that neither Product CF or Product CF falls within the items 85A(1) or 89(4). The decisions under review are affirmed.
