Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation [1980] AATA 60 (30 September 1980)

Last Updated: 13 March 2007

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Superannuation - invalidity retirement benefits determined under s.66(2) - application for review - whether benefit classification certificate and s.66(2) opinion can stand independently - meaning of "incapacity" - whether incapacity caused or substantially contributed to by conditions specified in certificate or by condition connected with conditions specified in certificate -meaning of "connected with"

Superannuation Act 1976 ss.16, 66, 154, 184.

Re: RONALD BRADMAN BURR

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Mr R.K. Todd (Senior Member)

Sir Ernest Coates (Member)

Dr A.H. Marsh (Member)

V80/3

) No. V80/3

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re: RONALD BRADMAN BURR

And: THE COMMISSIONER

FOR SUPERANNUATION

DECISION

The Tribunal decides to set aside the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation made upon reconsideration pursuant to s154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") on 3 January 1980 whereby he confirmed a decision taken on his behalf by his Delegate to issue under S184(2) of the Act a benefit classification certificate and to determine that the benefit entitlement of the applicant upon his ceasing to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined under s66(2) of the Act should be set aside, and in substitution therefor the Tribunal decides that his entitlement should be determined under s66(1) of the Act.

DATED this 30th day of September 1980.

REASONS FOR DECISION

1.This is an application for review of a decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation, taken upon reconsideration pursuant to s154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"), confirming a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner to issue a benefit classification certificate in respect of the applicant and determining that the benefit entitlement of the applicant upon his ceasing to be an eligible employee on 21 December 1978 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age of 65 years and having completed less than 20 years contributory service should be determined in accordance with the provisions of s66(2) of the Act.
2.The applicant was born in July 1930. He served in the RAAF for six years from 1950. He then worked for 18 years with a company in the automotive trade, and this was followed by two years as a Port Security Officer with the Victorian Harbour Trust. Then in 1975 he sought and obtained employment in the service of the Commonwealth. He was medically examined and the examining medical officer recommended that he be accepted to the Provident Account as provided for by the Superannuation Act 1922 that was in force until 1 July 1976. The reasons for this recommendation were stated as follows:

"Overweight - Big boned and big framed but some chest and abdominal obesity. Blood pressure readings - Diastolic after rest, rather high. Weight problem since stopping smoking (60 cigs per day) 2 years ago."

The actual blood pressure readings were 158/100 before rest, and 136/98 after rest. The Medical Officer's Report also contained a reference to a problem related to colour blindness. The applicant regarded himself as well at the time, and he had not been receiving medical treatment, no symptoms of any disabilities having presented themselves. At the time of the examination he weighed 92 kg, while he now weighs 81 kg. It was conceded at the hearing that the effect of the foregoing is that it was proper for there to have been issued, pursuant to S184(2) of the Act, a benefit classification certificate in respect of the applicant specifying the conditions "raised blood pressure", "overweight" and "colour blind" as being conditions that had been relevant for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1922 and pursuant to which the applicant had been admitted to the Provident Account. The decision to issue the certificate was said by counsel for the applicant to be not challenged, and it was also accepted at the hearing that the condition "colour blind" was not relevant for the purposes of our consideration of the matter. But the form of the relevant legislation makes it essential to consider it, in its context, as well as the concession that was made, in some detail.

3. Prior to 24 April 1978, the effect of the provisions of s.184 of the Act was that in the case of a person who on 1 July 1976, the date of commencement of the Act, had been a contributor to the Provident Account, a benefit classification certificate was deemed to have been issued specifying the physical or mental condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept that person as a contributor under Part III of the Superannuation Act 1922. Those provisions are discussed in previous decisions of the Tribunal, for instance Re D.E. Harrison and the Commissioner for Superannuation (No.78/12024,) reported (though not on this point) as Note 19 (1979) 2 ALN 352.

4. From 24 April 1978 however, the date of commencement of the Superannuation Acts Amendment Act 1978 (Act No.17 of 1978), s.184 of the Act was substituted. The provisions of s.l84(2) as substituted should be set out in full:

"184(2) Subject to sub-section (4), where -

(a) a person to whom this section applies who, immediately before the commencing day, was (otherwise than by reason of an election made under section 81 of the superseded Act) a contributor to the Provident Account ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of death or retirement on the ground of invalidity;

(b) his period of contributory service is less than 20 years and, on the day on which he ceases to be an eligible employee, he has not attained his maximum retiring age; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the death of the person or the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the person was caused, or was substantially contributed to -

(i) by the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, by those conditions or one or more of those conditions; or

(ii) by a physical or mental condition or conditions of the person connected with the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, with those conditions or one or more of those conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the person a benefit classification certificate in which there is or are specified the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(4) and to have been in force in respect of the person immediately before his death or retirement."

5. These provisions are then complemented by s66(2) of the Act, which overrides, where it is applicable, the provisions of s66(1) which are the provisions which apply in an ordinary case of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining the maximum retiring age. Section 66(2) is as follows:

"66(2) Subject to sub-sections (3) and (4) of this section and section 79, where -

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining his maximum retiring age;

(b) there was in force in respect of the person, immediately before the person's retirement, a benefit classification certificate; and

(c) the Commissioner is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for his retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition connected with such a condition or such conditions,

the person is entitled - ".

The subsection then goes on to prescribe certain limited benefits which become payable.

6. Taking into account the way in which, in s.184(7) of the Act, the phrase "the ... condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act ..." is interpreted, the effect of the provisions of the substituted s184 is that the formation of the opinion by the Commissioner pursuant to sl84(2) (c) which leads to the issue by him of a benefit classification certificate must inevitably lead him likewise to form the opinion the grounds of which are set out in s66(2). The issue of the benefit classification certificate is dependent upon his forming an opinion that, in the present context, the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the person was caused, or was substantially contributed to by, the physical or mental condition of the person by reason of which the Superannuation Board was not satisfied under s.5(1) of the Superannuation Act 1922 that the health and physical fitness of the person were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund, or by a physical or mental condition connected with the condition by reason of which the Board was not so satisfied. That opinion having been formed, the benefit classification certificate "shall issue", but when the Commissioner is in possession of the certificate, and turns his mind to the question of the application of s66(2) of the Act, the only change in the verbal formulation of the question which he presents to himself is that he is now concerned with a physical or mental condition specified in the certificate rather than with the conditions that were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act, but the distinction is one without a difference. The issue of the certificate and the formation of an opinion under s66(2) are logically inseparable, and it therefore follows that the Tribunal does not agree that the application for review can be considered on the footing that the certificate may stand but that the formation of the required opinion pursuant to s66(2) must be denied. What can be accepted is that the conditions that were "relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act" may be accepted as having been "raised blood pressure", "overweight" and "colour blind", and that the last of these is not relevant for present purposes. There are other problems of construction of these statutory provisions, but consideration of them should be deferred.

7.The applicant was confirmed as a permanent employee of the Commonwealth on 15 December 1975. He was appointed as an Orderly in the newly established Family Court of Australia. The holder of this office, we were told, combines the functions of Judge's Associate and tipstaff. The applicant told us that from the commencement of the Family Court's sittings there had been a continuous display of emotional and other problems in and about the courtrooms. In the premises used initially, there could be three hundred people confined in a small area. There were quite a few punches thrown, and there were instances of bad language being used to Judges, and of hysterical outbursts. One woman threatened to go home and murder her children. Another woman who felt very ill and had to retire to a waiting room died next day of a brain haemorrhage. On one occasion a solicitor came to the applicant and told him that her client's husband was outside with a gun and that he was going to shoot her. This man had to be searched accordingly. Often a female Orderly would have to appeal to a male Orderly for help. The applicant said that he was very upset by these incidents.
8.When he took up his duties, the applicant had felt well. By the middle of 1976 he was complaining to his own doctor of chest pain. Examination showed a blood pressure of 160/100. Otherwise examination, including electrocardiogram, was stated to be unremarkable. He was given various medications for his chest pains, including anginine, which appeared to keep his condition under control despite the emotional and physical strains to which he was subjected at his work.
9.Early in 1977 the applicant developed symptoms of diabetes mellitus but this appears to have been capable of being controlled by diet and it was agreed at the hearing that this was not of any significance for present purposes. The same applies to another condition that he appears to have developed about that time, namely gout.
10.On 31 October 1977 the applicant became an Orderly Supervisor, but the problems at the Family Court did not diminish. His own higher duties caused the applicant's responsibilities to increase, and a move to a larger court area which occurred at about this time meant that the pressure of people and the strains consequent thereon were increased. Some sort of problem occurred, the applicant thought, every three or four days, although Mr Pearce, the Office Supervisor at the Family Court told us, after hearing the applicant's evidence, that he thought that the applicant had if anything under-emphasised the frequency of the incidents. The applicant said that there were no police on duty in the Courts and that in the event of disturbances occurring he, and the Supervising Officers, were responsible for keeping order until the police could be summoned. It was said that usually there would be a delay of at least half an hour before police could arrive. On occasions, it was said, they had been unable to come because of duties elsewhere.
11.On 14 September 1978 a man commenced to cause a disturbance in the waiting area outside the Court. The applicant told him to control himself and to act in a proper and respectful manner in the Court area. This was ignored. The case in which the man was involved was called on and he went into the courtroom. The applicant called on Mr Pearce (previously referred to) for help and they stood at the back of the court. The man kept muttering and making rude remarks and the applicant told him to behave himself and to be quiet. When it came out in evidence that the man was awaiting the hearing of charges of kidnapping and armed robbery, he walked out of the Court and slammed the door. There was a commotion outside the Court and the applicant and Mr Pearce went out and tried to control him. He was screaming and being abusive and arguing with other people in the Court area about the Family Court in general, about the laws, and about the particular Judge. The applicant warned him that he could be charged with contempt. The applicant feared that at any moment the man might throw a punch at him. The applicant and Mr Pearce managed to keep the man there until his wife had left the court area, after which he rushed out.
12.After this incident the applicant went to the tearoom to calm down. He said that he was very distressed, that he broke out in very hot perspiration and was shaking. He sat in the tearoom, took some anginine tablets and tried to control himself but the chest pains kept repeating. They would go away for a little while and then come back and then he would take more tablets. When he went home that evening he was still upset and had more pains. He thought that he took 14 or 15 anginine tablets when he lay down. Normally he only took them during the day when he felt distressed. He said that in the morning he felt like a washed out rag. He rang his general practitioner who told him to report directly to hospital. He was admitted on the following day and ultimately, on 25 September 1978, open heart surgery was performed for a three-way by-pass operation. We were told by Dr Kay, whose evidence is later referred to in more detail, that this operation is normally used where there is triple vessel disease, the three vessels being significantly blocked, and a by-pass is put around to take the blood around the block.
13.After the operation the applicant did not return to work. He received a letter from the Attorney-General's Department saying that before he could recommence work he would have to be medically examined. He attended before Dr Genn, a Commonwealth Medical Officer, on 20 December 1978. Dr Genn asked whether he thought he would be able to return to work and the applicant told him that he had no option but to go to work. He explained to the doctor about his being in the Provident account when he joined and the doctor said that in his opinion it was not relevant to the situation. When the applicant expressed doubts about this, Dr Genn said "No, you should retire on the 70 percent pension" and then telephoned the administrative officer at the Attorney-General's Department. Dr Genn was said by the applicant to have told this officer that the "provident situation" had nothing to do with the matter of the applicant's "going out over the operation", and told the applicant to think it over. The applicant went to the Attorney-General's Department and saw the administrative officer and the personnel officer. They said that because of prospective years of service the pension payable would be 65 or 66 percent of salary. The applicant told the Tribunal that he had been very worried, and that the officers said that the doctor had said that that was what he was going to get and that that was it. The applicant sought advice in various quarters and, having told Dr Genn that he would retire, was informed by him that he would be retired as from 15 January 1979, which was the day on which his sick leave was due to expire. The applicant received a letter from the Attorney General's Department dated 9 January 1979 which advised him inter alia that the Commonwealth Medical Officer had recommended his retirement and that his pension would be 65 percent of salary "assuming your benefit is not reduced on medical grounds". In fact the retirement was later backdated to 21 December 1978.

14. The applicant's entitlement to the pension which he had been led to believe would be paid was however reviewed, and he said that he was informed that he would be paid an interim pension while such review was being carried out. This interim pension was paid for some months until in due course it simply failed to arrive. When the applicant enquired he was told that the pension had been cancelled. Ultimately he received an official notification to the effect that he was not entitled to the pension and that he was entitled to a lump sum refund of $5,684.50 (representing one half of final annual salary) less the amount of interim pension that had been received by him. The letter stated that the Commissioner was of the opinion that the applicant's retirement on invalidity grounds was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical condition specified in a Benefit Classification Certificate that was enclosed with the letter or by a physical condition connected with such a condition. The specified conditions have already been set out in paragraph 2 of these reasons.

15. In the circumstances set out above a determination of the entitlement of the applicant that would accord with the Commissioner's decision would require a positive answer being given to one of the four questions set out below which restate the requirements of s66(2)(c) in a convenient form, and in accordance with the questions posed by the Tribunal in Re Vauqhan and the Commissioner for Superannuation (No.78/12023) (1979) 2 ALN (Note 36) p.366:

(i) Was the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement ("the retirement incapacity") caused by a condition specified in the benefit classification certificate?

(ii) Was the retirement incapacity substantially contributed to by a condition specified in the benefit classification certificate?

(iii) Was the retirement incapacity caused by a condition connected with a condition specified in the benefit classification certificate?

(iv) Was the retirement incapacity substantially contributed to by a condition connected with a condition specified in the benefit classification certificate?

16. Dr Genn said that he had recommended retirement because he thought that the type of stress to which the applicant had been subjected would be detrimental to his health. He had doubts about the ability of the applicant to perform the job in terms of his physical condition, his chest pain, and his emotional condition in that stressful situation. Dr Genn had at the time stated the retirement conditions of the applicant as being coronary insufficiency 90 percent; diabetes mellitus 5 percent and gout 5 percent. In evidence he was asked, as he and the other doctors called to give evidence had been asked prior to the hearing, a number of questions based upon the four questions which had been formulated by the Tribunal in Re Vaughan. The representatives of the parties were in agreement that "coronary insufficiency" was the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement. Dr Genn said that he did not consider that the coronary insufficiency was caused by raised blood pressure or by the applicant's having been overweight, and he considered that the retirement condition, namely coronary insufficiency, was not substantially contributed to by one or other of those conditions. But he did consider that the incapacity which he identified as the ground for retirement, namely coronary insufficiency, was caused by coronary atheroma, that is to say fatty deposits in the arteries of the heart, sometimes called plaque. Dr Genn said that "risk factors" in the incidence of coronary atheroma included hypertension, elevated serum liquids, cigarette smoking, diabetes and obesity. He added that he thought that in the case of the applicant his work in the court had been a very substantial cause of his condition. But the presence of the risk factors to which he referred had led Dr Genn, in a letter written to the respondent before the hearing, to answer in the affirmative questions equivalent to questions (iii) and (iv) above, in the following form:

"(c) Was Mr Burr's incapacity which was the ground for retirement caused by a condition or conditions connected with the conditions 'raised blood pressure' and 'overweight';

(d) Was Mr Burr's incapacity which was the ground for retirement substantially contributed to by a condition or conditions connected with the conditions 'raised blood pressure' and 'overweight'."

Such affirmative answers would of course be inconsistent with the belief, entertained by Dr Genn at the time when a decision about the applicant's retirement was being considered, that his medical condition was not relevant to his situation in relation to the Provident Account. We can only say that we are not satisfied that Dr Genn appreciated that, in giving the affirmative answers referred to, he must be taken to have been elevating overweight and high blood pressure, which he described as risk factors in the incidence of coronary atheroma, to a situation in which they may be said to be connected with coronary atheroma. More than this, the evidence from the two cardiologists which we shall shortly set out, and upon which we consider we should principally rely, casts doubt upon Dr Genn's thesis.

17. Dr GR Wagner, a consultant physician specialising in cardiology, was called to give evidence on behalf of the applicant, whom he had examined in April 1980. He found no evidence of heart enlargement or of heart failure and he said that his general examination was unremarkable. The electro-cardiogram was not grossly abnormal but it was consistent with a myocardial infarction in an area of the heart less accessible to the electrocardiogram. In summary, it was his view that the applicant had coronary disease which was then relatively stable and static and for which he had had an operation some 20 months previously. He was slightly hypertensive but his blood pressure was not all that remarkable.

18.Dr Wagner said that statistically speaking there are risk factors which when present tend to bring on or be related to an earlier onset of coronary disease than if those risk factors are not present. Overweight, raised blood pressure and smoking are among those risk factors, yet there are many people who do develop coronary disease in the absence of such risk factors. This Dr Wagner described as a puzzle of modern medicine. In the case of the applicant, he did not think that the hypertension was of such a degree that it could be said that it caused the coronary disease.
19.It may be added that Dr Wagner said that the stress that a person can be exposed to at work, though perhaps less well defined than some other factors, does have an influence on coronary disease, and particularly what he called the "step-wise" deteriorations that can occur in it. The progressive hardening of arteries takes place even if none of the risk factors are present, but coronary occlusions, the actual blocking of a vessel, can definitely be related to stress, and then there is a step-wise aggravation of the condition.
20.Dr HB Kay, a consultant physician specialising in internal medicine and cardiology, was called to give evidence on behalf of the respondent. He had examined the applicant in May 1980. He said that his general examination was within normal limits and that his arteries appeared normal. His electro-cardiogram showed sinus rhythm with minimal residual changes. His heart appeared full size but was not sufficiently (scil. significantly) enlarged. His aorta was normal and his chest was clear. He was in Dr Kay's opinion suffering from coronary sclerosis, a term which he regarded as for this purpose interchangeable with coronary atheroma. His hypertension appeared to be adequately controlled, but he was about ten percent overweight.
21.Dr Kay said that he did not regard the applicant's condition at retirement as having been "coronary insufficiency". Like Dr Wagner, he identified the disease process as having been coronary sclerosis. He said that this led to myocardial ischaemia, which means that insufficient oxygen is supplied to the heart muscle. A doubt having been raised as to the proper expression of the applicant's retirement incapacity as "coronary insufficiency", an expression which the parties, and Dr Genn, had adopted, it is necessary to digress from the statement of Dr Kay's evidence to discuss this point.
22.The phrase "incapacity which was the ground for .... retirement" is not we consider necessarily to be seen as itself connoting a physical or mental condition. It would seem to relate more properly to the outward result that leads to a person being unable to perform, that is to say to lack the capacity to perform, his or her duties. The distinction between "incapacity" and a "condition" is clearly made in the legislative text, where the question is raised as to whether the incapacity was caused or substantially contributed to by a condition or conditions. We understand Dr Kay's difficulty with the phrase "coronary insufficiency" as having been that he did not regard it as a disease process. In Blakiston's Gould Medical Dictionary, 4th ed, coronary insufficiency is defined as "Prolonged precordial pain or discomfort without conventional evidence of myocardial infarction; subendocardial ischemia due to a disparity between coronary blood flow and myocardial needs". It is thus a result, a symptom, which is precisely what Dr Kay meant when he declined to regard coronary insufficiency as a disease. But it is we think appropriate to regard it as having constituted the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement. Likewise, coronary sclerosis may on the evidence be aptly regarded as having been the physical condition which caused or substantially contributed to the coronary insufficiency. Having so stated, the questions set out in paragraph 14 above may be restated in terms which incorporate the various phrases which we have been discussing:

(i) Was the coronary insufficiency caused by the condition overweight or by the condition raised blood pressure?

(ii) Was the coronary insufficiency substantially contributed to by overweight or by raised blood pressure?

(iii) Was the coronary insufficiency caused by coronary sclerosis, and was that condition connected with overweight or raised blood pressure?

(iv) Was the coronary insufficiency substantially contributed to by coronary sclerosis, and was that condition connected with overweight or raised blood pressure?

23.Having stated what we identify as the relevant questions, it may be said that Dr Kay's evidence was not directly related to the question whether overweight or hypertension was "connected with" coronary sclerosis. Nor of course need it have been. That is a question for the Tribunal. Broadly speaking, his evidence was directed to the question of what effect there was, if any, of overweight or raised blood pressure on coronary sclerosis.
24.As to overweight, Dr Kay said that it has no direct effect on coronary sclerosis although it is a risk factor in relation to coronary or myocardial ischaemia because if a person is overweight then any effort which he carries out may induce ischaemia somewhat earlier. It does not necessarily affect the actual coronary sclerosis. He then went on to refer to the same five risk factors that had been mentioned by Dr Genn. These five factors had, he said, been commonly found to have been associated with symptoms of coronary sclerosis. But he went on to say that there are differing views on the extent to which high blood pressure is a relevant contributing factor in coronary sclerosis. His view was that it had been established that high blood pressure has a clear effect on the tendency to heart failure and the tendency to stroke, but that there has been no clear evidence so far that it has any effect on the incidence of myocardial infarction which would be a sequel of developing coronary sclerosis. So far, he said, nobody has been able to show any clear statistical evidence in relation to its aggravation of the coronary sclerosis itself. He went on to say that a person who is overweight and/or has high blood pressure is more likely to produce symptoms. The applicant, in the light of his weight and blood pressure at the point of entry to the service, had, in Dr Kay's view, somewhere about double the risk of the ordinary person of producing symptoms. But this evidence does not lead the Tribunal to conclude that either the applicant's overweight or his raised blood pressure was connected with the coronary sclerosis. What Dr Kay said, and we thought said very clearly, was that the overweight or hypertensive person is less able to cope with problems that occur in relation to his heart. If a person is overweight, the risk of his dying of pneumonia is much higher. If he has a heart attack his risk of dying is about three times as high as it would be if he were fit. But there is, Dr Kay said, still no clear proof as to how high blood pressure could affect coronary sclerosis as distinct from the fact that if there is coronary sclerosis then there is another disease acting on the heart and there are apt to be symptoms. If a person has coronary sclerosis and is also overweight and hypertensive then, like smoking, these factors will produce pain more easily and the studies that have been made, he said, recognized symptomatic heart disease, but did not recognize whether the factors mentioned caused coronary sclerosis. They recognized the symptoms that appeared whether they be angina, sudden death, heart failure or myocardial infarction. Overweight and hypertension are, he said, risk factors in causing complications of coronary sclerosis.

25. It is true that in one passage in his evidence (Transcript pp.157-8) Dr Kay appears to be suggesting that blood pressure "does have a big effect on coronary sclerosis and with overweight in the same way". But this passage, which appears to the Tribunal to be quite inconsistent with the remainder of Dr Kay's evidence, is immediately followed by two sentences reading as follows: "The overweight has no direct - it hardly shows a direct effect on coronary sclerosis although it is a risk factor listed for coronary or myocardial ischaemia because if a person is overweight then any effort he carries out induces ischaemia sometimes earlier. It does not necessarily affect the actual coronary sclerosis." This passage is, it seems to us, entirely consistent with Dr Kay's evidence as previously set out, to the effect that overweight and high blood pressure are related to a person's tendency to produce symptoms given the occurrence of another disease acting on the heart. That Dr Kay did not in fact regard raised blood pressure as being connected with coronary sclerosis is clear from a passage in his cross-examination. As previously explained, he had regarded coronary sclerosis as the retirement incapacity. When he was referred to questions the equivalent of (iii) and (iv) above he was not regarding coronary sclerosis as a candidate for the position and, significantly, he said (Transcript p.168) that "we have already discussed the matter of coronary sclerosis as such, and I do not understand what other conditions would be involved. I think they were not relevant questions."

26.We can now turn to state the answers that we consider should be given to the questions posed in paragraph 22 above. As to questions (i) and (ii), no witness considered that coronary insufficiency could be said to have been caused or substantially contributed to by overweight or raised blood pressure, and it was conceded that each question should be answered in the negative. Dr Genn may be taken as having given an affirmative answer to questions (iii) and (iv), and we have commented on the problems in relation to his answers in paragraph 16 above, but the evidence of the two specialist cardiologists, and in particular that of Dr Kay who was called for the respondent, does not support the giving of affirmative answers to these questions.
27.If it should be thought that, contrary to what we have said in paragraph 22 above, coronary sclerosis should have been identified as the incapacity which was the ground for retirement, the position is even clearer. On no basis could the conditions overweight or raised blood pressure be said on the evidence to have caused or substantially contributed to the coronary sclerosis. No other condition connected with overweight or raised blood pressure was on this hypothesis suggested as a candidate for a supposition that it had caused or substantially contributed to the coronary sclerosis.
28.Mr Huttner addressed an argument to the Tribunal centred upon the need to take a broad view of the relevant provisions of the Act and to ensure that they are not interpreted in such a way as to impose unduly stringent restrictions upon the ability of the Authority to exclude from unqualified admission to the Fund persons whose medical condition at entry has something about it that makes him or her a risk for early retirement. Mr Huttner argued that s.l84(2) of the Act could not be considered as if it were in a vacuum. Rather he said that it should be interpreted in the light of what can be discerned from an analysis of the Act, from a consideration of what benefit classification certificates are and for what purposes they exist, and from a consideration of how the whole system created by the Act operates. A benefit classification certificate which the Commissioner has been obliged to issue under sl84(2) is by virtue of that sub-section deemed to be issued under S16(4). It is not obvious to the Tribunal why the legislation so provides, but Mr Huttner offered two explanations, the first being that it displayed a legislative intention to "inter-link concepts and interpretations". Sub-section 16(4) requires the Commissioner to form an opinion as to whether a person is not likely to continue to be an eligible employee until his normal retiring age. In order to reach that opinion, the sub-section requires him to consider the report of the examination which the person has undergone and entitles him, as Mr Huttner emphasized, to consider any other matter that he thinks relevant. For example there is nothing, it was contended, to debar him from looking at actuarial tables. Mr Huttner submitted that the fundamental concept of the superannuation scheme has at all times been to enquire whether or not a man is at some sort of risk for early retirement. Thus, he said, to postulate too high a requirement for likelihood of early retirement would be to defeat the purpose of the complex provisions of the Act dealing with "risk" candidates. Likewise in order not to defeat the purpose of S.16(4), the words "substantially contributed to" and "connected with" in S184(2) should not, it was said, be interpreted too stringently.

29. We consider that this argument takes us too far away from the ordinary processes of statutory interpretation. The only question of statutory interpretation which presents any difficulty in the present case as we see it relates to the meaning of the words "connected with". We have in the last resort to decide whether coronary sclerosis is a condition connected with overweight or raised blood pressure. That question is, it may be said, one of some difficulty in the circumstances of this particular case. We were in fact referred to a number of authorities as to the meaning of the words "connected with" but we did not see them as offering guidance in the present situation. The construction of the words "connected with" is simply a matter of the meaning of ordinary words of the English language and we do not see ourselves as being authorised to embark upon a consideration of the policy of other portions of the Act, nor do we consider that such a course is necessary. We appreciate the force of Mr Huttner's anxiety that the Act should not be construed so as to inhibit the extent to which limitations can be placed upon entrants to the Fund who, to take this case as an example, are overweight or have raised blood pressure. But why should it be concluded that a decision favourable to the present applicant should increase this anxiety? The thrust of our present reasoning, based on the evidence, is that the applicant's weight and blood pressure have not been shown to have had anything to do with his condition of coronary sclerosis, and if they had nothing to do with it they are not to be seen as having been connected with it, however those words are defined. Perhaps his diet or his previous smoking habits were connected with it. Again, if he had had a heart attack, or a stroke, that had led to incapacity as a ground for retirement, the position would on the evidence in all likelihood have been different. But these were not the facts. What happened was that his arteries became blocked, and an operation was necessary to by-pass them as a way of relieving his symptoms.

30. In truth we see no reason, save perhaps in one respect, to depart from the reasoning contained in the following passage which appears at p.11 of the Reasons for Decision of the Tribunal in Re Vaughan:

"It was suggested that 'connected with' does not in the context connote a causal connection but connotes only that the condition be related to or associated with, in this case, hypertension. For present purposes this analysis could, without any commitment for future cases, be accepted up to a point, but the connection would we consider have to be at least a real or demonstrable connection and not simply a casual connection, as for instance by the condition in question being simply one of a number of conditions that are related to one aspect of human physiology. The fact that a person has hypertension is a reflection of the general cardiovascular tree, but if he subsequently develops a coronary thrombosis it is difficult to say what precipitated that particular attack, and at what stage the generalised disease became more specific, so as to narrow it down to the heart muscle."

32.In argument Mr Huttner was critical of the words "real or demonstrable" which appear in the passage quoted. He said that if this meant that it was necessary to adduce medical proof of a connection, and that it was not enough to show that various risk factors were recognized as being related to various conditions, then too high a burden would be placed upon medical science and there would in effect be no way of excluding persons who were at risk from the Fund. We do not think that the words "or demonstrable" were intended to connote any notion of technical proof. They were intended to form a contrast with what we saw as a casual connection only. In terms of the present case, we do not see the required connection between overweight, or raised blood pressure, and coronary sclerosis as being established simply because they each are connected with, in the sense of "having something to do with", the applicant's cardiovascular tree.
33.Having regard to the foregoing findings, and for the reasons stated, the decision of the Tribunal is that the decision of the Commissioner, both to issue the benefit classification certificate and to determine that the benefit entitlement of the applicant should be determined in accordance with s66(2) of the Act, should be set aside, and the benefit entitlement of the applicant determined in accordance with s66(1) of the Act.

34 . An application was made by counsel for the applicant for an order in respect of witness' fees and allowances for expenses, but the power conferred by s67 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act is limited to a case where a witness has been summoned to appear, and as we understand it no summonses were here issued pursuant to s.40(1A) of that Act. Had such summonses been in fact issued, there would of course still have been a question whether such fees and allowances should be paid by the applicant or, in whole or in part, by the Commonwealth.
