Respondent: Commissioner of Taxation

Tribunal Numbers: 2023/9120, 2023/9121

Decision: Pursuant to subparagraph 105(c)(ii) of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth) the Tribunal sets aside the Objection Decision dated 13 October 2023 under review and remits the matter to the Respondent for reconsideration in accordance with the following orders:

The Amended Assessments for the Relevant Years be further amended to reflect the following findings of the Tribunal:

  1. No car fringe benefits arose under the FBTAA in relation to Car [1993] CthArbRp 1; 1, Car 3 and Car 4.
  2. Car fringe benefits arose under the FBTAA in relation to Car [1992] CthArbRp 74; 2, Car 5, Car 6 and Car 7, with the taxable value of such car fringe benefits being calculated by reference to the statutory formula method under section 9 of the FBTAA.

The Penalty Assessments for the Relevant Years are to be amended to reflect the amendments to the Amended Assessments as they relate to the quantum of any tax shortfall amount, with the base penalty amount being determined at 50% of the tax shortfall amount on the basis that the shortfall amount resulted from recklessness by the Applicant as to the operation of a taxation law, and no remission of any part of the penalty is appropriate.

Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. This application originally involved a dispute about the fringe benefits tax (FBT) treatment of seven vehicles (the Cars) owned by Prestige Form Group NSW Pty Limited (the Applicant). Broadly it related to whether the Cars were used by personnel of the Applicant other than for work or business purposes, and the correct methodology for determining any FBT amounts.
  2. The dispute arose in the context of an audit conducted by the Commissioner of Taxation (the Respondent) relating to the years ended 31 March 2018 to 31 March 2021 (the Relevant Years). The Respondent assessed the Applicant to additional amounts of FBT and imposed administrative penalties, against which the Applicant objected. The Respondent disallowed the Applicant’s objection in full and the Applicant then sought a review of the objection decision by the Tribunal.
  3. Between the date that the application for Tribunal review was lodged and the date of the hearing, the parties had agreed or conceded a number of issues such that the subject matter of the hearing mostly involved only two of the Cars and the appropriate methodology for calculating FBT amounts in relation to them. The two competing methodologies were referred to by the parties as the ‘operating cost method’ (OCM) and the ‘statutory formula method’ (SFM).
  4. The Tribunal made directions for the parties to file written closing submissions after the hearing, including submissions as to how the ‘agreed’ issues should be disposed of by the Tribunal.

BACKGROUND

  1. The Applicant conducts a business in New South Wales of providing concrete formwork services to clients and projects in New South Wales, including erecting and dismantling framework at large construction sites.
  2. The parties described the Cars originally the subject of the dispute as follows[1]:
    (a) Car 1: Land Rover Range Rover Sport

    (b) Car 2: Land Rover Discovery

    (c) Car 3: Toyota Kluger

    (d) Car 4: Toyota Kluger

    (e) Car 5: Nissan Patrol

    (f) Car 6: Land Rover Range Rover

    (g) Car 7: Jaguar F-Pace

  3. The Cars were acquired by the Applicant between February 2016 and October 2020, and were registered in the name of the Applicant during some or all of the Relevant Years.[2]
  4. The following individuals were identified as officers, employees or contractors of the Applicant:
    (a) Mr El Charkaoui, the sole director and shareholder of the Applicant since its incorporation in June 2014.

    (b) Ms Santa, the General Manager and an employee of the Applicant during the Relevant Years.[3]

    (c) Mr Jebara, the Construction Manager and an employee of the Applicant.

    (d) Mr Charkawi, the Office Manager and an employee of the Applicant during the Relevant Years.

    (e) Mr Taha, who stated that he was a self-employed sub-contractor to the Applicant who assisted with bookkeeping and office administration for the Applicant.

  5. Each of these individuals filed witness statements. Mr El Sayed, a contractor to the Applicant, also filed witness statements in relation to the ownership and use arrangements for Car 1. However, due to the narrowing of issues in dispute, only Mr El Charkaoui, Ms Santa, Mr Taha and Mr Jebara were ultimately required to attend the hearing for examination.

Assessment and review processes

  1. The Respondent commenced a review of the Applicant’s FBT affairs in April 2022, which was escalated to an audit in June 2022. The Respondent said that in November 2022 it had requested information and documentation from the Applicant to assist with the audit. However, this information had not been provided by December 2022, so the Respondent provided a ‘default assessment warning letter’ to the Applicant and requested that the Applicant lodge FBT returns for the Relevant Years.[4]
  2. The Applicant lodged FBT returns for the Relevant Years on 19 January 2023.[5] The Respondent said that the Applicant had not provided evidence supporting the amounts reported in these returns or its FBT calculations more generally. The Respondent’s view was that these returns understated the amount of FBT owing.
  3. On 22 March 2023, the Respondent issued Notices of Amended Assessments (the Amended Assessments) to the Applicant for the Relevant Years which increased the amount of FBT payable by the Applicant in each of the Relevant Years.[6] Broadly, these Amended Assessments were issued on the basis that the use of the Cars gave rise to car fringe benefits, and the taxable amount(s) of those fringe benefits was to be calculated under the SFM. In aggregate, the Amended Assessments represented a shortfall of payment of FBT of $505,298.88 across the Relevant Years.
  4. On the same date, the Respondent also issued Notices of Shortfall Penalty to the Applicant (the Penalty Assessments).[7] The Penalty Assessments totalled $224,149.95, determined on the basis of a false or misleading statement having been made by the Applicant or its tax agent in circumstances of ‘recklessness’.
  5. The Applicant objected to the Amended Assessments and Penalty Assessments on 27 May 2023 (the Objection)[8], and the Respondent disallowed the Objection in full by decision dated 13 October 2023 (the Objection Decision).[9] The Objection Decision specifically referred to Amended Assessments and Penalty Assessments for Cars 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 7. The reasons given for the Objection Decision included:
    (a) The Respondent was not satisfied on the information provided at that time that Cars 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 7 were not available for private use of an employee or employees.

    (b) In relation to Car 1, the Applicant had not provided information explaining the arrangement with Mr El Sayad. Therefore, the Respondent would not amend the taxable value of the car fringe benefit for Car 1 to nil as requested by the Applicant.

    (c) The Respondent was not satisfied with the logbook information provided by the Applicant for Cars 6 and 7, or had not been provided with requested calculation worksheets. The Respondent would, therefore, not allow the use of the OCM.

    (d) The Applicant had not provided information requested by the Respondent in relation to any of the vehicles. As there was insufficient substantiation of or supporting evidence for the Applicant’s claims, the Respondent would not allow any amendments to the existing returns.

  6. Car 5 was not referred to in the Objection Decision. The Applicant told the Tribunal that when it lodged the FBT returns in January 2023, it did so on a basis consistent with the position taken by the Respondent. The Respondent indicated that Car 5 was involved in the Amended Assessments for one of the Relevant Years.[10]

Tribunal processes

  1. The Applicant lodged an application for review of the Objection Decision on 29 November 2023, stating that the Amended Assessments and Penalty Assessments were excessive, that the Cars were ‘work related’ and that one Car was payment to a contractor.
  2. The Respondent provided to the Tribunal certain documents obtained from the Applicant during the audit and objection processes, including partial logbooks for some of the Cars. Copies of motor vehicle insurance records were also obtained under summons issued by the Tribunal.[11] Appended to the witness statements were further documents, including photographs of vehicles and odometer readings, invoices, contracts for sale, copies of pages of diaries, registration details and FBT calculations.
  3. The Applicant filed a Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions (SFIC) dated 15 November 2024. In this SFIC, the Applicant contended that Car [1993] CthArbRp 121; 2, Car 3 and Car 4 were used solely for the Applicant’s business purposes, such that no FBT was payable. In relation to Car 1, the Applicant said that this vehicle had been given to a contractor to whom the Applicant owed a debt, in satisfaction of that debt. Therefore, Car 1 had not been used by the Applicant, for work or personal purposes, during the Relevant Years. However, the Applicant said that Car 5 was being used by an associate of the Applicant’s employee for solely personal purposes.[12] The Applicant also agreed that Car 6 and Car 7 were used for a mix of personal and business purposes, but that ‘full logbook records’ were available for each of these Cars, such that the OCM could be used by the Applicant to calculate the relevant amounts of FBT.
  4. The Respondent filed a SFIC dated 14 February 2025. The Respondent contended that the Applicant had not provided sufficient evidence to discharge its onus of proof that Car 1, Car 2, Car 3 and Car 4 had not been applied to private use or available for private use of an employee of the Applicant. The Respondent acknowledged the Applicant’s statement that Car 5 was used for private purposes, and therefore submitted that Car 5 was subject to FBT which should be calculated on the basis of the SFM.[13] In relation to Car 6 and 7, the Respondent understood the Applicant’s position to be that each of these vehicles was subject to FBT. The Respondent did not agree that the OCM was available to the Applicant in relation to Car 6 and Car 7, and contended that the SFM was to be applied.
  5. The matter was originally listed for hearing in September 2025. In August 2025, the Tribunal listed a directions hearing to discuss the Applicant’s non-compliance with previous directions of the Tribunal, particularly in relation to filing of evidence and material. The Tribunal had also been told by the Applicant’s legal representative that they were having trouble contacting the Applicant. Ms Santa appeared at the directions hearing for the Applicant, confirming that the Applicant had faced certain challenges, including financial problems, and would not be ready for a hearing in September. She apologised to the Tribunal but said that the Applicant did wish to proceed with its application. The Tribunal agreed to an adjournment of the hearing, but made clear that it expected the Applicant’s compliance with future directions and its attendance at the relisted hearing. The Tribunal mentions this matter because a significant volume of evidence, including further witness statements containing additional supporting evidence, was only filed after this event. This included material that had apparently been sought by the Respondent for the purposes of, and even prior to the commencement of, this proceeding.
  6. The Applicant filed an amended SFIC dated 25 September 2025. In this amended SFIC, the Applicant conceded that FBT was payable on Car 5 using the SFM. However, the Applicant said that the resulting amount of FBT payable was the same as that disclosed in the FBT returns lodged in January 2023.[14] The Applicant otherwise maintained the position it had expressed in its original SFIC in relation to the other Cars.
  7. The Applicant filed an outline of submissions dated 10 November 2025. These submissions were generally aligned to the position it set out in its amended SFIC in relation to each of the Cars.
  8. The Respondent filed an outline of submissions dated 24 November 2025. In these submissions, the Respondent said that despite the issuing of the Amended Assessments, it had agreed to ‘confine the issues for determination in the proceeding’ to the determination of what the assessments should have been in relation to Car [1992] CthArbRp 74; 2, Car 5, Car 6 and Car 7.[15] The Respondent submitted that the Tribunal should make a decision affirming the Objection Decision to the extent that the Amended Assessments related to Car 2, Car 6 and Car 7 and otherwise remit the matter to the Respondent under subparagraph 105(c)(ii) of the ART Act to amend the Amended Assessments accordingly.[16] The Tribunal therefore understood the Respondent to have effectively conceded that no car fringe benefits arose in relation to Car [1993] CthArbRp 1; 1, Car 3 and Car 4 in any of the Relevant Years.
  9. The matter was relisted for a hearing commencing on 8 December 2025.
  10. At the beginning of the hearing, the Applicant’s representative advised the Tribunal that the Applicant was no longer pursuing its position in relation to Car 2, on the basis that it was too difficult for the Applicant to prove.[17] The Applicant said that it therefore did not propose to rely on Mr Charkawi’s witness statements and did not believe it was necessary for him to attend the hearing. The Respondent had not been advised of this change in position and the Tribunal suggested that the Respondent be given time to consider whether there was any other basis for Mr Charkawi being required for cross-examination before releasing him from the hearing.[18]
  11. Although the parties only advised the Tribunal of changes to their positions relatively late in the proceeding, the Tribunal acknowledges that the parties had made some effort to narrow the matters ultimately in dispute.
  12. At the conclusion of the hearing, it was agreed that the parties would be given time to provide written closing submissions. This was particularly appropriate in circumstances where the issues in dispute had changed right up to the opening of the hearing, and the parties required time to clarify the orders they were now seeking from the Tribunal in relation to each Car. It was agreed that each party would provide its written closing submissions to the Tribunal by 20 February 2026. It was also flagged that a further case management directions hearing might be required if matters arose from the closing submissions, such as the parties being unable to provide a form of wording of orders by consent for matters that had been agreed.
  13. Following the filing of the closing written submissions by each party, the Tribunal understands the positions of the parties at the conclusion of the hearing in relation to each Car to be as follows:
    (a) Car 1: The FBT status of this Car was no longer in dispute between the parties. The Respondent did not directly refer to a position on this vehicle in its closing submissions. The Tribunal therefore understands that the parties are agreed that no car fringe benefits arose in relation to Car 1, and the Tribunal was not otherwise required to determine whether any amendments to the Applicant’s assessments as they related to Car 1 were correct.[19]

    (a) Car 2: The FBT status of this Car was no longer in dispute between the parties. The Applicant advised the Tribunal that it did not have sufficient evidence to meet the burden of proof should it have sought to challenge the assessments as they related to this Car. Therefore, the Tribunal is satisfied that car fringe benefits arose in relation to Car 2 and that the taxable value of such fringe benefits should be calculated on the basis of the SFM.

    (b) Car 3: The FBT status of this Car was no longer in dispute between the parties. The Respondent did not directly refer to a position on this vehicle in its closing submissions but did not ask the Tribunal to affirm the Objection Decision in relation to that Car.[20] The Tribunal therefore is satisfied that no car fringe benefits arose in relation to Car 3.

    (c) Car 4: The FBT status of this Car was no longer in dispute between the parties. The Respondent did not directly refer to a position on this vehicle in its closing submissions, but did not ask the Tribunal to affirm the Objection Decision in relation to that Car.[21] The Tribunal therefore is satisfied that no car fringe benefits arose in relation to Car 4.

    (d) Car 5: The FBT status of this Car was effectively no longer in dispute between the parties. The Applicant told the Tribunal that it had previously made a concession to the Respondent that car fringe benefits arose in relation to Car 5 and that the FBT payable should be calculated using the SFM. The Tribunal is therefore satisfied that car fringe benefits arose in relation to Car 5 and were to be calculated on the basis of the SFM. The Applicant also told the Tribunal that the FBT returns lodged by the Applicant reflected an amount consistent with this approach and therefore it was not necessary for the Tribunal to make a decision in relation to the Amended Assessments in this regard. The Respondent has submitted that the Objection Decision be affirmed in relation to Car 5 but did not confirm whether it agreed that the FBT returns lodged by the Applicant were correct as to quantum of the taxable value of the relevant car fringe benefits. The Tribunal was unable to verify the calculations of FBT amounts relating to Car 5 included in any returns or assessments. The Tribunal has set out below how it believes this matter should be addressed.

    (e) Car 6: The parties agree that car fringe benefits arose in relation to Car 6. The Applicant has acknowledged that this Car was used by employees of the Applicant for both business and personal purposes and therefore the Tribunal is satisfied that car fringe benefits arose. The Applicant submits that it should be permitted to calculate FBT payable under the OCM in each of the Relevant Years. The Respondent submits that the Applicant must use the SFM in each of the Relevant Years. The parties therefore remained in dispute as to whether the OCM could be used by the Applicant to calculate the taxable value of such fringe benefits or whether it was required to use the SFM.

    (f) Car 7: The positions in relation to Car 7 are as set out in relation to Car 6.

  14. Following the various concessions made by the parties prior to and at the outset of the hearing, the focus of evidence and submissions during the hearing was in relation to the issue of whether the Applicant was able to use the OCM in relation to calculating FBT amounts for Car 6 and Car 7, or whether it was required to use the SFM. The Tribunal therefore did not need to consider whether any of the Cars were used for any private purpose (that is, applied for the private use of an employee or made available for the private use of an employee), rather than entirely business purposes. A substantial amount of documentary and witness evidence had been filed by the parties prior to the hearing relating to the question of ‘personal versus business’ usage, however, this material became largely irrelevant to the issues that the Tribunal was required to decided following the hearing.
  15. To the extent that the parties had reached an agreed position:
    (a) The Applicant submitted that the ‘parts’ of the Objection Decision under review relating to Cars 1, 3 and 4 (and the administrative penalties relating to those Cars) be set aside, and the parts relating to Cars 2 and 5 be maintained.

    (b) The Respondent submitted that the Objection Decision be affirmed to the extent that the Amended Assessments and Penalty Assessments related to Cars 2 and 5, and that the matter otherwise be remitted to the Respondent to amend the Amended Assessments and Penalty Assessments under subparagraph 105(c)(ii) of the ART Act.

  16. The submissions of the parties specifically relating to the Penalty Assessments are discussed separately below.

RELEVANT LAW

  1. It is not disputed that:
    (a) The Applicant was a person dissatisfied with an objection decision of the Respondent, which was a ‘reviewable objection decision’ and therefore the Applicant could apply to the Tribunal for review of the Objection Decision: subparagraph 14ZZ(a)(i) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (TAA 1953).

    (b) The Applicant has the burden of proving that the Amended Assessments and Penalty Assessments are excessive or otherwise incorrect and what those assessments should have been: subparagraph 14ZZK(b)(i) of the TAA 1953. The parties were also in agreement that the standard of proof that the Applicant was required to meet was the ‘balance of probabilities.’[22]

FBT provisions

  1. The following provisions of the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 (Cth) (FBTAA 1986) also appeared uncontroversial, but are restated to provide context to the dispute about the method for calculating the amount of any fringe benefits arising:
    (a) Tax is payable by an employer in respect of the ‘fringe benefits taxable amount’ of the employer for a year of tax: subsection 66(1) of the FBTAA 1986.

    (b) An employer’s fringe benefits taxable amount takes into account the taxable value of fringe benefits provided by the employer to its employees: Part IIA of the FBTAA 1986.

    (c) A ‘car fringe benefit’ is a ‘fringe benefit’ that is a ‘car benefit’: section 136.

    (d) The term ‘fringe benefit’ is also defined extensively in section 136, but for present purposes includes, in relation to a year of tax, a benefit provided during or in respect of the year of tax to any employee or associate of the employee by the employer of the employee, an associate of the employer.[23]

    (e) A ‘car benefit’ is defined in subsection 7(1). The circumstances in which a car benefit will arise include where, in respect of the employment of an employee, a car held by an employer is applied to a private use by the employee or an associate of the employee, or is taken to be available for the private use of the employee or their associate.[24]

    (f) A ‘car’ is defined in subsection 136(1) by reference to the definition of that term in subsection 995-1 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth). There was no dispute that each of the Cars was a ‘car’ for these purposes.

  2. The Respondent referred to recent authority of the Federal Court which held that a director of a company was to be treated as an ‘employee’ for the purposes of the FBTAA 1986: see Commissioner of Taxation v SEPL Pty Ltd as trustee of the SFT Trust [2025] FCA 581.[25] In relation to Car 6, the Applicant confirmed that this vehicle was driven by Mr El Charkaoui for both work and personal use, and that Mr El Charkaoui has been the sole director of the Applicant since its incorporation.[26] The Tribunal notes that an appeal from this decision was allowed by the Full Court of the Federal Court just prior to the Tribunal issuing its decision in this matter.[27]
  3. In relation to the competing methods for determining the taxable value of car fringe benefits provided by the Applicant:
    (a) Section 9 of the FBTAA 1986 provides that where one or more car fringe benefits in relation to an employer in relation to a year of tax relate to a particular car held by a particular person, the taxable value of that fringe benefit (or aggregate of the taxable values of those fringe benefits) in relation to that year of tax is to be determined in accordance with the statutory formula set out in subsections 9(1) and 9(2).[28] This is the SFM.

    (b) However, an employer may, in relation to a particular car, elect that the ‘cost basis’ (or OCM) set out in section 10 of the FBTAA 1986 apply in relation to all car fringe benefits, in relation to the employer, in relation to a year of tax that relate to that car: subsection 10(1). Subsection 10(2) sets out the formula for determining the taxable value under the OCM method, by reference to a ‘business use percentage’ and the operating cost of the car.

  4. In relation to the OCM, an election made by an employer under subsection 10(1) in relation to a year of tax must be made by notice in writing to the Respondent and lodged with the Respondent on or before the relevant ‘declaration date’: subsection 10(4). The declaration date is defined in subsection 136(1) by reference to the date on which the employer’s FBT return for the year is to be lodged.[29]
  5. Part X of the FBTAA 1986 contains substantiation provisions relating to evidentiary documentation required for statutory purposes, including:
    (a) Section 123 which provides that where certain records, such as logbook records and odometer records, are not maintained or kept by an employer, they are treated as never having been maintained.

    (b) The requirement for a business use percentage to be specified in writing on or before the declaration date for the relevant FBT year: section 123A.

    (c) The ability of the Respondent to decide that the substantiation rules do not apply where the Respondent is satisfied from the nature and quality of evidence that a person has that the taxable value of a fringe benefit is not more than the amount specified in a return for the FBT year: section 123B.

  6. Where the Respondent does not have sufficient information to make an assessment of the fringe benefits taxable amount of an employer of a year of tax, the fringe benefits taxable amount will be deemed to be the amount that, in the opinion of the Respondent, ‘might reasonably be expected to be’ that fringe benefits taxable amount: subsection 124(1).

Administrative Penalties

  1. Part 4-25 of Schedule 1 to the TAA 1953 contains provisions relating to ‘charges and penalties’ including Division 284 which sets out when and how administrative penalties will be imposed for false or misleading statements made by a taxpayer to the Respondent.
  2. A person will be liable to an administrative penalty if they make a statement to the Respondent that is false or misleading in a material particular because of things in it or omitted from it: subsection 284-75(1). However, they will not be liable for a penalty if they took ‘reasonable care’ in connection with the making of the statement: subsection 284-75(5).
  3. A person will also not be liable for an administrative penalty where they engaged a registered tax agent or BAS agent and gave the agent all relevant taxation information, the registered tax agent or BAS agent made the false or misleading statement and the false or misleading nature of the statement did not result from intentional disregard by the agent of a taxation law or recklessness by the agent as to the operation of a taxation law: subsection 284-75(6).[30]
  4. In determining the amount of any penalty to be imposed:
    (a) A ‘base penalty amount’ is determined by applying a percentage to a ‘shortfall amount’: sections 284-80, 284-85 and 284-90. A shortfall amount will arise to the extent that a tax-related liability, as worked out on the basis of a false or misleading statement, is less than it would be if the statement were not false or misleading.

    (b) The type of behaviour that resulted in the shortfall amount determines the percentage to be applied. It will be 50% of the shortfall amount if the shortfall resulted from ‘recklessness’ by the taxpayer or their agent as to the operation of a taxation law: see item 2 in the Table in subsection 284-90(1). [31]

    (c) The base penalty amount may be increased by 20% in certain circumstances, including where a base penalty amount has previously been worked out for the taxpayer in relation to the making of a false or misleading statement: section 284-220.

  5. There are no separate definitions of the terms ‘reasonable care’ and ‘reckless’ in the TAA 1953. The Explanatory Memorandum to the Bill which introduced the penalty provisions stated that:[32]
    Reasonable care requires a taxpayer to make a reasonable attempt to comply with the provisions of the ITAA and regulations. The effort required is one commensurate with all the taxpayer’s circumstances, including the taxpayer’s knowledge, education, experience and skill.
  6. In the context of what constitutes ‘reckless’ conduct, the Federal Court has stated that:[33]
    Recklessness in this context means to include in a tax statement material upon which the Act or regulations are to operate, knowing that there is a real, as opposed to fanciful, risk that the material may be incorrect, or be grossly indifferent as to whether or not the material is true and correct, and that a reasonable person in the position of the statement-maker would see there was a real risk that the Act and regulations may not operate correctly to lead to the assessment of the proper tax payable because of the content of the tax statement. So understood, the proscribed conduct is more than mere negligence and must amount to a gross carelessness.

And[34]:

‘the context makes it clear that recklessness means something more than failure to exercise reasonable care ... but less than an intentional disregard of the Act.’

  1. There is a discretion to remit all or part of an administrative penalty: section 298-20 of Schedule 1 to the TAA 1953. The TAA 1953 does not set out particular matters to be considered when exercising this discretion. However, case authority indicates that the decision maker must consider whether it is appropriate in ‘all the circumstances’ to remit the penalty[35] and that the power to remit must be exercised for a proper purpose, in accordance with the objects of the TAA 1953.[36]

APPROPRIATE METHODOLOGY FOR DETERMINING FBT AMOUNTS

Car 6

  1. The Applicant referred particularly to the witness evidence given by Mr El Charkaoui, Ms Santa and Mr Taha in relation to Car 6.[37]
  2. Car 6 was acquired by the Applicant on 15 April 2017.
  3. In relation to Car 6 the Applicant accepts that a car fringe benefit arose, being a car benefit provided by the Applicant to Mr El Charkaoui, on the basis that Car 6 was driven by Mr El Charkaoui for both work and personal use. However, the Applicant contends that it should be permitted to use the OCM to calculate the taxable value of that fringe benefit on the basis that:
    (a) Mr El Charkaoui kept a logbook for Car 6 between 20 April 2017 and 19 July 2017[38], and based on the entries in that logbook a business use percentage (of 84.93%) could be determined.

    (b) That logbook contained the details required by subsection 136(1) of the FBTAA.

    (c) The logbook was representative of Mr El Charkaoui’s work-related travel in the Relevant Years. As such, it was required to have been kept for a continuous period of not less than twelve weeks, and it was valid for five years.[39]

    (d) Although the Applicant had not made an election that the OCM apply to fringe benefits relating to Car 6 on or before the statutory ‘declaration date’, the Tribunal should, standing in the shoes of the Respondent, exercise the discretion to allow the Applicant to make that election on a later date.

  4. The Applicant also referred to an email dated 14 August 2023 from an officer of the Respondent which said that[40]:
    ‘Whilst this logbook does not appear to be contemporaneous, it does not contain any errors. We can apply the Commissioner’s discretion under s 123 of the FBTAA and accept that it is an accurate depiction of the use of the vehicle. Further, the type of vehicle, being a Land Rover, is more inclined to be used for business purposes. At this stage, we will accept the amendment of the Land Rover calculations from the statutory method to the operating cost method.

    However, to do this your client must provide me with the calculation worksheets for the operating cost method. We need a breakdown of the actual operating costs and any deemed costs, supported with documentary evidence. Please refer to Chapter 7 of the Fringe benefits tax – a guide for employers for more information on the calculation of the operating cost method.’

  5. In relation to the material provided by the Applicant in support of the OCM, such as calculation worksheets and breakdowns of costs supported by documentary evidence, the Applicant referred the Tribunal to information outlined by Ms Santa in her witness statements dated 7 June 2024 and 25 September 2025. The Tribunal notes that as these witness statements were obviously filed as part of these Tribunal proceedings, the information in them was provided some time after the Objection Decision was issued and not in response to the Respondent’s email of August 2023.[41] The Applicant acknowledged that it had prepared and provided this information and evidence during the course of this Tribunal proceeding.
  6. The Applicant also included with its written closing submissions several Appendices which ‘revisited, updated and entered into spreadsheets with Hearing Book references to the supporting evidence’ [42] for the stated purpose of responding to questions raised by the Respondent’s counsel at the hearing about how operating costs were calculated. After these closing submissions were filed, the Respondent wrote to the Tribunal with a concern that these Appendices constituted new information or evidence that had been filed after the hearing without permission of the Tribunal. The Tribunal wrote to the parties to confirm that it would have regard to the evidence as filed by the parties prior to the hearing and as given during the hearing. To the extent that material was contained in the Hearing Book, the Tribunal would have regard to such ‘source material’ but to the extent that the Appendices went beyond material that had been filed in accordance with the Tribunal’s directions, it would not have regard to new evidence or information provided after the hearing.[43]
  7. The Applicant appeared to provide the ‘revisited and updated’ information as the basis for a request that the Tribunal make a decision varying the reviewable decision to reflect the calculation approach put forward by the Applicant in its closing submissions. The Tribunal does not agree that it is appropriate for it to do so. The Tribunal sets out at the end of this decision the orders it believes should be made.
  8. In relation to its contention that the Tribunal should exercise its discretion to allow the Applicant to make its election to apply the OCM to Car 6 on a later ‘declaration date’, the Applicant referred to the Respondent’s email of 14 August 2023 as an acknowledgment by the Respondent during the objection process that this discretion could be applied, as well as treating the log book as an accurate representation of the vehicle’s usage.
  9. The Respondent’s position in relation to the use of the OCM by the Applicant was as follows:
    (a) The Applicant had not discharged its burden of proof in relation to what the operating costs of Car 6 were in the Relevant Years for the purpose of section 10 of the FBTAA.

    (b) The Applicant had not made a reasonable estimate of the number of business kilometres for Car 6 in each of the Relevant Years for the purpose of section 162F of the FBTAA.

    (c) The Applicant was not entitled to a reduction in the operating costs of Car 6 for business journeys for the purposes of section 10A or 10B of the FBTAA.

  10. The Respondent argued that the Tribunal should not exercise the discretion to extend the declaration date because:
    (a) The legislative intention is that the election to use the OCM and specification of a business use percentage should be made within a limited and not open-ended period: see section 68, which generally requires employers to lodge FBT returns by 21 May in the following year. The making of such elections and specifications within a short period of the FBT years to which they relate is consistent with the availability of supporting evidence for those matters.

    (b) The Applicant lodged its FBT returns for the Relevant Years in January 2023. This was up to five years after the regular due date for these returns. The Applicant did not specify the business use percentage it relied upon until June 2024 (and this was only in Ms Santa’s first witness statement filed with the Tribunal).

  11. Nevertheless, the Respondent said that even if the Applicant had made an OCM declaration within time, it had not substantiated its claims with sufficient documentary evidence in place of a logbook. The Respondent therefore contended that the SFM must be applied in determining the fringe benefits taxable amount for Car 6 during the time it was owned by the Applicant during the Relevant Years.
  12. Having regard to the criteria for application of the OCM to car fringe benefits for Car 6 the Tribunal makes the following observations and findings:
    (a) For section 10 of the FBTAA to apply (and the OCM to be used) an election must be made by an employer in relation to the car in accordance with subsection 10(4). That provision requires an election in writing to the Respondent, which is lodged with the Respondent on or before the ‘declaration date.’

    (b) The ‘declaration date’ is defined in subsection 136(1) of the FBTAA to mean the date of lodgement of the return of the fringe benefits taxable amount of the employer of the year of tax ‘or such later date as the Commissioner allows.’

    (c) The Applicant has acknowledged that it did not make any such election by the lodgement dates of the relevant FBT returns for the Relevant Years, and therefore that it must rely on the Tribunal (standing in the shoes of the Commissioner of Taxation) to allow a later date for making such an election.

    (d) The FBTAA does not prescribe particular matters that the Commissioner (or Tribunal) is required to, or may, take into account in determining whether a later date should be allowed.

    (e) Other than referring to the Respondent’s email of 14 August 2023, the Applicant did not provide the Tribunal with any real basis upon which a later date should be allowed as the declaration date.

    (f) The Tribunal does not view the Respondent’s email as weighing significantly in favour of allowing a later date. That email addresses the issue of whether the Respondent would accept the logbook ‘spreadsheet’ as a statutory evidentiary document for the purposes of section 123 of the FBTAA, being evidence of the use of Car 6 by the Applicant in support of the application of the OCM rather than the SFM. The Tribunal accepts that by implication it might be viewed as the Respondent flagging its potential acceptance of the use of the OCM more broadly. However, it is also possible that the Respondent or its officer had simply not turned their mind to the additional requirement under section 10 for the making of an election. The present question for the Tribunal is whether a later declaration date for making an election under section 10 should be allowed, not whether the Respondent had previously stated that the use of the OCM was possible.

    (g) The Tribunal also notes that the Respondent’s email expressly stated that acceptance of the OCM was subject to the Applicant providing the Respondent with particular information relating to costs for the vehicle and FBT calculations, together with supporting evidence. This information was apparently not given to the Respondent in response to its August 2023 email or otherwise as part of the objection process. To the extent that information has been provided, this was only within material filed by the Applicant with the Tribunal after the commencement of this proceeding.

    (h) The Tribunal does not believe that it would be appropriate to allow a later declaration date by which the Applicant could make an election for the purposes of section 10. Leaving aside the Tribunal’s view that the Respondent’s email of August 2023 does not provide convincing support for the Applicant’s contentions, the Tribunal has had regard to the fact that the Applicant had already lodged its FBT returns for the Relevant Years some considerable time after the ordinary due date for lodgement. The Tribunal agrees with the Respondent’s submission that there is a legislative intention for the making of elections in a timely manner which should not be overridden except in necessary circumstances.

    (i) The oral submissions made by the Applicant’s representative at the hearing did not direct the Tribunal to any further matters of relevance.[44] The Tribunal of its own initiative has reviewed evidence given by Ms Santa in relation to certain challenges faced by the Applicant as a result of a significant litigation matter and flooding at their business premises, to determine whether those circumstances provided any basis for allowing a later declaration date. The Tribunal’s view is that this evidence was of a general and high-level nature and would not weigh in favour of exercising any discretion in relation to the declaration date.

  13. On the basis that the Applicant failed to make the election required by subsection 10(1), the Tribunal finds that the Applicant cannot apply section 10 (the OCM) to car fringe benefits in relation to the Relevant Years in relation to Car 6. Although this is sufficient to deal with the question of calculation methodology, the Tribunal believes it should address the other contentions of the Applicant, particularly as certain witnesses were cross-examined extensively on record keeping matters, and the sufficiency of the Applicant’s records and systems is also relevant to the penalty matters discussed below.
  14. Subsection 10(2) contains a formula for determining the taxable value of the car fringe benefits that relate to the relevant car. One element of that formula is the ‘operating cost’ of the car.[45] A ‘business use percentage’ must also be determined with reference to sections 10A, 10B and subsection 136(1).[46] For the Applicant to discharge its burden of proof under section 14ZZK that the Amended Assessments were excessive or incorrect and what they should have been, it would have had to satisfy the Tribunal on the balance of probabilities of its position in relation to the elements of subsection 10(2).
  15. In relation to the ‘business use percentage’ of 84.93% identified by the Applicant in its Amended SFIC the basis upon which this was determined, and the reliability of that percentage, was not clear to the Tribunal. In particular:
    (a) Section 162F of the FBTAA provides that the number of kilometres that represents a reasonable estimate of business kilometres is determined having regard to ‘all relevant matters’ including logbook, odometer and other records.

    (b) The Applicant’s representative told the Tribunal that it was based on entries in the logbook kept by Mr El Charkaoui.[47] During cross-examination, Mr El Charkaoui said that he did not calculate the percentage, did not speak to anyone about it and was not asked about whether the percentage was accurate. He said that he simply provided data. He could not say that the percentage was correct but believed that it was.[48] Ms Santa, the Applicant’s General Manager, included some information about the business use percentage calculation in her witness statement dated 25 September 2025. However, she told the Tribunal that she was not involved in the calculation of that percentage and could not explain how the percentage was arrived at or what underpinned it. She said that this was a matter for the Applicant’s accountant.[49]

    (c) Mr Taha, who was employed by the Applicant during the Relevant Years and assisted with its ‘general book-keeping and administration’ (including maintaining logbooks),[50] told the Tribunal that he placed the data from the logbook into an Excel spreadsheet, and the business use percentage of 84.93% was generated from the Excel based data formula. He confirmed that he did not undertake any analysis or confirmation of whether that data was correct, such as whether a trip was recorded accurately or whether it was a business journey. This is not inconsistent with Mr El Charkaoui’s evidence of providing ‘data’ or the Applicant’s representative’s submission that the percentage was based on data in the logbook. However, the totality of this evidence does raise doubt about the reliability or accuracy of the ‘business use percentage’ produced for Car 6.

    (d) This is particularly so where the logbook in question recorded travel across a single period of 12 weeks within the Relevant Years and the logbook may not have been contemporaneous with the trips undertaken.[51] The Tribunal acknowledges that paragraph 162H(1)(b) provides for an ‘applicable log book period’ of not less than 12 weeks within the relevant holding period.[52] However, the Tribunal still needs to be satisfied as to the reliability and accuracy of the information in such a logbook. Mr El Charkaoui described reporting back to Ms Santa after business journeys ‘roughly’ how many kilometres he had travelled, such as ‘20 ks’ which Ms Santa then recorded.[53] This does not necessarily align with the formatted logbook provided to the Tribunal, which purports to record more precise distances based on odometer readings. Mr Taha said that there should have been physical agendas (diaries) or timesheets in the Cars for recording trips, but he did not monitor whether they were used correctly.[54] Copies of the agendas or timesheets were not provided to the Tribunal.[55] There was also conflicting evidence about whether or where those physical records were kept and retention and disposal practices of the Applicant relating to records.[56] In his second witness statement, Mr Taha said that he did not enter the logbook entries from 2017 into the Excel spreadsheet until January 2018.[57]

    (e) Although an officer of the Respondent may have indicated a willingness to exercise a discretion to accept the logbook for Car 6 as an ‘accurate depiction of the use of the vehicle’ for the purposes of section 123 of the FBTAA during the audit process, the Tribunal, having had the benefit of hearing the evidence given in this proceeding, doubts whether it is appropriate to do so.

    (f) The Applicant’s representative submitted that the logbooks kept by the Applicant for the Cars demonstrated records of odometer readings.[58] Ms Santa subsequently told the Tribunal that she was ‘not sure’ whether odometer records were kept for Car 6 (or Car 7) between 2017 and 2021.[59] Mr El Charkaoui initially said that odometer records were kept, but when Ms Santa’s response was put to him, he appeared uncertain.[60] Mr Taha said that there might have been some transcription errors in relation to odometer readings when transferring this information to a spreadsheet.[61]

  16. In relation to the ‘operating cost’ of Car 6, Ms Santa provided information about operating costs in Annexures 13, 14 and 15 to her first witness statement dated 7 June 2024, including a copy of the purchase invoice for Car 6. Ms Santa provided updated and additional information about operating costs in her third witness statement dated 25 September 2025, including information replacing Annexure 15 of her previous statement. The Applicant’s representative told the Tribunal that upon further analysis, and after discovering additional fuel card and insurance records, the Applicant had recalculated the operating cost and FBT amounts relating to Car 6.[62] The Tribunal observes that this updated information was filed by the Applicant quite late in the proceeding (after the original hearing date had been relisted).
  17. Attached to the third witness statement were copies of online statements or tax invoices for fuel cards and insurance premium document. Ms Santa was asked by the Respondent’s counsel to explain when and how this updated or additional information was located. The Tribunal was also interested to understand why it had not been filed previously, when the Tribunal had made clear directions for the filing of evidence and material by earlier dates. Ms Santa referred to a Zoom meeting with the Respondent and that she might have contacted a car dealership and the insurance company.[63] The Tribunal remained unclear as to the process by which the revised operating cost amounts were reached.
  18. A substantial portion of this additional information and increased operating costs were said by the Applicant to be referable to fuel purchases. Ms Santa told the Tribunal that she was not sure about the breakdown or identification of records supporting these amounts, although she believed this evidence had been provided, even if not attached to her witness statements. She said that, for example, a new set of tyres or a car service might dramatically increase the costings.[64]
  19. In relation to the many pages of fuel card statements or tax invoices filed in September 2025, the entries in those documents identified the name of the holder of the fuel card used for the purchase, the type of fuel purchased, the date and location of the purchase and the amount of the purchase. Except for one vehicle (which does not appear to be one of the seven Cars the subject of this proceeding) the vehicle being refuelled is not identified. The Tribunal appreciates that a distinction between diesel and petrol can be identified and therefore, it can be determined for example that non-diesel fuel purchases are not relevant to Car 6 (which is a diesel vehicle). The Tribunal also accepts that very large diesel purchases are more likely to have been made in relation to one of the Applicant’s trucks rather than Car 6. The Applicant submitted in its closing submissions that it had only included ‘standard size’ diesel purchases in the updated FBT calculations and therefore, the fuel operating costs should be no less than what was actually incurred for Car 6.[65] This was not explained in Ms Santa’s witness statements or in her oral evidence to the Tribunal, nor was there any other witness presented to explain this methodology. The Tribunal is unwilling to give significant weight to a post-hearing explanation of evidence[66] provided by the Applicant’s representatives, particularly where the Tribunal has heard directly from the witness when she was under oath that she was uncertain about the basis for the calculations and the documentary evidence as filed does not readily explain this matter.
  20. Similarly, Ms Santa indicated to the Tribunal that she was not sure what an item described as ‘additional unreported operating costs’ was based on or what expenses contributed to that item. Ms Santa could also not recall the bases for other amounts included in her third witness statement, indicating that the Applicant’s accountant would have the details of what operating costs were referable to fuel, maintenance or other items. Ms Santa said she was not sure whether the accountant was giving evidence to the Tribunal in this proceeding. At this point, the Member asked Ms Santa to confirm that she was aware that her witness statements were essentially intended to tell the Tribunal that the figures contained in them were correct. Ms Santa appeared to understand this, telling the Member that she believed the figures were correct but could not recall the detail of how they were reached. [67]
  21. Ms Santa referred to a number of matters which the Tribunal accepts may have presented challenges in relation to locating and analysing records. This included an industrial accident involving the Applicant which resulted in ongoing litigation by the Applicant against a supplier, water damage at the Applicant’s office caused by flooding and a family bereavement. The Tribunal also accepts that the Applicant’s accountant is likely to have had significant involvement in preparation of financial records and analyses. It was not clear to the Tribunal why the Applicant’s accountant did not provide evidence on these key points.[68]
  22. In summary, the Tribunal is not satisfied that the Applicant has met the requirements of section 10 of the FBTAA, not only in relation to the making of the necessary election to use the OCM for Car 6, but in keeping records which support the key elements of the statutory formula for applying the OCM. On that basis, the Tribunal finds that the Applicant was not able to apply the OCM in relation to car fringe benefits in relation to the Applicant which related to Car 6 in the Relevant Years. The absence of reliable evidence also means that the Applicant has not discharged its burden of proving that the Amended Assessments insofar as they related to Car 6 were incorrect, excessive or what they should have been.

Car 7

  1. Car 7 was acquired by the Applicant on 10 January 2017.
  2. In relation to Car 7, the Applicant accepts that a car fringe benefit arose, being a car benefit provided by the Applicant to Mr Jebara, on the basis that Car 7 was driven by Mr Jebara for both work and personal use. However, the Applicant contends that the Applicant should be permitted to use the OCM to calculate the taxable amount of that fringe benefit on the basis that Mr Jebara kept a logbook for Car 7 between 14 January 2017 and 13 April 2017[69], and based on the entries in that logbook a business use percentage could be determined.
  3. The Applicant also contended that the Tribunal should exercise its ‘discretion’ to allow a later ‘declaration date’ and accept the Applicant’s election to use the OCM under subsection 10(4).
  4. The Respondent did not accept that the OCM could be used in relation to Car 7 for any of the Relevant Years, for the same reasons as applied in relation to Car 6.
  5. The Tribunal observes that unlike Car 6, the Respondent’s email of August 2023 made it clear that the Respondent had refused to accept the logbook provided by the Applicant for Car 7 and that the OCM would not be considered by the Respondent. As set out above, the Tribunal did not place significant weight on the Respondent’s August 2023 email in determining whether it was appropriate to allow a later date in relation to the declaration date, even if that email might have implied some acceptance of the OCM for Car 6. There was clearly no implied acceptance for Car 7. In any event, for the reasons given above in relation to Car 6, the Tribunal does not believe that it would be appropriate to allow a later declaration date by which the Applicant could make an election under subsection 10(4) of the FBTAA in relation to Car 7.
  6. Also as set out above, this means that because the Applicant failed to make the election required by subsection 10(1), the Tribunal finds that the Applicant cannot apply section 10 (or the OCM) to car fringe benefits in relation to the Relevant Years in relation to Car 7.
  7. Had the Tribunal determined that a later declaration date was to be allowed and a late election permitted, the Tribunal would nevertheless have found that the Applicant had not satisfied the Tribunal that other criteria for the operation of section 10 were met in relation to Car 7. In particular:
    (a) The Tribunal is not satisfied that the ‘business use percentage’ of 85.01% put forward by the Applicant for Car 7 was supported by appropriate evidence, nor was the logbook provided by the Applicant, upon which the business use percentage was based, reliable. In addition to the evidence given by Ms Santa, Mr El Charkaoui and Mr Taha about their limited understanding of and involvement in the preparation of the relevant records, the Tribunal heard from Mr Jebara, the construction manager of the Applicant, who also used Car 7.

    (b) The Tribunal acknowledges there were some communication challenges during Mr Jebara’s giving of evidence, as he was located at a building site which created some connectivity issues and there was some English terminology that needed to be rephrased. However, Mr Jebara told the Tribunal that the nature of his work was such that accurate and complete record keeping might be difficult. He acknowledged the photocopies of handwritten entries from his diary, as attached to his witness statement dated 7 June 2024, as information that he passed on to Mr Taha. He told the Tribunal that he understood that information was required for tax purposes and he had been told to record expenses such as petrol and car washing. He recalled recording entries in the diary for a limited period and then after six months or a year it was no longer required. He said that because seven years had elapsed since he made those diary entries, it was difficult for him to recall the details of the journeys. The Tribunal accepts that Mr Jebara sought to give honest evidence to the Tribunal and that it would be difficult to remember the specifics of the trips involved after such a length of time.[70]

    (c) Beyond the dates of the logbook, Mr Jebara did not keep records of his use of Car 7, such as what proportion of journeys were business related as opposed to personal. However, he told the Tribunal that he also owned a car for his personal use at that time, which he preferred driving to Car 7 and therefore he suggested 95% of his use of Car 7 would have been business related only, using his personal car for family trips.[71]

    (d) In its email of 14 August 2023, the Respondent identified certain errors it had discovered in the logbook for Car 7. For example, one odometer reading went from 3177 to 3220 following one journey, then reverted to 3194 before the commencement of a later journey.[72] These were not explained by the Applicant, other than possibly by Mr Taha acknowledging that there might have been a transcription error on the part of the Applicant.

    (e) As noted above, the evidence given by Ms Santa, Mr El Charkaoui and Mr Taha was unclear about the reliability of odometer records, or whether records of odometer readings were even kept. This was applicable to Car 7 as well as Car 6.

    (f) The Tribunal is also not satisfied that the ‘operating cost’ put forward by the Applicant for Car 7, being the updated operating costs as set out in Ms Santa’s witness statement of 25 September 2025 is reliable.[73] The Tribunal again refers to the evidence of Ms Santa and Mr El Charkaoui as set out in relation to Car 6 above. This evidence generally related to records and evidence applicable to both Car 6 and Car 7, except where otherwise noted.

    (g) In relation to operating costs for Car 7, the Tribunal also heard evidence from Mr Jebara. Mr Jebara told the Tribunal that when he bought fuel or other items for Car 7, he would use a business fuel card or if he paid cash he would provide the receipts to Ms Santa for reimbursement. He would be reimbursed by the Applicant promptly so there was no need for him to keep records such as copies of receipts. Mr Jebara could not recall when he was given a fuel card. He could not recall whether Car 7 ran on diesel but told the Tribunal that he used the fuel card provided to him for purchases of diesel for a truck and a forklift. Sometimes he would give that fuel card to one of the other employees of the Applicant so they could refuel the truck, and on occasions he might have used Mr El Charkaoui’s card when they were travelling together.[74] He suggested that although he only ever used the fuel card allocated to him for fuel purchases, it was possible that others had used fuel cards provided by the Applicant to make other purchases at service stations, such as food or cigarettes.[75]

  8. In summary, the Tribunal is not satisfied that the Applicant has met the requirements of section 10 of the FBTAA, not only in relation the making of the necessary election to use the OCM for Car 7, but in keeping records which support the key elements of the statutory formula for applying the OCM. On that basis, the Tribunal finds that the Applicant was not able to apply the OCM in relation to car fringe benefits in relation to the Applicant which related to Car 7 in the Relevant Years. Again, the absence of reliable evidence means that the Applicant has not discharged its burden of proving that the Amended Assessments insofar as they related to Car 7 were incorrect, excessive or what they should have been.

ADMINISTRATIVE PENALTIES

Was a false or misleading statement made by the Applicant in any of the Relevant Years?

  1. Since the date of the Objection Decision, the Respondent advised the Tribunal that it would ‘confine the issues for determination in the proceeding’ to Car [1992] CthArbRp 74; 2, Car 5, Car 6 and Car 7.[76] The Tribunal understood this to mean that the Respondent now accepted the position of the Applicant in relation to the FBT treatment of Car [1993] CthArbRp 1; 1, Car 3 and Car 4 in the Relevant Years.[77] To the extent that the Penalty Assessments were issued on the basis of incorrect FBT treatment in relation to Car [1993] CthArbRp 1; 1, Car 3 and Car 4, the Tribunal finds that the Applicant has discharged its burden of proof and therefore the statements comprising the Applicant’s FBT returns for those years are not false or misleading insofar as they relate to material particulars for those three Cars. This will presumably affect the quantum of the tax shortfalls the subject of the Penalty Assessments. The Tribunal agrees that the appropriate course is for this aspect of its Objection Decision to be varied or reconsidered, and the Penalty Assessments to be amended accordingly.
  2. Also, since the date of the Objection Decision the Applicant has conceded that it cannot discharge its burden of proof in relation to the Amended Assessments as they relate to Car 2 and Car 5.
  3. Nevertheless, the Applicant argues that it did not make a false or misleading statement insofar as its FBT returns for the Relevant Years relate to either Car 2 or Car 5, as well as Car 6 and Car 7, for one or more of the following reasons[78]:
    (a) In relation to the proposition that the amounts in the FBT returns lodged by the Applicant differed from information held by the Respondent, the Applicant said that it took ‘reasonable care’ and its lodgements were based on its understanding at the time that the amounts of RBT it declared were correct. In this regard, the Applicant referred to Mr El Charkaoui’s difficulties in reading and writing English, Ms Santa’s absence of accounting training such that she and the Applicant relied on their accountant, and the Applicant’s lack of awareness prior to the Relevant Years that it had an FBT liability. The Applicant submitted that once it became aware of this FBT liability, it arranged for the necessary returns to be prepared and lodged[79], based in good faith on the records and information that the Applicant held at the time. The Applicant said that historically it had taken reasonable care to keep appropriate records for tax compliance purposes, such as the logbooks it maintained for the 2017 year.

    (b) In relation to the Respondent’s contention that the Applicant had not provided evidence or FBT calculations in support of its position (or in response to requests for its information about its position), the Applicant did not make any ‘statement’ to the Respondent and therefore it could not have made a false or misleading statement. [80]

  4. The Respondent took issue with the Applicant’s submissions on the basis that those submissions addressed the question of whether the Applicant’s position(s) on FBT matters in the Relevant Years was/were ‘reasonably arguable’[81] rather than whether the Applicant ‘took reasonable care’[82] in connection with the making of the relevant statement.[83]
  5. In the present circumstances, the Tribunal’s view on whether the Applicant made a false or misleading statement by its FBT returns for the Relevant Years as those returns related to the FBT treatment of any of Car [1992] CthArbRp 74; 2, Car 5, Car 6 and Car 7 differs from the submissions of both of the parties. More particularly:
    (a) The Tribunal disagrees with the Respondent’s contention that the Applicant mistakenly addressed factors relevant to whether a position was reasonably arguable rather than whether reasonable care was taken. The Tribunal believes that the factors outlined by the Applicant in its opening statement to the Tribunal[84] and in its written closing submissions do relate to the question of whether the Applicant took ‘reasonable care’ in making its FBT lodgements with the Respondent.

    (b) However, the Tribunal does not find the factors as argued by the Applicant as providing a sufficient basis for the Applicant being viewed as having taken reasonable care in making those FBT lodgements. Evidence was not provided of the extent of Mr El Charkaoui’s lack of proficiency in English, and in any event the Tribunal places little weight on this matter in circumstances where the Applicant conducted a not insubstantial business enterprise and had professional accounting support. Mr El Charkaoui apparently had been involved in other business enterprises over a period of many years, prior to the establishment of the Applicant. Similarly, Ms Santa’s lack of formal accounting training does not carry significant weight where the Applicant had access to external accounting services. The fact that the Applicant was not aware it had an FBT liability is not a basis for finding that it did not make a false or misleading statement. A person may make a false or misleading statement without deliberately setting out to do so.[85] The Applicant made assertions that historically had proper record keeping systems, although as set out above this was challenged by the Respondent during cross examination of Ms Santa and Mr El Charkaoui and the Tribunal’s findings in relation to Car 6 and Car 7 are based on a finding of deficiencies in record keeping, and specifically FBT records, by the Applicant.

    (c) Further, the Tribunal does not believe that the non-provision by the Applicant to the Respondent of information or calculations about FBT matters alters this position. The FBT returns lodged by the Applicant in January 2023 were or contained ‘statements’ made by the Applicant to the Respondent. To the extent that those FBT returns did not reflect the correct FBT treatment at law, whether by positively putting forward matters or omitting matters giving rise to incorrect FBT treatment, the requirements of subsection 284-75(1) were met, subject to any applicable exclusions. Division 284 does not require a taxpayer to have made a separate, formal or positive ‘statement’ to the Respondent about a matter. It is clear from the language of the provision that an ‘omission’ can also give rise to a false or misleading statement. Further, it would not appear consistent with the objectives of the penalty regime for the Applicant to be able to argue that no false or misleading statement was made on the basis that the Applicant had failed to lodge returns when required by law, and/or failed to respond to requests from the Respondent for further information to facilitate the issuance of accurate assessments of FBT.

    (d) The Applicant referred to work undertaken by an external accountant on their behalf. However that accountant did not give evidence of what work they undertook or what information they might have been provided by the Applicant. The only references to the accountant’s involvement were Ms Santa’s comments that certain matters would have been handled by their accountant, which was why she was unable to provide greater detail to the Tribunal. It was not confirmed whether that accountant was a registered tax agent or BAS agent. Therefore the Tribunal does not believe there is any basis upon which the ‘safe harbour’ provision in subsection 284-75(6) in relation to the engagement of a tax agent could assist the Applicant.

  6. The Tribunal therefore finds that the Applicant has not established that it took ‘reasonable care’ in relation to statements it made to the Respondent in relation to the FBT treatment of Car 2, Car 6 and Car 7 in the Relevant Years for the purposes of subsection 284-75(5). The Tribunal finds that the Applicant made false or misleading statements through its FBT returns in the Relevant Years insofar as those returns related to the FBT treatment of those Cars in the Relevant Years for the purposes of subsection 284-75(1).
  7. More particularly:
    (a) To the extent that the Penalty Assessments were issued on the basis of a false or misleading statement made in relation to Car 2, the Tribunal finds that the Applicant made statements that were false or misleading in a material particular by omitting the taxable value of car fringe benefits from its FBT returns in the Relevant Years. The Applicant told the Tribunal that it was unable to provide evidence which would demonstrate that Car 2 was not subject to FBT in the Relevant Years.

    (b) To the extent that the Penalty Assessments were issued on the basis of a false or misleading statement made in relation to each of Car 6 and Car 7, the Tribunal has found that the taxable value of FBT amounts in the Relevant Years should be calculated on the basis of the SFM and not the OCM, and therefore the Tribunal also finds that the Applicant made statements that were false or misleading in relation to the taxable value of car fringe benefits as disclosed in its FBT returns in the Relevant Years.

  8. In relation to Car 5, the Applicant told the Tribunal that its FBT returns for the Relevant Years were prepared on the basis that car fringe benefits arose in relation to Car 5 and the taxable value of those car fringe benefits were calculated by reference to the SFM and that there was no dispute between the parties in relation to Car 5.[86] If this is correct, then this aligns with the Tribunal’s (and Respondent’s) view of the proper FBT treatment of Car 5. The Tribunal was not able to confirm the details of the calculations as they relate to Car 5, although it notes that the Respondent submitted that the Objection Decision should be affirmed to the extent that the Amended Assessments and Penalty Assessments relate to Car 5.[87] The Tribunal therefore concludes that the FBT returns for the Relevant Years were not false or misleading in a material particular to the extent that any particular related to Car 5.

Did a shortfall amount arise due to the recklessness of the Applicant or its agent?

  1. The Applicant submitted that if the Applicant was found to be liable to an administrative penalty, the base penalty amount ‘should be remitted in full as there was no evidence of the Applicant intentionally disregarding the law, nor was it reckless.’[88] The Applicant submitted that the evidence and law supported the conclusion that ‘recklessness’ was not appropriate to this matter.
  2. To be clear, the base penalty amounts in the Penalty Assessments were not calculated on the basis of ‘intentional disregard’, nor did the Objection Decision indicate a finding of intentional disregard by the Respondent. A base penalty amount based on ‘intentional disregard of a taxation law’ would have represented 75% of the Applicant’s shortfall amount: see Item 1 of the table in subsection 284-90(1).
  3. The Penalty Assessments and Objection Decision reflected a base penalty amount of 50% of the shortfall amount, on the basis that a shortfall amount arose as a result of recklessness by the Applicant or their agent as to the operation of a taxation law: Item 2 of the table in subsection 284-90(1).
  4. At the hearing, the Applicant’s representative submitted that a taxpayer would act recklessly where their conduct clearly shows disregard or indifference to consequences that are foreseeable by a reasonable person as being a likely result of their actions. The Applicant’s representative said that there was no evidence that the Applicant had ‘actual knowledge’ that any statement it made was false, conducted itself in a way that fell short of the standard of a reasonable person in a similar matter or in a similar position, or showed disregard or indifference to risk that is foreseeable to the reasonable person. [89]
  5. The Applicant’s representative drew the Tribunal’s attention to a number of matters similar to those raised in the context of whether the Applicant had exercised ‘reasonable care.’ This included the fact that the Applicant only became aware of its liability to FBT after the Respondent commenced its audit and it lodged its FBT returns for the Relevant Years during the audit. The Applicant’s representative also said that during audit and objection processes, the Applicant provided the Respondent with ‘extensive information and documents’ about the vehicles and the Applicant had taken reasonable care with keeping records and having systems to support tax compliance. The Applicant’s representative also referred more generally to the educational limitations of Mr El Charkaoui and Ms Santa.[90]
  6. When the Applicant submitted that there was no evidence of recklessness on its part, the Tribunal was not clear whether this was meant to convey the Applicant’s position that it had not been reckless, or whether it was incorrectly suggesting that the Respondent bore some onus of proving that the Applicant had been reckless. Paragraph 14ZZK(b) of the TAA 1953 provides that the Applicant has the burden of proving (where the taxation decision the subject of an objection decision is an assessment) that the assessment is excessive or otherwise incorrect, and what the assessment should have been. The Respondent issued the Penalty Assessments in the amounts and on the bases set out in those assessments, the audit reasons and the Objection Decision. It is for the Applicant to prove to the satisfaction of the Tribunal (on the balance of probabilities) that the Penalty Assessments are excessive or incorrect and what they should be. The Respondent is not required to put forward evidence to prove the correctness of the Penalty Assessments.
  7. The Tribunal does not agree with the Applicant that it was necessary for the Applicant to have had ‘actual knowledge’ that a statement to the Respondent was false before a finding of recklessness can be made, or that a lack of actual knowledge that a statement was false was a factor weighing significantly against a finding of recklessness. The observations of the Federal Court in BRK (Bris) and Hart, as cited by both parties, refer to knowledge of a (real) risk that material might be incorrect, or indifference as to whether the material is true and correct.
  8. Having regard to the matters put forward by the Applicant in support of its contentions that it had not been reckless, the Tribunal repeats the observations it made at paragraph 80(b) above in relation to those matters in the context of reasonable care. It was not satisfied that those matters indicated that the Applicant took reasonable care in relation to the preparation of its FBT returns. Entities engaging in business enterprises can reasonably be expected to take steps to ensure that they comply with relevant taxation and other business-related laws and regulatory schemes. The Tribunal believes that the Applicant demonstrated at least an indifference to the risk that it had not properly disclosed a liability for FBT in the Relevant Years. The Applicant’s director Mr El Charkaoui has been involved in various business enterprises over a number of years. The Applicant had access to internal and external accounting resources prior to the commencement of the Respondent’s audit. The Tribunal does not accept that it is satisfactory for the Applicant to argue that it did not realise it was subject to FBT until after it became subject to a formal audit by the Respondent. Nor does the fact that the Applicant lodged FBT returns after the audit was commenced (and after the date on which such returns were due) support an argument that the Applicant was not reckless about their lodgement obligations. Based on the evidence given at the hearing, the Tribunal is also not satisfied more generally that the Applicant had adequate record keeping systems in place in the Relevant Years.
  9. On this basis, the Tribunal believes that the administrative penalty should be calculated at 50% of the shortfall amount, on the basis of recklessness by the Applicant as to the operation of the FBT laws.

Should the administrative penalty be remitted?

  1. The Applicant argued that the Tribunal should remit any penalty on the basis that it would be unjust to impose a penalty in circumstances where the Applicant took reasonable care and there was no evidence of intentional disregard of the law.[91] The Tribunal has provided its view as to why it believes that the Applicant has not demonstrated that it took ‘reasonable care’ in relation to the preparation of FBT lodgements (at least in relation to the FBT liability arising for three of the four Cars in question), why the Respondent is not required to provide evidence of intentional disregard of the law on the part of the Applicant and why the issue of ‘intentional disregard’ is not relevant to the imposition of administrative penalties more generally in this case. The Tribunal does not believe these matters tend in favour of exercising a discretion to remit all or part of an administrative penalty.
  2. The Applicant also referred to[92]:
    (a) Special circumstances arising in relation to the Applicant at the time of the audit and objection processes. At that time the Applicant was involved in Federal Court proceedings arising from a claim for damages of over $2 million. This litigation and the incident which gave rise to the litigation put the Applicant’s staff and management under significant financial, physical and mental stress which made it difficult for the Applicant to respond to requests from the Respondent about its FBT matter.[93]

    (b) The Applicant’s compliance approach following the commencement of the audit by the Respondent, including the lodgement of FBT returns for the Relevant Years in January 2023 prepared ‘in good faith’ and the provision of information to the Respondent.

    (c) The Applicant having made a concession that the SFM was appropriate for calculating FBT amounts for Car 5.

    (d) The Applicant historically taking reasonable care to keep appropriate records and systems for tax compliance purposes.

  3. The Tribunal does not believe these matters, individually or collectively, weigh in favour of an exercise of the discretion to remit any part of the penalty to be imposed on the Applicant. In particular:
    (a) The Tribunal accepts that the Applicant was involved in litigation relating to an incident on a building site. However, evidence or explanation as to how this affected the Applicant’s ability to comply with its FBT recordkeeping and lodgement obligations, or otherwise contributed to an unjust outcome, was not provided.[94]

    (b) Lodgement of FBT returns by the Applicant was a statutory requirement and the Applicant only made these lodgements nine months after the Respondent commenced its review, seven months after commencement of the formal audit and after the Respondent issued a ‘default assessment warning letter’ to the Applicant. The default assessment warning letter was apparently sent after a failure by the Applicant to provide information requested by the Respondent.

    (c) As discussed previously, the Tribunal did not necessarily accept that the Applicant had taken reasonable care in relation to its FBT compliance or that it otherwise had robust record keeping practices in place in relation to the Relevant Years.

  4. The Tribunal is not satisfied that it would be appropriate to exercise the discretion to remit all or any part of the penalty having regard to all of the circumstances raised before the Tribunal.

CONCLUSION AND FORM OF ORDERS

  1. The Tribunal believes that it is appropriate to remit the reviewable decision to the Respondent under section 105 of the ART Act for reconsideration and recalculation of the amounts of the Amended Assessments and Penalty Assessments in accordance with orders which reflect the following findings of the Tribunal:
    (a) Car fringe benefits arose to the Applicant in relation to Car 2, Car 6 and Car 7 in each of the Relevant Years.

    (b) Car fringe benefits arose to the Applicant in relation to Car 5 in relation to the Relevant Year ended 31 March 2021.

    (c) The Applicant did not satisfy the requirements of section 10 of the FBTAA in relation to any of Car [1992] CthArbRp 74; 2, Car 5, Car 6 or Car 7 in any of the Relevant Years and therefore the taxable value of car fringe benefits arising in relation to those Cars in the Relevant Years is to be determined in accordance with section 9 of the FBTAA (the statutory formula).

    (d) No car fringe benefits arose to the Applicant in relation to Car [1993] CthArbRp 1; 1, Car 3 or Car 4 in any of the Relevant Years.

    (e) To the extent that a tax shortfall amount arose to the Applicant in any of the Relevant Years arising from tax payable by the Applicant on fringe benefits taxable amounts in relation to Car [1992] CthArbRp 74; 2, Car 5, Car 6 or Car 7, the base penalty amount is to be worked out by reference to 50% of the shortfall amount on the basis that the shortfall amount resulted from recklessness by the Applicant as to the operation of the FBTAA.

    (f) No part of the penalty is to be remitted.

  2. Although the Tribunal has effectively found in favour of the Respondent in relation to the matters canvassed at the hearing (being the calculation methodology for Cars 6 and 7 and administrative penalties), and agrees with the Objection Decision under review in this regard, the Tribunal believes it is appropriate to set aside the Objection Decision and remit it to the Respondent for reconsideration, albeit on the basis of a series of orders reflecting the propositions above. The parties have changed their positions on a number of issues underlying the Amended Assessment and Penalty Assessments which were the subject of the Objection Decision since those assessments were issued and the Objection Decision was decided. These changes will have a material impact on the quantum of the assessments. Alternative calculations for relevant FBT amounts were suggested to the Tribunal but the Tribunal does not believe it has sufficient information to confirm the detail of those calculations.
  3. The Tribunal has referred to correspondence from the parties that followed the filing of their written closing submissions, and in particular a disagreement as to whether the Applicant was seeking to introduce new evidence or information in its closing submissions, particularly as they related to operating expenses and FBT calculations for Cars 6 and 7. On 14 April 2026, as the Tribunal was trying to finalise and issue its decision in this matter, the Respondent again wrote to the Tribunal requesting leave to file further submissions by late April and foreshadowing the possibility of a further case management directions hearing. The Tribunal declined to accept further submissions or schedule a further case event. Noting that it had been more than four months since the hearing of the matter, the Tribunal was satisfied that it had already dealt with the concerns relating to the provision of new material by the Applicant. As will now appear from these reasons for decision, the Tribunal has determined that the taxable value of fringe benefits arising in relation to Cars 6 and 7 should be calculated on the basis of the SFM and not the OCM. Accordingly, further debate about the manner in which amounts might be determined under the OCM for those Cars does not assist the Tribunal. Nor would any such additional information provide any basis for the application of the OCM where the Tribunal had determined not to allow a later ‘declaration date’ for the lodgement of an election under section 10 of the FBTAA.
