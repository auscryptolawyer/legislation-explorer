Re Winifred Brown and Acting Commissioner of Superannuation [1981] AATA 39 (29 May 1981)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: WINIFRED BROWN

And: ACTING COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. A80/34

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mr Ewart Smith (Senior Member)

Mr V.J. Skermer (Member)

Dr M. Glick (Member)

CATCHWORDS

superannuation _ determination of benefits _ benefit classification certificate specifying condition in incorrect leg _ power to amend or correct patent clerical error in certificate _ whether certificate valid on basis of falsa demonstratio non nocet _ whether s.184(4) of Superannuation Act 1976 prevents issue of corrected certificate _ whether certificate 'in force' if incorrectly expressed _ incapacity _ physical condition connected with specified condition _ meaning of 'connected with' _ thrombo phlebitis _ connection with varicose veins

Superannuation Act 1976, s.66(2), s.154(2), s.154(4), s.184(2), s.184(4), s.184(7)

Acts Interpretation Act 1901, s.33(1), s.33(3)

HEARING

CANBERRA 29:5:1981

ORDER

The Tribunal decides: (a) to affirm the decision of the Acting Commissioner for Superannuation of 12 September 1980 on the basis that the Benefit Classification Certificate issued in respect of the applicant on 8 May 1979 was validly corrected on 19 September 1979 or, alternatively, on the basis that the Certificate as originally issued should be read as if it referred to 'Varicose Veins Leg with healed ulcer'; and

(b) that the applicant's entitlements under the Superannuation Act 1976 are to be determined in accordance with sub- section 66(2) of that Act.

DECISION

1. The applicant, Mrs Winifred Brown, has appealed to this Tribunal against the decision of the Acting Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Acting Commissioner") of 12 September 1980 confirming the decisions taken by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation on 8 May 1979:

(i) to issue, under sub-section 184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976, a benefit classification certificate specifying the conditions 'Anxiety State' and 'Varicose Veins Left Leg with Healed Ulcer' in respect of the applicant; and

(ii)under paragraph 66(2)(c) of the Superannuation Act 1976 that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical condition specified in the benefit classification certificate or by a physical condition connected with such a condition or conditions.

2. The Tribunal had before it the documents furnished by the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") in pursuance of s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 which were, with the exception of the documents numbered T4 (the relevant legislative provisions) received in evidence as exhibits T1, T2, T3, T5 and T6, and heard evidence from Dr J. Scott-Findlay, a specialist surgeon who carried out the operation on the applicant's leg, Dr Helen Turner, a senior Commonwealth Medical Officer, and the applicant.

3. Leaving aside her earlier history as an officer of the Public Service, which does not appear to the Tribunal to be relevant for present purposes, the applicant became a contributor to the Provident Account under the Superannuation Act 1922 on 10 September 1975. She had undergone a medical examination for permanent appointment on 5 Sepember 1975 and upon this examination the examining Commonwealth Medical Officer, Dr Clarke, while finding her fit for temporary employment, was not prepared to recommend her acceptance for either appointment under Superannuation Fund conditions or appointment under Provident Account conditions (Exhibit T5, p.16). He recommended re-examination in 11 months. His reasons and remarks recorded on the Medical Report Form were as follows:

'Anxiety State

To observe sick leave record, particularly any breakdown of varicose condition left leg.

To re-weigh.'

4. In his comments on particular matters, Dr Clarke noted, opposite question 5(b) of the Medical Officer's Report page of the Medical Report Form (see Exhibit T5, p.15):

'Yes _ surgical scars. Healed pigmented area L leg. Varicose ulcer.'

And opposite question 9(d) (Does the candidate suffer from varicose veins? If so, state severity.) Dr Clarke noted:

'Yes, L. Leg. Skin pigmented and scarred. A few prominent veins.'

5. Notwithstanding the Medical Officer's Report, the delegate of the Superannuation Board determined on 10 September 1975 that Mrs Brown's health and physical fitness justified her acceptance as a contributor to the Provident Account (Exhibit T5, p.16).

6. In the period that followed until her accident in July 1978 Mrs Brown's service and sick leave record was good and she had no absences in that period because of her leg, nor did she have any problem with her varicose veins.

7. On 7 July 1978 Mrs Brown, then an officer of the Department of the Capital Territory, while at her desk at her place of employment, turned around quickly in her chair and knocked the lower part of her leg on the corner of a box containing computer reels encased in metal containers. The box had apparently been placed behind her chair without her knowledge in the course of movements of computer equipment occasioned by the installation of a new computer. She reported the incident but did not at once seek medical attention; she did so about a week later, when a 'big lump' came up where she had knocked it. She saw first her general practitioner (Dr David Wilson) and was referred by him to Dr Scott-Findlay who recommended an operation as soon as possible.

8. Dr Scott-Findlay subsequently gave her surgical treatment which he described as a stripping of the deep vein and of the perforators.

9. Mrs Brown underwent a medical examination for continued employment or retirement on invalidity grounds in November 1978, and was retired from the Public Service, under s.67 of the Public Service Act 1922 (i.e. on the ground of invalidity), with effect from 10 February 1979 (Exhibit T5, p.23). The Commonwealth Medical Officer (Dr R.A. McGregor) expressed the opinion (Exhibit T5, p.22) that she should be retired on the grounds of invalidity but that she may, in due course, partially or completely recover and become fit for some form of Commonwealth employment (temporary). He stated her condition as 'Thrombo phlebitis' and gave the following brief history of her condition:

'Suffered from varicose veins. On 7.7.78 she bumped the leg at work, causing Thrombo phlebitis. This became more extensive, and an extensive operation, at several sites, was needed. There has been some recovery but there is considerable lividity and several residual thromboses can be felt. The leg is still painful and liable to further injury.'

10. On 3 May 1979 a delegate of the Commissioner determined under s.184(7) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Exhibit T5, p.24) that, at the time of her medical examination on 5 September 1975, it was established that Mrs Brown had suffered or was suffering from Anxiety State and Varicose Veins (L) leg with healed ulcer and consequently could not meet the requirements of paragraphs 100(b) and 120 of the Handbook for Guidance of Medical Officers for acceptance as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund under Part III of the Superannuation Act 1922. The delegate declared his opinion that the physical or mental conditions, namely, Anxiety State and Varicose Veins (L) leg with healed ulcer were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act (the Superannuation Act 1922) and were the physical or mental conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board was not satisfied under sub-section 5(1) of the Superannuation Act 1922 that the applicant's health and physical fitness were such as to justify her being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund under Part III of the Superannuation Act 1922. The Tribunal is satisfied on the facts before it and, in particular, Exhibit T5, pp.15-16, that the delegate's determination and opinion in this respect were correct.

11. On 8 May 1979 a delegate of the Commissioner issued a Benefit Classification Certificate in respect of Mrs Brown pursuant to s.184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Exhibit T5, p.26) stating his opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for Mrs Brown's retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to:

(i) by the physical or mental condition or conditions that was or were relevant for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1922; or

(ii)by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with the physical or mental condition or conditions that was or were relevant for the purposes of that Act.

The Certificate then specified as the relevant condition or conditions 'Anxiety State' and 'Varicose Veins Right Leg with healed ulcer'.

12. The reference to 'Right leg' in this Certificate was clearly a clerical error and should have read 'Left leg'.

13. On the same date, the delegate made a decision under s.66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Exhibit T5, p.27) that he was of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for Mrs Brown's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to:

(i) by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the Benefit Classification Certificate; or

(ii)by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such condition or conditions.

This decision stated that the physical and mental conditions specified in the Benefit Classification Certificate were:

(i) Anxiety State

(ii)Varicose Veins (L) leg with healed ulcer.

14. The delegate commented in this decision that, in forming his opinion, he had had regard to the physical condition (Thrombo phlebitis) stated to be the condition contributing to the incapacity in the medical officer's report on her fitness for continued employment and the comments of the Adviser to the Commissioner. He stated that he had satisfied himself that:

(i) Mrs Brown had ceased to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining her maximum retiring age; and

(ii)immediately before her retirement, the Benefit Classification Certificate referred to in his decision was in force.

15. The error in specifying in the Benefit Classification Certification 'right' leg instead of 'left' leg was brought to the Commissioner's attention by the Australian Public Service Association, on behalf of Mrs Brown, in a letter dated 5 June 1979 (Exhibit T5, p.31).

16. In a letter from the Commissioner dated 14 June 1979 in response to the Association's letter, the legislative procedure and the context of the error were explained in the following terms, which we think se0s o0t t e 0os tion accurately:

'2. It might assist if I first explain the legislation and the procedures relating to the issue of a benefit classification certificate.

3. The issue of a benefit classification certificate in respect of Mrs Brown is incidental to the operation of sub-sections 184(7), 184(2) and 66(2) of the Superannuation At 1976. It is first necessary to determine, under sub-section 184(7), the conditions that in the opinion of the Commissioner were the physical or mental conditions (the relevant conditions) of Mrs Brown by reason of which the Superannuation Board was not satisfied that her health and physical fitness were such as to justify her being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund for pension benefits.

4. The delegate of the Commissioner, being of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for Mrs Brown's retirement, was caused or substantially contributed to by the relevant conditions or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with the relevant conditions, issued a benefit classification certificate under the provisions of sub-section 184(2). Following the issue of the benefit classification certificate a decision was taken under sub-section 66(2) and as a result of this decision the invaldity benefit was reduced on medical grounds.

5. Mrs Brown was advised of the decisions taken in her case in the letter dated 15 May 1979. I am enclosing for your information a copy of the decisions taken under delegation under sub-sections 184(7) and 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976. It will be seen that these decisions correctly refer to the condition varicose veins (L) leg with healed ulcer and accordingly the benefit classification certificate which referred to varicose veins right leg with healed ulcer was the result of a clerical error. I might also mention that the medical standards relating to issue of benefit classification certificates refer only to varicose veins, the presence of the veins being the main issue and not the location.

6. I am sending a copy of this letter and th benefit classification certificate showing the correct conditions to Mrs Brown with an apology for the error that occurred.'

17. On 19 September 1979 a corrected Benefit classification Certificate was signed by the same delegate of the Commissioner who signed the earlier certificate and was forwarded to Mrs Brown under cover of a letter of the same date (Exhibit T5, pp.35-36).

18. By letter dated 11 September 1979 (i.e. eight days before the 'corrected' benefit classification certificate was signed) Mrs Brown requested the Commissioner, in pursuance of s.154(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976, to reconsider his (delegate's) decision that her retirement on invalidity grounds 'was caused by a physical condition specified in the Benefit Classification Certificate' (Exhibit T5, p.34).

19. The reconsideration clearly proceeded on the basis of the original decisions under s.184(7), s.184(2) and s.66(2) and the 'corrected' certificate, i.e. that it was the left leg in respect of which the relevant condition was specified.

20. On 12 September 1980, after further investigations, the Acting Commissioner, as noted in paragraph 1 above, confirmed the decisions of the delegate taken on 8 May 1979. The form of the confirmation is of some significance, as will appear from Mr Wilde's submission referred to later, see paragraph 31, and we draw attention particularly to the confirmation in part being in the form of confirmation of a delegate's decision to issue a benefit classification certificate specifying 'Anxiety State' and 'Varicose Veins Left Leg with Healed Ulcer' in respect of Mrs Brown. In fact, as already noted, the certificate as issued on 8 May 1979, due to a clerical error, referred to 'Varicose Veins Right Leg with Healed Ulcer'.

21. It should be noted that the 'decision' to issue a benefit classification certificate is not a separate formal decision but is rather to be gathered from the determination under s.184(7), the provisions of s.184(2) and the actual issue of the certificate (which includes a statement of the formation of the opinion which is a prerequisite under s.184(2)(c) to the issue of a certificate). In one sense, that decision is merely to carry out the duty imposed by s.184(2) to issue a certificate once the Commissioner or his delegate has formed the opinion set out in s.184(2)(c) on the relevant substantive matters.

Effect of Error in the Benefit Classification Certificate

22. The inclusion in the benefit classification certificate of the word 'right' instead of 'left' appears to be a simple error, but, as with so many simple errors, the consequences are far from simple. That such errors should not occur is axiomatic, and the legal complications that have arisen from this particular error underline the importance of ensuring that such errors do not occur. The Act, not unnaturally, does not make express provision in relation to such matters and the resolution on of the resulting difficulties must largely be found in implications and the general law.

23. Before proceeding to examine the legal questions resulting from the error in the benefit classification certicate, it is desirable to set out the provisions of sections 184, 66(2) and 154 of the Superannuation Act so far as material for present purposes:

24. It was contended in argument by Mr Peter Crooks, who appeared for the applicant, that there was no power to correct or replace the benefit classification certificate of 8 May 1979 to remedy what was a patent error in specifying the leg in which the varicose vein condition occurred. This view was based on the non-applicability of s.33(3) of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901, the specific prohibition in the Superannuation Act 1976 against the issue of a certificate while another such certificate is in force (s.184(4)), and the absence of any express power in the Superannuation Act 1976 to revoke and replace, or amend, such certificates. On this basis, he submitted that the Commissioner or his delegate is bound by the first certificate, even though it was erroneous, with the result that the Commissioner (or his delegate) could not have been satisfied, under s.66(2) of the Act, that the incapacity or retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by the physical condition in fact specified in the certificate (i.e. varicose veins right leg with healed ulcer), presumably because there was no condition in the right leg that was relevant for the purposes of Mrs Brown's retirement.

25. Section 33 of the Acts Interpretation Act, so far as relevant, provides 33 1) Where an Act confers a power or imposes a duty, then, unless the contrary intention appears, the power may be exercised and the duty shall be performed from time to time as occasion requires.

...

(3) Where an Act confers a power to make, grant or issue any instrument (including rules, regulations or by-laws) the power shall, unless the contrary intention appears, be construed as including a power exercisable in like manner and subject to the like conditions (if any) to repeal, rescind, revoke, amend or vary any such instrument.

26. Sub-section (1) is directed to the on-going exercise of a power or duty, in the absence of a contrary intention in the relevant legislation, i.e. an intention, to be found either in the provisions of the legislation, or from the nature of the particular power or duty or the context in which it is conferred or imposed, that the power or duty is to be exercised only once in relation to a particular situation or only on limited occasions. Sub-section (3) complements sub-section (1) by permitting, again subject to any contrary intention and subject to fulfilment of conditions on which the principal power may be exercised, an instrument issued under a legislative power to be, inter alia, revoked, amended, or varied.

27. While there is no doubt that a benefit classification certificate is an instrument made or issued under a power conferred by an Act, we agree with Mr Crooks that s.33(3) has no application to the issuing of such certicates under s.184 of the Superannuation Act. In their essential nature, such certificates are not instruments to be amended, varied or revoked from time to time in the way contemplated by s.33(3) of the Acts Interpretation Act. We think that a contrary intention is to be found in the Superannuation Act displacing the application of s.33(3) in relation to the issuing of benefit classification certificates, both because of their essential nature and because of the provision made by s.184(4) precluding the Commissioner from issuing such a certificate, either of his own initiative or at the request of an eligible employee, if such a certificate is already 'in force' in respect of the person at that time. That is not the kind of situation in which the general provisions of s.33 of the Acts Interpretation Act have application.

28. The fact that the general power to amend or revoke conferred by s.33 of the Acts Interpretation Act does not have application does not, however, resolve the matter.

29. Mr W.R. Wilde, for the Commissioner, submitted that the misdescription in the certificate was only a clerical error that occurred in a series of decisions which were taken by delegates of the Commissioner on the basis of the correct medical condition and the correct leg in which the condition existed. He put the view that s.184 requires the Commissioner to determine, in appropriate cases, what conditions were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act, and then to specify those conditions, and no others, in a benefit classification certificate. Having regard to the decision taken under s.184(7) that the relevant condition related to the left leg, s.184(2) did not permit any other condition (i.e. a condition in relation to the right leg) to be specified in the certificate. He submitted that the reference to the right leg cannot operate at all in respect of the original benefit classification certificate.

30. From this he submitted there were two possible consequences. One was that the original certificate was void ab initio; that the later certificate would then become the original certificate; there was no valid reconsideration decision that could be taken; and acc'rdingly the Tribunal did not have power in respect of that and all the reconsideration decision amounted to was a primary decision under s.66(2), which would need to be reconsidered under s.154 before the matter could be brought to the Tribunal. The other was that there was a valid issue of a benefit classification certificate, but that it had the wrong conditions specified on it, and it can operate provided those conditions are corrected. He submitted there was power to rectify an obvious clerical error. His first basis for this was s.33 of the Acts Interpretation Act, which for the reasons noted above, we regard as having no application.

31. Leaving aside s.33 of the Acts Interpretation Act, Mr Wilde submitted that the effect of the reconsideration decision, having regard to the manner in which it is expressed, was to constitute a confirmation of a decision to issue a benefit classification certificate specifying the conditions of anxiety state and varicose veins left leg with healed ulcer, on the basis that the certificate as originally issued had been lawfully amended (or perhaps more strictly, corrected). But if that were not so, he submitted, s.154(4) in any event allows the Commissioner (or Acting Commissioner) to vary a decision under reconsideration in such manner as he thinks fit, and in confirming, as the Acting Commissioner's decision on reconsideration is expressed to do (Exhibit T1, p.1), the delegate's decision of 8 May 1979 to issue a benefit classification certificate specifying inter alia 'varicose veins left leg with healed ulcer', the Acting commissioner in effect himself varied the conditions in the original certificate to the correct conditions, without relying on the substituted certificate dated 19 September 1979.

32. As to Mr Wilde's first argument, we think that a power to correct a patent clerical error in a certificate to bring it into accord with the requirements of the Act and thus express the conditions that had in fact been determined under s.184(7) can and should be implied from the provisions of s.184. It could not have been contemplated by the Parliament that such an error should irrevocably determine the rights of persons under the Act. In Mrs Brown's case it may operate to her advantage to have her rights determined by relying on the error: in other circumstances, however, such an error may operate to the disadvantage of the person to whom it relates. We do not think that the view we take is precluded by s.184(4), which is concerned with the issue of a subsequent substantive certificate that might seek to add some new disability (condition) to those already determined under s.184(7) and consequently included in a certificate in accordance with s.184(2). The reference in s.184(4) to a certificate that is 'in force' should, in our view, be read as a reference to a certificate that is issued in the terms required by the provision in pursuance of which it is issued.

33. An implied power to correct a clerical error in the circumstances mentioned in the preceding paragraph would, we think, enable the issue of a substitute corrected certificate; in other words, to substitute the correct for the incorrect. There is a further question, however, whether this would have the effect of 'validating' a decision under s.66(2) that had previously been taken on the basis that the condition the subject of that decision was specififed in the certificate, when in fact at the time it was not. The s.66(2) decision (Exhibit T5, p.27) was taken in the present case on the basis that the relevant condition was correctly specified in the certificate: it referred specifically to 'Varicose Veins (L) leg with healed ulcer', which was the completely accurate description of the relevant condition as determined under s.184(7), and stated that that condition was specified in the certificate. In fact, it was not specified in those precise terms, at that time. Mr Wilde dealt with this difficulty by submitting that a corrected certificate operated in the same way, and from the same date, as the original certificate: that is to say, it related back, as provided by s.184(2), to the date immediately preceding the retirement of the person to whom it related. Not without some hesitation, we think that this is the preferable view, given the existence of an implied power to correct a patent clerical error in the certificate. The delegate's s.66(2) decision could then be seen as operating, not only on the condition that clearly should have been in the original certificate having regard to the determination under s.184(7) and the express duty placed on the Commissioner by s.184(2)(c), but on what was, by virtue of the correction, to be considered to be in that certificate at the time of the s.66(2) decision.

34. If the view is taken that there is an implied power to correct such a patent clerical error, then, leaving aside medical aspects that we consider later in these Reasons, the certificate and the s.66(2) decision of 8 May 1979 can be seen as valid and there would have been valid reconsideration action taken, so that the matter is properly before the Tribunal in terms of s.154 of the Superannuation Act. The same result would follow from the alternative ground we have relied on in paragraphs 36 et seq below.

35. We see some difficulty in the argument put by Mr Wilde that the Acting Commissioner had in effect himself varied the conditions in the original certificate. Given that the Acting Commissioner had before him the history of the documentation, including the purported correction of the certificate, we think it is clear that the Acting Commissioner acted on the basis that there was a clerical error that required correction and, on the view that it had not already been effectively corrected, he sought, by confirming the delegate's decision in the form he did, to achieve the result that his (the Acting Commissioner's) decision varied the original certificate by necessary implication. With this implied variation, he confirmed the decision to issue the benefit classification certificate and the decision under s.66(2) made on 8 May 1979 and his decision varying and confirming would have effect from the date of the original decisions. The difficulty we see in this argument is that, if the patent clerical error could not be corrected before the Acting Commissioner reconsidered the matter, and if the certificate and the delegate's s.66(2) decision were not already valid by virtue of the principle falsa demonstratio non nocet (see paragraphs 36-40 below) he may not have had a valid decision before him to reconsider. In that case, the Acting Commissioner's action on the purported reconsideration could have amounted at best to primary decision making. Against this, it can, however, be argued that the original certificate (if not able to be corrected in the way adopted on 19 September 1979) was, nevertheless, not a nullity in that it did include one valid condition in it (anxiety state) and that the Acting Commissioner could vary this in pursuance of s.154(4) by substituting the correct description of the other condition and make the correct s.66(2) decision himself. While not dismissing the possibility of supporting the validity of the s.66(2) decision on this basis, we think it is a less satisfactory approach than the other two bases on which we consider the validity of the decisions can be supported.

36. There is another basis, which was not argued before us, on which the validity of the original certificate, and thus the subsequent decisions related to it, can, in our view, be sustained, namely, by applying the rule contained in the maxim falsa demonstratio non nocet. The rule might sho0t$y be stated as being that, if there is an adequate and sufficient description, an erroneous addition does not invalidate that description. The existing condition that was the subject of the original decision was clearly that shown in the original medical report as 'Varicose Veins (L) leg with healed ulcer'. The description in the certificate that was issued in pursuance of the decision based on the medical report referring to the condition as being in the 'Right' leg was a misdescription that we think can be regarded as not going to the basic validity of the certificate. Had neither 'Left' nor 'Right' appeared in the certificate, the condition would, in our view, have been sufficiently identified, and as there was no comparable condition in the right leg that could have met the description in the certificate (there was no healed ulcer on the right leg), and that therefore needed to be distinguished, the reference to the specification of the particular leg in the certificate can be regarded as unnecessary and, being incorrect, can be disregarded. Certainly, it misled no-one.

37. If the reference in the original certificate to the right leg is to be regarded as falsa demonstratio, then the subsequent issue of the 'corrected' certificate is of no real legal consequence and a letter to the applicant would have sufficed to acknowledge the misdescription without the issue of any other formal certificate. The s.66(2) decision of 8 May 1979, which itself included a reference to the left leg ("(L) leg"), can, assuming the maxim falsa demonstratio is applicable to the original certificate, be supported on the view that the inclusion of '(L)' was unnecessary and an inconsequential inaccuracy in describing what in fact appeared on the certificate.

38. The maxim falsa demonstratio non nocet is, of course, most commonly called in aid in respect of deeds and wills; its application to statutory instruments is much less common. However, that the maxim is applicable to statutory instruments is confirmed by the Full High Court decision of Wingadee Shire Council v Willis [1910] HCA 35; (1910) 11 CLR 123, where Griffith CJ (at p.131), Barton J (at pp.139-140), Isaacs J (at p.144), and Higgins J (at pp.147-148) all held that an error in a rate notice issued under the Local Government Act 1906 (NSW) did not render the notice invalid. The error in the notice in that case was in specifying an incorrect date as the date on which the rates would become due, after it had properly said that they would become due after 30 days from the day of service. Both Isaacs J and Higgins J relied expressly on the maxim falsa demonstratio.

39. While in the Wingadee Shire Council case considerations in favour of applying the maxim were probably stronger than in the present case, as the incorrect statement in the Wingadee Shire Council case was in form a videlicet, we think that the principle is applicable to this case. As Higgins J said, at p.148:

'Errors such as this are not fatal to the rights of the Council ... The error is such that the ratepayer could detect by an examination of the Act, or of the notice itself.'

40. A more recent application of the principle is to be found in Marine Board of Strahan v Kirkpatrick (1967) Tas. SR 179, where the Tasmanian Full Supreme Court applied the principle in relation to a by-law under the Marine Act 1921 of Tasmania to ignore an incorrect sub-title in the by-law.

The Medical Issues

41. We turn now to the substantive issue involved in these proceedings, namely, whether the incapacity which was the ground for Mrs Brown's retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by the varicose veins condition in her left leg, or by a physical condition connected with that condition. It should be mentioned that it was not suggested that the condition 'Anxiety State' specified in the benefit classification certificate had any relevance to that incapacity.

42. As already observed, from the time of her appointment in September 1975 to the time of the accident at work on 7 July 1978, Mrs Brown's service record and medical record showed no adverse effects so far as her varicose vein condition was concerned. She did not take any sick leave on account of that condition during this period. It was the blow to the left leg that resulted in absence from work and surgical treatment.

43. The substantive question is primarily a medical question. The principal medical evidence was provided by Dr Scott-Findlay, a specialist surgeon who was called by the respondent, but who had treated Mrs Brown over some 20 years for what he described as a variety of conditions. He stated that 'some years earlier' (i.e. before her accident in 1978) he had 'exercised some anxiety about the varicose veins' and agreed that this would have been about 1969. Referring to the 'healed ulcer' on Mrs Brown's left leg, Dr Scott-Findlay said that it was caused by the varicose vein and that surgical treatment was required. He also said that a varicose condition 'has to be fairly severe to ulcerate'. In Mrs Brown's case it took a long time to heal and that 'the longer it takes to heal, the more complex is the varicosity situation, and the more veins, the more difficult it is". The ulcer was on the outside of Mrs Brown's left leg and the injury for which he treated her more recently was 'higher up and on the inside of the leg'. Dr Scott-Findlay's attention was directed to the letter he wrote to the Commonwealth Medical Officer on 29 October 1979 (Exhibit T5, p.40), in which he stated that Mrs Brown had had some varicose veins for a large number of years and that the thrombo phlebitis in the veins was worsened by the trauma to the veins. The letter went on:

'Obviously, if she has a varicose condition, the thrombo phlebitis from a traumatic condition will be worse in the varicose vein than the non-varicose vein due to stasis, and this will cause a greater propensity to thrombo phlebitis.'

Dr Scott-Findlay was also shown the extract from Pear's Medical Encyclopaedia at p.19 of Exhibit T5, and agreed with 'almost all of that'. In particular his attention was invited to two statements in it. The first was that phlebitis (inflammation of a vein with clot formation) 'is both a cause and the result of varicose veins'. The second was that 'phlebitis is particularly likely to occur in sufferers from varicose veins and the initial cause that starts it off may be an injury'. Dr Scott-Findlay agreed that the ulcer in Mrs Brown's case was a result of the varicose condition, that he thought her thrombo phlebitis was the result of her varicose condition, and agreed that there was no doubt that phlebitis is particularly likely to occur in sufferers from varicose veins when the initial cause that starts it off may be an injury. He added 'but the elderly who get varicose veins only need a minimal injury and they develop thrombo phlebitis'.

44. Dr Scott-Findlay was asked whether there was a connection between the condition of 'thrombo phlebitis' and the condition 'varicose veins left leg with healed ulcer' in Mrs Brown's case. The relevant questions and answers are as follows:

'Could you state whether or not the condition thrombo phlebitis is connected with the condition 'varicose veins' notwithstanding the accident which led to the thrombo phlebitis?-I thought I had just said that. Varicose veins do predispose towards thrombo phlebitis. It is not usually seen, except in extraordinary circumstances, in people that do not have a stasis, that is, a diminution of flow, and that only occurs in varicose veins. It is very seldom seen in people who have competent veins and competent valves.

So you would answer the question in the affirmative?-There is no doubt about that.'

45. In a report to the Director-General of Health dated 17 April 1980 (Exhibit T5, pp.47-50) for the purposes of a claim under the Compensation (Commonwealth Government Employees) Act, Dr Scott-Findlay, having expressed the opinion that Mrs Brown's incapacity due to thrombo phlebitis was likely to be permanent and to result in total incapacity, included an addendum in the following terms:

'I would like to add as an addenda (sic) to Mrs Brown that the ulcer on outer side of the leg is quite unconnected with all of this. That was a previous injury and quite unrelated, that is over her malleolus, and is a result of trauma, fairly common, which most people have sometimes during their lifetime, and in her case took a while to heal.

The injury over the other vein was a fresh one and quite unrelated.'

46. In evidence, Dr Scott-Findlay agreed that in saying that the injuries were unrelated he was 'talking about the actual injury at one site'. He said:

'She has got veins on one side and an injury on the other ... But they are in a different part of the leg, the ones on the inside and the ones on the outside.'

47. In cross-examination, Dr Scott-Findlay agreed that, if she had not had the accident at work, Mrs Brown may have been able to continue working and that for a substantial period before her retirement she had not sought his assistance for her varicose veins. The relevant questions and answers on the former aspect are as follows:

'If Mrs Brown had not had the accident at work, would she be able to continue working?-She may have.

I think you have said already that the condition had been minimal and had not required any treatment prior to the accident?-Not in recent years, no.

So it is reasonable to say that there was a real possibility that the condition could continue, that is, that situation?-It is possible.

Would you say it is probable in the event that she had not sought any treatment for the condition? -That would make me think that it was probable. On the other hand, she has already had ulceration and treatment for it and it is a treatment that you do not cure."

48. Dr Scott-Findlay also stated later in cross-examination:

'The original ulceration was on the area of the lateral side, very minimal. This one is higher up over the saphenous but they are all connected. You cannot single out one vein on a leg and say that that vein is varicose and by removing it you are curing it. It is a condition that is congenital.'

When he was referred to the original Commonwealth Medical Officer's report (Exhibit T5, p.13) and asked to say, in relation to the healed ulcer, that it was fair to say that at the time of the report there was no specific problem with that ulcer, Dr Scott-Findlay answered 'Yes', but when further asked to agree that it could be said that it could be anticipated that that would be unlikely to re-occur, he answered 'Not at all. She has varicose veins which has (sic) just healed. It just says that there is an ulcer which now has an epithelium over it.'

49. In re-examination, Dr Scott-Findlay was asked to advert again to page 47 of Exhibit T5 in relation to the statement that the varicose vein had been minimal and had not required any treatment and to clarify what the existence of varicose veins means. The relevant question and answer are as follows:

'You said in effect that the vein was a minimal problem, but that nevertheless it was varicosed, and I understand from what you said that with the system of veins being all connected, there was an indication perhaps of poor circulation in the leg, and there is likely to be either a pre-disposition to suffer from varicose veins in other areas or that they already existed, and it is this pre-existing condition which results, on injury or from one of the other reasons, in the recurrence of the thrombo phlebitis, and that this is what occurred, and but for that situation, there would not have been thrombo phlebitis in this case. Is that correct?-Yes, I think that is substantially correct. If you look at that paragraph, it says 'It had been minimal', but I do say that the varicose vein has been increasing in disability over the years. I think that answers the question.'

50. Dr Scott-Findlay, in answer to questions from the Tribunal, said that Mrs Brown's thrombo phlebitis was precipitated by the knock her leg sustained at work, in the sense that it was the knock that was the occasion for the thrombo phlebitis rather than its ultimate cause. The particular questions and answers were as follows:

'Is it right to say that in a varicosed condition, there is always a danger of thrombo phlebitis, whether from a knock or not?-Yes.

However, is it fair to say that in the normal course, in the absence of a knock, a person has a comparatively low risk of thrombo phlebitis?- That is true.

And then is it also fair to say that in this case _ the case we are considering at the moment _ thrombo phlebitis was in fact precipitated by the knock at work?-I think that is clearly documented and certainly would follow that injury.

One thing about 'precipitate". I think the ordinary understanding as distinct from medical, is that that means that was the occasion for it happening rather than the ultimate cause. I think that is what you were intending to convey?-That is right, because you need stasis. If you got a whack on the middle of your leg with a hockey stick, you would have a bruise, but it is unlikely that it would go on to thrombo phlebitis, because there is no stasis. It would injure the lining of the vein, but it would repair itself ... You would have a sore vein. When you have a pre-disposed condition, you can injure sometimes by putting an intravenous needle into the vein, and it clots fairly readily, particularly in elderly people, because most of their veins, even in the arms, are varicosed, and putting in an intravenous needle in the normal course of post-operative surgical management could cause a thrombosis more easily, whereas people under the age of 50 who are in fairly good health do not usually get it."

51. Dr Turner's evidence was principally concerned with confirming from medical records that it was the varicose veins in Mrs Brown's left leg that were the subject of the original denial of her acceptance as a contributor to the old Superannuation Fund and with affirming that a very strict standard was applied in accepting persons with varicose veins. She also confirmed from clinical cards the report of Dr McGregor on his examination of Mrs Brown in relation to her retirement. She stated that there was no reference in the clinical cards to Mrs Brown's past history of illness since she went into the Public Service. 52. Mrs Brown gave evidence as to how the ulcer in her left leg occurred in 1972. She said 'It was just a small spot and I scratched it. It itched and I scratched it and it made an ulcer.' She said she had had no trouble with the ulcer since Dr Scott-Findlay healed it and suffered no pain in that location. She explained how the accident occurred while at her place of employment. She stated that she did not, from the time of her 1975 medical examination to the time of her accident, suffer from the varicose veins or take any sick leave on account of them. She had been able to cope with her work and her normal housekeeping duties.

53. The question that has to be determined is whether s.66(2) is, in the light of the evidence, applicable to Mrs Brown. If it is not, then she would be entitled to the benefits provided for by s.66(1). Section 66(2), so far as relevant, is set out in paragraph 23 above.

54. Paragraph (a) of s.66(2) is not in issue and, as to paragraph (b), on the view we have taken there was, or more correctly was deemed to be, in force immediately before Mrs Brown's retirement a benefit classification certificate specifying 'Varicose veins left leg with healed ulcer' or 'Varicose veins leg with healed ulcer'.

55. On the evidence before us, we think that there is no doubt that the incapacity which was the ground for Mrs Brown's retirement was the effects of the condition thrombo phlebitis and that these effects were necessarily caused or substantially contributed to by the thrombo phlebitis. The question then becomes whether the condition thrombo phlebitis (which is not itself specified in the benefit classification certificate) was 'connected with' the condition specified in the certificate as noted in the preceding paragraph.

56. The expression 'connected with' in s.66(2) has been considered by this Tribunal in Re Vaughan and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 2 ALN No.366, and in Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) V80/3 (decision given 30 September 1980). We respectfully agree with what was said in those cases concerning the expression, and regard it as involving at least a real or demonstrable, as distinct from a mere casual, connection.

57. The evidence clearly shows that Mrs Brown had not in fact had any trouble with her varicose veins that affected her duties in the Public Service or caused her to be absent from those duties in the period since she re-joined the Service in September 1975 until the accident in July 1978. What precipitated her troubles was that accident. It is, of course, nothing to the point, for the purposes of the present proceedings, that that accident occurred in the course of her employment. Her position for present purposes would have been no worse and no better if the injury to her leg had occurred while at home.

58. In the light of the medical evidence, however, particularly that of Dr Scott-Findlay quoted above, we think the conclusion is inescapable that the thrombo phlebitis from which Mrs Brown was suffering at the time of her retirement was 'connected with', in the sense we have read the expression, the varicose vein condition in her leg, as specified in the benefit classification certificate. The evidence shows that, while the bump to the leg precipitated the condition of thrombo phlebitis, it was the existence of the varicose vein condition that was the underlying cause, in the sense that without its existence the thrombo phlebitis would not have occurred. We have had regard to the fact that the injury caused by the bump to the leg was not an injury to the vein that was the subject of the original ulceration, i.e. it was in a different site on the leg, but as Dr Scott-Findlay said in evidence:

'The original ulceration was on the area on the lateral side, very minimal. This one is higher up over the saphenous but they are all connected. You cannot single out one vein on a leg and say that the vein is varicose and by removing it, you are curing it. It is a condition that is congenital.'

Dr Scott-Findlay agreed that the injury was the precipitating cause of the thrombo phlebitis but made it clear that in a normal long saphenous vein the result would be only soreness, not thrombosis. We draw attention particularly to his evidence quoted in paragraph 44 above.

Conclusion

59. As, in our view, the thrombo phlebitis was connected with the condition specified in the benefit classification certificate, it follows that all the conditions of paragraphs (a), (b) and (c) of sub-section (2) of s.66 were satisfied and the delegate and the Acting Commissioner were correct in concluding in effect that Mrs Brown's entitlement fell to be determined under that sub-section.

60. In the light of the foregoing, the Tribunal affirms the decision of the Acting Commissioner of 12 September 1980, on the basis that the Benefit Classification Certificate issued in respect of the applicant on 8 May 1979 was validly corrected on 19 September 1979 (see paragraphs 32-34 above) or, alternatively, on the basis that the Certificate as originally issued should be read as if it referred to 'Varicose Veins Leg with healed ulcer' (see paragraphs 36-40 above), and that the applicant's entitlements under the Superannuation Act 1976 should be determined in accordance with sub-section 66(2) of that Act.
