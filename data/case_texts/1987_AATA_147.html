HEARING

PERTH

23:4:1987

ORDER

By majority (Mr. D.J. Trowse dissenting), the decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

The questions for decision in this reference, which concerns the year of income ended 30th June, 1983, may be stated as follows:

(a) Whether an amount of $52,175 was correctly

included in the assessable income of the

taxpayer/applicant (herein called the

applicant) for the year ended 30th June, 1983,

pursuant to ss.19 and 92, and to the

definition of "partnership" in sub-s.6(1) of

the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

(b) Whether various documents as existed which

purported to evidence the derivation of the

income of an accountancy practice by the

trustee for a family unit trust were shams and

had no legal effect.

(c) Also, whether various contracts, agreements or

arrangements which were not evidenced in

writing but which purported to evidence the

derivation of the practice income by the

trustee for the family unit trust were shams

and had no legal effect.

(d) As an alternative to (b) and (c), whether the

agreements and transactions constituted an

arrangement which s.260 of the Assessment Act

operated to void as against the Commissioner

of Taxation (herein called the Commissioner).

A subsidiary question was raised by ground 4 of the objection concerning the application of ss.82AAS and 82AAT of the Act, which provisions deal with deduction for contributions made to a qualifying superannuation fund. However, we were given to under stand by counsel for both parties that this question would be resolved by our decision in relation to the substantive matters raised, and therefore we make no further reference to it.

2. Upon the Commissioner's disallowance of the applicant's claims, and the objection against the disallowance, the decision on the objection was, at the request of the applicant, referred to a Board of Review for review. The Board of Review concluded its hearing of the matters at issue before 1st July, 1986, and reserved its decision. Under the provisions of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986, the functions of the Taxation Boards of Review were transferred to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal with effect from 1st July, 1986.

3. It is emphasised that the year of income in issue is that which ended on 30th June, 1983, and that the success of the Commissioner's case in defence of the assessment depends substantially upon his making good the proposition that, in terms of s.260 of the Assessment Act, there was a contract, agreement or an arrangement which was void as against the Commissioner and which was made or entered into by the applicant, either orally or in writing, on or before 27th May, 1981. It will be observed that, with the introduction of Part IVA into the Act with effect as from 28th May, 1981, s.260 does not apply to any contract, agreement or arrangement made or entered into on or after the last mentioned date.

4. Under s.43(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, we, as the duly constituted Tribunal pursuant to s.223(1)(c) of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986, may exercise all the powers of the Commissioner for the purpose of reviewing his decision on the objection in issue. Under s.43(1) of that Act, we are required to make a decision in writing to either affirm the decision under review, vary it or to set it aside and make a decision in substit ution for it or to remit the matter for reconsideration by the Commissioner in accordance with any directions or recommendations that we might make.

5. It is to be observed that under existing legislation our powers are concerned with the decision of the Commissioner on the objection in issue and not, as was the case under the repealed legislation (see former s.195(1) of the Assessment Act), with the assessment in respect of the year of income in issue. On the other hand, the applicant, in terms of s.190(b) of the Assessment Act, is still charged with the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive.

6. During the course of the hearing before the Board of Review, counsel for the Commissioner made it clear that the substantive matter in issue concerned the application of s.260 of the Assessment Act and did not raise for our consideration Part IVA of the Act which might, providing the provisions of that Part were satisfied, have application to any "scheme" (as defined in s.177A of the Act) which was entered into and/or carried out or commenced to be carried out on or after 28th May, 1981, and on or before the end of the year in issue, being that which ended on 30th June, 1983. In this connection, counsel gave us to under stand that the Commissioner had not made any determination in relation to Part IVA, with the consequence that the operation of that Part in respect of the period mentioned could not be in issue before us.

7. With respect, we accept the position that we have no power to consider the application of Part IVA in the circum stances described. However, it appears to us that, having regard to the unusual circumstances of the case, it would undoubtedly be open to the Commissioner, on an appeal by him against a decision of the Tribunal to argue that the Tribunal was in error in not upholding his decision on the objection on the basis that s.260 was properly applicable. However, a further basis of argument might also be available to the Commissioner to the effect that, even if the Tribunal's decision were correct, the applicant had nevertheless failed to discharge the onus of proving that the assessment was excessive because, following upon taking the necessary steps subsequent to the Tribunal's decision and in applying the provisions of Part IVA to the facts in the case, it emerged that the assessment was soundly based, notwithstanding the error of his decision in terms of s.260. The latter argument might also be available to the Commissioner in the event that the other party, the applicant, should appeal against the Tribunal's decision.

8. However, despite the possibilities adverted to, which in fairness to the parties we have decided to include in our reasons, we have proceeded to make our findings of fact and to state our reasons in law for our decision based upon the propos ition that, if the Commissioner's decision on the objection in terms of s.260 is found by us to be in error, the applicant has effectively discharged the onus of proof imposed upon him pursuant to s.190(b).

9. At the hearing of the reference before the Board of Review, the applicant, as previously indicated, was also repres ented by counsel. The applicant, and one other witness called to give evidence on his behalf, gave evidence under oath. One witness was called to give evidence on behalf of the Commissioner.

10. Turning to the evidence, it emerges that the applicant is a qualified accountant and is a member of the two major Australian accountancy bodies. He also has served on various committees with one such body and for several years spent some 300 hours each year in an honorary capacity in the pursuit of duties associated with that body. In carrying out those duties, he acquired a sound knowledge of the rules and ethical require ments of the accountancy profession.

11. After some 10 years spent in employment, the applicant commenced in about 1973 to operate his own tax consultancy practice, which we will refer to as "D and Associates". While retaining the same firm name, he was joined in partnership by V in 1974, by W in 1976 and by G in 1978. Under the partnership arrangements, the applicant was responsible for the overall administration of the firm, while W was directly concerned with its auditing activities, V with its accountancy functions and G with the estate planning side of the firm's business. No formal or written partnership agreement was entered into at any time. The net income of the partnership was shared equally by the partners. However, the partnership arrangements did not encompass any provision for the payment of superannuation to the partners.

12. By about 1981, it became apparent to the partners that the business conducted by them would expand and that a further partner was necessary. Following negotiations, to which we shall return later, the then existing partnership structure was abandoned and replaced by a unit trust in which units were held by those four former partners and by the incoming partner, and by family trusts of those persons.

13. We return now to consider further aspects of the applicant's position prior to 1981. In May 1977, the applicant had set up a family trust with the "V" nominee company as its trustee. Within a short time thereafter, the applicant trans ferred all of his important private assets, including his interest in the residential home, to the company trustee. It seems that several years later the applicant acquired a rent-producing property which apparently was registered in the name of the trustee. Those actions were taken, it seems, to enable his wife's assets to be built up and, at the same time, to protect his personal estate from professional negligence claims which were a matter of concern at the time, especially in the area of the partnership's audit activities. Under this trust arrangement, as finally determined by a supplemental deed executed in September 1977, the applicant was excluded as a beneficiary of that trust and, it seems, no income was passed directly from the partnership to the trust.

14. However, under a second family trust established under a deed executed on or about 1st May, 1981 (stamped on 1st May, 1981), the applicant, together with his wife and other beneficiaries not here relevant, became entitled to distributions from this trust if and when made in the absolute discretion of the trustee. The trustee in this instance (which we refer to as "O" Nominees Pty. Ltd.) was also a family company which was, it seems, under the effective control of the applicant. The significance of this trust arrangement, under which the applicant became a beneficiary, will emerge more fully in the light of other develop ments which took place in the affairs of the partnership to which we now turn.

15. On 2nd June, 1977, "B" Nominees Pty. Ltd. was incorporated for the purpose of becoming the corporate trustee of a service trust created under a deed executed on 22nd June, 1977. The unit holders in the service trust were the V nominee company referred to earlier and partner G and his wife. The company, as trustee of the applicant's first family trust, of partner V's family trust and of partner W's family trust, held a total of 27 units, of which nine units were held in respect of each family trust. Partner G and his wife held nine units in respect of their family trust. The object of the service trust was to provide secretarial services to the partnership, referred to earlier as "D and Associates". The provision of those services was authorised under an agreement dated 29th August, 1977, between the former partners and "B" Nominees Pty. Ltd. as trustee for the service trust. Under the agreement, all office furniture and equipment of the partnership was transferred to the trustee and provision was made for the appointment of the trustee as manager to control the secretarial activities and for the payment of remuneration by the partnership to the trustee for services rendered. The net income from the service trust was distributed to the family trusts of the former partners. It appears that, like the applicant under his first family trust, the other partners were also excluded as beneficiaries under their family trusts, the income of which included distributions from the service trust.

16. The term of the service agreement was five years. However, with the replacement of the partnership structure by a unit trust structure to which we now turn, the term was extended by a further five years and appropriate action was taken, it seems, to ensure that the secretarial services would continue to be provided by the service trust to the unit trust and that the net income of the service trust would be distributed equally between the family trusts of the former partners and X.

17. On 18th June, 1981, D and Associates Pty. was incorporated as an unlimited liability company having a capital comprising 100,000 shares of $1 each, and became the trustee of the D and Associates Unit Trust which was created under a trust deed executed on 22nd June, 1981. The subscribers to the company's memorandum of association were the four former partners and X. The main objects of the company as disclosed in the memorandum were:

"(a) to carry on the business and to perform any of

the functions of a public accountant which a

company is permitted by law to perform; and

(b) to undertake the office of trustee of a trust

or unit trust or any other office or situation

of trust or confidence and to perform and

discharge the duties and functions thereof,

and to carry out and perform all or any of the

powers, authorities or discretions conferred

or implied by the deed of trust or by law,

either gratuitously or otherwise.

....."

Additionally, each former partner and X became entitled to 20 ordinary units in the total of 100 such units issued in the D and Associates Unit Trust, and the corporate trustees of the family trusts of those persons each became entitled to four special par units, valued at $1 each in the total of 20 such units issued. The holding of special par units did not confer any right to vote at any meeting of unit holders or provide any right to the advancement of capital in the unit trust. Upon the termination of the trust, the holders of the special par units were entitled to share in the capital of the trust to the extent of $1 only per unit in priority to any distribution that might otherwise become available to the holders of ordinary units.

18. Subject to those qualifications as broadly stated, the holders of both special par and ordinary units were entitled to receive distributions from the D and Associates Unit Trust in the absolute discretion of D and Associates Pty. as trustee. Of significance is the following clause contained in the Schedule to the unit trust deed:

"In respect to Special Par Units -

(a) pursuant to clause 11 (dealing with the general

power of Distribution of Income) the Trustee may pay,

apply or set aside the whole or such part of the net

income of the Trust Fund of each accounting period to or

for the benefit of any one or more of the holders of

Special Par Units as the Trustee thinks fit And the

payment, application or setting aside may be to or for

the holders in respect of one or more Special Par Units

to the exclusion of or an amount different from that

paid, applied or set aside to or for the holders of

other Special Par Units And the expressions 'units' and

'unit holders' contained in clause 11 shall except as

provided in this provision refer only to ordinary units

and to the holders of ordinary units respectively;

....."

The above power enabled the five directors (comprising the erst while partners and X) to determine when and what amounts should be distributed to the two classes of unit holders and to particular unit holders within each class. Any distributions decided upon could, in the discretion of the same five persons, whether as directors or as controllers of their family trusts, be drawn out and invested in outside assets or otherwise, or left in the business as loan accounts. We were advised that the special par units were transferred to the former partners and X some time after 10th April, 1984, being the date upon which the 1983 assessment in dispute was issued. It seems that this action was taken to avoid continuing problems with the Commissioner.

19. It appears that the accounting practice conducted by the former partners was sold for an amount of $500,000 to D and Associates Pty. with effect from 22nd June, 1981. This amount was not paid immediately by the Unit Trust and became a debt payable on demand. No interest was payable in respect of the debt. However, we were given to understand that all of the debt, or most of it, has since been paid. The consideration of $500,000 represented largely the value of the goodwill attaching to the practice and provided a basis for selling a fifth share of the equity in the business to X. Upon the payment of $100,000 as an ingoing by X, the former partners and X, through their respective family trusts, shared equally in the equity of the business conducted by D and Associates Pty. as trustee for the D and Associates Unit Trust. As from 22nd June, 1981, the former partners and X were able, pursuant to the various arrangements entered into, to control the conduct of the accountancy business acquired by D and Associates Pty. and to distribute its net annual income to themselves and the family trusts in any manner they chose. As employees of the corporate trustee, they became entitled to the payment of salaries and, consistent with sharing the equity equally, they received salaries of similar amount.

20. At a meeting of directors (comprising the former partners and X) of D and Associates Pty. as trustee of the D and Associates Unit Trust, held on 25th June, 1981, a deed was executed for the purpose of setting up a superannuation fund for the benefit of directors and selected employees of the trustee company. It was resolved at the meeting that the company would make contributions to the fund in respect of those persons, having regard to the maximum benefits allowable by the Commissioner of Taxation, the nature of the investments held by the fund and the level of benefits desired for each member of the fund. In its initial stages, the fund was what was described as an internal "unallocated pension fund" which had been recommended by outside professional advisers. It was altered to an "allocated lump sum fund" some time in 1983. A proprietary company, in which the directors were the subscribers, was incorporated on 25th June, 1981, and was appointed on that day as trustee of the fund.

21. In the 1981 income year, contributions, totalling $72,540, were made to the superannuation fund on behalf of the former partners and X and it seems that this amount, with the exception of the $40, was lent back to the Unit Trust on an unsecured basis. In the 1982 year, it appears that contributions of about $25,000 were made on behalf of the former partners and X, and all of that amount, it seems, was lent back to the Unit Trust, also on an unsecured basis. The evidence does not permit us to say whether interest was paid or payable on the amounts so lent back and, if payable, when it would be payable. We do not know what the position was in the 1983 year, but it seems possible that the contributions made in that year were invested externally.

22. From the above narrative, it will be apparent that neither D and Associates Pty., nor the trustee company of the superannuation fund, and D and Associates Unit Trust, came into being until after 27th May, 1981, being the last day on which the provisions of s.260 could apply. Therefore, in order to examine the Commissioner's contention that there was a contract, agreement or an arrangement made or entered into, orally or in writing, before that date in respect of which s.260 applied, it is necessary for us to examine more closely the various actions taken, and negotiations entered into, during a period of several months prior to that date. It will also be necessary, in exam ining the Commissioner's contention, to have regard to the practical effects of those actions and negotiations after that date and before 30th June, 1983, for the purpose of determining whether it should be concluded that the result of what happened was, directly or indirectly, to reduce the applicant's liability to pay tax in the 1983 income year in terms of the tests provided in paragraphs (a), (b), (c) or (d) of sub-s.260(1).

23. From what has been said it will be apparent that the applicant's interest in estate planning took a practical form in 1977 with the setting up of his first family trust, and that it also led to the setting up of his second family trust in 1981. However, before the latter step was taken, a number of events occurred which have a direct bearing on the matters in issue before us.

24. It seems that in January 1981, or thereabouts, the applicant and the other three former partners in D and Associates embarked on a course of action that extended over several months and had as its objective the obtaining of a fifth partner to better enable the practice to cope with its expanding business. We have referred to this person as X. It seems that X was known to partner G because of a working relationship that had existed between them as employees of the same accountancy firm some years earlier. It also seems that, while X was satisfied with the terms and conditions of his employment with that firm and was prepared to continue on as one of its employees, he was interested in joining D and Associates providing the following three main conditions were satisfied:

(i) He would become a principal of the firm and

have a say in its conduct.

(ii) He would undertake work in the firm of a

similar nature to that undertaken by him as an

employee of his then employer.

(iii) Superannuation entitlements would be provided

by D and Associates, which would equal those

provided by his then employer.

From the viewpoint of D and Associates, X was a suitable person to join the firm, having regard to such matters as his age, his accountancy experience and his expertise in the areas of tax and estate planning.

25. In about January 1981, G was authorised by his fellow partners to enter into negotiations with X with a view to securing his services. In the course of these negotiations it was found that D and Associates, under its existing structure, could satisfy conditions (i) and (ii), but that it could not satisfy condition (iii) which concerned superannuation. It will be recalled that D and Associates did not have a superannuation scheme which could be utilised in providing the benefits sought by X.

26. It was said in evidence that problems associated with the provision of superannuation benefits for X led to thoughts of incorporation of D and Associates as a means of over coming those problems. The use of a structure of that kind (with or without the involvement of trusts) as a means of conducting a business and/or for estate and/or tax planning purposes, would have been well known to the existing partners because of their business and personal experiences. In this connection, it will be recalled that G was responsible for the estate planning side of the firm's business and that, by that time, each of the partners had a family trust which had been brought into existence to allocate income to members of their respective families. It will also be recalled that the applicant, in his capacity as a senior committee man of an accountancy body, was conversant with the current rules and ethical requirements of the accountancy profession which, subject to various conditions which need not concern us here, permitted accountancy firms to conduct business under a structure which comprised an incorporated body as trustee with family trusts as unit holders in it.

27. Faced with the problem mentioned, and bearing in mind the experience of the partners concerning trusts and the advice available to them in relation to the ethics of the profession surrounding such a structure, it is not surprising, nor in any professional sense reprehensible, that they should consider such a structure and adopt it in preference to some other means of continuing the firm's business. With the future structure of the firm decided upon, at least in general terms, G advised X, early in March 1981, that the three conditions stipulated by him could be met. It appears that soon thereafter, X gave his employer the requisite several months notice of his intention to terminate his employment, which event happened, it would seem, in the following month. As indicated earlier, X was a subscriber to the memorandum of association of D and Associates Pty. on its incorporation on 18th June, 1981, and became entitled to 20 ordinary units in his own right and, through the corporate trustee of his family trust, to four special par units in the D and Associates Unit Trust which came into existence on 22nd June, 1981. As also indicated earlier, D and Associates Pty. commenced to operate the business of the former partnership as from 22nd June, 1981, with X and the erstwhile partners as full-time salaried directors. Their super annuation rights were secured under the arrangements entered into on 25th June, 1981.

28. For the purposes of determining whether there was a contract, an agreement or an arrangement in terms of s.260, which was made or entered into on or before 27th May, 1981, it is necessary to examine in some detail discussions and events that were entered into and that took place in the period January 1981 to 27th May, 1981. We therefore turn to the evidence in this connection of the applicant and his former partner G, who were the only two witnesses who gave evidence in support of the applicant's case.

29. However, before proceeding with that examination, it is relevant to observe that when X notified his employer in March 1981 of his intention to resign from the firm in several months time, he was apparently happy in his work and well paid and entitled to superannuation benefits of $1,000,000 at retirement in the normal course of events, whether at age 60 or 65 years, or at some other age not disclosed to us. It will also be observed that he was required to pay an ingoing of $100,000 when joining the new unit trust in June 1981. X did not give evidence before us, and we do not therefore know what his understanding was of various discussions entered into or of the proposals that would enable his conditions to be met. However, it seems inconceivable to us that a person in X's position, with professional abilities of a high order and wide experience in taxation and financial matters generally, would contemplate resigning his existing position with the attendant risk of jeopardising his career, unless he was completely satisfied by G (and probably by all former partners) that proposals to meet his conditions had been formulated and agreed upon. Furthermore, we believe that he would be anxious to ensure that his conditions were capable of being implemented, having regard, inter alia, to the rules and ethical requirements of the relevant accountancy body. It seems to us that, to be so satisfied, X must have been appraised in quite considerable detail of how the proposals were to be implemented, of their timing and of their potential to also provide the means by which estate planning and tax savings might be achieved.

30. The applicant's evidence in relation to the proposals and various discussions concerning them appeared to be somewhat guarded and, on occasions, equivocal. However, we accept his evidence that the initiating factors that gave rise to the actions taken had their origin in the firm's need to obtain the services of a senior and experienced accountant and that, with a view to realising that end with X in mind, it was necessary to re organise the existing partnership structure so as to be able to satisfy X's conditions, including, in particular, the provision of adequate superannuation benefits. However, there is no evidence to suggest that the inclusion of the corporate trustees of the family trusts as special par unit holders in the D and Associates Unit Trust was an essential or even a required part of the proposed structure for the purposes of satisfying X's conditions. In fact, as indicated earlier, the corporate trustees of the family trusts ceased to hold the par units as from 1984 with no apparent threat to the continued fulfilment of X's conditions. We draw the conclusion, therefore, that the inclusion of the family trusts with entitlements to distributions of income from D and Associates Unit Trust was an essential part of the proposed structure for the sole purpose (albeit as part of estate planning) of enabling the diversion of income to the families with the consequential reduction in the tax payable by the former partners and X as directors/employees of the company.

31. It seemed to be suggested by the applicant that his contribution to the discussions and negotiations concerning the proposals was limited for the most part to imparting his knowledge to G concerning procedures to be followed in obtaining the accountancy body's approval for the proposals (which he anticipated would be forthcoming), and to expressing his view that the structure to be adopted should enable estate planning to be carried out. Bearing in mind the applicant's position as the founder and senior partner of D and Associates (which name, incidentally, continued in use after the sale of the practice) and his experience and knowledge, both personally and professionally, in the finance and tax fields, we find it difficult to accept without serious reservations that his contribution was as limited as suggested.

32. While we accept that G was charged with the responsibility for undertaking detailed negotiations with X and with the day-to-day intricacies of planning the new structure, which included instructing a solicitor in the matter, we are left with the over-riding impression that the applicant was conversant at all relevant times with the broad thrust of the proposals, of the time frame within which the proposals should be implemented and, in March 1981, or thereabouts, of the need to create the second family trust as a matter of some urgency if his desires for estate planning under the new structure were to be fulfilled.

33. As was the case with X, we consider that it would be unrealistic for us to accept what appears to be implicit in the proposition that was put to us, that the applicant was content, allegedly without detailed knowledge of the proposals and their effects upon his present and future financial position, both personally and from the firm's viewpoint, to remain detached from discussions and the decision-making processes which surely must have preceded advice to X that his conditions would be satisfied and which led him, X, in turn, to give notice to his employer of his resignation.

34. Similarly, we consider that it would be unrealistic to conclude that the creation of that trust, which was basic to the fulfilment of the applicant's desires concerning estate planning, represented a last minute refinement to the proposals. Further, we consider that the decision to issue ordinary units in the Unit Trust to him and to the other former partners and X, and to issue special par units in the Unit Trust to the corporate trustees of their respective family trusts, was not a last minute decision or something that happened by chance, but rather was a key element in the proposals which had been carefully considered and decided upon in about March/April 1981, and certainly before 28th May, 1981. To conclude otherwise would be, in our opinion, to ignore the fundamental nature of the proposals which concerned the firm's future prosperity and that of the individuals concerned and their future rights and responsibilities in relation to each other and to third parties under the structure to be brought into existence. Therefore, we reject the applicant's suggestion that the distributions in fact made in the 1982 and 1983 income years to the special par unit holders had not been determined in principle in March 1981, or thereabouts, notwithstanding that they coincided in principle with his thinking and desires at that time. We accept without reservation that the actual amounts distributed were not determined in 1981, nor could they be, having regard to the need to determine at the end of each year the amount available for distribution.

35. In relation to the part played by the applicant concerning the discussions and proposals, G's evidence was uncon vincing and at times confusing. In his evidence-in-chief, the applicant made it clear that his contribution in connection with the arrangements to be entered into concerned incorporation, the use of a unit trust and estate planning which, in effect, meant the diversion of income to members of partners' families, particularly wives, to enable them to build up assets. The applicant gave us to understand, based on discussions concerning those matters, that he expected that his suggestions would be accepted, as in fact they were. In cross-examination, the applicant agreed that, following a discussion with G in April 1981, his second family trust was created under the deed stamped on 1st May, 1981. On the other hand, G, in his cross-examination concerning those matters, denied that he had discussed estate planning with the applicant in April or thereabouts and seemed to suggest that such a discussion did not take place until some time later, possibly in June 1981. However, he conceded that the family trusts, including that of the applicant, were brought into being for asset protection and became unit holders in the Unit Trust as already indicated.

36. In the circum stances, and having regard to the evidence that we have already discussed, we find on the balance of probabilities that the solicitor's letters were written in good faith and in accordance with instructions and that they provide further evidence that a corporate structure with family trusts as the holders of units in the Unit Trust had been decided upon as firm policy, probably in the March/April period of 1981, but certainly before 28th May, 1981. For present purposes it is not decisive one way or the other, in our view, in determining whether an arrangement existed prior to 28th May, 1981, that D and Associates Pty. was not incor porated and that the D and Associates Unit Trust was not created until 18th June, 1981, and 22nd June, 1981 respectively. Similarly, it is not important, in our view, that formal approval for the structure was not obtained until receipt of a letter dated 16th October, 1981, from the relevant accountancy body. In concluding this part of our narrative, we would mention that all necessary notifications to government authorities, bankers and such like bodies, concerning D and Associates Pty. and the various trusts, were duly attended to.

40. In respect of the 1982 income year, all of the distributable net income of the D and Associates Unit Trust was distributed to the family trusts as special par unit holders and, in the case of the applicant, all of his family trust's share was distributed to his wife. In respect of the 1983 year, all of the distributable net income of the Unit Trust was distributed to the family trusts and, in the applicant's case, one-half of his family trust's share was distributed to the applicant and one-half to his wife. Therefore, whilst the tax payable by the applicant and his wife was at the same rate of 30% in respect of the two years, it automatically follows that the tax payable by the applicant in respect of the year in issue was less, to the extent of the tax otherwise payable by him on the amount of the income diverted to his wife in that year. It seems to us, therefore, that, in terms of s.260, subject to the authorities to which we will turn later, there was, because of the action taken, an altering of the incidence of income tax or a relieving of the applicant from liability to pay income tax or a defeating, evading, or avoiding by him of a liability imposed by the Act.

This conclusion has not been reached on the basis of what those units might achieve, but rather on the basis of what happened in fact. In concluding our narrative concerning the facts, we would mention that a great deal of evidence was led in relation to the modus operandi of the Unit Trust and the part played by its employees in the derivation of its income. Whilst we acknowledge the importance of those matters to the administrative stability and ongoing financial success of the Unit Trust, and the employees' contribution to the Unit Trust's income, we do not consider that those matters of themselves (subject to the contention discussed later in these reasons in paragraph 70 that the income derived by the erstwhile partners and X could not be regarded as income from personal exertion) materially assist us in relation to the question of whether s.260 has application. On the other hand, those matters enable us, together with the other evidence already examined in some detail, to conclude that the various legal and other documents brought into existence were what they purported to be and that they created legal rights and oblig ations as between the parties concerned. On the basis of the evidence, it appears that the actions of the parties, including the Unit Trust and the various corporate trustees, were properly taken in accordance with those rights and obligations. In the circumstances, we do not consider it to be necessary to consider further the question of whether those documents and actions were in the nature of, or gave rise to, sham transactions. In our opinion, they were not and did not.

43. We turn now to consider whether s.260 applies in the circumstances of this case. At the outset we would make the preliminary point that, in our opinion, there can be little doubt that the provisions of that section may apply after 27th May, 1981, subject to the criteria provided by that section being satisfied, to any contract, agreement or arrangement made or entered into on or before that date, notwithstanding that Part IVA of the Act, and not s.260, applies to any "scheme", as defined, which has been entered into or carried out on or after 28th May, 1981. Section 177D quite clearly indicates that the latter date is the time at which Part IVA begins to operate and, equally clearly, it states that Part IVA does not operate in relation to a scheme that was entered into or carried out before that date. The relevant part of the section reads as follows:

"177D. This Part applies to any scheme that has been or

is entered into after 27 May, 1981, and to any scheme

that has been or is carried out or commenced to be

carried out after that date (other than a scheme that

was entered into on or before that date),

....."

It will be observed that Part IVA cannot have retrospective application to schemes entered into before 28th May, 1981, and that there is nothing in that Part or elsewhere in the Act which precludes the operation of s.260 after that date in circumstances where its provisions have been satisfied in relation to a contract, agreement or an arrangement entered into before that date.

44. We refer to one further point as a preliminary matter. As already indicated, D and Associates Pty. was registered as an unlimited liability company, which means that its members were in effect guarantors of its obligations without any restriction on amount. This personal liability for the debts of a company would usually deter intending members from using such a structure in preference to a company limited by shares in which each member's liability in the event of a winding-up is limited to unpaid amounts on shares held by him. However, in the circum stances of the instant case, it seems possible that advantages in using an unlimited liability company were seen to include a less complicated means of returning capital to the members on a winding-up, and, more importantly perhaps, a satisfying of ethical considerations that, despite the structure used, the former partners and X would be personally responsible for their professional conduct and for debts and any claims or damages for negligence that might be proven. If the latter be the correct position, it seems inconsistent with the thrust of this objective that it could have been put at risk by the diversion of the Unit Trust income to the families of the former partners and X. However, as indicated earlier, this possible risk was removed in 1984 with the transfer of the special par units to the former partners and X.

45. We do not find it practical, because of the huge variety of fact situations, to examine all the decisions of the High Court that have been concerned with s.260. One case, however, possessing similarities to the fact situation now facing us is that of Peate v. F.C. of T. (1959-1962) 12 ATD 507. Very briefly, the salient facts of that case were that the taxpayer was a doctor who had practised in partnership for some years with a number of other doctors. The partnership was dissolved so as to enable the adoption of a plan which would serve several purposes, including the improvement of the doctors' tax position. By a series of concerted steps, including the formation of a company for each doctor and the execution of service agreements between those companies and their principals, and of further agreements between those companies and a new entity, A.E. Westbank Pty. Ltd., a situation was brought about in which the doctors practised no longer in partnership but as employees of their respective family companies, which in turn supplied the doctors' services to the central operating company, Westbank. The latter agreements provided in effect that each doctor's family company would, for a fee, arrange for the doctor to serve Westbank as a medical practitioner and that the patients of the practice would contract with Westbank and that payment for treatment should be due to that company. Dr. Peate's own family company was called W. Raleigh Pty. Ltd. Before examining the various judgments in Peate, it is perhaps appropriate to say that, until the decisions in F.C. of T. v. Gulland, Watson v. F.C. of T. and Pincus v. F.C. of T., 85 ATC 4765 (which decisions will be examined later on in these reasons), it was considered by some legal writers that the correctness of the decision in Peate was debatable and, at best, was limited to its own special facts and therefore operated in a very narrow field only. Certainly, it was seen as being inconsistent with the later decisions of Mullens & Ors. v. F.C. of T., 76 ATC 4288, Slutzkin & Ors. v. F.C. of T., 77 ATC 4076, and Cridland v. F.C. of T., 77 ATC 4538. However, that charge of inconsistency was expressly rebutted by Gibbs C.J., speaking as one of the majority in Gulland (see p.4773), and it would seem that the decision in Peate has been restored as leading authority in the interpretation of s.260 by the Gulland, Watson, Pincus trilogy of cases. Harking back to the Peate case, Menzies J., speaking as the Judge in the court of first instance, had the following to say at p.517:

"In view of recent decisions, it is unnecessary

for me to re-state the meaning and effect of this

section (s.260). Its application depends upon whether

there is here an arrangement having the purpose or

effect of avoiding a liability for tax that would other

wise fall upon Dr. Peate. The facts stated indicate

this purpose and this effect, for despite other reasons

that were advanced by Dr. Peate for so much of what was

done as consisted in putting Westbank in the place of

the partnership, when all that was done is looked at and

in particular when the role of Raleigh is examined,

there is a strong prima facie case that the purpose and

effect of what was done was to obtain increased tax

deductions from assessable income and to divide what

would otherwise have been Dr. Peate's taxable income

between himself, his wife and his children."

46. On Dr. Peate's unsuccessful appeal to the Full High Court against the decision of Menzies J., Kitto J. (whose judgment and reasons were agreed to by McTiernan, Windeyer and Owen JJ.), at pp.348-349 in (1963-1965) 13 ATD 346, had this to say:

"The plan and all that was done under it plainly

constituted an 'arrangement' in the sense of s.260; for

Newton's Case (1958) AC 450, at p.465; [1958] UKPCHCA 1; 11 ATD 442,

at p.445, establishes, as this Court had held in earlier

cases, that the word 'arrangement' in the section

comprehends both a plan made between two or more persons

and all the transactions by which it is carried into

effect. Newton's Case makes clear also that the

question whether an arrangement has or purports to have

the purpose or effect of avoiding a liability to tax

under the Act is a question as to the purposes or

effects of the arrangement itself, rather than of the

purposes in the minds of the parties. That is to say

that it is a question whether, upon consideration of the

overt acts which have been done in carrying out the

plan, the arrangement is to be recognized as a means for

the avoidance of a tax liability, whether or not it be a

means to other ends also.

The arrangement in the present case,

considered objectively as is thus required, may well

seem to be characterized by several purposes and

effects, some of them unconnected with taxation,

including the protection of individual members of the

group against liability for negligence; the making of

superannuation provision for employees, including

doctors employed to assist the group; the better

organization of the group's activities and particularly

its methods of accounting; and the making of provision

for the doctors' families. (All of these purposes,

indeed, the appellant swore were actually contemplated

in the formation of the plan.) But the question

remains, whether the overt acts that were done under the

plan are fairly explicable without an inference being

drawn that tax-avoidance is a purpose of the arrangement

as a whole. Menzies J. thought they were not, and with

respect I agree. The arrangement bears ex facie the

stamp of tax-avoidance. An understandable purpose of

providing for the doctors' families, and doing so quite

honestly, is perfectly evident; but what is equally

evident is a purpose of doing so by a method which will

divert income away from the participating doctors to or

for the benefit of their families, to the end that a

substantial part of the tax might be avoided which would

have been incurred if the income had first been derived

by the doctors and then applied by them for the benefit

of their families."

In his separate judgment, also dismissing the appeal, Taylor J., at p.353, stated:

"As to the appellant's first submission it is,

I think, not open to doubt that the purpose and effect

of the arrangement which is now attacked was to avoid

tax. It is true, no doubt, that it had other ends in

view such as the making of provision for the appellant's

wife and children. But avoidance of tax was the means

to those ends and a diminution in the appellant's tax

was not merely an incident of what might be regarded as

an ordinary family settlement; as I have already

indicated avoidance of tax on income produced by the

professional activities of the eight medical practit

ioners in question was at the very heart of the

arrangement which was about as far removed as possible

from any concept of ordinary business or family dealing.

Further, it possessed no other feature to deny its true

character, that is an arrangement having the purpose or

effect of defeating, evading or avoiding income tax."

47. On appeal, the Judicial Committee, by majority, concluded that the decisions arrived at by the Full High Court and by Menzies J. were correct ((1964-1967) 14 ATD 198). Whilst the dissentient, Lord Donovan, for reasons which need not concern us here, would have allowed the appeal, he nevertheless found, in common with the other ten members of the High Court and Privy Council who heard the matter, that the conditions precedent prescribed in s.260 existed in Dr. Peate's case. At p.207, he commented:

"There is no doubt, in my opinion, that the

conditions precedent prescribed in s.260 exist in

Dr. Peate's case. The whole arrangement was designed to

relieve him of some liability to tax, and it was, in fact

successful in doing so in the first year of its existence.

One need hardly consider therefore the remaining content

of s.260, though I should have thought it could also

rightly be said that the arrangement had the object of

altering the incidence of income tax on what would

ordinarily have been the doctor's professional earnings."

48. In paraphrasing the dicta of Menzies J. in relation to the facts in the instant case, it emerges, in our opinion, that there was an arrangement entered into on or before 27th May, 1981, which had the purpose or effect of avoiding a liability for tax that would otherwise have fallen upon the applicant. The facts found indicate this purpose and this effect, for, despite other reasons that were advanced by and for the applicant for much of what was done as consisted in putting D and Associates Pty. and the D and Associates Unit Trust in the place of the former partnership, when all that was done is looked at, and in particular when the role of that structure is examined, there is a strong prima facie case that the purpose and effect of what was done was to divide what would have been the applicant's income between himself and his family with the aim of avoiding tax. While, in the instant case, it was necessary to make provision for superannuation to meet the requirements of X, it was totally unnecessary for that purpose that the structure used should include a provision for special par units. In any event, it will be recalled that, although it might have been a part of the over all plan decided upon in the period of March/April 1981, the superannuation fund was not established until 25th June, 1981, and then as a separate action, which was after the creation of the corporate and Unit Trust structure. When the role of the Unit Trust structure is examined by itself in relation to the former partnership, it emerges, in our opinion, that, apart from admin istrative adjustments made necessary by the introduction of X into the firm (which to its clients continued to be known as D and Associates), few changes, if any, of a substantial nature concerning personal responsibilities and liabilities eventuated, which could, of themselves, be regarded as available only under the Unit Trust and as indispensible to the continued prosperity of the firm. It would seem that the main administrative changes stemmed from the fact that X took over the direction of the estate planning work from G, which change in its turn led to a re- allocation of work responsibilities as between G and V. Subject to those shifts in responsibilities, the direction of the practice remained unchanged.

49. Having regard to the facts in the instant case in the light of the dicta of Kitto J., it is apparent, in our view, that the arrangements and plans made by the former partners and X in March/April 1981, constituted an "arrangement" in terms of s.260, which, when considered objectively, as is required to be done, may well seem to be characterised by several purposes and effects, some of them unconnected with taxation, including the making of superannuation provision for the former partners and X, and perhaps the better organisation of the firm's activities and its methods of accounting. But the question remains, as pointed out by Kitto J. in Peate's case, whether the overt acts that were done under the plan are fairly explicable without an inference being drawn that tax avoidance was a purpose of the arrangement as a whole? In our opinion they were not. They bear, it seems to us, ex facie, the stamp of tax avoidance. In applying the reasoning of Taylor J., it seems to us that tax avoidance was at the heart of the arrangements entered into, which provided the means through the mechanism of the special par units by which provision would be made for the accumulation of assets in the hands of the applicant's family.

50. However, before attempting an analysis of "the doctor cases", it is well to point to some differences which, on one view, might be regarded as significant between the fact situation which existed in Peate and that of the instant case. It will be recalled that, in Peate, the various doctors became employees of their respective family companies and it was the company, Westbank, that carried on the business of providing medical services. That arrangement caused the primary Judge, Menzies J., to say at pp.517-518:

"It is true that I do regard the incorporation of Raleigh

and the seven other doctors' family companies as

colouring everything that was done here but, even

without this, I would have concluded that it was not an

ordinary business transaction for a body of professional

men who are entitled to sue for fees for medical

services to transfer their practices, their libraries

and their instruments to a company which could not sue

for fees and to become that company's servants in the

conduct of their profession, particularly in the circum-

stances that, to the extent to which patients paid fees

to the company, their expenditure was not deductible

under s.82F. What, outside a profession, might be

regarded as an ordinary business transaction may, within

a profession, have an altogether different appearance."

The above reservations expressed by Menzies J. as to the ethical considerations of the arrangement in Peate do not apply in the instant case. For the arrangement which the applicant and his partners entered into received the approval of the relevant accounting body and all that was done was in conformity with the requirements of that body. Also, whilst Menzies J. in 1962 saw fit to refer to the ingenuity of doctors seeking to circumvent professional controls in order to minimise the incidence of tax, the establishment of unit trusts by accountants (and other professional people) to conduct their practices had become common- place by 1983. Accordingly, what was novel in the case of Dr. Peate was certainly not so as regards the situation of the accountant applicant before us. It is arguable as to the weight which the members of the various courts which heard the matter in Peate gave to the successful circumvention by the eight doctors of the professional controls imposed on them by the appropriate Medical Practitioners' Act, but the matter was doubtless considered relevant, although certainly not conclusive. We simply make the point that those sorts of considerations have no relevance in the instant case.

51. From the majority decisions of the Full High Court in F.C. of T. v. Gulland, Watson v. F.C. of T. and Pincus v. F.C. of T., supra, to which we now turn, it is clear, as previously indicated, that the decision in Peate's Case cannot be regarded as other than good law. In our opinion, therefore, the Commissioner's decision in the instant case must be confirmed having regard to the facts and Peate's Case, unless the principles emerging from the three cases referred to are seen to be in conflict with the provisional conclusions reached by us.

"..... when the decision to dissolve the partnership was

other arrangement. In fact the partnership was formally

dissolved by a 'Deed of Dissolution and Sale', made on

14 August 1978, by which the assets of the partnership

were sold to the two doctors as trustees. There was no

lapse of time between the dissolution of the partnership

and the other arrangements. In any case it would be

wrong to regard the income after the dissolution as

coming from a new source; the source remained the

practice by the taxpayer of his profession at Stafford

Road.

.....

Section 260 renders an arrangement void as

against the Commissioner only in so far as it has or

purports to have one of the purposes or effects

specified in the section. The dissolution of the

partnership, in itself, had no such purpose or effect,

but it formed an integral part of the arrangement to

which sec. 260 applied. The other elements of the

61. Whilst Brennan J. agreed with the conclusions and reasons of Dawson J. in respect of the three cases, he went on to say at p.4778:

"..... The choice principle is inapplicable when the

arrangement does not attract a tax benefit conferred by

a specific provision but is an arrangement which, as in

the present cases, seeks to divert income from one

taxpayer to another."

At p.4779, his Honour continued:

"..... Section 260 does not operate on an arrangement

unless it has a purpose as well as the effect of

avoiding tax..... If it can be predicated of an

arrangement that among the purposes for which it was

made is the purpose of avoiding tax then, provided that

purpose is a substantial, not merely incidental, purpose

of the arrangement, it is an arrangement on which sec.

260 operates..... A purpose of avoiding tax is not

taken to exist when the means adopted to carry the

arrangement into effect are fairly referable to ordinary

business or family dealing and the avoidance of tax is

merely incidental. If a purpose of avoiding tax is

found to exist, the arrangement is caught by sec. 260

even though the advancing of ordinary business or family

interests is one of the purposes and one of the effects

of the arrangement."

At p.4780, his Honour also commented:

"..... When the source of income is ..... a business

carried on by the taxpayer personally, the business

activity in which the taxpayer engages may be in reality

the source of income. And if that be so, it is

immaterial that the taxpayer disposes of the business by

an arrangement which transforms him into an employee of

the disponee. Of course, when the source of income of a

business is the activity of the proprietor and he

remains working in the business after he sells or

otherwise disposes of it, the question whether the

income remains his for the purposes of the Act or

whether it is the income of the disponee depends on

whether the sale or disposition was fairly referable to

ordinary business or family dealing and the avoidance of

tax was merely incidental. If the sale or disposition

of the business is for the purpose, inter alia, of

avoiding tax in the sense explained in Newton, the sale

or disposition is avoided as against the Commissioner

and the taxpayer is taken to continue to receive income

from his business activity."

At p.4781, his Honour concluded:

"The doctors who were conducting the respective practices

with which we are here concerned have continued to

conduct them, though the ownership of each practice has

passed to the trustees of a unit trust..... The

respective assessments to tax were made on the footing

that the gross fees or an appropriate proportion of the

gross fees earned in the respective practices are

assessable income of the respective taxpayers. That was

the correct footing for making the respective

assessments."

62. Dawson J., in his reasons concerning the case of Dr. Gulland, at p.4793, stated:

"There are some observations which can be made

about sec. 260 in a preliminary way. The purpose or

effect of the arrangement must be seen from the terms of

the arrangement itself or from the acts by which it was

implemented. The purpose of the arrangement is not the

same thing as the motivation of the taxpayer to reduce

the income tax for which he would otherwise be liable in

the future.....

.....

It is unnecessary for the purpose or effect of

an arrangement to fall within sec. 260 that it be the

sole or, it would seem, even the dominant purpose. This

flows from the terms of the section itself which is

expressed to operate only so far as the purpose of the

arrangement falls within one or other of the

descriptions which it contains, thus recognizing the

possibility of other purposes to which the section has

no application."

At p.4796, his Honour continued:

"Section 260 is not explicitly directed at

contrived arrangements or arrangements which are out of

the ordinary, but the fact that an arrangement is of

that nature is a circumstance which, when the purpose or

effect of the arrangement is to do any of those things

which are referred to in para. (a) to (d), may indicate

that as a matter of construction the arrangement is one

for the avoidance of tax to which sec. 260 is intended

to apply.....

The whole of the circumstances must be

considered in each case and often there will be special

features which will point to the proper conclusion. For

example, it will be significant in cases such as Peate's

case and the present case that the arrangement in

question does not facilitate the practice of the

profession which it governs.....

It should also be pointed out that the

authorities draw a distinction between an arrangement

which modifies an antecedent transaction or situation

and one which merely orders a taxpayer's affairs in

relation to income from a new source or in relation to a

deduction from which the previous transaction or

situation did not preclude him. In the former case, but

not the latter, sec. 260 may have an application for

there may be an alteration of the incidence of income

tax, etc."

At p.4797, his Honour continued:

"Looking at the arrangement as a whole, it

cannot be doubted that its purpose and effect was to

alter the incidence of income tax and relieve

Dr. Gulland from a liability to pay income tax which

would otherwise have been imposed upon him. There could

be no business or family purpose served by the

arrangement other than to provide Dr. Gulland with

63. In his reasons concerning the case of Dr. Watson, Dawson J., at p.4800, stated:

"There is, in my view, no distinction of any

significance to be drawn between the arrangement in this

application of sec. 260. True it is that in Gulland's

case the taxpayer was engaged in practice as a sole

whereas in this case he was in partnership. That,

64. In his reasons concerning Dr. Pincus, Dawson J., at p.4805, commented:

"..... The form of arrangement under which he has

practised from time to time has not altered the source

medical practitioner whether he has been a sole

..... The legal structure within which an

income may be derived by a taxpayer may vary but the

65. In applying the principles emerging from the dicta cited above to the instant case, it seems to us that, whilst the avoidance of tax was not the "sole purpose" of the arrangements entered into, it was nevertheless one of the main purposes of the arrangements, at least in the sense that it was not "inessential or merely incidental" to the arrangements. As pointed out earlier, it was not necessary for the purposes of providing super annuation to have a unit trust structure, and it was certainly not necessary to have a two-tiered unit structure in the Unit Trust which provided the mechanism in the form of special par units through which income could be diverted. As already explained, the evidence indicates, in our opinion, that that particular structure was part and parcel of the arrangements entered into and was at the very heart of them for the specific purpose of providing the means by which the applicant's income could be split and diverted to his family.

66. We are not in the position to say whether the arrangements were "common or prevalent" and, even if they were, the test indicated is not a relevant consideration for the purposes of s.260. However, the arrangements were not "normal or regular" in the sense of being "ordinary business or family dealing(s)", and they went far beyond what was necessary for superannuation purposes or for other purposes concerned with the conduct of the firm's business. Viewed in 1981 as an arrangement intended to operate in future years, the objective purpose of the structure created was, in our opinion, an attempt to avoid tax. Further, the structure as contemplated in March/April 1981, and put into effect before the end of June that year, had the purpose or effect of altering the incidence of tax in the applicant's hands in the relevant year of income ended 30th June, 1983.

67. As pointed out in the dicta cited, the fact that the partnership ceased to carry on business did not in itself provide the means for the avoidance of tax. However, that happening, at or about the time of the creation of the Unit Trust structure, was a necessary step preparatory to the sale of the goodwill to the Unit Trust and the replacement of the former partnership by the Unit Trust as the recipient of the firm's income which, in turn, enabled the splitting of income for tax avoidance purposes. In the light of the dicta, the actions taken to alter the legal form of the income producing structure and entitlement to income did not, of themselves, mean that the source of the income had changed. As already indicated, the firm's income producing activities (including those of the staff) as a Unit Trust were much the same as they had been when undertaken by the former partnership.

68. While s.260 is not explicitly directed to contrived arrangements or to those out of the ordinary, they are factors to be considered. In the instant case there were what appear to be contrived arrangements or those out of the ordinary which included the creation of new family trusts followed by the creation of D and Associates Pty. and the D and Associates Unit Trust, both of which were under the control of the former partners and X, the cessation of the former partnership and the taking over of its business by the Unit Trust (with the former partners and X as employees), the creation of its corporate trustee as an unlimited liability company, the provision of a two-tiered unit structure in the Unit Trust, which enabled the diversion of income to the family trusts as special par unit holders, and the fact that all of the Unit Trust's net income for the 1982 and 1983 income years was distributed to the family trusts. In making comment on the issue of the special par units, we are mindful that redeemable preference shares were issued in F.C. of T. v. Casuarina Pty. Ltd., 71 ATC 4068, with the particular object of avoiding tax and, notwithstanding that fact, s.260 was ruled to have no application. Accordingly, we recognise that there need not be anything sinister in issuing units which have special rights to income. Neverthe- less, in the role that the issue played in the instant case, we see it not as an issue of units simpliciter but as a necessary part of the overall plan by which the applicant and his fellow principals sought to avoid tax. As indicated earlier, the arrangements entered into did not facilitate in any substantial way the conduct of the firm's business and they were entirely unnecessary for the purposes of providing superannuation for X. In our view, an aura of artificiality pervaded all of the arrangements entered into, which included the loan-back to the Unit Trust of superannuation contributions and distributions made to the family trusts on an unsecured interest-free basis. The arrangements do not, in our opinion, enable the application of the "choice" principle, referred to in a number of cases, but particularly in W.P Keighery Pty. Ltd. v. F.C. of T. [1957] HCA 2; (1958-59) 100 CLR 66 and F.C. of T. v. Casuarina Pty. Ltd. (supra), by which the applicant might be seen to have availed himself, through the arrangements entered into, of provisions in the Assessment Act which might have enabled a reduction in the tax otherwise payable.

69. It would appear that the origins of the "choice" principle might lie in the long-standing authority on s.260 of Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Purcell [1921] HCA 59; (1920-21) 29 CLR 464. (See the dicta of Lee J. in Gregrhon Investments Pty. Ltd. & Ors. v. F.C. of T., 86 ATC 4906 at p 4915.) Mr. Purcell was a grazier in Queensland and was the owner of a number of pastoral holdings and livestock. He executed a declaration of trust in favour of himself, his wife and his daughter. In so doing, he reserved to himself wide powers of management and control. He retained his legal interest in the whole of the property and, in addition, a one-third beneficial interest in it. His wife immediately became entitled to a one-third interest in the corpus and it was further provided that his daughter should eventually become entitled to it absolutely. Despite the right of the settlor to retain possession of the property and to continue to manage it, the Full High Court found that the declaration of trust was valid and binding on the settlor. More importantly for our purposes, the Court found that the trust arrangement was not caught by s.53 of the then Assessment Act (corresponding to the present s.260). At p.473, Gavan Duffy and Starke JJ. commented as follows:

"The section, as the Chief Justice says, does not

prohibit the disposition of property. Its office is to

avoid contracts, etc., which place the incidence of the

tax or the burden of tax upon some person or body other

than the person or body contemplated by the Act. If a

person actually disposed of income-producing property to

another so as to reduce the burden of taxation, the Act

contemplates that the new owner should pay the tax."

Applying that dicta to the instant case, we can say that s.260 could have no application if the arrangement had amounted to nothing more than a sale of the practice and the incidence of tax had thereby been varied. After all, in Keighery, where the "choice" principle operated so as to exclude the operation of s.260, the principal of the taxpayer company candidly admitted in evidence that the substantial object of turning the private company into a public company was to escape Division 7 tax (see citation of case at p.4081 in Casuarina, supra). But the point remains true (see the decision of the Full High Court in Bell v. F.C. of T. [1952] HCA 34; (1952-53) 87 CLR 548, which decision was expressly approved by the Privy Council in Newton's case (supra)) that a conveyance or transfer of property may be caught by s.260 as being part of a wider course of action which may be said to constitute an "arrangement" in the relevant sense of that word, and we consider, for the reasons previously indicated, that such a wider course of action was in fact implemented in the instant case.

70. Much was made by the applicant's representative of the fact that the partners contributed only between 25%-30% of the practice's total fee income, with the bulk of it being derived by the professional staff. From that proposition, it was contended that the income derived by the erstwhile partners in the year of income in issue was not income from personal exertion, and therefore the dicta enunciated by Gibbs C.J. in Gulland (at p.4773), as regards the constraints in treating personal exertion income as though it were derived by a trust, could have no application in the instant case. Based on the observations of the majority of the High Court in F.C. of T. v. Everett, 80 ATC 4076, we would tend to the view that the partners' shares of the practice's income could not properly be regarded as income from personal exertion. However, in the doctor cases, particularly the case of Watson, the High Court (by majority) seemed to be of the view that the income was from personal exertion, even though a partnership was involved. Also, in the case before us, it seems that senior employees or managers had the day-to-day control over operational matters, with the erstwhile partners having more a specialist role in advising on and working on special projects (see p.118 of the transcript). Accordingly, the fee income which they brought into the firm could be said to be derived from the rendering of personal services no differently than was the case with any member of staff. More importantly, however, it should be clearly stated that, whether or not our conclusion is correct, it is no way conclusive of the matter. That kind of fact situation would tend, we think, to support the view taken in Watson (albeit expressed as obiter dicta only) and by Murphy J. in his dissenting judgment in Everett as to the income of the principals being regarded as personal exertion income. However, as an adminis trative body only, we feel that we have no alternative but to follow the more authoritative pronouncement of the High Court made in Everett, and hold that the partners' shares of the business' income were not personal exertion type income. Accordingly, the authorities would suggest that that income was capable of being legally diverted, but that is not to say that s.260 could not operate so as to void the transaction against the Commissioner, if a main purpose associated with the diversion was to avoid tax.

71. From the foregoing comments it will be apparent that, in our opinion, the decisions of the Full High Court in the cases of Drs. Gulland, Watson and Pincus, establish quite clearly that the decision in Peate's case (supra) remains good law and that those decisions are supportive of our provisional conclusion, adverted to earlier, that Peate's case is conclusive as against the applicant. In any event, we are of the opinion that the decisions of the Full High Court in the three "doctor cases" are in themselves decisive as against the applicant's claims and supportive of the conclusion that s.260 voids as against the Commissioner the arrangements entered into, which have already been outlined in some detail.

72. Finally, we would draw attention to several recent cases in which the decisions in the trilogy of "doctor cases" have been considered. These cases are Case T4, 86 ATC 123, Booth v. F.C. of T., 86 ATC 4049, Insomnia (No. 2) Pty. Ltd. v. F.C. of T., Insomnia (No. 3) Pty. Ltd. v. F.C. of T., 86 ATC 4145, and Gregrhon Investments Pty. Ltd. & Ors. v. F.C. of T. (supra). The decisions in those cases were based upon the particular facts in each case which are distinguishable from the facts in the instant case. In our opinion, the reasons for the decision in each of those cases do not add to, nor detract from, the reasons advanced by us for our conclusions in the instant case. In the circumstances, we consider that, for present purposes, nothing would be gained by discussing those cases in any further detail and we refrain from so doing.

73. For the above reasons, we consider that the amount of $52,175 in issue was correctly included in the assessable income of the applicant for the year of income ended 30th June, 1983, pursuant to the provisions of ss.19 and 92, and to the definition of "partnership" in sub-s.6(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

I have had the advantage of reading the findings of fact and reasons in law for the conclusions reached by my colleagues, Mr. K.P. Brady and Mr. J.E. Stewart. However, whilst I am in substantial agreement with those findings of fact and some of their conclusions, I am unable to accept their decision as to the applicability of s.260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act. Notwithstanding that divergence of opinion, I have no hesitation in joining with them in their conclusion that the transactions in question were not shams and that, as between the parties, they had legal effect. Furthermore, I share their view that those arrange ments were in place as at 27th May, 1981, and that the matter in dispute falls for consideration under s.260.

2. The facts of this matter were, to a large extent, analogous to those existing in Reference No. WT85/77 and which was heard at the same time. My reasons for finding in favour of the applicant are similar to those expressed in that other reference and, with that in mind, I shall be brief and to some degree repetitious in the presentation of my decision.

3. In the first instance, I find that the gross fees passing to the partnership did not represent income flowing from the applicant's personal exertion and, in reaching that conclusion, I have had due regard to both the extent and degree of services rendered to clients by all employees of the organisation as well as the substantial element of goodwill existing within the practice. On the authority of the decision of the High Court in F.C. of T. v. Everett, 80 ATC 4076, the relevant share was payable to the applicant because of his interest in the partnership as opposed to the notion that it represented income derived from his personal exertion. To my mind, it represented a stream of income which flowed from a joint source consisting of capital and labour, with the labour component being a mix of services rendered by all employees including the former partners. On this basis alone, I distinguish a variance of some considerable significance as between the factual position now under review and those which existed in the cases of Gulland, Watson and Pincus.

4. Next, I find that the transactions entered into between the relevant parties were made in good faith and that it was their intention to be bound by the conditions attaching thereto. In terms of those arrangements, the goodwill of the practice was to be acquired by the trustee of the unit trust at a cost of $500,000 and there is no doubt that the possession of that asset was fundamental to the derivation of future professional income, which, as a point of some interest, amounted to very nearly $940,000 in the first full year of operation by the unit trust. Before leaving this issue, it should also be recalled that the same figure of $500,000 had been used in the calculations whereby X acquired an interest in the business, and thus it seems reasonable to conclude that such a purchase price had been determined only after the making of a genuine appraisal of all the relevant material. Having regard to all of the evidence, I see no reason to doubt the bona fides of the transaction, and thus I accept that the ownership of the practice was legally transferred to the unit trust on 22nd June, 1981.

5. In my view, the decision of the High Court in favour of the taxpayer in D.F.C. of T. v. Purcell [1921] HCA 59; (1920-1921) 29 CLR 464, is determinative of the current issue, and for that reason it is essential that the factual position which there existed be fully recognised and understood. Purcell, a primary producer and owner of certain pastoral property and livestock pastured thereon, had declared himself a trustee of those assets for himself, his wife and his daughter equally and, in declaring the trust, he reserved to himself wide and unusual powers of management, control and investment. The Commissioner treated the income yielded from the grazing activity undertaken on that property and conducted by Purcell in the same way as before as having been derived by Purcell alone, and in doing so he invoked the provisions then contained in s.53 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1915-1916, and which were the equivalent of s.260. It seems to me that the facts of Purcell are not dissimilar to those now in focus, and I find the following common factors of compelling significance -

. The stream of income flowing from the activities

had as their source a pool consisting of a

combination of income producing assets and the

provision of labour by the former owners and

others.

. The presence of those income producing assets

was as vital to the derivation of income as was

the contribution of labour.

. The former owners continued to be involved in

the management and control of the respective

enterprises.

A careful examination of the Purcell decision (see particularly comments of Knox C.J. at p.466 and those of Gavan Duffy and Starke JJ. at p.473) leads me to the conclusion that s.260 has no application where income producing assets are transferred in terms of a bona fide arrangement and that that situation prevails not withstanding a continuation of the same management and control as before. Furthermore, the existence of a purpose to reduce the burden of taxation does not alter that position. However, as demonstrated by the High Court in its majority decision in the cases of the three doctors, not all arrangements involving a transfer of assets are protected from the force of s.260. In this regard, it seems to me that a finding that the income was and continues to be generated from the personal exertion of the former principals takes the matter beyond that contemplated in Purcell. But that is not the case here, and thus I am of the view that the limitation adverted to has no application.

6. In summary, I am not able to perceive any justification for the conclusion that the disposition was not genuine, nor am I able to discern any differences of particular significance as between the facts of this reference and Purcell, other than in Purcell there existed an acknowledgment of a purpose to reduce the burden of taxation. Accordingly, I find that the income earned throughout the year under review was derived by the unit trust and that the incidence and burden of tax falls precisely as the Act intended, namely upon the new owners. Whilst it is appreciated that there exists dicta which indicates that certain transfers might still be caught by s.260 (see Bell v. F.C. of T. [1952] HCA 34; (1952-53) 87 CLR 548 and Hancock v. F.C. of T. [1961] HCA 90; (1962-63) 108 CLR 258), I see little semblance of relationship as between the facts there under consideration and those of the present reference. Finally, I would add that the decision in Purcell was referred to with approval by the Privy Council in Newton v. F.C. of T. [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1957-58) 98 CLR 1, and more recently by Gibbs C.J., Brennan and Dawson JJ. in the cases of Gulland, Watson and Pincus.

7. In the event that I have misconstrued the breadth of application afforded by Purcell, it seems appropriate that the facts of this matter be examined against the predication test which takes its origin from the following comments made by Lord Denning in Newton's case (supra):

"In order to bring the arrangement within the section

you must be able to predicate - by looking at the overt

acts by which it was implemented - that it was

implemented in that particular way so as to avoid tax.

If you cannot so predicate, but have to acknowledge that

the transactions are capable of explanation by reference

to ordinary business or family dealing, without

necessarily being labelled as a means to avoid tax, then

the arrangement does not come within the section."

8. I now turn to a consideration of those aspects of the arrangement which, in my opinion, are relevant to a determination as to whether it is possible to so predicate or conversely whether the transactions are capable of explanation by reference to ordinary business or family dealing.

9. In the first place, I accept that the restructuring of the ownership was motivated primarily by the need to satisfy the demand of X that superannuation benefits be provided in his new position and that the change in attitude of the Australian Society of Accountants to permit a corporate body to conduct a practice of public accounting created the means for achieving such a result. Furthermore, I acknowledge that all of the former partners were attracted to the prospect of being provided with like benefits and that such a factor was an essential ingredient in the decision to transfer the ownership of the practice to the unit trust. In my opinion, the quest for superannuation benefits for X and the former partners represented the main purpose of the re-arrangement. Having arrived at this stage, it should be recognised that the establishment and operation of a super annuation fund does not attract the application of s.260, notwithstanding the opportunity it offers to deduct contributions made from assessable income (see judgment of Dawson J. in Gulland). Before leaving this topic, I admit to some preliminary doubts as to the real nature of the fund, i.e. whether the true object was the provision of death and retirement benefits or was it no more than a device to maximise income tax deductions. Having regard to the investment policy ultimately adopted by the trustee, and the admission of several other employees, I am persuaded that the intention was the provision of benefits to retiring and deceased members.

10. Secondly, I am prepared to accept the applicant's evidence that asset protection was another factor at the core of his decision to move away from the partnership structure. Such an acceptance is facilitated by the abundance of caution previously demonstrated by him in the transfer of private assets, including his home, to the first of the two family trusts. It seems to me that the possibility of a claim for professional negligence is always present for people engaged in this type of activity, and I am well able to understand the applicant's wish to curtail further asset growth in his own right.

11. Thirdly, it would appear from various judgments handed down by the courts over the years that the presence of artificial, complex or contrived elements in an arrangement may infer a purpose of tax avoidance and to which s.260 is intended to apply. It seems to me that those adjectives must be viewed in a relative context and that one should have regard to the extent and degree of those elements if, in fact, they are found to exist. The pertinent facts of this issue are that four accountants, previously carrying on business as a partnership, sold their practice to a nominee company which acted as trustee for a unit trust; that trust employed and provided them with superannuation benefits and any profits remaining were to be shared equally between their and X's family trusts. I do not perceive such an arrangement to be either artificial, complex or contrived to the extent that those elements prevailed in the cases of Peate, Gulland, Watson and Pincus.

12. Fourthly, reference is made to the fact that the whole of the arrangement was undertaken in accordance with the by- laws and approval of the Australian Society of Accountants as this is, in my opinion, a matter of some significance in assessing whether the series of transactions might be explicable as ordinary business or family dealings. In considering situations purporting to vest an activity in a person or body which is prevented by professional or statutory controls from pursuing that activity, the courts have clearly indicated that that kind of breach stands in direct conflict with arrangements of a "normal or regular nature", and that accordingly it might be assumed that the real purpose was tax avoidance (see Hollyock v. F.C. of T., 71 ATC 4202 and Millard v. F.C. of T. [1962] HCA 28; (1962-63) 108 CLR 336). Conversely, it seems to me that the express provision that members of the Society of Accountants might conduct their practices through a trust adds considerable weight to the view that within the accountancy profession the arrangement had the character of being an ordinary business dealing. It is notable that such a feature was not present in the case of Peate.

13. The final matter requiring comment is the use of the applicant's family trust to hold units in the unit trust and, more particularly, whether, because of that involvement and thus the opportunity for splitting income, it should be inferred that the main, or one of the main, purposes of the whole arrangement was the avoidance of tax. In deciding this issue, it is necessary to have regard to the commercial environment which prevailed in the late seventies and early eighties, and especially the types of business structures then coming into operation. From my memory of that environment, I believe it is fair to say that the utilisation of a variety of trusts was extremely popular with professional people in the conduct of their affairs. Furthermore, I would suggest that it would have been a rarity to find a unit trust of the non-public variety with individuals acting in their own right as beneficiaries. In my experience, it was "common, prevalent, normal and regular" to find the units in a trust held by a trustee of a discretionary trust. Notwithstanding those comments, it is clear that some of the majority of the High Court in Gulland, Watson and Pincus were of the view that the creation of a unit trust to conduct a professional practice and act as employer, with the units in the trust being issued to family trusts of erstwhile principals now employees in the practice could, in a given situation, amount to an arrangement to avoid tax and so be caught by s.260. In my understanding of those cases, the Judges so minded only reached that conclusion after a full appraisal of all of the circumstances in the three situations before them, and it would be incorrect to conclude that the implementation of trusts to conduct businesses, even if they were to follow the same model as appertained in the above "doctor cases", would inevitably attract the operation of s.260. As Dawson J. stated in Gulland (see p.4796): "the whole of the circumstances must be considered in each case and often there will be special features which will point to the proper conclusion".

14. Of itself, the issue of units to the applicant's family trust does not, in my opinion, have the effect of stamping the arrangement as one of tax avoidance, and in forming that view I have had due regard to the particular class of unit so issued and to the fact that, whilst such a holding carried an entitlement to share in the distribution of income, it carried no right to vote and only a restricted right to participate in distributions upon the termination of the trust. To my mind, the existence of those kinds of conditions are commonplace (e.g. the issue of most preference shares are subject to those same sorts of restrictions) and were designed to ensure that managerial control remained with those persons possessing the ability and training to successfully conduct the business. I see them in the same light as any other tool of management, and thus I am not prepared to draw any adverse inference from such a differential in entitlement.

15. Taken as a whole, I am unable to predicate - by looking at the overt acts by which the arrangement was implemented - that it was implemented in that particular way so as to avoid tax. Rather, it seems to me that the transactions are capable of explanation by reference to ordinary business or family dealing, without necessarily being labelled as a means to avoid tax. With those views in mind, my conclusion is that the arrangement entered into in June 1981 did not have a main purpose of tax avoidance and, on that basis, s.260 has no application.

16. For the reasons stated, I allow the applicant's objection in respect of the year ended 30th June, 1983.
