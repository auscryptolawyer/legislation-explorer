Re Taxation Appeals [1992] AATA 104; (1992) 23 ATR 1280 (26 March 1992)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. V T91/197-199

AAT No. 8231

Income Tax

[1992] AATA 104; (1992) 23 ATR 1280

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

Dr P Gerber(1) (Deputy President), Mr W McLean(1) (Member) and Mr B Pascoe(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Assessable income - Asset betterment assessments issued to taxpayer - Whether betterment income attributable to gambling wins - Onus of proof - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1986, secs 167, 190(b).

Evans v FC of T 89 ATC 4540

Graham v Green (1925) 2 KB 37

HEARING

MELBOURNE

26:3:1992

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decisions on the objections in each of the three years under review.

DECISION

The main issue in this case turns on the question whether the taxpayer won, by way of gambling, the amounts set out below in the years ended 30 June 1986, 1987 and 1988 respectively, the increase in his assets during the years in question being arrived at by the Commissioner as the result of an audit. No attack on quantum was made, the case being fought on an "all or nothing basis". The assessments under review were prepared on the asset betterment method which showed an understatement of income as follows:

Year ended 30 June 1986 $477,928

Year ended 30 June 1987 $119,245

Year ended 30 June 1988 $ 37,203

2. Prior to 1976, the taxpayer worked on the dockyards. Thereafter, it seems, he was not engaged in any form of remunerative employment apart from husbanding his investments; his time and energy being spent, according to him, in cultivating sources from which he could derive valuable gambling information denied to other punters, thus enabling him to make successful bets on horses and dogs. To obtain this valuable information, the taxpayer would, for example, take jockeys on fishing trips in exchange for tips. It is also alleged that the taxpayer was a successful gambler at two-up, card games and backing snooker players "for wages", a quaint term for what, in all the circumstances, can be said to constitute "small change" from his other purported betting activities.

3. Since the taxpayer was unable to say whether his purported betting activities - other than on horses and dogs - resulted overall in any appreciable gain, we propose to limit our enquiry to those betting activities which were alleged to have led to the substantial gains, the subject of the betterment statement.

4. In the immediate years following the taxpayer's retirement from gainful employment, he volunteered only one substantial bet on horses, a $5000 each way bet at 20/1 on a horse called Always Welcome, which returned him some $125,000 in 1987. It was put in his mouth by counsel that he may have wagered money on two other horses, both owned by a man called Donald Cox - Recoil and Cast A Fly - and, his memory thus jolted, the taxpayer recalled he had won money on those horses. However, since Cast a Fly won at Bairnsdale in 1987 and Recoil had two wins, the first at a Maiden Handicap at Wangaratta in 1984, and the second at Mt Wycheproof in 1986, the taxpayer readily conceded that these wins could not have been significant in the overall wins said to have been achieved in these years. In addition, several photos of winning dogs and horses were made exhibits. The dogs were mainly owned either solely or jointly by Cox. No specific bets on any dogs were adduced in evidence. We have not been persuaded that these photographs have any evidentiary value.

5. The taxpayer deposed that in or around 1985, his friend Cox took over another friend's SP bookmaking business, save that Cox henceforth merely acted as a conduit, "laying off" all bets that were telephoned through to him. It seems that this afforded the taxpayer a further opportunity to "get the good mail", and, as he put it, I jumped in".

6. We must say at the outset that the taxpayer's attempt to explain how this mere sitting in on Cox's telephone activities enabled him to reap a fortune from other SP bookmakers lacked conviction, and it was only when Cox gave evidence that it transpired that persons engaged in the racing industry, such as strappers, would, on occasion, ring through information which would enable the cognoscenti to conclude that a particular horse was likely to run faster than the rest.

7. The taxpayer "sat in" with Cox until some time in 1987. Cox gave the SP business up in 1988. Significantly, the taxpayer did not tell us of one single bet that he placed through Cox throughout this period, surely a surprising omission in one who claims to have made a fortune from gambling on horses. Indeed, the taxpayer's evidence of his betting activity was so vague that it can barely be said to have qualified as "evidence". The best he could come up with, apart from the bets aforementioned, was a vague reference to some winning bets on two horses - Strawberry Road and Manikado. However, the taxpayer was unable to inform the Tribunal when these bets were placed, the amounts wagered or the end result. In the circumstances, we are confident that no reliance can be placed on his testimony. Our view of the unreliability of his evidence is strengthened when that evidence is placed against a letter signed by him on 3 August 1989, a letter, exh "B", drafted for him on his instructions by professionals who must be assumed to have been fully cognizant of the effect of the admissions contained therein, the more so since the letter was penned as a plea in mitigation with respect to an audit which was then threatened and pending. This letter reads as follows:

3rd August, 1989

Deputy Commissioner of Taxation

350 Collins Street

MELBOURNE VIC 3000

Dear Sir,

Re: Years of Income 1 July, 1984 - 30 June, 1988

I refer to the abovementioned years of income and the

income tax returns lodged in respect thereto.

I now believe that my income for the above years may

have been substantially understated and wish to make a

full and true disclosure of all relevant circumstances

of those years. In doing so I wish to co-operate fully

with you in the correct ascertainment of my taxable

income for the abovementioned years so as to facilitate

a correct assessment for those years.

The circumstances I wish to inform you of are as

detailed below:

1. I am an investor by profession or trade, but have

gambled in one form or another since approximately

1976. For approximately six years between 1976 and

1983 I gambled extensively on horse racing and "two-up".

2. By 1983 I had accumulated approximately $100,000.00

in savings and decided to extend my activities into

"Starting Price" Bookmaking in about September, 1985. I

ceased "Starting Price" Bookmaking in about October

1986 and no longer engage in that activity. My tax

agent was not advised of the existence of my earnings

from the above bookmaking activity and was not

instructed to include them in my annual returns, as I

believed that, as "gambling wins" and possibly illegal

activities, they were non-assessable. I have now

received advice that these winnings should have been

included for those years. Although I specifically

maintain that they are not assessable I am disclosing

the details of these earnings in full with a view to

co-operating with you to correctly assess my income

over those years.

3. Attached hereto are all the relevant details

concerning my winnings from the years mentioned as far

as I can recall. No written records have been retained

by me for this period except for the investments made

as detailed hereto. Also attached hereto are copies of

the Statement of Assets as at 30 June, 1988 and 27

July, 1989 for myself and my investment company,

Reynold Hall Pty. Ltd.

4. To the best of my knowledge the investments detailed

hereto account for the sum total of my winnings from

"Starting Price" Bookmaking during the years mentioned.

I submit that at no time during the years mentioned did

I intend to make any false or misleading statement to

you. However, in the event that my statements were

false and/or misleading, to the extent that they were

false and/or misleading, such was as a result of an

honest mistake on my part acting alone in

misunderstanding my obligations. I made no attempt to

deliberately evade any taxation liability and was not

indifferent to the consequences of my omissions. From

a considered, albeit possible erroneous stance, I made

the decision not to include in my assessable income for

the years of income 1983-1984 to 1987-1988 those

earnings I received from my activities in "Starting

Price" Bookmaking.

I offer the above information and my co-operation in

this matter freely and submit that no penalty should be

imposed.

8. Attached to the letter is a schedule setting out the whole financial history of the taxpayer from the time he finished his employment at the dockyards to the last transaction on 25 May 1989, and concluding with the statement that total cash injected from winnings from 1986 amounted to $485,206.40, an amount which, of course, is significantly less than the amount arrived at by the Commissioner in his Betterment Statement.

9. Turning to the letter, some time in July 1989, Mr Marinucci, of the firm of Cadwallader, Marinucci, Veal and Co, chartered accountants, was advised that his client, this taxpayer, was about to be subjected to an audit. Mr Marinucci, who had looked after this taxpayer's affairs for some years, was sufficiently concerned about the threat of such an audit to seek urgent advice from a tax lawyer, Mr Kovess, of the Barker Gosling Group. The reason for Mr Marinucci's concern is not far to seek. In 1986, his firm had accepted, at the very least, some $98,000 from the taxpayer for investment, sums presumably received in cash (no bank records were tendered). Mr Marinucci made an occasional and desultory enquiry as to the source of these funds, but added: "the less you know the better".

10. It seems that a conference took place between Messrs Kovess, Marinucci and the taxpayer at Mr Kovess' home in later July 1989 during which Mr Kovess, after being told that the substantial gains, none of which had been disclosed in the returns, were alleged to be the proceeds of racing wins, is said to have made some such reply as: "They won't believe that". From here on, just how the letter was cobbled together becomes obscure. We know that there were some conferences between Marinucci and Kovess, between Kovess and the taxpayer, and between Marinucci and the taxpayer.

11. A Mr Florian Greene, then a first-year solicitor employed by Barker Gosling, was called to give evidence. He deposed that he was the author of the letter, although he claimed that his main concern was to prepare the schedule. The witness was vague as to how the admissions came about that the taxpayer's income "may have been substantially understated", and that the taxpayer had engaged in SP bookmaking between about September 1985 and October 1986, the very period during which the Commissioner asserts that his assets increased by some $500,000. What the witness clearly recalls is that the draft letter was "faxed" at the very time the audit was scheduled to begin, and contained the remark on the front page: "Letter and schedule of acquisitions herein for your information and examination. Sign both letter and schedule where indicated".

12. It goes without saying that this letter calls for a cogent explanation. To the extent that it was sought to suggest on behalf of the applicant that SP bookmaking during the period referred to in the letter was an invention of Mr Kovess, designed to evidence a willingness to co-operate fully with the investigators "in the correct ascertainment of my taxable income for the abovementioned years so as to facilitate a correct assessment for those years", we reject that explanation without hesitation. Nor can we accept the evidence of Mr Marinucci that he did not read the letter before asking his client to sign it. For good measure, the taxpayer likewise claims that he signed the letter without reading it. The letter was received on 3 August 1989, at the very moment the taxpayer, Mr Marinucci and two investigators from the tax Office were all assembled in Mr Marinucci's office, apparently awaiting the arrival of this letter before commencing their interviews.

13. Against the whole of the background giving rise to the composition of this letter, we find that it is highly improbable that Barker Gosling would permit a first-year solicitor to write a letter on his own initiative, containing such damning admissions, without ensuring that its contents corresponded with that firm's instructions, let alone sending it without Mr Kovess' approval. It is also improbable that Mr Marinucci, who was sufficiently concerned about his client's affairs to consult Mr Kovess, would permit his client to sign a letter from Mr Kovess (or Mr Greene) without reading it, the more so since it was headed "For your information and examination". Finally it is improbable that the taxpayer would sign a letter which had not been read by his accountant.

14. Multiplying all these improbabilities, the odds that these events occurred in the way as alleged in evidence are about as high as the odds that this taxpayer won nearly half a million dollars as deposed.

15. We now briefly turn to the evidence of the supporting witnesses.

16. Mr Marinucci was unable to assist the Tribunal as to the source of the increase in the taxpayer's assets, his sole knowledge being derived from statements made by the taxpayer. However, he was to add significantly: "the less you know the better". To the extent that the witness sought to recall the events leading up to the letter of 3 August 1989, we take into account that these events occurred nearly five years ago, so that it is not surprising that such minor details as to whether or not a particular letter was read before signing become somewhat blurred.

17. Mr Cox, the friend who owned dogs and recalled the taxpayer's bet on Always Welcome, was vague as to other bets, allegedly made through him, on any other horses or dogs. He deposed that the taxpayer sat in with him for part of the time he was engaged as the conduit in the SP operations. In response to a Member of the Tribunal who asked him whether, overall, he was able to say whether the taxpayer won more than he lost, he replied that he thought that the taxpayer won some $200,000 or so between 1986 and 1988, but conceded that this amount was no more than an intelligent guess. In any event, this amount falls far short of the amount of assets claimed to have been won by the taxpayer during that period.

18. We have already dealt with the evidence of Mr Greene. We are satisfied that he was a truthful witness. We accept that his role was to prepare the schedule attached to the letter of 3 August 1989, and that he spent a considerable amount of time compiling same. We do not believe, on balance, that he played any, or any significant, role in composing the letter. Just how the admission that:

from a considered, albeit possible erroneous stance,

I made the decision not to include in my 1983-1984

to 1987-1988 those earnings I received from

activities in 'Starting Price' Bookmaking

came about will never be known. It may be that Mr Kovess could have shed some light on these events. Alas, he was not called.

19. Turning to the evidence of the taxpayer. We found him to be evasive and showing little concern whether his answers bore any resemblance to the events as they occurred. One incident will suffice. He claimed that in 1986, some farming equipment was acquired and paid for by his two sons with the proceeds of a compensation claim, an amount he put at $12,000. It subsequently transpired that the compensation payment was not received until March 1989, the amount being $11,115.60. In passing, we feel bound to point out that the taxpayer, far from being the simple, uneducated person with a poor memory for detail, as claimed for him by his counsel, struck us as a shrewd investor, with sophisticated investments, such as a Cessna aircraft, a Rolls Royce lease, various real estate properties, a flower farm and a marina mooring. For good measure, the bulk of investments were made through a family trust with a corporate trustee or an investment company. In short, we reject the taxpayer's evidence that the various accretions to his assets were derived from betting wins as a punter. The only part of his evidence we are prepared to accept is that he won some $125,000 on Always Welcome in 1977, i.e. some nine years before the years now under review, albeit he may well have won some other, smaller amounts on Cox's horses and dogs, and on Strawberry Road and Manikado, how much we are unable to say. What became of the proceeds from any of these wins would be a mere guess on the evidence. In the letter of 3 August 1989, it is said that the applicant "had accumulated approximately $100,000". Before us, the taxpayer alleged that this amount was nearer $250,000. Given the total unreliability of his evidence, we are unable to say how much - if any - cash the applicant had in 1985, the first year the Commissioner investigated. It is, of course, trite to point out that a taxpayer who alleges that increases in his assets are attributable to wins at the races, must identify what those wins were in circumstances where the totality of his evidence is disbelieved.

20. Having rejected the taxpayer's evidence of betting wins as a punter as the source of the amounts set out in the asset betterment statement, we are left with the letter of 3 August 1989. That letter, drawn or settled by the taxpayer's solicitor and handed to him for signature by his accountant, contains the following admissions:

(ii) I now believe that my income for the above

years may have been substantially understated;

(ii) By 1983, I had accumulated approximately

$100,000 in savings and decided to extend my activities

into "Starting Price" Bookmaking in about September

1985. I ceased "Starting Price" Bookmaking in about

October 1986. ... For a considerable, albeit possible

erroneous stance, I made the decision not to include in

my assessable income for the years 1983-1988 those

earnings I received from my activities in "Starting

Price" Bookmaking.

21. The admissions are clear and unambiguous. What "explanation" for the admissions were advanced have not persuaded us.

22. The Tribunal is thus presented with two alternative interpretations of the letter; one the implausible one offered by the applicant, the other the natural meaning which the words present. In these circumstances, and in particular having regard to the source of the document, and the fact that it is the applicant himself who put it forward, we think that the rule contra proferentem, albeit taken from a different context, should be applied.

23. The result is that the disputed accretions have, as the most likely source, the business of "Starting Price" Bookmaking. It was not suggested that such an activity is not a business, and that the proceeds are not income under ordinary concepts.

24. Mr Searle, of learned counsel for the applicant, has said all that could be said. But not even Mr Searle can make bricks without straw. Having rejected the taxpayer's evidence in toto, it is no discourtesy to learned counsel that we refrain from dealing with the numerous decisions on which he relied, cases dealing predominantly with the self-evident proposition that gambling simpliciter does not involve the carrying on of a business; cf Evans v FC of T 89 ATC 4540. On the other hand:

It has been settled that a bookmaker carries on a

taxable vocation. What is the bookmaker's system? He

knows that there are a great many people who are

willing to back horses, and that they will back horses

with anybody who holds himself out to give reasonable

odds as a bookmaker. By calculating the odds in the

case of various horses over a long period of time and

quoting them so that on the whole the aggregate odds,

if I may use that expression, are in his favour, he

makes a profit. That seems to me to be organizing an

effort in the same way that a person organizes an

effort if he sets out to buy himself things with a view

to securing a profit by the difference in their capital

value in individual cases. (per Rowlatt J.; Graham v

Green (1925) 2 KB 37, at 41-2).

25. That statement of law has stood the test of time; we can see no good reason for not applying it to the activities of this taxpayer in the years now under review.

26. It follows that, having rejected the evidence of the taxpayer that the increase in his assets came from betting on horses and other gambling activities, coupled with the circumstances of the positive statements made in writing at the commencement of the relevant audit, he has in no way discharged the onus of proof which he bears. It is therefore again, with no discourtesy to counsel, that we refrain from dealing with the numerous and illuminating cases he relied on dealing with onus of proof where betting wins are put forward as the explanation for accretions to assets.

27. On the issue of penalties, the Commissioner has, apart from the standard interest component, added a further 40% described as "culpability". Given the fact that the taxpayer has chosen deliberately to conceal his taxable activities in his returns, and persisted in denying them before this Tribunal, we can see no good reason for reducing the penalties imposed year by year. We therefore affirm the decisions on the objections in each of the three years under review.
