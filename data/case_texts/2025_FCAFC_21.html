  1. The appeal be dismissed with costs.

REASONS FOR JUDGMENT

LOGAN J:

  1. On 4 June 2015, the Supreme Court of Victoria, acting under the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth) and its inherent jurisdiction, approved three schemes of arrangement in respect of a group of companies then known as AusNet Services. This group consisted of AusNet Services (Transmission) Limited (Transmission), AusNet Services (RE) Ltd as trustee of the AusNet Services Finance Trust (Finance) and AusNet Services (Distribution) Limited (Distribution). Each unit in Finance was linked (or “stapled”) to a share in Transmission and a share in Distribution. The effect of this linkage was that, before the implementation of the approved schemes of arrangement, none of the shares or units could be transferred or otherwise dealt with without the others. These stapled securities had been listed on the Australian and Singapore stock exchanges.
  2. In accordance with the orders of the Supreme Court of Victoria, these schemes of arrangement were implemented in succession on 18 June 2015. By the end of that day, the appellant, AusNet Services Ltd, hitherto a shelf company, had acquired all the shares in Transmission, all the units in Finance and all the shares in Distribution. The former holders of the stapled securities became shareholders of the appellant. One feature of the implementation of the schemes of arrangement was that Distribution became a subsidiary of the appellant.
  3. As Kennett J observes in his judgment, which I have had the privilege of reading in draft, an agreed statement of facts (referred to as the JSAF) was tendered in the original jurisdiction to which was annexed a bundle of documents.
  4. Both for the purposes of the trial of the taxation appeal and this appeal, the facts so agreed had all the characteristics for which s 191 of the Evidence Act 1995 (Cth) (Evidence Act) provides. They were not required to be proved by evidence, were not to be disputed and could not be the subject in the proceedings of contradictory evidence. The effect of the section was that it was not open to the Commissioner to advance a factual submission contrary to a fact to which he had agreed: Mornington Inn Pty Ltd v Jordan [2008] FCAFC 70; (2008) 168 FCR 383, at [84], per Stone and Buchanan JJ.
  5. Section 191 of the Evidence Act is a “modern extension” of the facility which has long existed for a party to narrow the factual issues in a proceeding by the making of an admission on the pleadings or otherwise formally at trial: Damberg v Damberg [2001] NSWCA 87; (2001) 52 NSWLR 492 (Damberg v Damberg), at 519 per Heydon JA. For reasons which I recently canvassed in Cheung v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2024] FCA 1370, at [25]-[26], it is desirable in taxation litigation, perhaps even more so than in other civil litigation, to confine issues of fact and law. That in a taxation appeal the onus of proving an assessment to be excessive lies on a taxpayer (s 14ZZO, Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth)), does not prevent this. If the parties do so confine the issues, it is important in the administration of justice that the judicial branch decide cases accordingly, save where that might be subversive of such administration and then only after affording the parties an opportunity to be heard.
  6. Such subversion might be found in circumstances where an admission made in conformity with s 191 of the Evidence Act is inconsistent with other evidence led at trial. A court might not, subject to observing procedural fairness as to an apprehended inconsistency, act on that agreed fact: Damberg v Damberg, at 522. That is not this case. Instead, given the agreed facts, it was not open to the primary judge to doubt them: Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Star City Pty Ltd [2009] FCAFC 19; (2009) 175 FCR 39, at [95] per Goldberg J (with whose reasons Dowsett and Jessup JJ generally agreed).
  7. Further, it seems to me necessarily to follow from the prohibition of contradictory evidence that a trial judge is prohibited by s 191 from drawing inferences contrary to an agreed fact. Yet this is what the primary judge did by failing to act on the values agreed in the JSAF. That could, as the appellants submitted, be characterised as a denial of procedural fairness but I prefer to characterise it, as the appellant also put it, as a violation of s 191 of the Evidence Act.
  8. The learned primary judge offered a detailed summary of most but not all of the facts so agreed in her Honour’s reasons for judgment (at [9] – [36]). I do not repeat that summary. It does not follow that I accept that all the findings of fact made by her Honour were permissible, in light of the way the case was conducted before her by the parties, especially including the tender of the JSAF. Like Kennett J, I refer to the facts as so summarised and other agreed facts only to the extent necessary to explain my reasons for judgment. I likewise gratefully adopt, without repeating, save for explanatory purposes, the extracts of pertinent statutory provisions and summaries of submissions of the parties and related grounds of appeal, as set out in his Honour’s judgment.
  9. The primary judge termed the AusNet Services group of companies “the Distribution group” in her reasons for judgment. Her Honour observed (at [79]) of that group that, “As a matter of economic substance, from the time the stapling arrangements were put in place, the business of the Distribution group had been managed and operated as though it were part of a single economic entity that included Transmission and Finance.” Her Honour also observed (at [82]) of the sequel to the implementation of the schemes of arrangement that, in relation to Distribution, the effect was “to align its legal structure with its economic structure. Economically it had been operated as though it were part of a wider group involving Transmission and Finance. Post the Distribution scheme, Distribution was legally owned within the same corporate group with a single parent company.”
  10. It is indisputably correct that, prior to 18 June 2015, in economic substance, there existed a single entity, as described by the primary judge, and that the end result of the implementation of the schemes of arrangement on 18 June 2015 was an economically equivalent, single economic entity, although the legal structure was changed.
  11. A perception of Transmission, Finance and Distribution as a single entity is, to an extent, reinforced by Pt 3-90 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) (ITAA 1997) pursuant to the provisions of which treated them as a tax consolidated group(s) (TCG) of which Transmission was a head company of one (the Transmission TCG) and Distribution the other (the Distribution TCG). The changes effected by the implementation of the schemes of arrangement still yielded a TCG, albeit one of which thereafter, Distribution became a subsidiary member, with the appellant as the head company.
  12. The question in the present appeal is whether, as the primary judge found, Div 615 of the ITAA 1997 applies to the arrangements by which the appellant came to be the holder of the shares in Distribution, with the consequence that it is taken to have made a valid rollover election under that Division and thus is not entitled to an increase in the cost bases of the assets of the former Distribution TCG? As the primary judge correctly observed (at [5]), “An uplift in those cost bases would have the effect of, amongst other things, increasing the capital allowance deductions that could be claimed by the [appellant]”.
  13. However, with all respect to the learned primary judge, that question is not to be answered by conceptions of economic substance or equivalence but rather by reference to the text of Div 615 in the agreed events which transpired concerning the legal entities affected by the implementation of the schemes of arrangement as approved by the Supreme Court of Victoria. The answer depends on what was done in fact and in law, not economic substance or equivalence: Inland Revenue Commissioner (NZ) v Europa Oil (NZ) Ltd [1971] AC 760 (Europa Oil), at 771 per Lord Wilberforce, Lord Pearson and Sir Frank Kitto; Europa Oil (NZ) Ltd v Inland Revenue Commissioner (NZ) (No 2) [1976] 1 WLR 464 (Europa Oil No 2), at 471-472 per Viscount Dilhorne, Lord Diplock, Lord Edmund-Davies and Sir Garfield Barwick (Europa Oil No 2); Mullens Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1976] HCA 47; (1976) 135 CLR 290 (Mullens), at 301 per Barwick CJ.
  14. More recently, in WorkPac v Rossato (2021) 271 CLR 456, at [62], Kiefel CJ, Keane, Gordon, Edelman, Steward and Gleeson JJ observed:
    1. To insist upon binding contractual promises as reliable indicators of the true character of the employment relationship is to recognise that it is the function of the courts to enforce legal obligations, not to act as an industrial arbiter whose function is to synthesise a new concord out of industrial differences. That it is no part of the judicial function to reshape or recast a contractual relationship in order to reflect a quasi-legislative judgment as to the just settlement of an industrial dispute has been emphatically the case in Australia at the federal level since the Boilermakers Case.
[Footnote references omitted]
These observations are by no means confined in their relevance to industrial cases. In the absence of some charter flowing from the text of a taxation statute, it is likewise no part of the judicial function on a taxation appeal (or in assessing the Commissioner’s) to reshape or recast a contractual relationship in order to reflect a quasi-legislative judgment as to the just taxation of a particular corporate or natural person or a trust which substitutes what is perceived to be the substance of a transaction over its contractual form.
  1. Of course, even in the absence of an anti-avoidance provision, there are taxation cases where, viewed against the context of wider taxable facts, the tax consequence of a contractual transaction may be different from viewing that transaction in isolation. For example, an isolated receipt from a contractual transaction might, when viewed against a wider factual matrix, not aptly be characterised as one on capital account but instead just an incident of the carrying on of a business and income under ordinary concepts: Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Myer Emporium Ltd [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 163 CLR 199. But such an outcome in revenue law does not mean that the legal obligations between the parties are recast to be anything other than those created by a governing contract.
  2. In dismissing one basis on which the appellant had cast its submission that Div 615 had no application, which concerned an inability, in light of market values agreed between the parties, for the ratio requirements specified in s 615-20 of the ITAA 1997 to be satisfied, given what was entailed in the staggered implementation, over the course of 18 June 2015, of the approved schemes of arrangement, the learned primary judge also observed (at [101]):
Because of the legal interdependence of the schemes, there is an air of unreality to the applicant’s submission that immediately prior to the completion of the Distribution scheme, the Transmission and Finance schemes had transferred value to the applicant that was independent of the value of the Distribution. The reality was that all the value accrued to the applicant and in the shares issued by it at the same moment because either all the schemes were completed or none were. There was no moment in time when the applicant could reflect the value of Transmission and Finance and not also reflect the value of its obligation to acquire Distribution in exchange for the issue of shares.

[Emphasis added]
  1. However, and once again with all respect, not only is the “reality” upon which her Honour relied to reject this submission at variance with the progressive legal consequences of the staggered, transactional implementation of the approved schemes, and thus also contrary to Europa Oil, Europa Oil No 2 and Mullens, it is also, as will be seen, at variance with facts as to market value agreed between the parties and thus contrary to s 191 of the Evidence Act and the application of s 615-20 of the ITAA 1997 to those facts.
  2. There was never any suggestion by the Commissioner in this case that the transactional steps entailed in the implementation of the approved schemes of arrangement were shams.
  3. Nor was ever there any suggestion by the Commissioner that the applicability of Div 615 arose because the approved schemes of arrangement fell afoul of Pt IVA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) (ITAA 1936). Unlike with s 177D(2)(b) within Part IVA of the ITAA 1936, nothing within Div 615 of the ITAA 1997 conferred upon the primary judge a charter to compare form and substance. Further, unlike its predecessor, s 260 of the ITAA 1936, s 177D of the ITAA 1936 does not operate of its own force. The authority for raising or amending an assessment to cancel a tax benefit in circumstances where it would be concluded that a scheme entered into by taxpayer was entered into for the dominant purpose of obtaining that tax benefit is engaged by the making by the Commissioner of a determination pursuant to s 177F of the ITAA 1936. In this case, there was no such anterior determination by the Commissioner. And as Hill J (Ryan and Cooper JJ agreeing) observed in Peabody v Commissioner of Taxation [1993] FCA 74; (1993) 40 FCR 531, at 547, “this Court cannot stand in the shoes of the Commissioner and exercise discretions which the legislature has committed to the Commissioner” (Commissioner’s appeal subsequently dismissed by a unanimous High Court (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Peabody (1994) 181 CLR 359)). That is in marked contrast to the Administrative Review Tribunal (and its predecessor, the Administrative Appeals Tribunal), which does stand in the shoes of the Commissioner when undertaking the jurisdictionally distinct role of reviewing an objection decision and which can itself, subject to observing procedural fairness, make a determination under s 177F of the ITAA 1936: Fletcher v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1988] FCA 362; (1988) 19 FCR 442.
  4. Yet further, the very presence in Australian income tax legislation of a general anti-avoidance provision means that a charter for a judicial comparison of form and substance to the end of determining a tax liability by reference to the “economic reality” of events is not to be found in the doctrine of “fiscal nullity” established by the House of Lords in WT Ramsay Ltd v Inland Revenue Commissioners [1981] UKHL 1; [1982] AC 300 and Furniss v Dawson [1984] 2 WLR 226: Oakey Abattoir Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1984] FCA 316; (1984) 55 ALR 291, at 297-299.
  5. Instead, if one adheres to the text of Div 615 and to s 191 of the Evidence Act in relation to agreed market values and gives effect to those values in light of the legal consequences that progressively occurred on 18 June 2015 as each approved scheme of arrangement was implemented in succession that day, the inevitable result is that s 615-20 of the ITAA 1997 could never be satisfied.
  6. On the appeal, the Commissioner embraced the reasoning of the primary judge. For reasons already given, this entails, outside the confines of a contemporary tax avoidance case, endeavouring to resurrect the long-discredited notion of taxation by end result, rather than resolving the controversy by reference to the text of the provisions concerned as applied to the events which in fact and legal character occurred.
  7. As it happens, the Commissioner’s submissions as to the meaning to afford the text of the provisions of Div 615 entail a departure by him from reasoning which underpinned an early ruling he issued (TR 97/18) on like predecessor rollover provisions, to the exclusion of an adoption of that reasoning by the appellant. Although the Commissioner’s position is not without irony, it is axiomatic that the meaning of the text cannot be dictated by a priori assumptions howsoever based, only by the text itself, as read in context and in light of the evident purpose of the provision: Commissioner of Taxation v Consolidated Media Holdings Ltd [2012] HCA 55; (2012) 250 CLR 503 (Consolidated Media Holdings).
  8. Principled eschewing of a priori assumptions is one thing; an ex post facto consequence of the construction of the text of a statutory provision in accordance with the approach mandated by Consolidated Media Holdings and the authorities discussed therein is quite another. Contrary to a conclusion reached by the primary judge (at 120] and [130]-[135]) and a related submission of the Commissioner, a feature of Div 615 is, as the appellant submitted, that the interposed company must be a shelf company an ex post facto consequence of approaching its construction in this way, not an a priori assumption.
  9. Against the background of these general observations and related conclusions, it is now necessary to examine in greater detail the issues raised by the grounds of appeal and, related to that, set out my reasons for these conclusions.
  10. A threshold difference between the parties was whether, as the Commissioner contended and the primary judge found, within the composite term “scheme for reorganising its affairs” in s 615-5(1)(c), the word “affairs” carried with it the characteristic that the “affairs” must relate to the “original entity’s (be that company or unit trust) ownership structure or whether “affairs” was, as the appellant submitted, not so limited.
  11. The Court was pressed in submissions with authorities concerning the meaning of “affairs” in other statutory contexts, some perhaps analogous. But error can lie in analogy in statutory construction. I consider it preferable to approach the meaning of “affairs” just with reference to the meaning of the word as used in the context of Div 615 and having regard to the purpose of that Division.
  12. That a difference of views has emerged is not surprising. With all respect to its drafting author, s 615-5(1)(c) employs a confusion of concepts. As a matter of ordinary English, a reference to the “affairs” of a company does not point naturally to its ownership structure or, more perhaps better put, not only to that subject but to everything related to the conduct of the company’s existing business or undertaking. Yet immediately after “scheme for reorganising its affairs”, s 615-5(1)(c) offers the qualifying description, “the exchanging members dispose of all their shares or units”. In my view, when read in context, that qualifying description does indeed narrow the focus of “affairs” in the way the primary judge concluded.
  13. Assistance for this conclusion is found in s 615-1. It contains a similar but at least consistent confusion of concepts. Unconstrained by context and as a matter of ordinary English, the “reorganisation” of a “business” might embrace a change in underlying ownership but it might equally, for example, entail a change in managerial arrangements from centralised control to decentralised control without any change at all to the formal ownership structure. Further, as with the “affairs” of a company, a business would not be “reorganised” if that business were substantially altered. Instead, a new set of corporate affairs or a new business would have been created. Once again though, read in context, a focus on a change in ownership of a described type is evident in what is meant in s 615(1) by the “restructuring” of a “business”.
  14. Regard to purpose also supports the conclusion of the primary judge as to the meaning of “affairs”. Division 615 is entitled “Roll overs for business restructures” and is in turn part of Part 3 80, which is entitled “Roll overs applying to assets generally”. Division 615 can be seen as having the purpose of offering relief in defined circumstances from the tax consequences which would otherwise follow from the changes in the ownership of assets in circumstances where the formal ownership structure of a business has been changed but, subject to ratios, underlying ownership has not been and the business itself is continued. This purpose is achieved by affording “affairs” the meaning assigned by the primary judge.
  15. For all that, s 615-5(1)(c) nonetheless requires that the scheme be one “for” reorganising “its”, i.e. a particular company’s (or unit trust’s) affairs. The preposition “for” introduces the purposive criterion of reorganising. The possessive “its” carries with it a particularly of entity focus. Here, that means there must be a scheme for reorganising [Distribution’s] affairs, not those of some other entity or even those of Distribution and some other entity. The ratios for which s 615-20 provides underscore this singularity of entity focus in s 615-5.
  16. The conclusion reached concerning the meaning of “affairs” means that the non-reorganisational features of the Distribution scheme to which the appellant pointed are not relevant to the application or otherwise of Div 615.
  17. Even accepting, as I do, that “affairs” carries the meaning assigned by the primary judge, there is merit in the appellant’s further submission that her Honour’s reasoning impermissibly conflates separate businesses and transactional events and, related to that, contravenes in fact finding s 191 of the Evidence Act.
  18. By virtue of para 14 of the JSAF, the following were agreed facts:
As at 18 June 2015:
(a) The Transmission tax consolidated group of which Transmission was the head company (Transmission TCG) owned and operated Victoria’s primary regulated electricity transmission network (which was privatised in 1997). This network was regulated by the Australian Energy Regulator, and the Transmission TCG’s right to provide transmission services throughout Victoria was outlined in its electricity transmission licence. As at 31 March 2014, this network consisted of approximately 6,500 kilometres of transmission lines.

(b) The Distribution tax consolidated group of which Distribution was the head company (Distribution TCG) owned and operated both the electricity distribution network and gas distribution network assets (which were acquired in 2004). The electricity and gas distribution networks were also regulated by the Australian Energy Regulator. As at 31 March 2014, the Distribution TCG distributed electricity to approximately 670,000 customers within its network covering eastern metropolitan Melbourne and eastern Victoria, and distributed gas to approximately 633,000 customers located in its distribution area in central and western Victoria.

(c) Finance operated to provide finance to the Transmission TCG and Distribution TCG, and had loans of approximately $2.8bn in total to the Transmission TCG and Distribution TCG on issue.

  1. Further, it was also agreed (JSAF at [15]), and the primary judge recorded (at [29]), that, as at that time, the entities which conducted those separate businesses had separate market values:
(a) Finance’s market value was $2.874 billion;

(b) Distribution’s market value was $2.025 billion; and

(c) Transmission’s market value was $0.525 billion.

  1. The agreed facts admitted only of a finding at trial that the Transmission TCG, the Distribution TCG and Finance operated separate (but not unrelated) businesses. Perhaps that is what the primary judge meant at [78] in stating that, before the Distribution scheme “Distribution did not operate a separate stand-alone business”. However, her Honour’s further statement (at [79]), in the face of expressly acknowledging (at [29]) the agreed fact of separate valuations, that, there is “an air of unreality in positing a moment in time in which Distribution is hypothesised as carrying on a business that had an existence and value independent of Transmission and Finance” is not reconcilable and tells otherwise.
  2. The point is that it was agreed that as at the start of 18 June 2015, separate businesses with separate market values existed. Nothing in the Stapling Deed changed this. Nothing in the Implementation Deed in respect of the schemes of arrangement changed this.
  3. Further, the very first of the successive steps for which the Implementation Deed provided entailed the “unstapling” of the hitherto stapled entities of Finance, Distribution and Transmission. One might find an “unreality” about this if the Implementation Deed were but a sham. But it bears repeating that there was never any such suggestion. That “unstapling” was a reality in law. At that point, the stapling hitherto provided for by the Stapling Deed dropped away. So even if, contrary to my view, and the agreed facts, that Deed created but one business carried on by the three entities, that was no longer so. Being a reality in law and fact, and in the absence of any statutory warrant, the primary judge was not entitled to look to economic substance to contradict this.
  4. Division 615 contains very precise temporal focal points. Section 615-20(2)(b)(ii) requires a determination of market value which is “worked out immediately before the first disposal, redemption or cancellation”. Section 615-30(2) requires attention to consolidated group positions “immediately before” and “immediately after” the “completion time”.
  5. On the agreed facts, and as the appellant correctly submitted, “immediately before the completion of the Distribution scheme, Distribution’s attributes were that of a business with an existence and value independent of Transmission and Finance, such that its business affairs could be reorganised independently to those entities”.
  6. It is necessary to remember that in this case there were three “schemes”. The Distribution Scheme was but one of them. It is only that scheme which is aptly described as a “scheme” for the reorganising of Distribution’s affairs. Although each scheme was implemented on 18 June 2015, each had different temporal focal points that day entailing different legal relationships. That remained so even though, at the start of that day, there was an expectation of implementation in succession over the course of the day (flowing from cl 5.1(b) of the Implementation Deed, which provides for the schemes to occur sequentially). There is provision in cl 5.1(c) of the Implementation Deed that, if it becomes clear (whether on or before the Implementation Date) that some or all of the steps will not take place, no party is compelled to complete any further steps but that clause does not invalidate a step that has already occurred. Contrary to a finding of the primary judge (at [98]), the three schemes were not the same transaction. The three schemes are most accurately characterised as separate but interdependent. However, the description “interdependent”, “is simply shorthand for the combined operation of the various explicit provisions by which the operation of one contract is related to the operation of the other”: Commissioner for ACT Revenue v Araghi [2013] ACTSC 43; (2013) 7 ACTLR 262, at [53]. It does not mean there was but one scheme implemented on 18 June 2015.
  7. Division 615 required that the position immediately before and immediately after each scheme in question be considered. The statute contained no warrant for conflation of them by reference to a position at the start of 18 June 2015 and the end of that day, looking to economic substance or end result to the exclusion of the positions which sequentially existed in fact and law as each scheme was implemented over the course of that day.
  8. Looking to the Distribution scheme and adhering to the required temporal focus in relation to that scheme, it was a factual given that, immediately before that scheme, the appellant was not a shelf company. On the agreed facts, and for reasons detailed below, only if it were a shelf company would the ratio requirements in s 615-20 be satisfied. In that circumstance, and as the appellant correctly submitted, no question of what was termed in the case the “boost” would arise “because there would not be material shares in the appellant already on issue”. The “boost” was a shorthand reference to a submission made by the appellant before the primary judge and us that, as a result of the Distribution scheme, the value of the pre-existing shares in the appellant increased. This conclusion entails no a priori assumption as to the meaning of that provision, rather an understanding of the consequence of applying it according to its terms.
  9. Before detailing further why the s 615-20 ratio requirements could not be satisfied, it is convenient to resolve a separate statutory controversy as to the meaning and effect of the “and nothing else” qualification, as found, presently materially in s 615-5(1)(c). That specifies as a criterion of application, “under a *scheme for reorganising its affairs, the exchanging members *dispose of all their shares or units in it to a company (the interposed company) in exchange for shares in the interposed company (and nothing else)”.
  10. On the appellant’s case the “boost” was also said to be pertinent because it was “something else” received in the “exchange” referred to in s 615-5(1)(c). Other such “something elses” flowing from the Distribution Scheme were said to be an expectation of increased franked dividends and the benefit of the repayment of outstanding Finance loans without adverse tax consequences. The latter two were, undoubtedly, benefits of the schemes as implemented on 18 June 2015. The appellant submitted that the primary judge erred in not adopting a construction of s 615-5(1)(c) which recognised that more was received for the “exchange” than just shares in the interposed company.
  11. Considered as an abstract proposition, divorced from reading the text of s 615-5(1)(c) in context, there is much to recommend the appellant’s submission. However, read in context, the focus of this provision is just on the consideration for the exchange. The intention of the parenthetical qualification “and nothing else” is to confine the application of this criterion just to an exchange where shares in the interposed company, as opposed to additional consideration, are received. It does not look to incidental consequences of the “exchange”. Adopting that construction is congruent with the notion of the reorganisation, already discussed, for which s 615-5(1)(c) provides and with s 615-1, “in exchange, you become the owner of new shares in another company”. It is also congruent with the heading of s 615-5, “Disposing of interests in one entity for shares in a company”. I do not therefore accept the construction of s 615-5(1)(c) pressed for the appellant.
  12. I return then to the subject of s 615-20 and the ratios for which that section provides. That section requires that the ratios in s 615-20(2)(a) and s 615-20(2)(b) equal one another. It was a feature of the approved schemes of arrangement that, although each was implemented on 18 June 2015, there were different “completion times” that day, flowing from their sequential implementation. The temporal focus in s 615-20 flowing from “completion time” is on the completion time in respect of a specific scheme, materially here the Distribution Scheme. More particularly, for the purposes of s 615-20, the denominator in the ratios referred solely to the shares either issued or disposed of under that scheme.
  13. In their submissions at trial, which were repeated before us, the appellants offered this description of the provision in s 615-20 for ratios – “s 615-20 ratios test whether the relative value of shareholdings both before and after the reorganisation, measured as a fraction of the market value of shares issued in exchange under the reorganisation are exactly equal”. That description should be accepted as correct. It well conforms with the evident purpose, discussed above, of Div 615. The relief offered is, in effect, where all that has occurred as a result of a given scheme is a reorganisation.
  14. Having regard to the agreed market values of Finance, Distribution and Transmission (set out above) the progressive implementation of the approved schemes necessarily carried with it the “boost” alleged by the appellant.

  5. There is no warrant for reading any words into the text of s 615-20(2). The cautionary note sounded in PMT Partners Pty Ltd v Australian National Parks & Wildlife Service [1995] HCA 36; (1995) 184 CLR 301, at 310, about the reading of limitations and qualifications into the text of statutory definitions is just as important in relation to the text of statutory eligibility criteria. Doing this in this case necessarily changes the integers of, and thus fundamentally alters, the mathematical ratios evident on the face of the text of the provision. The inserted words are, with respect, nothing more than a rationale for a conclusion impermissibly reached by an economic substance driven approach.

This was an odd submission. If it be right to qualify this expression by the addition of unstated, qualifying words in the one provision, it is no less right in a consistent way to likewise qualify that same expression within Div 615 in the other. On the other hand, just adhering to the text of s 615-25(3)(a) yields a rational meaning well in conformity with the overall purpose of Div 615. As the appellant nicely put it, adhering to the text, “prevents outsiders from owning shares in the interposed company, and avoids entities being able to satisfy the definition of ‘exchanging members’ by virtue of a pre-existing scheme different to the scheme under consideration”. That leaves s 615-25(3)(b) providing for a de minimis exception in circumstances where the “interposed company” has had up to five shareholders that have retained their shareholdings but the market value of their shares is merely trifling.
  8. Construing s 615-20 and s 615-25 in this way does not result in a capricious or irrational meaning such that, in accordance with principles of statutory interpretation discussed in Cooper Brookes (Wollongong) Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 26; (1981) 147 CLR 297, a departure from the literal meaning of the text is warranted. It yields a consistent meaning, revealing that Parliament’s intended purpose with Div 615 was to afford rollover relief on a reorganisation in circumstances where the interposed company was of but nominal value. Further and consistently, it discloses it was intended that, if the reorganisation concerned the shareholdings in an entity other than the target entity were also reorganised, Division 615 roll-over would be attracted only where that other entity was of but nominal value.
  9. It is possible to discern a like intention in predecessor provisions to Div 615 – s 160ZZPA of the ITAA 1936 and then, s 124-385(3) within Subdivision 124-G. In turn, it is possible to discern in explanatory memoranda touching on such provisions an understanding that the interposed company had to be a shelf company to attract rollover relief to a reorganisation scheme: see in the explanatory memorandum to the New Business Tax System (Capital Gains Tax) Bill 1999 (Cth) which introduced Subdivision 124-M, paras 2.48 and 2.49 and, in paras 2.6, 2.7 and 2.11 of the explanatory memorandum to the New Business Tax System (Consolidation and Other Measures) Bill (No.1) 2002 (Cth), it is made explicit that the consolidation provisions ss 703-65 to 703-80 were being introduced on the basis that the criteria in Subdivision 124-G required a shelf company. A like statement was made by the Minister in the Second Reading Speech concerning that Bill.
  10. Ordinarily, the relevance of such secondary materials, which do not relate directly to Div 615, would be moot to say the least. However, with respect to the Tax and Superannuation Laws Amendment (2014 Measures No. 6) Bill 2014 (Cth), which proposed the introduction of Div 615, the related explanatory memorandum stated (para 1.31) that the reason for its introduction of Div 615 was to “consolidate the two separate Subdivisions which provide effectively identical roll-overs for restructures of trusts and companies into a single Division”. No intention to change the then prevailing intended reach of the rollover relief is evident from this explanatory memorandum. Even given this linkage with predecessor provisions, perhaps all that can be said of the secondary materials canvassed is that they provide a level of comfort in respect of a meaning evident enough from the text of Div 615, as read in context. In contrast, those same materials serve only to deepen a disquiet about the construction promoted by the Commissioner.
  11. It does not follow from accepting the appellant’s construction of s 615-20 that it entails acting on an a priori assumption that the interposed company must be a shelf or nominal value company. That is but a consequence of adopting an orthodox approach to statutory construction.

  2. The preferable construction of s 615‑20(2)(a) is that identified by Kennett J at [136]. If one were to read the provision completely literally, it would produce arbitrary results for no discernible purpose whilst also failing to meet the objective evident in the terms of s 615-20 read in context. Introduction

  1. This appeal concerns one aspect of a series of transactions that took place on 18 June 2015, by which the appellant (Ausnet) was inserted into the ownership structure of three entities—AusNet Services (Transmission) Limited (Transmission), AusNet Services Finance Trust (Finance) and AusNet Services (Distribution) Limited (Distribution)—which had until then been a stapled group. This was the last of a pre-ordained sequence of transactions.
  2. The issue that divides the parties is whether the rollover provisions in Div 615 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) (ITAA97) apply to Ausnet’s acquisition of the shares in Distribution. It was the position of both parties that this resolved into two questions, namely:
(a) whether there was a scheme for reorganising the affairs of Distribution for the purposes of s 615-5(1)(c) of ITAA97; and

(b) whether the requirement in s 615-20(2) of ITAA97, that specified ratios concerning the interests of shareholders be equal, was satisfied.

The statutory scheme

  1. Division 615 provides for what is termed “roll-over relief” in respect of transactions by which the shareholders in a company (or the unit holders in a unit trust) transfer all their shares to a company and become the shareholders in that company. Section 615-1, which is headed “What this Division is about”, provides:
You can choose for transactions under a scheme to restructure a company’s or unit trust’s business to be tax neutral if, under the scheme:

(a) you cease to own shares in the company or units in the trust; and

(b) in exchange, you become the owner of new shares in another company.
  1. The consequences of a transaction coming within Division 615 are set out in Subdivs 615-C and 615-D. Relevantly, for the interposed entity, s 615-65(4) provides as follows.
(4) The first element of the *cost base of the interposed company’s *shares or units in the original entity that are not taken to have been *acquired before 20 September 1985 is:
(a) the total of the cost bases (as at the completion time) of the original entity’s assets that it acquired on or after that day; less
(b) its liabilities (if any) in respect of those assets.
The first element of the *reduced cost base of those shares or units is worked out similarly.
  1. The application of s 615-5 depends on a choice being made by a taxpayer (which can be deemed to have been made in some circumstances) concerning a specific kind of transaction, and on that transaction meeting certain requirements. The choice is provided for in Subdiv 615-A. Relevantly, s 615-5 provides:
615-5 Disposing of interests in one entity for shares in a company

(1) You can choose to obtain a roll-over if:
(a) you are a *member of a company or a unit trust (the original entity); and

(b) you and at least one other entity (the exchanging members) own all the *shares or units in it; and

(c) under a *scheme for reorganising its affairs, the exchanging members *dispose of all their shares or units in it to a company (the interposed company) in exchange for shares in the interposed company (and nothing else); and

(d) the requirements in Subdivision 615-B are satisfied.
Note 1: For paragraph (c), see section 124-20 if an exchanging member uses a share sale facility.

Note 2: After the completion of the scheme, later dealings between the interposed company and the original entity may be subject to the rules for consolidated groups (see Part 3-90).

(2) You are taken to have chosen to obtain the roll-over if:
(a) immediately before the completion time (see section 615-15), the original entity is the *head company of a *consolidated group; and

(b) immediately after the completion time, the interposed company is the head company of the group.

Note: The consolidated group continues in existence because of section 703-70.
  1. In these reasons I will use the terms original entity, exchanging members and interposed company in the senses established by s 615-5(1)(a), (b) and (c) respectively.
  2. Section 615-10 (which is not in play here) makes similar provision in relation to transactions in which the interposed company acquires some of the shares or units in the original entity and the others are redeemed or cancelled.
  3. The requirements that apply to the transaction are set out in Subdiv 615-B. One of those requirements is that the interposed company must be the owner of all of the shares in the original entity immediately after all the exchanging members have had their shares or units in that entity disposed of, redeemed or cancelled (defined as the completion time) (s 615-15).
  4. The other provisions of Subdiv 615-B that are relevant here are ss 615-20 and 615-25, which provide as follows.
615-20 Requirements relating to your interests in the original entity

(1) Immediately after the completion time, each exchanging member must own:
(a) a whole number of *shares in the interposed company; and
(b) a percentage of the shares in the interposed company that were issued to all the exchanging members that is equal to the percentage of the shares or units in the original entity that were:
(i) owned by the member; and

(ii) disposed of, redeemed or cancelled under the *scheme.
(2) The following ratios must be equal:
(a) the ratio of:
(i) the *market value of each exchanging member’s *shares in the interposed company; to

(ii) the market value of the shares in the interposed company issued to all the exchanging members (worked out immediately after the completion time);



(b) the ratio of:
(i) the market value of that member’s shares or units in the original entity that were disposed of, redeemed or cancelled under the *scheme; to

(ii) the market value of all the shares or units in the original entity that were disposed of, redeemed or cancelled under the scheme (worked out immediately before the first disposal, redemption or cancellation).

Bill owned 15 shares in A and received 150 shares in B in exchange.

The ratios

  1. It is useful to address the argument concerning s 615-20(2)(a) first, as it brings into focus the role of the legislative history and the parties’ positions concerning the intended scope of Division 615.
  2. The primary judge dealt with this issue at [106]-[135]. Because the question is one of statutory construction, as to which all of the arguments were presented afresh in this Court, it is not necessary to trace the course of her Honour’s reasoning. As will appear below, her Honour’s conclusion on the issue was correct.

The issue

  1. Section 615-20(2) requires two ratios to be equal. One applies to the exchanging members’ interests in the original entity that are disposed of (or redeemed or cancelled) pursuant to the scheme, immediately before that disposal occurs. The other applies to the exchanging members’ interests in the interposed company immediately after the completion time. Broadly, the market value of each exchanging member’s interest must be the same, in proportion to the total of all exchanging members’ interests, at the end of the process as it was at the beginning. There is a similar (but, as noted below, differently expressed) requirement in relation to the percentage of shares issued to each exchanging member in s 615-20(1)(b). However, the drafting of s 615-20(2)(a)(i) introduces a complication.
  2. In para 615-20(2)(b), in relation to the position before the scheme, both of sub-paras (i) and (ii) (which it is convenient to refer to as the numerator and the denominator) expressly refer to the value of the shares or units in the original entity that were disposed of under the scheme. It is clear that the focus is on the respective shares of the exchanging members in that pool of shares or units. (This specificity may be unnecessary, because roll-over relief is only available if the exchanging members own all of the shares or units in the original entity and dispose of all of those shares to the interposed entity: s 615-5(1)(b)-(c).)
  3. In para 615-20(2)(a), however, as to the position immediately after the completion time, the denominator refers to the shares in the interposed company issued to the exchanging members, while the numerator refers to each exchanging member’s “shares in the interposed company” simpliciter. Thus, if sub-para (i) is construed according to its ordinary English meaning, it directs attention to all shares held by each exchanging member in the interposed company whenever and however those shares were acquired. The result is that the ratios in paras (a) and (b) cannot be equal if any exchanging members have shares in the interposed company other than those issued as part of the scheme.
  4. Ausnet contends for a construction in accordance with this understanding of sub-para (i). The Distribution scheme fails the test if Ausnet’s construction is correct. If correct, it bears out Ausnet’s thesis that the interposed company must be a shelf company. However, as noted earlier, reasoning in the other direction (from the thesis to the preferred construction) risks error.
  5. The Commissioner resists that construction and says that sub-para (i) should be understood to be referring to a subset of the shares mentioned in sub-para (ii). On this construction, it does not matter whether there are shares in the interposed company other than those issued pursuant to the scheme; what matters is the proportionate interests of the exchanging member in the shares that are issued pursuant to the scheme. In other words, Division 615 is imposing requirements in relation to the actual transaction but not the choice of the corporate vehicle which is to become the interposed company. On this understanding, the Distribution scheme passes the test in s 615-20(2).

Principles

  1. As noted earlier in these reasons, statutory construction is a text-based activity: it must begin and end with the text itself: Consolidated Media at [39] (French CJ, Hayne, Crennan, Bell and Gageler JJ); Alcan at [47] (Hayne, Heydon, Crennan and Kiefel JJ) and the cases cited there; Northern Territory v Collins [2008] HCA 49; 235 CLR 619 at [99] (Crennan J); Baini v The Queen [2012] HCA 59; 246 CLR 469 at [14] (French CJ, Hayne, Crennan, Kiefel and Bell JJ). However, the text must always be read in its context. Thus, a distinction is sometimes drawn between the “legal meaning” of a provision and its “grammatical meaning” (eg Project Blue Sky Inc v Australian Broadcasting Authority [1998] HCA 28; 194 CLR 355 at [78] (McHugh, Gummow, Kirby and Hayne JJ)), although there is in principle no such thing as a completely “acontextual utterance” (Leeming M, Common Law, Equity and Statute (Federation Press, 2023) at 90; see eg Harvey v Minister for Primary Industry and Resources [2024] HCA 1; 98 ALJR 168 at [106] (Edelman J)). The “grammatical meaning” of a statutory provision can be understood to denote the meaning that its words would convey to a reader as a matter of ordinary English, if read with any relevant definitions but without broader consideration of the scheme of the legislation, its history and the mischief to which it is directed. Modern case law emphasises that, in the search for the “legal meaning”, these matters of context are always relevant; there is no threshold test of ambiguity that needs to be passed before regard is had to them.

The grammatical meaning and the immediate statutory context

  1. The signification of s 615-20(2)(a)(i), without reference to the particular context of Division 615, is reasonably clear. It refers to all of the shares in a particular company (the interposed company) that a person (the exchanging member) holds.
  2. Despite this, the Commissioner resists the suggestion that his construction of s 615-20(2)(a)(i) involves reading words into the provision or is non-literal. He submits, correctly, that the description of the ratio in para (a) needs to be read as a whole. The timing requirement included in parentheses at the end of sub-para (ii) (“worked out immediately after the completion time”) must be understood to be applicable to both sub-paragraphs if the description of the ratio is to make any sense at all; so that the two elements cannot be read in isolation from each other. Thus, it is necessary on any view to read para (a) as if the final words of sub-para (ii) were a separate tailpiece (applicable to both sub-paragraphs) rather than part of sub-para (ii).
  3. The Commissioner submits, further, that the words “issued to” in sub-para (ii) should also be understood to qualify sub-para (i); so that both sub-paras relate only to shares issued pursuant to the scheme. However, this cannot be achieved by any ordinary process of syntactical analysis. Rewriting, or at least a strained construction, is needed.
  4. Thus, it is necessary to read the paragraph as if sub-para (i) contained a qualification limiting “each exchanging member’s shares in the interposed company” to shares issued to that member pursuant to the scheme. Reading a statutory provision as if it contained words in addition to those adopted by the legislature is a large step, but not unknown. In Taylor v Owners – Strata Plan No 11564 [2014] HCA 9; 253 CLR 531 at [37]- [38] (Taylor) French CJ, Crennan and Bell JJ observed:
Consistently with this Court's rejection of the adoption of rigid rules in statutory construction, it should not be accepted that purposive construction may never allow of reading a provision as if it contained additional words (or omitted words) with the effect of expanding its field of operation. As the review of the authorities in [Director of Public Prosecutions (Vic) v Leys [2012] VSCA 304; (2012) 296 ALR 96] demonstrates, it is possible to point to decisions in which courts have adopted a purposive construction having that effect. And as their Honours observed ..., the question of whether a construction “reads up” a provision, giving it an extended operation, or “reads down” a provision, confining its operation, may be moot.

The question whether the court is justified in reading a statutory provision as if it contained additional words or omitted words involves a judgment of matters of degree. That judgment is readily answered in favour of addition or omission in the case of simple, grammatical, drafting errors which if uncorrected would defeat the object of the provision. It is answered against a construction that fills “gaps disclosed in legislation” or makes an insertion which is “too big, or too much at variance with the language in fact used by the legislature”.

(Footnotes omitted.)
  1. In the same case Gageler and Keane JJ (dissenting in the result) said (at [65]-[66]):
Statutory construction involves attribution of legal meaning to statutory text, read in context. “Ordinarily, that meaning (the legal meaning) will correspond with the grammatical meaning ... But not always.” Context sometimes favours an ungrammatical legal meaning. Ungrammatical legal meaning sometimes involves reading statutory text as containing implicit words. Implicit words are sometimes words of limitation. They are sometimes words of extension. But they are always words of explanation. The constructional task remains throughout to expound the meaning of the statutory text, not to divine unexpressed legislative intention or to remedy perceived legislative inattention. Construction is not speculation, and it is not repair.

Context more often reveals statutory text to be capable of a range of potential meanings, some of which may be less immediately obvious or more awkward than others, but none of which is wholly ungrammatical or unnatural. The choice between alternative meanings then turns less on linguistic fit than on evaluation of the relative coherence of the alternatives with identified statutory objects or policies.

The applicable provision fixed the appeal period as “within 42 days after the court’s decision is given to the party”, while an earlier provision had fixed the period as “not later than 42 days after the pronouncement of the decision”. Philippides J (with whom the other members of the Court agreed) held at [25] that this was “a significant and a material difference”, which “cannot be explained as directed to the use of a clearer or simpler style”, and that s 14C therefore did not apply. Her Honour observed (also at [25]) that “[t]he use of significantly different wording signifies a legislative intention to convey a different meaning”.
  3. The change from a ratio expressed by reference to a proportion of “the replacement shares owned by the taxpayer” to one expressed by reference to “each exchanging member’s shares” is of a similar order of magnitude to the difference between the earlier and later provisions considered in Melville. Section 124-365(3) did not, at least on its face, “express the same idea” as s 160ZZPA(1)(m). It expresses a different idea. Section 1-3 therefore comes into play only if the context requires a conclusion that the “idea” being expressed in s 124-365(3) (and now expressed in s 615-20(2)(a)) diverged from its grammatical meaning and aligned with the “idea” in s 160ZZPA(1)(m). Section 1-3(2) does not itself bring about that understanding and thus does not resolve the issue of construction. More specifically, s 1-3(2) is not sufficient to overcome the inference (which would ordinarily arise) that the difference in expression reflected a difference in intention.
  4. In these circumstances, it is necessary to refer to relevant aspects of the extrinsic materials.
  5. Ausnet placed some reliance, in connection with the construction of Division 615 generally, on Taxation Ruling TR97/18 (which was issued on 17 September 1997). This Ruling predated the enactment of Division 124 of ITAA97 and set out in some detail the Commissioner’s understanding, at that time, of the operation of ss 160ZZPA to 160ZZPD of ITAA36. It does not appear to have been prepared as part of the process leading to the enactment of corresponding provisions of ITAA97 or put before Parliament as an explanation of those provisions. It does not assist in their construction.
  6. Chapter 2.16 of the Explanatory Memorandum to the Bill for the 1998 Act (the Tax Law Improvement Bill (No. 2) 1997 (Cth)) was headed “Replacement asset roll-over events” and dealt with the provisions which were enacted as Division 124. The Overview of the chapter (at 118) indicated that Part A was a summary of the rules proposed to be enacted; Part B explained “the changes to the 1936 Act”; and Part C identified “provisions that have not been rewritten”. The provisions “not rewritten”, listed in Part C, were provisions of ITAA36 identified as having become redundant and can be put to one side. Part B identified three “changes” relating to the “other requirements to be satisfied” in what became Subdivs 124-G and 124-H (at 126–127). These were:
(a) to require explicitly that each exchanging shareholder or unit holder be issued a whole number of shares in the interposed company (see now s 615-20(1)(a));

(b) to remove an express requirement in the ITAA36 provisions (seen as unnecessary in the light of other provisions) that an exchanging shareholder or unit holder dispose of all their shares or units in the original entity at the same time; and

(c) to change the time at which the ratio of the value of the taxpayer’s shares or units in the original entity to the value of all of the shares or units in that entity was to be measured (compare the second ratio in s 160ZZPA(1)(m) with the current s 615-20(2)(b)(i) and (ii)).

  1. The absence of any reference to the first of the ratios in s 160ZZPA(1)(m) (the equivalent of the first ratio in s 124-365(3) and subsequently s 615-20(2)(a)) in this list of “changes” is an indication that the legislature, in rewriting the ratio, did not intend to change its content. It thus supports the Commissioner’s construction of s 615-20(2)(a)(i).

Conclusion: the preferred construction of s 615-20(2)(a)(i)

  1. The apparent absence of any appreciation that the ratio now enacted in s 615-20(2)(a) was different in substance from that in s 160ZZPA(1)(m), the absence of any discernible reason for the difference in approach from s 615-20(1)(b) and the surprising effect of a literal construction of s 615-20(2)(a)(i) lead me to conclude that the legal meaning of s 615-20(2)(a)(i) is not the same as its grammatical meaning. As to the last of these points, if the intention was to limit the coverage of Division 615 to schemes in which the interposed company was a shelf company, that could have been done in a much clearer and more direct way. Ausnet’s construction has the effect that that significant limitation on the coverage of the provisions was introduced by a side wind. The shares referred to in sub-para (i) of s 615-20(2)(a) should be understood to be a subset of the shares referred to in sub-para (ii); that is, both the numerator and the denominator should be understood to be referring to shares issued pursuant to the scheme.
  2. The situation brings to mind Lord Diplock’s extra-curial aphorism that “if ... the courts can identify the target of Parliamentary legislation their proper function is to see that it is hit: not merely to record that it has been missed”: “The Courts as Legislators” in Harvey BW (ed), The Lawyer and Justice (Sweet and Maxwell, 1978) 263 at 274 quoted in Kingston v Keprose Pty Ltd (1987) 11 NSWLR 404 at 424 [D] (McHugh JA); see also, eg, Melway Publishing Pty Ltd v Robert Hicks Pty Ltd [2001] HCA 13; 205 CLR 1 at [95] (Kirby J). In this case, identifying Parliament’s target has involved inferences from a range of materials; and seeing that that target is hit involves a distorted reading of the text of the provision. The process has involved detailed argument, judicial time and (it can be assumed) considerable expense on both sides of the record. It illustrates a drawback of the drafting style adopted in Division 615.
  3. As will be apparent from the way I have expressed my conclusion above, I prefer to view the necessary reasoning process as one of identifying the universe of discourse of s 615-20(2)(a) (ie, the class of shares with which it is concerned) rather than “reading in” specific words which the legislature is taken to have left out by some oversight. If the preferable understanding is that s 615-20(2)(a)(i) needs to be construed as if it contained additional words, it is necessary to return to Taylor.
  4. The majority in Taylor, in the course of summarising the reasons of McColl JA in the New South Wales Court of Appeal, referred (at [18], [22]-[25]) to three “conditions” for the insertion of additional words which were drawn from the speech of Lord Diplock in Wentworth Securities Ltd v Jones [1980] AC 74 at 105 and applied, together with a fourth condition, by the Court of Appeal. These were (adopting the summary later set out by Thawley J in BBlood Enterprises Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [2022] FCA 1112; 114 ATR 851 at [286]):
(1) the precise purpose of the provision can be identified – see: Taylor at [22];

(2) there has been an inadvertent failure to deal with an eventuality that must be dealt with if the provision is to achieve its purpose – see: Taylor at [23];

(3) the words that Parliament would have included can be clearly identified – see: Taylor at [24];

(4) the words that might be read into the text are consistent with the wording otherwise adopted – see: Taylor at [25].

  1. The majority in Taylor noted (at [39]) that the third of these conditions had been reformulated in Inco Europe Ltd v First Choice Distribution [2000] UKHL 15; [2000] 1 WLR 586 at 592 (Lord Nicholls) so as to refer to the court being abundantly sure of the substance, although not necessarily the precise words, that the legislature would have enacted. Their Honours then observed that the principles as reformulated accorded with Cooper Brookes (Wollongong) Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 26; (1981) 147 CLR 297 and that:
...McColl JA was right to consider that satisfaction of each could be treated as a prerequisite to reading s 12(2) as if it contained additional words before her Honour required satisfaction of a fourth condition of consistency with the wording of the provision. However, it is unnecessary to decide whether Lord Diplock's three conditions are always, or even usually, necessary and sufficient. This is because the task remains the construction of the words the legislature has enacted. In this respect it may not be sufficient that “the modified construction is reasonably open having regard to the statutory scheme” because any modified meaning must be consistent with the language in fact used by the legislature.

(Footnotes omitted.)
  1. Their Honours’ ambivalence in this respect is consistent with their observations at [37] – [38] (set out above) concerning the rejection of “rigid rules” and the judgment of “matters of degree”. Nevertheless, it is appropriate to note that the four “conditions” referred to are met by the construction which I have identified as preferable.
(a) The “precise purpose” can be identified (by an admittedly torturous path) as requiring equality between each exchanging shareholder’s proportionate interest in the original entity (measured by market value immediately before implementation of the scheme) and that member’s proportionate interest in the body of shares issued by the interposed company pursuant to the scheme (measured by market value immediately after the completion time), but not requiring the same equivalence in respect of the total interests of exchanging members in the interposed company.

(b) The eventuality not dealt with (but needing to be dealt with if the purpose is to be achieved) is a situation in which some or all exchanging shareholders hold other shares in the interposed entity.

(c) The words that would have been included, had this eventuality been addressed (or at least the substance of those words) can conveniently be taken from the formulation of the primary judge at [117(6)]:

... the reference to the market value of the exchanging member’s shares in the interposed company in s 615-20(2)(a)(i) should be taken as a reference to each exchanging member’s shares in the interposed company issued to that member in exchange for that member’s shares in the original company under the scheme.

(Underlining added.)
(d) The text thereby inserted results in s 615-20(2)(a)(i) describing a category of shares that is a subset of the category identified by applying the grammatical meaning. It thus fits comfortably within, and does not disturb, the structure of s 615-20 and the Division as a whole. The modified category is also a subset of the category described in s 615-20(2)(a)(ii) with which it is to be compared. In this respect, the modification aligns the ratio in para (2)(a) with that in para (2)(b) and with the percentage calculated under para (1)(b).

“Scheme for reorganising its affairs”

  1. The asterisk before the word “scheme” in s 615-5(1)(c) indicates that it is a defined term in ITAA97. However, the definition (which is in s 995-1) does not assist in resolving the issues in this case. “Scheme for organising its affairs” is not a defined term, and it was not said to be a legal term of art.
  2. It is clear from the structure of s 615-5(1) that “its affairs” in para (c) is a reference to the affairs of the original entity (here, Distribution). The Distribution scheme was therefore capable of being the subject of an election under s 615-5 if it was a scheme “for reorganising [Distribution’s] ‘affairs’”.
  3. Ausnet’s submissions, which reflected those advanced before the primary judge, involved two main propositions.
(a) The Distribution scheme was not one for reorganising Distribution’s affairs because it went beyond reconstruction and was more akin to an amalgamation or merger. Distribution was not carrying on substantially the same business after implementation of the scheme as before. It went from being a company with a market value of $2.025 billion to being an element of Ausnet, a significantly larger entity.

(b) It was not a scheme for reorganising Distribution’s affairs because it did not affect Distribution’s affairs alone. It also involved significant reorganisation of Ausnet’s affairs.

  1. Both of these propositions should be rejected.
  2. “Reorganising” and “affairs” are ordinary English words with fairly broad meanings. They are not defined in ITAA97 and it was not submitted that s 615-10(1) uses them in any technical sense. In this context, “scheme for reorganising its affairs” should be understood to be doing nothing more than to require a distinctly identifiable “scheme”, involving “reorganisation” of the original entity. Paragraphs (1)(a)-(e) do the work of defining the characteristics that the scheme must have: it must encompass a change of ownership of shares or units of the kind referred to in those paragraphs. The concluding words of s 615-10(1) require that the “scheme” also be one in relation to which the requirements of Subdiv 615-B are satisfied.
  3. The expression’s lack of particularity is illustrated by the fact that s 615-1, in explaining “What this Division is about”, uses different words to designate the same thing. Its summary of the Division is that “you” (the taxpayer) “can choose for transactions under a scheme to restructure a company’s or unit trust’s business to be tax neutral” (emphasis added). In some legal contexts there may be shades of difference between “reorganising” and “restructuring”, and between “affairs” and “business”; however, the drafters of Division 615 were evidently not focusing on such distinctions. There was therefore (as the primary judge noted at [84]) no warrant for construing “reorganising” by reference to concepts employed in company law and treating it as necessarily distinct from a “merger” or “amalgamation”.
  4. The Distribution scheme plainly “reorganised” the “affairs” of Distribution in the sense of transforming it from a widely held corporation into a wholly owned subsidiary of Ausnet (whose shareholders were the former shareholders of Distribution). Clearly, it is a reorganisation in that sense that (if the requirements in Subdiv 615-B are met) opens the door to an election for roll-over relief under s 615-5.
  5. Turning to Ausnet’s first proposition, therefore, it would not matter for the purposes of s 615-5(1)(c) if the Distribution scheme had in some sense changed the scope or character of Distribution’s business. However, the case was argued on the basis that the relevant “scheme” was the Distribution scheme only (and not the whole series of transactions on 18 June 2015 or some subset of them): “the scheme”, by reference to which (for example) ss 615-15 and 615-20 operate (and which framed the debate on the ratios, considered above), is clearly the “scheme” that is referred to in s 615-10(1)(c). There was (as the primary judge noted at [86]) no change in Distribution’s business undertakings, asset base or relationships with other entities effected by the Distribution scheme. Distribution remained a separate legal entity from Transmission and Finance, and it retained (and did not add to) its assets and business activities. All that changed, pursuant to the scheme, was the identities of its shareholders.
  6. As to Ausnet’s second proposition, it is impossible to imagine a “scheme” in which all of the shares or units in the original entity are transferred to an interposed company, in exchange for shares in that company, which does not involve some “reorganisation” of the “affairs” of the interposed company. The Division would therefore do no work if the fact that the interposed company’s affairs are altered, by the acquisition of the original entity and the issue of shares to the exchanging shareholders, took the relevant “scheme” outside s 615-10(1)(c). This is fatal to Ausnet’s submission that “its affairs” is used in a sense that excludes schemes which affect the affairs of other entities as well. Ausnet’s second proposition was correctly rejected by the primary judge at [87].
  7. There may be a point where a “scheme” has too many disparate elements to be properly seen as one “for” the reorganisation of a particular entity’s affairs. However, noting again that the parties have treated the Distribution scheme as the relevant scheme throughout the proceedings, that point has clearly not been reached here. No doubt, there were many moving parts involved in the sequence of transactions on 18 June 2015 and many consequences (including important tax consequences) envisaged for the relevant entities and their shareholders. However, it was never submitted that the relevant “scheme” involved all of these elements.
  8. For these reasons, the primary judge did not err in concluding that the Distribution scheme was a “scheme for reorganising its affairs” within the meaning of s 615-5(1)(c).

“Nothing else”

The argument

  1. The primary judge described the argument as put to her as follows (at [50]).
It was further contended that the Distribution scheme did not satisfy the “and nothing else” requirement as required by the concluding words of s 615-5(1)(c). It was submitted that as a result of the “features of the [a]pplicant by the time of the Distribution Scheme, the shareholders entering into the Distribution Scheme received shares in an entity [the applicant] carrying on businesses that Distribution did not previously carry on”. The shareholders of Distribution became shareholders in a company with substantial franking credits with the ability to immediately pay franked distributions (unlike Distribution). As discussed further below, the applicant submitted that an examination of the ratios in s 615-20 discloses that mathematically it could be demonstrated that the Distribution shareholder received not only shares in the applicant but also “something else” in the form of an uplift in value in the shares each of them held in the applicant as a result of the Transmission and Finance schemes.
  1. The main emphasis appears to have been on the proposition that shareholders received an “uplift” or “boost” in the value of the shares that they already held in Ausnet. The primary judge’s reasoning rejecting this argument (at [90]-[102]) is the subject-matter of ground 2 in the notice of appeal. The way that her Honour dealt with an aspect of the JSAF in the course of this reasoning is the subject of ground 5.
  2. The starting point is JSAF [15], which recorded the following as agreed facts.
As at the beginning of 18 June 2015:
a) Distribution, Transmission and Finance each had 3,466,913,009 shares or units (as applicable) on issue, with each security holder holding the same percentage shareholding or unitholding (as applicable) in respect of each of Finance, Distribution or Transmission, as set out in Annexure 19.


b) Finance’s market value was $2.874 billion;

c) Distribution’s market value was $2.025 billion; and

d) Transmission’s market value was $0.525 billion.

(Footnotes omitted.)
  1. The sequence of transactions that occurred on that day, in accordance with the implementation deed and the orders of the Supreme Court, has been summarised above. Ausnet’s argument can be summarised as follows.
(a) After completion of the Transmission scheme, Ausnet had 3,466,913,009 shares on issue and a market value of $0.525 billion.

(b) After completion of the Finance scheme (and immediately before the Distribution scheme), Ausnet had 6,933,826,018 shares on issue and a market value of $3.399 billion. (The shares that had been issued in exchange for Transmission shares were now half of the total shares and therefore worth $1.7 billion. The shares issued in exchange for Finance units had the same value.)

(c) After completion of the Distribution scheme, Ausnet had 10,400,739,027 shares on issue and a market value of $5.424 billion. Two thirds of those shares were pre-existing shares (ie, those acquired pursuant to the Transmission and Finance schemes). Their value was now two thirds of $5.424 billion, ie $3.616 billion.

  1. Some of the argument before the primary judge proceeded by reference to a hypothetical shareholder with a one percent interest in each entity at the start of the day. It is probably easier to follow using the summary set out above (which draws on the way the argument was explained during the course of the appeal). The underlying point is that each of the three schemes implemented on 18 June 2015 involved the issue of the same number of shares but each brought a different amount of value into Ausnet. The value of the shares already issued therefore (it was submitted) changed as a result of the Distribution scheme.

Reasoning of the primary judge

  1. The primary judge described the argument as “premised on the factual assumption that the shares issued in the applicant immediately prior to the completion of the Distribution scheme had a market value equal to the agreed market value of Transmission and Finance”, which was not accepted (at [90]). Her Honour made the following points.
(a) The basis for the valuation attributed to Distribution in the JSAF was not explained. Because the securities were stapled, financial results of Distribution were not separately reported. The shares in Distribution could not be separately traded and did not have an identifiable trading value on their own (at [91]).

(b) The Transmission, Finance and Distribution schemes were interrelated, interdependent and all to occur on the same implementation date (at [92]).

(c) As a consequence, immediately after the Finance exchange, the market value of the shares in Ausnet could not be calculated without taking into account the impending Distribution exchange, which was effectively certain to occur; and it would be “artificial to regard the Distribution exchange as giving rise to something ‘else’” (at [95]-[96].

(d) The terms of the implementation deed were such that all of the schemes were to be implemented or none were. Read together, the Transmission, Finance and Distribution schemes were “different, though connected, parts of the same transaction” (at [97]-[98], citing Isles v Daily Mail Newspaper [1912] HCA 18; (1912) 14 CLR 193 at 205 (Isaacs J) (Isles)).

(e) It was therefore not accepted that the value of Ausnet incrementally increased in the manner suggested, because of the “inter-conditional and interdependent” nature of the schemes. Although the schemes were to occur sequentially, the instant each step was performed there was a binding obligation to perform the next step (at [99]). Thus:

Embedded in the value of the shares in the applicant issued under each of the schemes was the value of the obligations to which the applicant was then subject.
(f) For this reason “there was no instant in time when shares in the applicant held by a security holder had a value that was independent of the value of Distribution” (at [100]). There was an “air of unreality” to Ausnet’s submission (at [101]). The “reality was that all the value accrued to the applicant and in the shares issued by it at the same moment because either all the schemes were completed or none were” (at [101]).

(g) The market value of a share is affected by the market’s expectation of the effect of future events (at [102] (citing Kenny & Good Pty Ltd v MGICA (1992) Ltd [1999] HCA 25; 199 CLR 413 at [50]- [51], [56] (McHugh J))).

Ausnet’s submissions

  1. Ausnet submits that this reasoning was erroneous for the following reasons.
(a) In the light of both the agreed fact recorded in JSAF [15] as to the respective values of Transmission, Finance and Distribution and an agreed position recorded in the parties’ written submissions, it was not open to the primary judge to reject the “factual assumption” that the shares in Ausnet had a particular value immediately before the Distribution scheme was implemented.

(b) While the schemes were interdependent, each was a separate transaction and the subject of separate orders of the Supreme Court. The implementation deed provided for the transactions to occur sequentially; and, in the event that some or all of the steps did not take place, steps that had already taken place were not invalidated. Further, if there was truly a single transaction, there would be one global quid pro quo (ie, all of the shares issued in Ausnet), which would fail the “nothing else” requirement.

(c) Each step gave rise to legal consequences and each transfer of shares to Ausnet increased its overall value. It could not be said, for example, that Ausnet had a value of $5.424 billion at a time when only the shares in Transmission (with a market value of $0.525 billion) had been transferred to it. Her Honour was thus incorrect to regard all the value as having accrued to Ausnet “at the same moment”. What was needed was a disciplined analysis of the legal effect of each step rather than a generalised appeal to “practical reality”.

Analysis

  1. The first issue that requires attention is whether any “boost” to the value of shares that the Distribution shareholders already held in Ausnet is relevant to the scheme satisfying the requirements of s 615-5(1)(c). The provision, it will be recalled, refers to a disposal of shares “in exchange for shares in the interposed company (and nothing else)”. The critical integer, therefore, is what the shareholder receives “in exchange” for their shares or units in the original entity. That language directs attention to consideration in the traditional contractual sense: it is concerned with what tangible benefits pass from the acquirer of the shares or units (the interposed company) to the person disposing of those shares or units. It does not invite a more general inquiry into the consequences of the transaction. The primary judge recognised this (at [103]) and it formed part of her Honour’s reasons for rejecting Ausnet’s submissions.
  2. This understanding is supported by the legislative history.
(a) The former s 160ZZPA(1)(b), which played the role now played by this aspect of s 615-5(1)(c), referred to “the consideration” for the disposals.

(b) Reference to consideration did not appear in s 124-360(1)(c) (which was relevantly in the same terms as the current provision), but the expression “in exchange for” does not suggest that any different meaning was intended.

(c) The change from “consideration” to “in exchange for ... (and nothing else)” is clearly an instance of expressing the same idea in different words “in order to use a clearer or simpler style”. There is nothing in the text or context indicating the contrary. Accordingly, even if there were shades of difference between these forms of expression, s 1-3(2) of ITAA97 would require them to be given the same meaning.

  1. Although senior counsel for Ausnet sought to describe the (asserted) “boost” in the value of pre-existing shares as something “moving from” Ausnet to the Distribution shareholders in exchange for their shares, the description is not persuasive. First, the Distribution scheme expressly identified the entitlement to receive shares in Ausnet as the (only) “consideration” for the transfer of the Distribution shares (a point noted by the primary judge at [104]). Secondly, any boost to the value of pre-existing Ausnet shares was a consequence flowing from the augmentation of Ausnet’s asset value as a result of the Distribution shareholders disposing of their shares to it. Looked at more broadly, it was an aspect of a coordinated sequence of transactions by which those shareholders retained the overall value of their portfolios by moving shares and units into Ausnet in exchange for shares in it. All that Ausnet did was issue shares. It did not do anything to add value to the shares it had issued, other than to receive transfers of the Distribution shares. To the extent that value was added to the pre-existing shares, that came in substance from the shareholders themselves.
  2. Any “boost” is therefore irrelevant to whether the criterion in s 615-5(1)(c) was satisfied. Ground 2 must fail.
  3. In any event, the primary judge did not err in concluding that there was no such “boost”.
  4. In JSAF [15] the parties agreed the respective “market values” of Transmission, Finance and Distribution at the start of 18 June 2015 (ie, before any of the steps which were to take place that day). On the basis of these values it can be accepted that each share in Transmission and Distribution and each unit in Finance had an identifiable value at that time (based on the numbers of shares and units on issue, which were also agreed), even though that value is somewhat academic because these shares and units could not be traded individually.
  5. Ausnet’s written submissions before the primary judge set out a series of propositions concerning the market value of the shares issued by Ausnet before the Distribution scheme and the consequences of the Distribution scheme, including in respect of the “market value” of a one percent shareholding (at [81]). This was in the context of an argument concerning the ratios in s 615-20(2). Responding to this, the Commissioner’s written submissions said (at [96]):
With respect to the position of the representative 1% member, the parties agree (see AS[81]) that:

(a) before the share-for-share exchange in relation to Distribution:
(i) the Applicant had approx. 6.94b shares on issue with an aggregate market value of $3.4b (a 1% interest having a market value of $34m); and

(ii) Distribution had approx. 3.47b shares on issue with an aggregate market value of $2.0b (a 1% interest having a market value of $20m);

(b) the share-for-share exchange in relation to Distribution involved the issue of an additional 3.47b shares by the Applicant to the exchanging members (those shares equating to a one-third interest in the Applicant, with an aggregate market value of one-third of $5.4b, or $1.8b); and

(c) following the share-for-share exchange in relation to Distribution, the market value of the Applicant was $5.4b (a 1% interest having a market value of $54m).

  1. At this stage, however, the “boost” argument had not been raised in any clear way. Ausnet’s written submissions had included only one short paragraph on the “nothing else” requirement, observing that the benefit of the provision is only available “where there is a strict “shares-for-share” exchange in the relevant entity” (at [52]). Ausnet’s appeal statement had included a contention that it had a particular “market value” immediately before the Distribution scheme (at [19(a)]), but this went only to the argument (discussed above) that the scheme was for rearranging its affairs as well as those of Distribution. Ausnet’s amended notice of appeal (which the primary judge granted leave to file in May 2023) also did not characterise a “boost” to the value of existing shares as part of what was received “in exchange for” the shares issued as part of the Distribution scheme; instead, it contended that “the property the taxpayer gave to acquire the shares in Distribution was the shares it issued to the shareholders of Distribution” (at [2(d)(i)(A)]). The “boost” argument emerged only during oral argument before the primary judge.
  2. In these circumstances, the agreement reflected in the parties’ written submissions is to be understood as laying an agreed foundation for the statutory construction arguments in relation to s 615-20(2). The key point for that purpose was that the shares in Ausnet held by each shareholder at the completion time included shares acquired as part of the Transmission and Finance Schemes, and part of the “market value” of each shareholder’s interest in Ausnet came from the assets already held in Ausnet before the Distribution scheme took effect. Counsel for the Commissioner would almost certainly have framed paragraph 96 of their submissions more guardedly if the “boost” argument had been in view.
  3. Meanwhile, no amendment was sought to be made to the JSAF, in which the parties had carefully set out, for the purposes of s 191 of the Evidence Act 1995 (Cth) (the Evidence Act), the facts which they had agreed for the purposes of the proceeding. Section 191(3)(a) provides that s 191(2) (which both obviates the need to adduce evidence as to an agreed fact and prevents such a fact being contradicted without leave) does not apply unless the fact is “stated in an agreement in writing signed by the parties” (or by the lawyers acting for them). The parties’ submissions as reproduced in the Appeal Book do not bear the signatures of any of the practitioners who prepared them, which is probably sufficient to take them outside s 191(3)(a). Accepting that the names of the parties’ counsel which appear on the written submissions may be sufficient for them to be regarded as “signed”, I would nevertheless hold that “an agreement in writing” in which a fact is “stated” means a single document signed by responsible persons on both sides of the record (or at least the adoption by one side of a specific form of words advanced by the other). The passages in the parties’ written submissions referred to above did not adopt identical wording and were not framed in the terms of an amendment or addition to the JSAF. Given the circumstances in which (and purpose for which) this additional agreement was expressed, it should not be treated as constraining the arguments open to the Commissioner (or the findings open to the primary judge) in respect of the values to be attributed to shares in Ausnet at various stages in the series of steps that occurred on 18 June 2015.
  4. The facts that were formally agreed in the JSAF, or emerge from documents in the agreed bundle, include the following.
(a) At the start of 18 June 2015 (which must be taken to mean the moment immediately after midnight), Transmission, Finance and Distribution had the values (and the number of shares on issue) set out in JSAF [15] (quoted at [155] above).

(b) At that time the shares in Transmission and Distribution and the units in Finance were “stapled” to each other. They were separate bundles of rights, which in principle had distinct values, but (until they were “unstapled”) could not be bought or sold independently of each other.

(c) In addition, trading in the stapled securities had been halted at the end of 5 June 2015. Nobody could buy or sell any of the securities on 18 June 2015.

(d) Shares in Ausnet had commenced trading on 9 June 2015 but only on a “deferred settlement” basis (on the ASX) or a “when issued” basis (on the SGX). The settlement date for such trades was fixed for 24 June 2015.

  1. Focusing on the moment before the Distribution scheme took effect, therefore, Ausnet had (as noted above) 6,933,826,018 shares on issue and held all the shares in Transmission and Finance. Those entities’ combined “market value” had been $3.399 billion, which presumably reflected the value of their assets, and those assets were now indirectly owned by Ausnet. Ausnet’s value was therefore around $0.49 per share. However, anybody entering into a contract to sell a parcel of Ausnet shares would have known that by the end of the day (and before the transaction could settle) there would be 10,400,739,027 Ausnet shares on issue and the company would be worth $5.424 billion (ie, around $0.52 per share). Had any sale occurred at that time, the price would have been agreed in contemplation of what was effectively certain to occur. It is obviously very unlikely that any seller would accept $0.49 as the price for shares that would be worth $0.52 moments later.
  2. What follows is that, while the Distribution scheme can be said to have increased the value of assets per share in Ausnet, and increased the “value” of the shares in that sense, that did not make any measurable practical difference to the value of the shares considered as property in the hands of the shareholders. Considered as items of property able to be traded or offered as security, at all times on 18 June 2015 they would have had a market value (assuming the existence of a market) that embodied the effect of the transactions programmed to take place on that day. Correspondingly, shares in Distribution, which had a value of $2.025 billion, or around $0.58 per share, would not have sold at that price (if it had been possible to sell them), because they were about to be transferred to Ausnet in exchange for Ausnet shares that would be worth $0.52.
  3. Accordingly, while the primary judge may have gone too far in describing the Transmission, Finance and Distribution schemes as “different, though connected, parts of the same transaction” (at [98], citing Isles), the close relationship between the schemes (and the effective inevitability of all three being completed once the process had begun) means that her Honour was correct to describe the value of performance of the remaining obligations on 18 June 2015 as “[e]mbedded in the value of the shares in the applicant issued under each of the schemes” (at [99]) and to conclude that none of the shares issued in Ausnet had a value that was independent of the Distribution scheme.
  4. For this reason, also, ground 2 fails.
  5. Properly understood, also, the approach of the primary judge was not inconsistent with anything in the JSAF. The “factual assumption” that her Honour identified and rejected at [90] did not include any of the facts identified as having been agreed in the JSAF. Rather, the “assumption” (which is perhaps better described as an inference) was the putative value of shares in Ausnet, after the Finance scheme had taken effect but before the Distribution scheme, which Ausnet’s submissions attempted to establish by extrapolation from the market values agreed in JSAF [15] (those values, as noted earlier, having been agreed as at the beginning of 18 June 2015). The area of debate was not whether any proposition in the JSAF was correct or whether the agreed facts were sufficient to dispose of the issues in dispute; it was what followed as a matter of inference from the facts established by the JSAF and the contents of the documents annexed to it. No issue arises as to the circumstances in which it is permissible for a court to refuse to accept facts that are agreed under s 191 of the Evidence Act.
  6. After the hearing, the primary judge invited further written submissions from the parties on the effect of cl 5.1(c) of the implementation deed (in particular its provision that completion of all the steps was interdependent) on the market values being contended for in their respective submissions. Procedural fairness did not require her Honour to go further than this. While valuation evidence might have shed light on the issue, her Honour was not required to invite such evidence and neither party sought leave to adduce it. Ausnet chose to develop an argument by extrapolation from the JSAF. Because of the late stage at which the argument emerged the lack of evidence to fill out the JSAF was likely to be, if anything, disadvantageous to the Commissioner.
  7. For these reasons ground 5 also fails.

Disposition

  1. The appeal should be dismissed with costs.
