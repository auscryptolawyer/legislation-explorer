ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Exempt Income - Association of pony clubs -Whether a "public educational institution".

Estate Duty Assessment Act, 1914-1947

REASONS FOR DECISION

26 May 1989 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. The issue for determination upon this reference is whether the Pony Club Association of New South Wales Limited ("the Association") is exempt from liability to income tax pursuant to the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936 ("the Act") on interest derived by it as assessable income. The Association bases its claim to exemption on the provisions of section 23 of the Act which (inter alia) provides:

"The following income shall be exempt from income tax:-

...

(e) the income of a religious, scientific, charitable or public educational institution;

...

(g) the income of a society, association or club which is not carried on for the purposes of profit or gain to individual members and is -

(i) ...

(ii) a society, association or club established for musical purposes or for the encouragement of music, art, science or literature.

(iii) a society, association or club established or the encouragement or promotion of an athletic game or athletic sport in which human beings are the sole participants; or

(iv) a society, association or club established for the encouragement or promotion of animal races;

(h) the income of a society or association not carried on for the purposes of profit or gain to the individual members thereof, established for the purpose of promoting the development of ... the agricultural, pastoral, ... resources of Australia.

2. The Association was incorporated as a company under the provisions of the Companies Act (New South Wales) in 1979. It does not have the benefit of a section 24 certificate (now s.66 of the Companies Code) entitling it to dispense with the use of the word "Limited" in the presentation of its title. Whether that is because such a certificate was not sought or, upon being sought, was refused was not disclosed by the evidence. In my view nothing turns on the point. Prior to its incorporation the Association existed as a voluntary association. As such it had been established in the 1950's. From its establishment it has been modelled on the principles espoused by the Pony Club of the United Kingdom and other Pony Clubs throughout the world. (At this point it is appropriate to say that the use of the name "pony" is in some sense misleading. The animals with which the Association is concerned are not confined to ponies (defined as less than 14 hands high) but extends to galloways (14 to 14.2 hands) and horses (14.2 hands or more).

3. The horse is an noble animal and the association of man and horse is an ancient one. The horse has proved to be a very useful animal for man and on the whole has been respected. The horse has been the means of transport in peace and war; his work-mate in hunting, agriculture and forestry; his beast of burden in the carriage and haulage of goods and people; a means to pleasure in sport and recreation; and a hoped for means to wealth through breeding, racing and gambling.

4. In days before the development of the internal combustion engine the horse played a far more significant role in society than it does today. But the horse is still favoured for its unique qualities, especially by those who see in the horse something more than a work animal or a means to wealth. Such horse lovers are concerned to pass on to coming generations their own interest in and respect for horses and to introduce those coming generations to a world in which the horse is a source of pleasure and the object of affection and concern. Throughout Australia many such persons join together in local associations with a view to bringing their own children and the children of others together to instruct them in the art of riding horsemanship; and the craft of horsemastership - the art of providing and caring for the horse without mounting it.

5. The extent of their interest is indicated by the evidence given that in the State of New South Wales there are approximately 278 clubs gathered together in a total of 26 regional associations (called "zones"), all affiliated with the Association as the State controlling body. The Association in turn is affiliated with similar Associations in other States, just as it is with the Pony Club in the United Kingdom. The evidence of community participation was imprecise but it was estimated (and the estimates were not challenged) that in all of the Pony Clubs of Mew South Wales there would be some 14,000 to 15,000 persons in the under 17 years age group from; some 4,000 members in the 17-25 age group; and about 8,000 adult supporters. Some 2,000 of the 17 years plus members would play some role in life of the clubs as instructors.

6. The Association exists to serve those interests by co-ordinating the activities of the zones and the clubs within them; by promoting standards of instruction for adoption in all clubs; by providing courses of instruction for instructors, and also for pupils seeking to advance their qualifications by examination in practice and theory; by introducing overseas and other specialist coaches to give instruction throughout the State; by arranging for members to gain experience in the world of equitation internationally; and by prescribing grading standards for different classes of certification as to competance and experience.

7. In those undertakings it seeks to bring together in combination highly skilled riders on well trained horses. Achievement of that aim requires that the rider becomes proficient in caring for his horse and in recognising and attending to its needs.

8. The formal definition of those objectives as expressed in the Memorandum of Association of the Association is as follows:-

"(2) The objects for which the Association was established are

  1. to encourage the formation of amateur Pony Clubs in New South Wales and to regulate the general line of instruction in horsemanship and horsemastership.
  2. to endeavour to bring about at exhibitions conducted by affiliated Pony Clubs and Agricultural and Pastoral Association Shows uniformity in the judging of Children's equestrian classes, also in other amateur competitive events in horsemanship and horsemastership in Australia, in accordance with the method of instruction as laid down by the Association.
  1. to endeavour to bring about uniformity in conditions governing competitions and the furnish affiliated Pony Clubs and agricultural and pastoral associations with particulars of events which may be included in their show programs in accordance with the by laws of this Association.

...

  1. to encourage young people to ride and learn to enjoy all kinds of sport connected with horses and riding. To provide instruction on riding and horsemanship and to instil in members the proper care of their animals. To promote the highest ideals of sportsmanship, citizenship and loyalty, thereby by cultivating strength of character and self discipline.

1. to encourage and promote races and contests with horses."

9. Some of the young riders later become professional riders, such as jockeys, stockmen and police officers. Others work in closely related fields, such as staffing studs and stables; and as teachers in courses presented by public colleges and schools in relation to horsemanship and horsemastership. Some go on to participate, even at Olympic standard, in recreational and sporting competition,

10. It was not suggested that a high percentage of youngsters attain any of those standards of proficiency at a high level. Most riders must content themselves with a lesser level of accomplishment, even if they would prefer to be more proficient. No doubt most are simply content with their lot. However, hopefully from the point of view of the Association, their experience in the clubs will have given them a greater respect for horses and concern for them. That respect and concern should be reflected in, and reflective of, a great sense of civic responsibility in the club member.

11. In pursuit of those matters the Association carefully monitors the selection of instructors at club level. Those instructors join together at zone level under the leadership of a Chief Zone Instructor. It is principally through the instructors that every reasonable endeavour is made to improve standards of equitation, with special assistance being rendered to any clubs in need.

12. With an organisation of the size of the Association; with nearly 280 clubs grouped in 26 zones, the need exists for a general administrative body with an Executive Director as principal officer. She works under the direction of an Executive Committee which in turn is accountable to a general Council of the Association. I am satisfied that that organisational structure exists to support the implementation of the objectives of the Association and, in particular, to assist the instructors in their undertakings.

13. Recognising the potential hazards for youngsters and inexperienced riders if faced with the challenges of a horse or task beyond their capacity, special care is taken to ensure that the capacity of the rider is not over-rated. To that end there is a regular on-going assessment of performance. To ensure continued improvement in theoretical and practical skills there is a regular systematic course of instruction according to a pre-planned and thoroughly documented syllabus which is followed throughout the State. In formulation of the syllabus care is taken to confer with similar bodies in other States. To that end the several State bodies join together in a National Conference at regular intervals.

14. The Association recognises that it needs to encourage children to continue about their courses of instruction and to accept the discipline of continual care for their animals. That being so, the Association accepts the need to stimulate their interest by presenting challenges and diversions; challenges by way of the stimulus of competition; and diversions by way of a variety of experiences directed to the development of horsemanship skills.

15. Since the principal objective of the Association is to bring into harmony a skilled rider on a trained horse which is in sound condition, the Association seeks to develop skills in two fields; horsemanship and horsemastership. The former is presented through a program of exercises which embrace lungeing; dressage; show jumping; cross country riding; and a variety of novelty events directed to testing the skills of horse and rider in combination.

16. Sponsorship of competition between riders is promoted as a means to encourage youngsters to improve their skills and performance. As a result rallies of different types are periodically organised at different levels within the total organisation. Those rallys provide opportunities for instructors to confer; and for their performances and those of their charges to be assessed; and for assistance to be organised for clubs in need.

17. For the individual pupil the regimen is one calling for daily care of the horse; regular club training - usually weekly; and occasional special local, regional or State functions at which the pupil may be selected to represent his club or zone in competition, and at which his own proficiency may be assessed for certification purposes. Progression is basically through a series of certificates known as D, C, B and A certificates, with few only progressing beyond C certificate. Seminars and conferences are also organised for the instructors.

18. The clubs and the Association all rely heavily on the voluntary unpaid support of instructors and other supporters, such as parents and others, who are, perhaps vicariously, devotees of equitation. It is only in exceptional circumstances, such as the recruiting of highly qualified instructors from overseas or interstate, that any person engaged in instruction is paid for his services.

22. The case was argued on the basis that the Commissioner accepted that the principle of mutuality would exclude from liability to assessment any surplus derived by the Association through its dealings with its members. As nothing was said of it by either Counsel, it seems to have been assumed that the grants received annually from the Department of Sport, Recreation and Racing were not considered to constitute assessable income, despite their recurrent nature and despite the fact that, at least in some sense, the grants operated as a "subsidy". As the matter was argued before me the only question to be determined is whether the Applicant is assessable to income tax upon its investment income. It was not suggested that any portion of the expenses incurred by the Association was attributable to the derivation of that investment income.

23. As it now 1989 and as the first year of assessment is the year of income ended 30 June 1981, it is appropriate to acknowledge the sense of injustice felt by the Association in relation to one aspect of its dispute with the Commissioner. I refer to the existence of section 23(g) of the Act; introduced with effect from 1 July 1985. The Act now provides that the income of bodies such as the Association is exempt from tax if the Association is

"established for the encouragement or promotion of animal races" (sub-paragraph (iv))

but not if it is for

"the promotion of an athletic game or athletic sport (unless human beings are the sole participants)" (sub paragraph (iii ) ) .

24. If the view of the Commissioner in the matter is correct, an organisation such as the present Applicant which promotes the welfare of horses and the development of children will not be exempt from taxation. But horse racing clubs which, it might be thought, are not dominated by concern for children or regard for horses, beyond a respect for their quality as thoroughbreds and, thereby, their capacity to provide entertainment for owners and spectators; to generate income for punters and bookmakers; and, in both of the last mentioned cases, for the State through the taxation of betting revenues, will be exempt from taxation. Following the passage of that amending legislation the Association amended the objects clause in its Memorandum of Association to incorporate clause 2(1) (previously quoted) to specify as an object:-

"to encourage and promote races and contests with horses."

25. In doing so I accept that nothing in substance had changed as to the objectives of the Association. Rather what had occurred was that the Association was seeking by the alteration to express its objectives in terms which it hoped might be thought by the Commissioner to have satisfied the new statutory provision. In doing so it sought to include within the concept of "racing" the time/distance factors in its own training programs as being as much "racing" as the more common form of having regard only to achieving minimum time over a specified distance, whether the events be by way of gallops, trotting or other competitive racing.

26. The first and principal submission for the Applicant is that I should find that it is a "public educational institution" in the sense in which that phrase is used in section 23(e) of the Act. That paragraph provides

"(23) The following income shall be exempt from income tax:-

(e) The income of a religious, scientific, charitable or public educational institution;

27. In considering that phrase I consider it appropriate to have regard, not only to the terms used to make up the phrase, but also to the circumstance that it is a compound expression. It sould be recognised that, as Fullagar J. said (in Lloyd v F.C.T. [1955] HCA 71; (1955) 93 C.L.R. 645 @ 667) when considering another compound expression -

"public educational purposes" - used in a statute, and intended to define the nature of certain gifts which are to be exempt from duty (that) the elements which make up that compound expression cannot be separated and considered in isolation from one another."

As Dixon C.J. said in the same case (ibid, p660):

"It is a compound phrase and like most compound phrases its application cannot safely be ascertained by taking each separate word of which it is composed and then exploring the uses of which by itself the word is capable."

28. But their Honours did not say that the ordinary meanings of the separate words were to be ignored. In the context of this case I am satisfied that the Association did constitute an "institution". If authority be needed for that proposition I refer to the decision of Gibbs, J. (as he then was) in Stratton v Simpson ((1970) [1970] HCA 45; 125 C.L.R. 138 @ 158) where his Honour said:

"in its ordinary sense 'institution' means an establishment, organisation or association instituted for the promotion of some object, especially one of public utility, religious, charitable, educational etc. (the Shorter Oxford English Dictionary)".

It means, as was said in Mayor etc. of Manchester v McAdam (1896 A.C. 500 at 511), "an undertaking formed to promote some defined purpose" or "the body (so to speak) called into existence to translate the purpose as conceived in the mind of the founders into a living and active principle".

29. I am also satisfied that, in the ordinary sense of the phrase "public institution", the Association in "public". Its concerns are the concerns of a substantial group in the community and it represents a large number of persons from within that group who are spread geographically throughout the State. Membership is open to all who share that common interest. Through the organisation those in the State of New South Wales having that interest are able to share in that interest with others of like mind interstate and overseas and, in association with them, to promote the purposes of the organisation throughout Australia and even overseas. I am also satisfied that, considered alone, the organisation might also be described as "educational", even if only in the sense expressed by Dixon C.J. in Lloyd (ante p660) when his Honour said:-

"No doubt the word "educational" may be used with reference to any process of instruction direction or control to which the young are submitted, however intermittently, if it has a purpose or tendency, or supposed tendency, of developing some of the faculties or forming character but having said so much of the individual terms the question remains - and it is a difficult question - as to whether it is appropriate to characterise the organisation as "a public educational institution".

30. In approaching that question it is appropriate to recognise that the law, like education itself, is a living and changing thing, which should be ever responsive to the changing needs and perceptions of the community. What may be recognised as "educational" in one generation will not necessarily be recognised as "educational" in another. At the present time it is my view that the term "educational" is not to be considered as confined to "classical studies" or to the pursuits of those intelectually disposed to learning. Nor is it to be considered as a term to be construed in contrast to terms such as "training"; nor with a view to a result whereby institutions fostering intellectual development have conferred upon them a tax exempt status to be denied to organisations devoted to the development and promotion of manual or physical skills. Nor is there any reason to consider that the arts of music, painting and dance are to be seen as more educational than the craft skills of horsemanship or motor mechanics.

31. In determining whether the Applicant satisfies the criteria for a "public educational institution", I must be guided principally by the decisions of the High Court of Australia. No authority better expresses the principles to be applied, and the difficulty of applying them, than the decision in Lloyd. The question to be determined in that case was whether a testamentary bequest in favour of the Geelong Branch of the "Navy League Sea Cadets" was exempt from estate duty under the provisions of Estate Duty Assessment Act (1914-1947):(Commonwealth) by reason of being a gift for "public educational purposes". A divided court (McTiernan, Webb and Kitto, J.J.: Dixon C.J. and Fullagar, J. dissenting) held that the League had been formed as a voluntary association in 1932 as a "strictly non-political and non-sectarian" body, with all positions in the organisation to be honorary. Its declared objective was

"to keep alive the sea spirit of our race, also to assist cadets to become good citizens of Australia and the Empire by voluntarily accepting discipline and by doing their duty in the spirit of the motto, "for God, Queen, the Empire and Australia".

These aims were to be achieved by "training" including – as referred to by Dixon, C.J. -

(1) character building;

(2) swimming, lifesaving (first aid);

(3) squad drill, marching;

(4) mariners compas construction and uses;

(5) helm construction and uses;

(6) anchors, construction and uses;

(7) boat management, oars and sail;

(8) knots, bends, hitches and splices;

(9) rule of the road;

(10) lead line and markings;

(11) semaphore signalling; and

(12) healthy recreation. "

32. It was found that, in the implementation of those objectives, there were regular sporting competitions; and that the cadets assembled for comparatively brief periods twice a week; but that there was no organised course of study, no systematic preparation for a defined end.

33. The majority of the Court considered that structure program to be sufficient to qualify the organisation for the exemption sought. McTiernan, J. (at page 662) said

"If the subjects of the program of instruction are taken individually it may be said with force that instruction in a number of them would not ordinarily be regarded as education. I think the proper method is to consider the subjects as a combination designed as a preparation for the calling of a sailor and a way of teaching youths its traditions and ideals of service. Viewing the work of the corps in that way I think it is correct to say that its purposes are educational".

34. Bearing in mind the need to consider the phrase in question in context, it is also appropriate to record what his Honour said of the "public" element. He said (at page 662) :

"I think that the navy cadet corps at its Geelong branch satisfy that test. Their objects are entirely public. Neither of them is carried on purely for private gain. Upon the facts stated in the case I think the educational activities in which the central body of the organisation and this branch engage are conducted for public benefit and especially for the section of the public consisting of the youth who are training as cadets".

Webb, J. said (at page 665):

"It is also, I think, an educational institution in as much as its main purpose is systematically to impart useful knowledge of a technical nature to youths with a view to such knowledge being employed eventually to the public advantage as well as their own. It is not necessary that the instruction imparted should be of a general educational nature; Royal Choral Society Case ((1943) 2 A.E.R. 101). The recreation provided for is merely incidental to the main purpose. Lastly it is clearly for the benefit of a section of the public".

Kitto J. said (at page 676)

"The training which is the raison d'etre of the corps is such as would teach a lad whose ambitions lie towards a sea-going career some of the rudiments of his intended craft and afford him some intellectual equipment and some stimulus towards further preparation. It is evident, of course, that the majority of cadets will not be of this kind and will join and continue in the corps for the sake of the interest, and the fun which they get out of its activities. That fact however, seems to me to be irrelevant, for it cannot affect the true characterisation of the purpose to which property is devoted when given to a branch of the corps. Those purposes, seriously pursued as they are by a responsible body acting in a organised and disciplined manner, appear to me to be essentially and predominantly educational."

35. In those passages it can be seen that the Court was concerned rather more to analyse what the adult generation which controlled the organisation was seeking to impart to youth for the sake of youth, and not for the financial advantage of their elders. For that reason the Court was less concerned with the question as to whether the youngsters were enjoying themselves as with the question as to whether they were learning anything of community value. Against that background it becomes instructive to consider closely the reasons for dissent expressed by the Chief Justice. He said (at page 661):

"But the application to the corps of the expression "public educational purposes" as a compound expression strikes one as most unnatural. The central or basal characteristics to which the exemption is directed may be seen in the schools, colleges, universities, technical schools and schools of art which are so familiar to us. It may by conceded that the application of the exemption is not restricted to such institutions. Instruction and training of the young may take many different forms and no doubt the expression "public-educational purposes" will find applications to systematic methods or procedures for the inculcation of knowledge, whether scholastic or vocational, for the cultivation of the mental and physical powers and for the development of character although they may vary widely from those practiced or illustrated in the examples of formal education that are more familiar to the community. But even so the Sea Cadet Corps seems remote indeed in objects organisation and procedure from anything that may be supposed to fall within the denotation of the expression. Its organisation and discipline are reflected from the Navy. The cadets assemble for comparatively brief periods twice a week. There is no organised course of study, no systematic preparation for a defined end. What is done is for the promotion of the welfare of youth by providing associations, interests and discipline arising from the sea and the naval and maratime services. Highly as this may be commended, it does not seem to me to be what the legislature meant by "public educational purposes".

36. As I understand it from that passage, his Honour was pointing to the inadequacy of the system of instruction and to the nature of the activities undertaken. But in considering those views it must be remembered that the Chief Justice and Fullagar, J. were dissentients.

37. The question of status of the League arose for consideration in the period following World War II. The testator had died in 1946. Now, a similar question has to be addressed in relation to the early 1980's with reference to the principal public organisation dealing with youth instruction in horsemanship and horsemastership. In comparing the Association of the 80's with the League of the post Second World War period, I do not consider it to be significant that the Association is concerned with both sexes; or that a military orientation is lacking; or that there is little by way of declarations of patriotism and of loyalty to "Queen, Empire and Australia".

38. However, what I do find to be significant is the absence in the organisers of a concern to advance their own financial interest; their commitment to advance the well-being of their pupils and to promote the crafts and skills of horsemanship and horsemastership; and the planned, ordered and regular system of instruction. They are all factors pointing strongly towards recognition of the Association as a "public educational institution" being appropriate. Against that, the program does carry substantial emphasis on recreation and the development of sporting skills, developed (inter alia) by competition in an internationally acknowledged field of sporting activity, all overlying what is for these instructors, and is planned to be for their pupils, a pleasant recreational and sporting activity which for a few only will lead to a related income-producing activity in later life.

39. I have taken into account the circumstance that the adult organisers seek no profit for themselves, either as member supporters or as persons active in its undertakings. In doing so I acknowledge that, in limited circumstances, income is derived through Association activities by some few persons such as the Executive Director and, occasionally, by reason of special capacity to promote the cause, for highly qualified instructors. Despite that, the organisation readily falls within a category of those institutions, whether religious, scientific, charitable (in the non-technical sense), or otherwise, which exist to serve the public and to do so without profit, or prospect of profit, for organisers or members. That circumstance no doubt goes a long way towards explaining the "grant" paid by the State.

40. Since the "profit" generated by the activities of the organisation is something which must be committed to the service of the community by its use in pursuit of the objects of the organisation, and in particular through the education of a coming generation of youth, it is not altogether surprising that along with religious, scientific and "charitable" organisations, its community role should be such that the community at large does not make any claim upon its revenues at the expense of a small but significant element of the community.

41. In all the circumstances, and notwithstanding the cogency of the view of Dixon, C.J., but respecting the authority of the decision in Lloyd, I have concluded that the Applicant is exempt from liability to taxation. Having formed that opinion, I express no view on the alternative contentions argued before me.

42. The order of the Tribunal will be that the decisions of the Commissioner under review shall be varied and the objections of the Applicant wholly allowed.
