Re Robert Arthur Secourable and Commissioner of Superannuation [1993] AATA 94; (1993) 29 ALD 879 (19 March 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: ROBERT ARTHUR SECOURABLE

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. W92/177

AAT No. 8609

Number of pages - 6

Superannuation

[1993] AATA 94; (1993) 29 ALD 879 (extract)

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

T E Barnett(1) (Senior Member), J G Billings(1) (Member) and R D F Lloyd(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Deferred benefit - election outside prescribed period - discretion to recognise election - whether discretion should be exercised - whether failure to inform employee of rights - misleading information - long delay - whether acceptable explanation for delay - prejudice to respondent - decision affirmed

Commonwealth Superannuation Act 1976 ss.135, 136, 137, 157

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 s.37

Re Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT unreported decision No. 8553; 26 February 1993)

Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456

Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Minister for Home Affairs and Environment (1984) 58 ALR 305

Lucic v Nolan (1982) 45 ALR 11

HEARING

PERTH, 25-26 February 1993

19:3:1993

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

T.E. BARNETT, J.G. BILLINGS AND R.D.F. LLOYD This is an application to review a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation dated 18 September 1990 and confirmed on 7 July 1992. The decision, made under subsection 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") was that the Commissioner declined to direct that an election made by the applicant on 17 June 1990 under subsection 137(1) should be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that subsection of the Act. The relevant parts of s.137 are as follows:

"s.137(1) A person who -

(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to

which paragraph (b) does not apply and, upon so ceasing, is

not entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit

in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or

(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by

virtue of which the person is to be deemed, under subsection

58(3), to have retired involuntarily;

may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be

an eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the

Commissioner that this Division apply in relation to the

person.

(2) If a person makes an election for the purposes of this section

within 1 month before he ceases to be an eligible employee, the

election has effect as if it had been made on the day after he

ceased to be an eligible employee.

(3) Subject to subsection (4) an election under this section is of

no effect unless the person who made the election gives notice in

writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such further

period as the Commissioner allows, after expiration of the period

that is the prescribed period in relation to him -

(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of the

prescribed period and, if so, the name and address of the

employer; and

(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the

prescribed period a member of the superannuation scheme

applicable in relation to that employment and, if so,

specifying the scheme concerned."

2. Subsection 157(1) contains a power to recognise an election made after the prescribed time (of 21 days) in certain circumstances. It is in these terms:

"s.157(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act,

where an election under this Act is made by a person after

the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for

the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied

that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable

that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may

direct that the election be treated as if it had been made

within the period allowed and the election shall have effect

accordingly."

3. The applicant was born on 4 January 1931 and was first employed by the Postmaster General's Department (now known as Telecom Australia) as an assistant technician from 1956 to 1979. On 14 March 1961 he became a permanent employee of Telecom and automatically became a member of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme ("CSS"). He also became an eligible employee pursuant to s.3(1) of the Act.

4. On 24 May 1979 the applicant tendered his resignation from Telecom to take effect from 6 June 1979 and the resignation was accepted by Telecom.

5. The applicant gave evidence that his purpose in resigning was to enter into a business partnership with a friend running a newsagency. His evidence that he consulted a current Telecom booklet setting out terms and conditions of employment (the yellow book) is accepted by the Tribunal and it is clear that the booklet specified that employees who resigned were able to receive a refund of the employees contributions to the fund plus interest. It made no mention at all of the other option which had been introduced by the Act in 1976 which allowed them to preserve their benefits by leaving their contributions in the fund. The effect of exercising this second option (option 2) would be that on reaching normal retirement age whether or not the employee ever resumed employment with the Commonwealth, the employee would be entitled to the full benefits of the superannuation scheme including pension rights and lump sum payment. In the event of his death prior to that date his widow would have been entitled to pension rights.

6. After receiving his notice of resignation the personnel section of Telecom processed the resignation by forwarding to the applicant an application for benefits form.

7. The applicant cannot now recall which form was forwarded to him and signed by him, but he says that he clearly remembers that the form did not alert him to option 2 that he could preserve his benefits. He said that the form only provided for him applying for a refund of his contributions. The T documents contained a staff resignation advice form for the applicant which indicates in the bottom right hand corner by a tick in a box that "SB2 sent to contributor". The SB2 form was a superseded form which did not mention anything about the option to preserve the benefits and merely dealt with an application for refund of contributions. A great deal of evidence was received about which form was probably signed by the applicant and the Tribunal is reasonably satisfied on the balance of probabilities that he did in fact sign the form S2A. On this point the Tribunal was impressed by the evidence of Mr Berry and Ms O'Neill about the procedures adopted in the personnel section in 1979 when an S2A form was received. The staff in the section would fill in by hand the appropriate details of the resigning employee on a photocopy of the departmental report section on the S2A form. The information would be obtained from computer print outs and other sources and would be filled neatly into the sections provided on the photocopy of the S2A form. During this process a carbon copy would be kept. The handwriting would then be handed to a typist with the S2A form signed by the employee and the typist would type in the handwritten details neatly into the marked spaces on the bottom of the signed S2A form. The carbon copies of the handwritten and typed information were tendered as part of the T documents in respect to the applicant's application for benefits and it is clear that they fit neatly into the S2A spaces on the form. They do not fit into the SB2 form however. For this reason the Tribunal, having heard the evidence of both Mr Berry and Ms O'Neill, is satisfied that the applicant signed an S2A form when applying for his benefits.

8. In order to persuade the Tribunal to extend the time for making his election the applicant is obliged to satisfy the Tribunal that it would be desirable to do so, the applicant's arguments are mainly based on the fact that he was never sufficiently informed of his right to elect option 2 to defer his benefits. For this reason it is important to look at the opening sections of form S2A which were as follows:

APPLICATION FOR REFUND OF ACCUMULATED CONTRIBUTIONS/

LUMP SUM BENEFIT OR

ELECTION FOR PRESERVATION OF SUPERANNUATION RIGHTS

Note: This application form should be completed by a contributor who resigns or a contributor who retires and who elects to exercise an option to take a Lump Sum Benefit in lieu of a pension entitlement.

This form should not be completed by a contributor who is applying for a pension and a refund of accumulated contributions or supplementary contributions.

I, ....................................................................

First Names Surname (BLOCK LETTERS)

of .......................................................Postcode.....

Postal Address

formerly employed by .................... in the State of..............

Department/Authority

as...............................................

Designation

apply for a refund of accumulated contributions )

hereby * apply for payment of a lump sum benefit )

under the provisions of the Superannuation Act

elect for preservation of superannuation rights )

* Delete whichever is inapplicable

Signature of Applicant.................................................

ETC.....

9. The Tribunal feels that the form is ambiguous in that it states in the opening instructions that it should be completed by a contributor who resigns "or a contributor who retires and who elects to exercise an option to lump sum benefit in lieu of a pension entitlement". As the applicant was a person who was resigning not a person who was retiring it is reasonable for him to have interpreted that phrase as applying only to a person who was retiring and who would therefore have the right to make the election.

10. The Tribunal notes that when Telecom wrote to the applicant notifying him of the acceptance of his resignation it provided advice about seeking a refund of superannuation but did not mention of the other option of applying to preserve his benefits by leaving his money in the scheme.

11. The Tribunal accepts the applicant's submission that at the time of his retirement in May-July 1979 he did not have knowledge of the fairly recent provision allowing him to preserve his benefits and he was not sufficiently advised by the respondent or by Telecom of this option. That finding is based on the ambiguity in form S2A, the failure to refer to option 2 in the letter accepting his resignation, the failure to mention that option in the Telecom terms and conditions of service booklet and because no other information verbal or written was provided to the applicant on this subject.

12. The Tribunal accepts the applicant's evidence that he commenced the partnership business and that he received a refund of his own contributions amounting to $5,500.48. It also accepts his evidence and that of his wife's uncontested statement, accepted into evidence, that the refunded money was paid into an RandI Bank Account and then spent during the next twelve months on a variety of non-essential purchases and modifications to their house.

13. The applicant gave evidence that if he had been advised of his right to leave his superannuation contributions in the fund and that this would have meant he would eventually gain the benefits of the 18 years of employer's contribution (and that his would be pensioned if he died), he would have elected to preserve his benefits. He also gave evidence that within a month of retiring he took out an insurance policy in favour of his wife to ensure her of a lump sum entitlement in the event of his death. The Tribunal accepts his claim that if he had been properly advised in 1979 he would have elected to preserve his benefits.

14. Had the applicant become aware of his entitlement to preserve the benefits within the next few months after retirement and sought to have the time for making the election extended, the application would probably have been granted.

15. This did not happen however, and after spending the refunded money the applicant continued in his business and later in outside employment until 1984 when he rejoined Telecom in his old job. After rejoining Telecom he again became an eligible employee and a member of the superannuation scheme. Evidence was given that a new contributor to the scheme would almost always have been given the leaflet for new contributors and the Tribunal feels that it is more likely than not that this leaflet was also given to the applicant though his evidence is that he has no recollection of that. If he received that leaflet he should have seen that it clearly set out the rights for people who resigned under the CSS to opt for preservation of their benefits.

16. Even if the applicant was not given the new contributors leaflet he has agreed that on several occasions he was given the annual information to contributors document and the documents for 1987 and 1988 were tendered in evidence. On the front of these documents a statement of the applicant's account with the scheme is set out, and the back of the document contains more details of the scheme including quite clearly a section describing the option for persons resigning to elect to preserve their rights by leaving their contributions in the fund.

17. The applicant says that he never noticed those details about the scheme set out on the back of the information document and that he therefore did not receive information about his rights to preserve his benefits. He further says that in any event it would only have informed him of the entitlements as at the date shown on the document (1987 or 1988), it would not have alerted him to the fact that he had been entitled to the same option on the date of his retirement in 1979.

18. The Tribunal feels that this is a very different situation to that which faced the applicant in 1979. In deciding whether it is desirable to allow the period for making the election to be extended the question of unreasonable delay becomes quite crucial as the longer the period which passes before the election is made the more detriment is created for the respondent and the more difficult it is for the factual situation to be properly explored. When the applicant rejoined Telecom he was an older man than the 48 year old who resigned in 1979. In 1984 he was 53 years old and approaching the time when he would be entitled to take early retirement at age 55 under the scheme. The Tribunal considers that he should and would have been trying to make himself more aware of his entitlements to superannuation in these circumstances. He must have realised on rejoining Telecom that he was now joining a superannuation scheme very late in life and he must have been regretful that he was unable to get the benefit of his 18 years of previous contributions. The applicant was now receiving information regularly about entitlements of contributors to the scheme, including the current entitlement of resignees to elect to preserve their benefits. This should have alerted him to make inquiries about his possible entitlement back in 1979. The Tribunal considers that a combination of all the circumstances existing between 1984 and 1989 should have been alerting the applicant to make those inquiries, and his failure to do so has contributed to the very long delay before he finally made his application in 1990. With some regret the Tribunal finds that by 1984 any misleading effect of the Telecom booklet, the ambiguous forms and the failure to provide information in 1979 should have been offset by the fact that he was now receiving accurate information and was in the position to make further inquiries.

19. The authorities indicate that when a person is applying for an extension of time to lodge applications for judicial review there is an evidentiary onus on that person to establish satisfactory reasons for the delay in bringing that application and that these are quite firmly enforced by the courts. Lucic v Nolan (1982) 45 ALR 411 per Fitzgerald J p 416:

"I consider that an applicant for an extension of time maintains

throughout the burden of showing why in all the circumstances

the extension of time should be granted. I do not think that,

given proof of certain matters by an applicant, for example an

explanation of his delay in making application, an evidentiary

onus shifts to the respondents to establish that prejudice will

result if the extension is granted. Nor, in my opinion, if the

delay is explained and there will be no personal prejudice to

the named respondents, should an extension always be granted."

Hunter Valley Developments v Minister for Home Affairs and Environment (1984) 58 ALR 305 per Wilcox J at p 311 (dealing with extension of time under s.11 ASDJR Act):

"(a) Although this section does not in terms place any onus of

proof upon an applicant for extension, an application has to be

made. Special circumstances need not be shown, but the court

will not grant the application unless positively satisfied that

it is proper so to do ... to extend time."

As pointed out in Re Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT decision no. 8553; 26 February 1993) when an applicant is applying for an extension of time which if granted will give him substantive rights, the Tribunal should be even more rigorous in insisting that there are adequate explanations for the delay.

20. The Tribunal is content to accept the definition of Deputy President Forgie of what is "desirable" Liddle v Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; 14 AAR 456:

"It can only be desirable to recognise a late election if there

are objectively good reasons which justify the person not being

required to comply with the time limits otherwise clearly

specified in the act."

The Tribunal also accepts the way Deputy President McMahon in Lander at p 21-25 applied the principles of the Hunter Valley case in order to determine whether in all the circumstances it is desirable to recognise the late election.

21. After considering all the evidence and materials before it the Tribunal finds that it was not reasonable for the applicant to delay making the election to preserve his entitlements after he rejoined the CSS in 1984 and started receiving regular information about his superannuation entitlements. In the words of Deputy President McMahon, at that stage he really only had himself to blame for not informing himself of his entitlements. Had he made inquiries this would have indicated that he did have an entitlement in 1979. In the circumstances of this case the Tribunal finds that it is not desirable that the late election should be recognised and treated as if it had been made within the period allowed in s.137 of the Act. Decision

22. The decision under review is affirmed.
