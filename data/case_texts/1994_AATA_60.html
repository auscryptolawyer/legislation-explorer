Re Taxation Appeals [1994] AATA 60; (1994) 28 ATR 1001 (9 March 1994)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. WT93/44

AAT No. 9356

Number of pages - 7

Income Tax

[1994] AATA 60; (1994) 28 ATR 1001

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

P. GERBER (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Trading stock - Request by taxpayer for leave of the Commissioner of Taxation to adopt a basis of valuation of his live stock at end of the year of income different to basis on which valuation was made at the end of the previous year - Request based on decline of market value of live stock during the year of income - Discussion of circumstances when leave should be granted - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, s 33.

HEARING

PERTH, 21 October 1993

9:3:1994

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr R W F Sceales

Solicitors for the Applicant: Phillips Fox

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr K J Martin

Solicitors for the Respondent: Australian Government

Solicitor

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the objection decision under review.

DECISION

P. GERBER At all relevant times the applicant was a partner in a family farming partnership ("the partnership"). Both the applicant's and the partnership's income tax return for the 1990 year of income included a "Section 169A request for ruling"

CHANGE IN VALUATION OF SHEEP - 30 JUNE 1990

The partners at 30 June 1990, valued sheep on hand at MARKET

SELLING VALUE.

This action represented a departure from the previous method of

adopting AVERAGE COST.

The decision to effect this change resulted from the downturn

in the rural sector, and in particular the crisis in the sheep

and wool industry, causing sheep values to decline

dramatically. A decline expected to continue for some

considerable time.

The continuance with average cost valuation created an

accounting profit, which in the prevailing industry conditions

was not capable of realisation.

It is requested that in accord with the terms of Section 33 of

the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (as amended) the

Commissioner grant leave for the partners to adopt the changed

valuation for sheep at 30 June 1990.

2. The "Income Tax Adjustment Sheet" for the 1990 year of income which accompanied a Notice of Assessment, dated 15 May 1991, stated with respect to the sec 169A request:

... Based on the information provided you are advised that the

change in valuation method of sheep in accordance with section

33 has not been granted.

This resulted in an adjustment, increasing the applicant's "primary production income" by $5,738.

3. On 5 July 1991, the applicant objected against that assessment. By letter dated 13 August 1991, the respondent advised that the objection was disallowed. By letter dated 7 October 1991, the applicant advised that he was dissatisfied with the objection decision and requested that the matter be referred to this Tribunal for review. On 18 May 1993, the respondent did as the applicant had requested.

4. At the hearing of this application the applicant was represented by Mr R W F Sceales of Phillips Fox, Perth, and the respondent by Mr K J Martin of Counsel, instructed by the Australian Government Solicitor. Shortly after its commencement, the hearing was made public at Mr Sceales' request on behalf of the applicant.

5. I was advised that there was no issue as to the values attributable to the sheep at the relevant time, and that the various necessary calculations and schedules were set out in the "T" documents (exh "A"). I also had before me a page setting out various statistics from the Australian Wool Corporation in relation to wool prices for the period 1987/88 to 1992/93 inclusive (exh "B").

6. If the value of the flock were to be calculated at the end of the 1990 year of income as contended for by the respondent, i.e. using the cost price method, then a gross profit of $11,246.21 would have accrued to the partnership from the sale of sheep during that year. If, however, the partnership were given leave to alter its method of valuation to the market selling value method (a method inconsistent with that used to value the flock at the commencement of that year of income and in previous years of income), the partnership would incur a gross loss of $17,445.06 from the sale of sheep during that year of income.

7. Section 33 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") provides that:

A taxpayer shall not, except with the leave of the

Commissioner, adopt a basis of valuation of his live stock

taken into account at the end of the year of income different

from the basis on which the valuation of his live stock was

made when it was last taken into account at the end of a

previous year, whether under this or the previous Act.

8. The issue before the Tribunal is whether, on the facts as disclosed in the evidence, it, standing in the shoes of the Commissioner, should grant the partnership (with consequences for the applicant) leave to adopt the change in valuation method sought in the "sec 169A request" in respect of the 1990 year of income.

9. The only witness to give evidence before the Tribunal was a Mr Edward Pedlow. This gentleman's experience in the sheep and wool industry dates from 1947. Between 1947 and 1982 he was intimately involved in the industry, working in very senior and responsible positions involving the purchasing and export of wool for CIL, a firm of French wool merchants. From 1964 to late 1988 he was also involved in the industry as an owner of a property on which he ran some 13,500 sheep. In recent times he has been chairperson of various entities involved in the processing of wool. It is fair to say that Mr Pedlow is a person who, over a lengthy period of time, has acquired an expansive knowledge of matters concerning the sheep and wool industry, including exporting, wool merchanting and production, and the farming of sheep. He was an impressive witness whose evidence relevant to this application I accept without reservation.

10. Mr Pedlow described the operation of the minimum price maintenance arrangement in respect of Australian-grown wool, which commenced in the early 1970s, thus:

Well, approximately 25 years ago or slightly less ... the

Government and the growers decided they would bring in a

reserve minimum price and wool could not be sold below that

price to the trade and indeed, it would be purchased by the

Australian Wool Corporation ... and kept in stock. In other

words there was a maintenance of price. This went right through

until February 1991 ...

when this arrangement was abandoned.

11. According to Mr Pedlow, the overall effect of that arrangement was that:

it caused confidence in the market because you knew what value

of wool your sheep was going to produce - the minimum value of

wool your sheep could produce. Therefore that caused a

confidence in the price of sheep and which indeed reflected on

the price of all types of sheep; mutton, sheep in general, the

export, wethers, the whole lot because it underwrote the value

of the wool you would get from that sheep. ...

When asked what effect it had on the mutton market, the witness replied:

Well, the mutton market itself has been relatively poor since

the 1960s when there was an enormous increase in the price of

the killing sheep ... it was progressive over a period of time

- and from that time on, the price of mutton wandered along at

a relatively low level and that price was basically maintained

right through until 1991.

12. It is informative to set out the information contained in exhibit "B".

MARKET INDICATOR AVERAGE PRICE AVERAGE PRICE

(cents per PER BALE PER FLEECE

kilo clean)

1987/88 1091 $1093.63 $36.45

1988/89 893 1119.35 37.32

1989/90 874 976.41 32.54

1990/91 570 725.28 24.17

1991/92 545 631.35 21.04

1992/93 444 555.63 18.52

13. Mr Pedlow was able to inform the Tribunal that during the 1987/88 year:

the slightly higher price was caused by a squeeze on fine wool.

China came into the market on fine wool ... and they pushed the

price so high that they lifted the average price for all the

clip and that's why there was an abnormal thing that occurred

that one year but it's abnormal for the year but it's normal in

the price of wool. That type of thing happens when the fashion

is for fine wool or for coarse wool.

14. Whilst Mr Pedlow saw the 1988/89 year as "a normal market", his perception was that in the 1989/90 year, the market was "basically normal", although, "a lack of confidence was started in the market because the wool growers didn't quite trust the Government on the reserve price." He went on to describe how that uncertainty was fuelled by the Australian Wool Corporation buying "increasingly large quantities" ("sometimes it was as much as 40 and 50 per cent of the catalogue") and stockpiling this wool.

15. In relation to the 1990/91 year:

when the reserve price was withdrawn by the government, the

market indicator was 712 cents. Four months later, at 30 June

'91 it had dropped to 570 cents ... because there was no

support. ... It was around the 500 mark at the close of the

season.

It was Mr Pedlow's opinion "that a farmer needs 500 cents to produce wool, and anything over and above 500 cents clean, he makes a profit."

16. In the 1991/92 year the market indicator for wool "went down to about 480 and came back to 545." In the 1992/93 year, "it went down to about 380 and it came back to 444 at the close of the season." At the time of the hearing (21 October 1993), Mr Pedlow stated that the market indicator was 450.

17. Mr Pedlow confirmed that the effect of the drop in wool prices (as illustrated by exh "B" and his explanation) has been a very large drop in the market value for sheep. Indeed, he was able to say from his research into the wool industry from the 1920s to the present that the fall in market price of wool caused by the withdrawal of the reserve price was "abnormal and without precedent", although he did concede that the original intervention into the market by the introduction of the minimum floor price arrangement was also unprecedented.

18. Much of the remainder of Mr Pedlow's evidence outlined the current plight of wool growers with depressed wool prices "causing the farmer to produce it at a loss" and the difficulty of selling grazing properties at anything other than a fraction of their previous values. Predicably, he took issue with Mr Martin's proposition that conditions since the withdrawal of the minimum floor price for wool were just a normal trough in a market, "that like all markets, (it) has its highs and lows."

19. The arguments on behalf of the respondent (without, I hope, doing any disservice to the well structured argument of Mr Martin) are best summarised in paragraph 14 of the respondent's outline of submissions:

The Commissioner's essential position therefore, is that the

taxpayer must point to something out of the ordinary in the

year in question, apart from a mere fluctuation in livestock

prices, in order to justify using - in that year - differing

valuation methodology as between opening livestock values and

closing livestock values. Or, in other words, for adopting in

that year an artificial calculation, rather than a consistent

valuation approach in the 12-month period. A rationale for so

departing from the norm in the present application, has not

been made out.

20. Looking at the terms of s 33 of the Act and the context in which it appears, I accept the proposition that a taxpayer must demonstrate that "something out of the ordinary in the year in question", in the sense of extraordinary, has occurred before leave should be granted to make the change of methodology envisaged in that section. In my view, this has not been demonstrated in this case with respect to the 1990 tax year, the only year before me.

21. Mr Sceales said all that could be said for the applicant, and said it eloquently and well. However, the evidence of Mr Pedlow was clear: the wool market in the 1989/90 year was "basically normal", although it evidenced the start of "a lack of confidence" due to uncertainty surrounding the Australian Wool Corporation's buying and "stockpiling" of "increasingly large quantities" of wool. The relatively small drop in the market value of wool, with a consequent reduced market value for sheep, during that year is, I consider, a "normal" hazard of the industry. What needs to be demonstrated is something extraordinary, such as, for example, the sudden withdrawal of the reserve price for wool, with the resultant dramatic effect on sheep values which occurred in the following year, before I would feel justified in granting leave under s 33 of the Act.

22. In the circumstances, I do not have to deal with Mr Martin's other submissions, although I am tempted to comment briefly on several aspects of his argument. Thus, he submitted, borrowing words from the explanatory memorandum to s 33 when it was introduced in 1936, that leave pursuant to s 33 should only be granted if "the revenue will not be prejudiced by the change". Speaking for myself, I have some difficulty in divining such intent in the language of s 33. Indeed, to imply such intent would rob the section of much of its possible operation. It is the very reason that a desire to change methodologies invariably results in the revenue being "prejudiced" that the requirement for leave to do so is present.

23. It was also suggested that the existence of the entitlement of primary producers to average their incomes over a number of years and their ability to participate in the income equalisation deposit scheme was another reason why leave under s 33 should not be granted when the request for such leave is based upon fluctuations in live stock prices, which in turn cause fluctuations in primary producers' incomes. To express it so, is, I consider, to put it too highly. Certainly, the existence of those entitlements are factors which may be taken into account when deciding whether to grant leave under s 33, but they should be viewed no higher. For example, it would, I believe, be quite wrong to classify the fluctuation in wool and sheep values brought about as a result of the withdrawal of the reserve price for wool as "a general fluctuation" or "just another normal low in the market"; nor should it simply be dismissed as being compensated by the abovementioned entitlements. Although ultimately irrelevant to the decision in this case, it seems to me that that is one instance where it would be appropriate for leave under s 33 to be given.

24. For the above reasons, the objection decision under review is affirmed.
