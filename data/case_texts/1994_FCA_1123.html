DECISION

JENKINSON J Questions of law referred by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal, pursuant to s.45 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975.

2. The questions arose in the Tribunal's review of the respondent Commissioner's decision disallowing an objection to the applicant's income tax assessment in respect of the year of income ended 30 June 1989. During that year the applicant had received a payment of $338,750 which the Commissioner treated in assessment as assessable income by reason of the operation of Part IIIA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. The payment was received as consideration for the applicant's participation in a transaction contemplated by the City of Adelaide Development Control Act 1976. The Act prohibited building and demolition work in that city without the approval of the Council of the Corporation of the City of Adelaide, to which application for approval had to be made. Section 24(6) provided:

"In deciding whether to grant an application, the Council must have

regard to the Principles and the regulations."

The regulations are not relevant to this case. The expression "the Principles" is defined in the Act to mean "the matter contained in a document relating to development control within the City of Adelaide designated The Principles of Development Control signed by the Minister for Planning and the Lord Mayor of the City of Adelaide and dated 21 October 1976 and includes that matter as from time to time amended in accordance with this Act". Section 7(1) provides: "The Principles are approved." Part II of the Act empowers the Governor to make amendments of the Principles, the amendments to be formulated in compliance with procedures ordained in that Part. 3. At all material times the applicant was the registered proprietor of land, known as 111 Hutt Street, situated in the Hutt Street North Precinct of the Frame District of the City of Adelaide. BHAS Employees Superannuation Fund Pty. Ltd. ("BHAS"), by which the payment of $338,750 was made to the applicant, was at relevant times the registered proprietor of other land in the same district. The applicant's land was at material times a conservation site, within the defined meaning of that expression in the Principles, as being an item in the Register of City of Adelaide Heritage Items, but there was no conservation site on the other land. The money was paid in consideration of the applicant's "transfer" of 1355 square metres of "floor area" from its site "for use on" the payer's land, in accordance with the provisions of Principles 15, 16 and 17.

4. The expression "plot ratio" in the Principles is defined to mean "the ratio between the total floor area or areas of a building or buildings, and the site area of the land upon which such building or buildings are or are intended to be erected". The expression "floor area" is defined to mean "the sum of the gross horizontal areas of each and every floor of a building contained within the inner faces of the outer walls measured at a height of 1.5 metres above the floor. It includes the areas occupied by internal walls and columns, lift shafts, staircases, lobbies, corridors, toilets and vertical service ducts, but excludes any area permanently set aside for the parking, manoeuvring, unloading or loading of vehicles together with ramps or other means of access. It also excludes the accommodation of mechanical or electrical plant or equipment servicing the building, and landscaped space common to all users of the building whether or not such a space is roofed". Section 39d of the City of Adelaide Development Control Act 1976 provided:

"(1) The Council may enter into an agreement with any person relating

to the development, preservation or conservation of land within the

municipality of which that person is the owner.

(2) The Council has power to carry out on private land any work for

which provision is made by agreement under this section.

(3) An owner of land cannot enter into an agreement under this section

without the consent of all other persons having a legal interest in the

land.

(4) The Registrar-General will, on the application of the Council made

with the consent of the owner of the land, register such an agreement

and enter a memorial of the agreement on the certificate of title or

other instrument of title to the land.

(5) Where a memorial of an agreement has been entered under subsection

(4), the agreement is, on transfer of title to the land, binding on, and

enforceable by or against, the successors in title to the owner who

entered into the agreement.

(6) The Registrar-General will, if satisfied on the application of the

Council or the owner of the land that an agreement in relation to which

a memorial has been entered under this section has been rescinded or

amended, enter a memorial of the rescission or amendment on the

certificate of title or other instrument of title to the land.

(7) An agreement under this section may provide for remission of rates

that would otherwise be payable to the Council on the land but except as

so provided such an agreement does not affect the statutory obligations

of an owner of land."

7. Mr. Murphy of counsel for the applicant submitted that the "asset", the disposition of which by the applicant attracted the operation of Part IIIA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, was the right which was conferred on the applicant to give to BHAS "floor area" of the kind which Principles 16 and 17 conceive as "available for transfer from a donor site". It was submitted concerning that right -

(a) that it came into existence on 11 October 1988, when the

permission of the Council contemplated by Principle 17(1) was granted, and not before then;

(b) that it was a proprietary right; and

(c) that it was acquired by the applicant on 11 October 1988

and thereafter was the subject of disposition to BHAS by the applicant on 30 November 1988, on which date a memorial of the agreement between the Council, the applicant and BHAS was entered on the certificate of title to the latter's land, and on the certificate of title to the applicant's land, under s.39d(4) of the City of Adelaide Development Control Act 1976.

8. Reliance was placed on several authorities concerning conceptions utilised in resolutions of the Sydney City Council which find expression in a document called "Code for Control of Development and Floor Space Ratio". The Council was the authority by which applications for permission to develop land in Sydney were determined in accordance with the provisions of an Act and a planning scheme ordinance made under the Act. It is said, in the reasons for judgment of Loveday J in Halwood Corporation Ltd. v. Chief Commissioner of Stamp Duties (N.S.W.) (1992) 92 ATC 4155 at 4156, that the resolutions were passed "(t)o provide further guidance to would-be developers", that is, further than the Act and the planning scheme affords guidance. In those authorities, and by counsel in this proceeding, it was accepted that the Code had no legal effect and did not free the Council from any of the requirements which the Act and the planning scheme imposed on the Council as to the manner of exercise of its functions. But the conceptual framework of the Sydney Code is similar to that of Principles 16 and 17. Loveday J had to determine whether a deed between the owner of a donor site and a recipient site by which the donor "purported to sell" 5574 square metres of what the Code calls "transferable floor space" to the owner of other land was "an agreement for the sale or conveyance of any property" within the meaning of s.41 of the Stamp Duties Act 1920 (N.S.W.). Loveday J decided that it was. His Honour said (92 ATC at 4161): "The transferee of the transferable floor space has a right recognised by the Council to have a development application considered by the council taking into account the existence of the transferable floor space. This is a valuable right not possessed by an applicant for development approval without transferable floor space. The reality is that commerce regards transferable floor space as a proprietary right. The courts should do likewise". His Honour discussed another authority (in which the expression "air space" is used as a synomym for transferable floor space) in these terms (92 ATC at 4160-4161):

"In Uniting Church in Australia Property Trust (NSW) v. Immer (No. 145)

Pty. Ltd. (New South Wales Supreme Court, Young J 15 November 1990

unreported) the vendor of transferable floor space sought specific

performance of a contract in the form of a deed to sell the floor space.

His Honour refused relief holding that the purchaser was entitled under

the terms of the deed, to rescind and stated:

'I cannot see that the City Council, in keeping a register of

bonus floor space, creates any proprietary right at all.

Accordingly, it is difficult to see how a decree for specific

performance could be made.'

The Court of Appeal however reversed his Honour's decision and made an

order for specific performance (Court of Appeal (1991) NSW ConvR para.

55-603). At p.59,416 Meagher JA. (with whom Samuels AP and Handley JA

agreed) said this:

'A "transfer" of "air space" amounts to that which would have

astonished a Roman Lawyer, the assignment in gross of a ius altius

tollendi. McLelland J in Depsun Pty. Ltd. v. Tahore Holdings

Pty. Ltd. (1900) NSW Conv R 58,902 has held that "an air space" is

not "a legal or equitable estate or interest" in land within the

meaning of s.74F of the Real Property Act 1990. No doubt this is

no. However, in the same decision his Honour held that they do

not constitute "proprietary" rights. This seems to me debatable.

They are transferable, and I assume transmissible; they are of

large commercial value; and I see no reason why they are not

"proprietary" rights in the same way as goodwill, patents or

shares in the capital of a company are "proprietary" rights.'

Mr. Gzell has submitted that his Honour's remarks are obiter dicta and

that they do not amount in any event to a definite statement to the

effect that transferable floor space is a proprietary right. It is true

that his Honour in using the expression 'this seems to me debatable'

suggested doubt but as I read the passage he was at least expressing a

tentative opinion that rights to transferable floor space are

proprietary rights. The order for specific performance made by the

court is not an order appropriate to a mere 'expectation'. It is

however appropriate to a right in the nature of a proprietary right. In

this regard Meagher JA. said this:

'I should have thought that rights of "air space" ... are

sufficiently unique to attract the doctrine. Specific performance

is available as a remedy to enforce a contract for the sale of

"property" in circumstances where damages would not afford an

adequate remedy. See Dougan v. Ley [1946] HCA 3; (1946) 71 CLR 142 at

153/154.'"

His Honour noticed also some characteristics of the conception of transferable floor space which are not found in the legislation applicable to Adelaide. If the development application for the support of which the transferable floor space has been acquired be denied approval by the Sydney City Council, the owner of the land the subject of the application may support other development applications in respect of other land of his by the transferable floor space or he may sell the transferable floor space to the owner of other land for use in relation to the development of the latter owner's land.

9. The use of expressions such as "transfer" and "floor area" in the Principles, and the use of similar expressions in the Sydney Code, tends to distract attention from the fact that what the recipient receives is not anything which the donor ever had. The recipient is enabled to have his development application considered on the footing that for his site the maximum permissible floor area is greater (by the number of square metres of "floor area" transferred) than that which multiplication of the basic plot ratio by the site area of his land would produce. That advantage the donor never had. The donor does not transfer or sell to the recipient, nor confer on the recipient's land, anything which the donor himself had. In relation to the "donor site" the 1355 square metres is a part of that floor area which is the product of multiplication of the site area by the basic plot ratio, and which on a consideration of a development application with respect to that site the Council is required by the Principles to treat as the maximum permissible floor area. (I have left out of consideration the possibility that bonus plot ratio of the kind specified in the first part of Principle 16 may be granted.)

10. If the capacity of the applicant to confer on other land "floor area" is a "form of property", within the meaning of that expression in s.160A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, and therefore an "asset" for the purposes of Part IIIA, it was not suggested by either party that the applicant had given consideration in respect of the acquisition of that asset. The applicant's covenants in the deed dated 9 November 1988 were not proposed as consideration in respect of the acquisition. Mr. Murphy submitted that the circumstances of the applicant's acquisition of the asset - from the Council, as he contended - were those specified in paragraph 160ZH(9)(a) - "the taxpayer acquired the asset from another person and did not pay or give any consideration in respect of the acquisition" - and that in accordance with sub-section 160ZH(9) "the taxpayer shall be deemed to have paid or given as consideration in respect of the acquisition of the asset an amount equal to the market value of the asset at the time of the acquisition". If that argument were accepted the disposition of the asset would have produced no capital gain. (The special case does not state the market value, but Mr. Murphy suggested that the consideration paid to the applicant by BHAS was evidence that the market value was $338,750.)

11. Mr. Slater submitted that no asset which vested in BHAS was an asset acquired by the applicant from the Council. I accept that submission. If the asset acquired by the applicant were the capacity to confer on the land of BHAS "floor area", there was no disposition to BHAS of any capacity to confer on its land, or on any other land, "floor area". The disposition, if disposition there was, by the applicant was the conferring of "floor area", by the exercise of that capacity, on the land of BHAS. While it may be possible to conceive of that capacity as having been acquired by the applicant from the Council, by reason of the circumstance that without the Council's permission that capacity was lacking, it is not in my opinion possible to conceive of what was vested in BHAS - the "floor area" conferred on its land - as having been first acquired by the applicant from the Council.

12. Mr. Murphy submitted that his submissions were consonant with the operation of s.160M(5)(c) which provides:

"For the purposes of this Part -

...............................................

(c) the creation of an asset by or for a person constitutes the

acquisition of the asset by the person."

According to the submission the asset here in question was created by the Council for the applicant and accordingly the creation of the asset constituted the acquisition of the asset by the applicant. Even if that were so, it could not be said that the rights which vested in BHAS were created by the Council for the applicant. At the most it might be said that the Council had created for the applicant the capacity to confer those rights on BHAS. But that capacity was not the subject of disposition by the applicant to BHAS. It is unnecessary to consider the doubtful points of the construction of s.160M(5)(c) considered in Hepples v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1992] HCA 3; (1992) 173 CLR 492.

13. The right vested in BHAS - to have a development application in respect of the recipient site considered on the footing that the maximum permissible floor area is greater by 1355 square metres than would have been the case if the transaction under consideration had not been effected - is one which would no doubt pass to a transferee of any estate in the recipient site the ownership of which would give standing to make a development application in respect of that site. (The special case does not show - nor was it submitted - that the right is transferable to any other site, whether owned by BHAS or by another person.) The conception of "transferable floor area" owes its existence to the statute which authorised and expressly approved the Principles. The agreement which Principle 17(10) compels the Council to require of the owner of the donor site and the owner of the recipient site "as a condition of approving the transfer of floor area pursuant to principle 17(1)" is made "binding on, and enforceable by or against, the successors in title to" the owner of the recipient site: s.39d(5) of the City of Adelaide Development Control Act 1976. It is in my opinion implicit in that statutory provision that the right vested in the owner of the recipient land by the transfer of floor area will enure to successors in title to the owner of the recipient land. Mr. Slater submitted, and I accept that there is no enforceable right to the benefit of a plot ratio or to transferred floor area by which a plot ratio has been augmented. All that an applicant for development approval has is a right to a determination, of his application, in which the Council has regard to that augmentation as an element of the Principles. But in my opinion that is sufficient to justify characterisation of the right as a proprietary right, and as an "asset" for the purposes of Part IIIA.

14. If the right be an "asset", it is in my opinion an asset such as s.160M(6) comprehends. That sub-section provides:

"A disposal of an asset that did not exist (either by itself or as part

of another asset) before the disposal, but is created by the disposal,

constitutes a disposal of the asset for the purposes of this Part, but

the person who so disposes of the asset shall be deemed not to have paid

or given any consideration, or incurred any costs or expenditure,

referred to in paragraph 160ZH(1)(a), (b), (c) or (d), (2)(a), (b), (c)

or (d) or (3)(a), (b), (c) or (d) in respect of the asset."

The right vested in BHAS was created by the making of the agreement, the approval by the Council, the execution of the deed and the entry of the memorial on the certificates of title. The right did not in my opinion exist, either by itself or as part of another asset, before its creation. Notwithstanding the necessity of approval by the Council, the creation of the right was by the act in the law of the applicant. If the right was an asset, there was a disposal of the asset for the purposes of Part IIIA, by virtue of s.160M(6), in my opinion. And that would be so even if that sub-section were regarded as applying "only where the asset disposed of was created out of or over an existing asset", as Mason CJ and McHugh J did in Hepples Case (173 CLR at 497, 547-549). The rights which ownership of the donor site conferred included the right, subject to the Council's approval, to confer on the owner of a recipient site an advantage of a particular kind. The conferring of that advantage had for a necessary consequence the diminution of another advantage, of a similar kind, which ownership of the donor site conferred: see Principle 17(4). It can in my opinion be said that the advantage conferred on BHAS was created out of the ownership of the donor site and that that advantage was created over that ownership, in the sense that the rights conferred by that ownership were diminished in consequence of the creation of the advantage.

15. If there was a disposal by the applicant of an asset in the circumstances specified in s.160M(6), the applicant is deemed by that sub-section not to have paid or given any consideration, or incurred any costs or expenditure, referred to in the provisions specified in the sub-section, in respect of the asset.

16. If, however, the right vested in BHAS is not an asset for the purposes of Part IIIA, because it is not a proprietary right, or if for other reasons my opinion be wrong that s.160M(6) will apply if that right is an asset, then in my opinion s.160M(7) will apply to the transaction. That sub-section provides:

"Without limiting the generality of sub-section (2) but subject to the

other provisions of this Part, where -

(a) an act or transaction has taken place in relation to an asset or

an event affecting an asset has occurred; and

(b) a person has received, or is entitled to receive, an amount of

money or other consideration by reason of the act, transaction or

event (whether or not any asset was or will be acquired by the

person paying the money or giving the other consideration)

including, but not limited to, an amount of money or other

consideration -

(i) in the case of an asset being a right - in return for

forfeiture or surrender of the right or for refraining from

exercising the right; or

(ii) for use or exploitation of the asset,

the act, transaction or event constitutes a disposal by the person who

received, or is entitled to receive, the money or other consideration of

an asset created by the disposal and, for the purposes of the

application of this Part in relation to that disposal -

(c) the money or other consideration constitutes the consideration in

respect of the disposal; and

(d) the person shall be deemed not to have paid or given any

consideration, or incurred any costs or expenditure, referred to

in paragraph 160ZH(1)(a), (b), (c) or (d), (2)(a), (b), (c) or (d)

or (3)(a), (b), (c) or (d) in respect of the asset."

17. The asset to be taken as that to which reference is made in s.106M(7) is the applicant's freehold estate in the donor site, in my opinion. The transaction, consisting of the making of the agreement, the execution of the deed and the entry of the memorial on the certificate of title to the donor site, was in my opinion a transaction in relation to that donor site. And it was by reason of that transaction that the applicant received from BHAS $338,750. Accordingly the sub-section operates to make the transaction constitute a disposal by the applicant of an asset created by the disposal, the consideration for which for the purposes of the application of Part IIIA in relation to that disposal is deemed to be that $338,750; and the applicant is deemed not to have paid or given any consideration, or incurred any costs or expenditure, referred to in the provisions specified in paragraph 160M(7)(d), in respect of that notional asset.

Assessment Act, and if so

(b) By reason of which provision, and,

2. If the answer to 1(a) is yes,

(a) What is the cost base of the property disposed of?

(b) What is the consideration received by the Applicant for the

(c) Did the Applicant, within the meaning of sec 160Z, derive a

3. Is the amount of $338,750 (or some other, and if so, what amount)

19. The references to "property" instead of "asset" and to "capital profit" instead of "capital gain" are inapt. Question 3(a) assumes that the applicant incurred during the year of income no capital loss, which would have reduced the net capital gain below $338,750. It is the net capital gain which s.160ZO operates to include in assessable income. Unlike the special case in Hepples' Case, this special case does not state that no capital loss was incurred. However, the inference that it was not may be drawn from the applicant's income tax return, the notice of assessment and the notice of objection, which are annexed to the special case.

20. I would answer question 3(a): Yes. I would not answer the other questions, but would declare that the rights conferred on BHAS by the transactions recited in paragraphs 7 and 8 of the special case constituted an asset within the meaning of that word in Part IIIA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 and that by reason of s.160M(6) of that Act a disposal of the said asset for the purposes of that Part was constituted and that a capital gain in the sum of $338,750 was deemed for the purposes of that Part to have accrued to the applicant in respect of that disposal. However, I am content to join in the making of the order proposed by French J, whose reasons for judgment I have had the advantage of reading: an affirmative answer to question 3(a) without more. The respondent's costs of the proceeding in this Court should be ordered to be paid by the applicant.

FRENCH J The Naval, Military and Airforce Club of South Australia (Incorporated) (the Club) has occupied its premises at 111 Hutt Street, Adelaide since they were purchased in February 1957. Juristic Character of Transferable Floor Area

18. By virtue of s.7(1) of the City of Adelaide Development Control Act 1976 (SA) the Principles are "approved". That is a declaration of parliamentary approbation which extends to the Principles as amended from time to time. It does not however confer upon them the force of law or, indeed, any legally binding operation. In Sankey v. Whitlam (1978) 142 CLR 1, it was held that mere statutory approval of an agreement does not give to the rights and obligations created by the agreement the same effect as if they had been contained in the statute - at 31 (Gibbs ACJ), 77 (Stephen J), 89-90 (Mason J), 105-106 (Aickin J). The same is true of the legislative approval of an international treaty to which Australia is a party - see generally the discussion and cases cited in Minister for Foreign Affairs and Trade v. Magno [1992] FCA 566; (1992) 37 FCR 298 at 303-305 (Gummow J). The proposition common to both cases is that statutory approval of a non-statutory instrument cannot of itself confer upon the contents of that instrument the force of law. Legislation does not create, alter or continue rights, duties, powers or privileges unless, as a matter of logical analysis it purports to do so either expressly or as a matter of proper implication. Statutory statements of parliamentary sentiment do not make the law. That is not to say that they may not have legal consequences. The parliamentary approval of an agreement may be a condition upon which its operation is contingent. It may also, along with express statements of legislative purpose or objects, be a guide to the construction of the operative provisions of an Act.

19. The Act goes beyond mere approval of the Principles and by s.24(6) requires that the Council have regard to them. But even that directive, which plainly imposes a legal duty on the Council, does not confer upon the Principles statutory force or a legally binding operation. It is not unusual in contemporary legislation to find rule-making powers authorising the creation of administrative or executive guidelines which are not intended to be a species of delegated legislation but give content to some statutory direction to apply or have regard to them. For example, s.23DN(2) of the Health Insurance Act 1973 (Cth) authorises the Minister to determine principles "to be applied by the Minister in the exercise of the Minister's powers" under sub-s.23DN(1). The word "principle" in that context has been held to carry the ordinary English meaning of "a general rule or a guide to action". Nevertheless by virtue of the statute the Principles so determined effectively regulate the exercise of the Minister's powers - Preci Services Pty Ltd v. Minister for Health, Housing and Community Services [1992] FCA 51; (1992) 36 FCR 395 at 404. They give content to an ambulatory statutory requirement. Another somewhat contentious example is that of ministerial plans of management determined under s.7B of the Fisheries Act 1952 (Cth). The powers and functions of the Minister under the Act are to be exercised "in accordance with the plan of management and not otherwise" (s.7B(8)). There have been some differences of view in the cases on whether the making of such plans is a legislative or administrative act - see generally Secretary, Department of Primary Industries and Energy v. Collins [1992] FCA 61; (1992) 34 FCR 340; Azevedo v. Secretary, Department of Primary Industries and Energy [1992] FCA 84; (1992) 35 FCR 284; Austral Fisheries Pty Ltd v. Minister for Primary Industries and Energy [1992] FCA 351; (1992) 37 FCR 463; Latitude Fisheries Pty Ltd v. Minister for Primary Industries and Energy [1992] FCA 385; (1992) 110 ALR 209.

20. The statutory direction to the Council in the present case, requires it to have regard to the Principles in considering whether to approve a development. That does not require the Council to apply them. It leaves each case to be considered on its own merits. The Principles must be taken into account, considered and given due weight but in the end the Council retains its own discretion - Ishak v. Thowfeek (1968) 1 WLR 1718 at 1725 (PC); R v. Hunt; Ex parte Sean Investments Pty Ltd [1979] HCA 32; (1979) 53 ALJR 552 at 554; R v. Toohey; Ex parte Meneling Station Pty Ltd [1982] HCA 69; (1982) 158 CLR 327 at 333 (Gibbs CJ). It may also be the case that the Council could not in the ordinary course depart from the Principles without first affording to any person affected by its decision an opportunity to be heard on the question whether the Principles should be applied and on the merits of the development application in the event they are not to be applied - R v. Liverpool Corporation, Ex parte Liverpool Taxi Fleet Operators Association (1972) 2 QB 299; Kioa v. West [1985] HCA 81; (1985) 159 CLR 550 at 582-3 (Mason J); Century Metals and Mining NL v. Yeomans [1989] FCA 273; (1989) 40 FCR 564. If the promulgation of the Principles has led persons to act in reliance upon them questions of estoppel may arise according to the circumstances of the particular case. In the end, however, the Principles are guidelines which do not bind the Council. They do not have the force of law, nor do they of themselves create property rights. If there are rights related to the content of the Principles they are derived from the Act. The obligation on the Council to have regard to the Principles in determining a development application will give rise to a correlative and important right on the part of the applicant for development approval to have the application considered by Council having regard to the Principles. There may be an ancillary right (of common law origin or arising from statutory implication) to procedural fairness in so far as the Council might consider departing from the Principles in a particular case.

21. Where parties conclude an agreement in relation to transferable floor area, on the common assumption that they are dealing with an interest in land, that fact alone cannot justify the characterisation of the transferable floor area as such for that particular case:

"...great detriment would arise and much confusion of

rights if parties were allowed to invent new modes of

holding and enjoying real property, and to impress

upon their lands and tenements a peculiar character

which should follow them into all hands, however

remote." - Keppell v. Bailey (1834) 2 My and K 517 at

536 (39 ER 1042 at 1049) (Lord Brougham LC)

In Depsun Pty Ltd v. Tahore Holdings Pty Ltd (1990) NSW Conv R 55-253, where the above passage was cited by McLelland J, his Honour held that a deed assigning the benefit of "floating floor space" under a non-statutory City Development Control and Floor Space Ratio Code, and purporting to treat the "space" as real property did not give rise to an interest in land. This conclusion was not questioned by the Court of Appeal in the subsequent decision in Uniting Church in Australia Property Trust (NSW) v. Immer (No. 145) Pty Ltd (1991) 24 NSWLR 510; (1991) 74 LGRA 255. But a further conclusion that the "space" did not constitute proprietary rights was thought by Meagher JA (Samuels and Handley JJA agreeing) to be debateable. His Honour there said of space "rights" under the Code:

"They are transferable and I assume transmissible; they

are of large commercial value; and I see no reason why

they are not "proprietary" rights in the same way as

goodwill, patents or shares in the capital of a

company are "proprietary rights"." (NSWLR at 511)

The High Court reversed the decision of the Court of Appeal, but on grounds not related to the character of the space "rights" - Immer (No. 145) Pty Ltd v. Uniting Church in Australia Property Trust (NSW) [1993] HCA 27; (1993) 67 ALJR 537; 112 ALR 609. The decision of the Court of Appeal was later applied by Loveday J in Halwood Corporation Ltd v. Chief Commissioner of Stamp Duties 92 ATC 4155, to support a conclusion that "transferable floor space" under the Sydney Code was descriptive of a property right. Merely because the Council had a discretion which might lead to enjoyment of transferable floor space being withheld from a particular person was not fatal to its characterisation as a property right. The content of the right on this analysis derived from the commitment of the Council to take into account the existence of the transferable floor space when considering a development application. His Honour said at 4161:

"Registration in the council's register confers an

exclusive entitlement to the floor space. The

transferee of the transferable floor space has a right

recognised by the council to have a development

application considered by the council taking into

account the existence of the transferable floor space.

This is a valuable right not possessed by an applicant

for development approval without transferable floor

space. The reality is that commerce regards

transferable floor space as a proprietary right. The

courts should do likewise."

The Sydney Code lacked the express statutory support of the Principles adopted by the City of Adelaide in the present case. It was embodied in resolutions passed by the Sydney City Council which had no legally binding effect upon the Council or any prospective developer. In the Supreme Court of Queensland, Moynihan J applied these authorities in Re FAI Leasing Pty Ltd (Supreme Court of Queensland, unrep. 13 September 1993) to hold that "transferable site areas" under a town plan which had statutory force, while not comprising part of the fee simple in the land was "a proprietary right capable of assignment and transfer" (at p.13). It was accepted in the Supreme Court of South Australia in McEwin v. Valuer General (1993) 169 LSJS 146, (at 152 Mullighan J) that "Transferable Floor Area", created pursuant to the Principles, is not an interest in the land but is a proprietary interest of the kind described by Meagher JA in the Uniting Church case (supra).

22. Principle 1 defines a two dimensional spatial quantity designated as floor area. It is used as a measure for defining the limits of permitted development by reference to specific plot ratios in Principles 15 and 16. Principle 17 says that Council may permit the owners of a conservation site to "transfer floor area from that site for use on another site to enable the floor area for the purposes of development, on the recipient site to be increased". This is rule making by metaphor. The expression "transfer of floor area" is no more than an elliptical way of referring to a commitment by the Council, subject to discretion in particular cases, to permit a greater degree of development on a recipient site than would ordinarily be the case in consideration of a corresponding diminution of the degree of permitted development on the donor site. The difficulty with labelling the "transferable floor area" as a proprietary right is that the metaphor so characterised masks the underlying legal principles and distracts from an analysis of their operation.

23. Prior to the registration of the agreement of 9 November, which took place on 30 November 1988, the Club had a right to make a development application and have it considered with regard to the then current plot ratio. That was an entitlement to which the Council had committed itself by its adoption of the Principles. In the present case the Council resolved on 11 October 1988 to approve the transfer of the floor space. But the transfer did not take effect until the agreement of 9 November had been registered as a memorial on the certificate of title of the Club land. The registration was effected on 30 November 1988. At that time the Club's right to apply for development approval in respect of its land was diminished to the extent defined by the floor area notionally transferred. A right was simultaneously created in BHAS to have a development potential in respect of its land considered with regard to the notional transfer. What occurred was the partial surrender of one right and the creation of a corresponding new right. Neither was an interest in land.

Application of Capital Gains Tax Provisions

24. The question first to be determined is whether the right lost by the Club and that conferred on BHAS on 30 November are properly regarded as forms of property for the purposes of s.160A. The Full Court in Commissioner of Taxation v. Cooling (1990) 22 FCR 42, held that the definition of "asset" in s.160A extends to items of property and interests in property but not to rights of a non-proprietary kind - at 59 (Hill J, Lockhart and Gummow JJ agreeing). The Court took the same view in its decision in Hepples v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1990) 22 FCR 1 at 9 (Lockhart J), 20-27 (Gummow J). The High Court did not decide that question on appeal - see the discussion by Hill J in Reuter v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1993] FCA 576; (1993) 111 ALR 716 AT 733. The distinction between proprietary and personal rights was considered at some length by Gummow J in Hepples in the Full Court and the point made that while assignability is not an essential characteristic of a right of property, by statute some forms of property being expressed to be inalienable, none the less it is generally correct to say that a proprietary right must be capable in its nature of assumption by third parties - R v. Toohey; Ex parte Meneling Station Pty Ltd [1982] HCA 69; (1982) 158 CLR 327 at 342-343 (Mason J); National Provincial Bank Ltd v. Ainsworth [1965] UKHL 1; (1965) AC 1175 at 1247-1248 (Lord Wilberforce).

25. In my opinion the right lost by the Club on 30 November related to, although it was not, an interest in the Club's land. Similarly, the right acquired by BHAS related to, but was not an interest in, its land. It was not transferable independently of the land. The Principles contemplate transfer of floor area from a donor site which must be a conservation site, to a recipient site, which must not (Principle 17(1) and 17(8)). They do not provide for further transfer from a recipient site to another recipient site. The rights lost by the Club and gained by BHAS did not fall into the category of mere personal rights. They were proprietary rights, an incident of the enjoyment of the land with which they were associated. They were therefore capable of being treated as assets for the purposes of Part IIIA.

26. The question follows, was there a disposition of an asset by the Club for the purposes of Part IIIA? It can be accepted that if there was a disposition then it happened at some time on or between 5 October and 30 November 1988. It would then have been a disposition to which Part IIIA applied by virtue of s.160L(1). The asset lost by the Club and that acquired by BHAS on 30 November were legally distinct, each relating as it did to the respective site with which it was associated. The case is therefore unable simply to be characterised as a change in the ownership of "an asset" for the purposes of s.160M(1). Nor does s.160M(2) which specifies classes of change of ownership caught by s.160M(1) avoid the requirement that the change be in the ownership of "an asset". On the other hand, s.160M(3) operates as a deeming provision to catch the cancellation, surrender or abandonment at law or in equity of an asset being "a debt, a chose in action or any other right, or an interest or right in or over property". The metaphor of "transfer of floor space" masks the underlying reality of the transaction in this case which involved at least a partial cancellation, surrender or abandonment by the Club of its right to apply for development approval on its land on the basis of a particular plot ratio. It does not matter that the Club may not, in effect, have lost the full right to apply for development approval on the basis of the full plot ratio, as by s.160R a reference to a disposal of an asset includes, unless the contrary intention appears, a reference to a disposal of part of an asset.

27. In my opinion the cancellation, or surrender or abandonment of the Club's right which came into effect on 30 November was the disposal of an asset for the purposes of s.160M(3). If that is correct, then s.160M(6) has no application as it is concerned only with the case of an asset that did not exist before the disposal but is created by the disposal. Section 160M(7) does operate, however, in relation to the disposal of the Club's asset. For it can be said, that on 30 November 1988 an event affecting the asset had occurred, namely registration of the agreement of 9 November 1988. The Club received an amount of money by reason of that event, namely $338,750, which can be characterised in terms of s.160M(7)(b)(i) as an amount of money in return for forfeiture or surrender of "an asset being a right". The event was, for that reason, a disposal by the Club and the money it received constituted consideration in respect of that disposal. It may be observed that there is an overlap between s.160M(3) and s.160M(7) in respect of the notion that forfeiture or surrender of a right is a deemed disposal of it.

28. Notwithstanding that the surrender of the Club's asset became effective on 30 November 1988, the time of the deemed disposal under s.160M(3) or s.160M(7) is to be determined by reference to s.160U. The disposal was "under a contract", a term which is confined to the contract that directly effected the disposal - Elmslie v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation 93 ATC 4964 at 4976 (Wilcox J). In this case that was the contract of 5 October 1988. The date of the disposal for the purpose of the Act was the date upon which that contract was made.

29. It is next necessary to have regard to s.160ZH to ascertain the cost base of the asset to the Club. The land was acquired in 1957. The right which I have identified as the Club's right to apply for development approval on the basis of a designated plot ratio derived its existence from the City of Adelaide Development Control Act 1976 (SA). There is nothing to suggest that any component of the original 1957 purchase price of the land was referable to that development right. In my opinion the relevant cost base is zero. On that basis the Club derived a capital profit of $338,750 on the disposal. By reason of s.160ZO, and absent any evidence of capital losses, that amount is to be included in its assessable income.

2. The respondent Commissioner included in the assessable income of the appellant for the year ended 30 June 1989 the sum of $338,750 as a capital gain.

3. The appellant's liability to tax falls to be decided according to the provisions of Part IIIA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 as they stood at the time of the transaction in 1988, and before amendments effected by Act No. 35 of 1990, and subsequent amending Acts.

4. Section 160Z(1) provided for the ascertainment of the amount of a capital gain. Relevantly that subsection provided:

"160Z(1) Subject to this Part, where an asset other than a

personal-use asset has been disposed of during the year of

income -

(a) if the consideration in respect of the disposal exceeds the

indexed cost base to the taxpayer in respect of the asset - a

For the purposes of the present case the application of this subsection requires that there has been a "disposal" of an "asset" and it is necessary to ascertain the relevant "consideration" and "indexed cost base" (although if the disposal occurs within 12 months after the day on which the asset was acquired the reference to "indexed cost base" shall be construed as a reference to the "cost base" of the asset: s.160Z(3)).

5. For the purposes of Part IIIA "asset" means any form of property and is defined to include "an option, a debt, a chose in action, any other right, goodwill and any other form of incorporeal property": s.160A. The applicant contends that the relevant asset was the TFA, and that it was a form of property; that this asset was acquired by the applicant on 11 October 1988, the date upon which the agreement to transfer the TFA to BHAS was approved by the Council; that the asset was then disposed of on 30 November 1988 when the agreement required by the Council pursuant to Principle 17(10) was registered as a memorial on the Certificate of Title pursuant to s.39d of the City of Adelaide Development Control Act and Principle 17(3); that the consideration in respect of the disposal was the payment of $338,750; that the cost base of the asset was its market value as at 11 October 1988 (see s.160ZH(9)(a) as it stood in 1988); that the market value at that date was the same as the consideration received by the applicant; and that in these circumstances there was no capital gain which accrued to the applicant, and no part of the sum of $338,750 should have been included as assessable income.

6. The respondent contends that the making of an agreement to "transfer" floor area under the Principles conferred no interest in the land comprising the donor site. Further it is contended that the agreement conferred on the owner of the recipient site no more than an opportunity to apply to the Council for a higher plot ratio when seeking development approval, and to have the Council take into account and give weight to the Principles. At most the "transfer" brought about a commercial expectancy that the Council, in having regard to the Principles, would probably approve a higher plot ratio, so long as the application was otherwise deserving of approval. It is contended that the right to make a development application with the benefit of TFA, was not a proprietary right; but even if it were, that proprietary right was not acquired by the applicant "from another person" (i.e. from the Council) so as to attract the provisions of s.160ZH(9)(a). It is to be treated as an asset that was created by agreement with the Council so that the relevant subsection in s.160M, which deals with what constitutes a disposal or acquisition, was s.160M(6). The primary position, however, of the respondent is that because the right created in BHAS by the transaction to make a development application with the benefit of the TFA was not a proprietary right, it was not an "asset" as defined by s.160A. For this reason the primary contention of the respondent is that s.160M(6) did not have application, and that the transaction fell within s.160M(7) with the consequence that there was deemed to be a disposal of a notional asset which had no relevant cost base. In the result there was a capital gain to the applicant of $388,750 less the costs of disposal (see s.160ZH(1)(e), as to which there is no evidence).

7. The applicant relies on decisions concerning schemes similar to that established in the Principles relating to the "transfer" of floor area or "air space" from heritage sites to the development sites in Sydney and Brisbane.

8. In Depsun Pty Ltd and Anor v Tahore Holdings Pty Ltd (1990) NSW Conv. R. 55-523 the purchaser of land agreed to grant the vendor the benefit of "floating floor space" rights pertaining to the land. The floating floor space was defined in the relevant agreement as an amount of floor space potential not presently utilised in respect of the property and available for use under the Sydney City Council's Development Control and Floor Space Ratio Code. That Code comprised a set of rules which enabled the Council to exercise discretionary powers including the power to impose conditions and grant or refuse development consents. Under the Code the benefit of floating floor space could be used, subject to the requirements of the Council, in connection with the development of an Alternative Site. Floating floor space benefits became available under the Code in respect of "places and structures on the Council's Register of Places and Structures of historic or architectural significance". At all times material to the decision the property the subject of the agreement between the vendor and purchaser had not been placed by the Council on that Register. The vendor after settlement registered a caveat over the land. The purchaser and a mortgagee sought to have the caveat removed. McLelland J held that the agreement between vendor and purchaser created no legal or equitable estate or interest in the land capable of being protected by a caveat. His Honour also held that the rights in respect of the subject property created by the relevant agreement between the parties "are clearly personal and not proprietary rights".

9. Depsun Pty Ltd and Anor v Tahore Holdings Pty Ltd was considered by the Court of Appeal of the Supreme Court of New South Wales in Uniting Church in Australia Property Trust (NSW) v Immer (No. 145) Pty Ltd. The decision is briefly reported at (1991) 24 NSWLR 510, but a more comprehensive report is contained at (1991) 74 LGRA 255. The following references are to the latter report. The appellant agreed to sell the respondent "air space" rights above an historic site in the City of Sydney. The appellant sought specific performance of the contract but failed at first instance. Meagher JA, with whom Samuels AP and Handley JA agreed, considered the novel juridical concept of "air space". He said, at p.256:

"A transfer of 'air space' does not, as its name would suggest,

transfer dominion over the space above a building; rather it

confers a right on the owner of land to build higher than would

normally be permitted if he 'acquires' from the owner of

another building the rights which the latter would have had if

he had utilised them to build the second building as

extensively as the relevant codes had permitted. There is,

apparently, no statutory basis for this concept, but it arises

from a council's power to grant development applications."

His Honour noted that the nature and incidents of "air space" are set out in two Council documents, a Transferable Air Space policy document and a Development Control and Floor Space Ratio Code. He continued, at p.256:

"A 'transfer' of 'air space' amounts to that which would have

astonished a Roman lawyer, the assignment in gross of a ius

altius tollendi. McLelland J in Depsun Pty Ltd v Tahore

Holdings Pty Ltd (1990) NSW Conv R 58,902 has held that an 'air

space' is not 'a legal or equitable estate or interest' in land

within the meaning of s 74F of the Real Property Act 1900

(NSW). No doubt this is so. However, in the same decision his

Honour held that they do not constitute 'proprietary' rights.

This seems to me debatable. They are transferable, and I

assume transmissible; they are of large commercial value; and

I see no reason why they are not 'proprietary' rights in the

same way as goodwill, patents or shares in the capital of a

company are 'proprietary' rights."

Meagher JA concluded, at 263, that rights to "air space" are sufficiently unique to attract the doctrine of specific performance, and specific performance was ordered.

10. Meagher JA noted, at 257, that the intervention of the Council was necessary at three points for a transfer of "air space" to occur.

11. First, the Council had to recognise the existence of "air space" rights in the donor. That had occurred on 14 September 1988 when the Council had notified the appellant that a notification had been placed on the Council's register that floor space from the appellant's site may be transferred. It is to be noted that in Depsun Pty Ltd and Anor v Tahore Holdings Pty Ltd that step on the Council's part had not occurred; the "right" which the vendor sought to protect had not been recognised by the Council by inclusion of the property on the "Register of Places and Structures of historic or architectural significance". For this reason it may have been correct to say that the rights in respect of the property created by the agreement were not proprietary rights.

12. Secondly, the Council had to accord the status of transferability to those rights. This the Council had done in mid 1989 by resolving to grant consent to the transfer of "air space" from the subject site, provided that a number of conditions were fulfilled as to legal documentation, the completion of certain "restoration work" on the subject site, the payment of the proceeds of sale of the "air space", and consent to any development in the City which incorporated the "air space".

13. Thirdly, the Council had to sanction the actual transfer of the "air space" rights from the donor to the recipient. This occurred in September 1990 when the Council resolved that all the conditions attached to the resolution of mid 1989 had been met, and that the purchaser would be entered as the holder of the "air space" upon compliance with the formalities of the Conveyancing Act 1919 (NSW).

14. In regard to the second and third of the stages of intervention, there are similarities under the Principles concerning the intervention of the Adelaide City Council. The Principles do not state that the Council must first recognise the existence of TFA rights in the owner of the donor site by placing a notification of TFA in a register. However, as a matter of fact the Special Case states that the applicant on 11 July 1988 received from the Council the letter informing it of the TFA Scheme. After explaining the Scheme, the letter advised that the Council was preparing an informal list of owners of heritage items who may wish to participate in the TFA Scheme which would be circulated to the "development industry". The applicant was invited to submit details of its property if it wished to be included in the list. This informal process would have served the same end as entry on a register under the Sydney City Council Scheme. The Adelaide City Council's intervention at the second stage arises under Principles 17(1), and 17(3) which empower the Council to permit an owner to transfer TFA to a nominated recipient site. It is at this point, when permission is granted, that an entry is made of the proposed transfer in a Register of Development Rights. Intervention by the Adelaide City Council at the third stage arises under Principle 17(10) when the owners of the donor site and the recipient site enter into an agreement with the Council pursuant to and in accordance with s.39(d) of the City of Adelaide Development Control Act.

15. In Uniting Church, Meagher AJ held, at 258, that at the date of the contract for the sale of "air space" which was 14 October 1988 (i.e. after recognition of the existence of "air space" rights by entry on the register at the time of the Council's first intervention, but before the Council's second intervention when permission for the sale of "air space" from the donor site was given), "the rights sold by the deed simply did not exist". It follows that the Court of Appeal considered the rights the sale of which was ordered to be specifically performed came into existence by force of the resolutions of the Council after the first stage.

16. In Halwood Corporation Ltd v Chief Commissioner of Stamp Duties (NSW) (1992) 92 ATC 4,155 the question on appeal from an assessment of ad valorem stamp duty assessed by the respondent on a deed by which the parties agreed to sell and transfer "transferable floor space" was whether the deed constituted an agreement for the sale or conveyance of any property. Section 3 of the Stamp Duties Act 1920 defined "property" so as to include:

"Real and personal property and any estate or interest in any

property real or personal, and any debt, and any thing in

action, and any other right or interest."

In Commissioner of Stamp Duties (NSW) v Yeend [1929] HCA 39; (1929) 43 CLR 235 the High Court had construed "any other right or interest" as limited to "proprietary rights".

17. The facts in Halwood Corporation Ltd were that the owner of an historic building in Sydney had been awarded a large area of transferable floor space which was noted by the Council in the Transferable Floor Space Register. The building owner transferred that transferable floor space to Daines Investments Pty Ltd which was the other party to the subject deed with the plaintiff which had been assessed to duty. By the deed Daines sought to transfer to the plaintiff part of the transferable floor space. The transferable floor space had already been accorded the status of transferability when those rights were transferred by the owner of the historic building to Daines.

18. For the plaintiff the argument was advanced that the transferable floor space was not a proprietary right, but a mere expectation that a proprietary right might come into existence in the future. The transfer of that expectation did not confer any rights enforceable against anyone; the Council's policies had no legal effect as the Council was still required to comply with the provisions of the Environmental Planning and Assessment Act 1979 (NSW) and was bound to refuse consent to the development if it were proper to do so having regard to criteria specified in the Acts. This is a similar argument to that advanced in the present case by the respondent as to the legal effect of the Principles.

19. The argument was rejected by Loveday J. His Honour applied the conclusions of Meagher JA in Uniting Church, and also the test propounded by Lord Wilberforce in National Provincial Bank Ltd v Ainsworth [1965] UKHL 1; (1965) AC 1175 at 1247-1248 to ascertain whether a new concept of right is to be regarded as a proprietary right. Lord Wilberforce said:

"Before a right or an interest can be admitted into the

category of property, or of a right affecting property, it must

be definable, identifiable by third parties, capable in its

nature of assumption by third parties, and have some degree of

permanence or stability."

The transferable floor space rights had these characteristics. His Honour concluded:

"Registration in the council's register confers an exclusive

entitlement to the floor space. The transferee of the

transferable floor space has a right recognised by the council

to have a development application considered by the council

taking into account the existence of the transferable floor

space. This is a valuable right not possessed by an applicant

for development approval without transferable floor space. The

reality is that commerce regards transferable floor space as a

proprietary right. The courts should do likewise."

The deed was held to be a transfer or conveyance of property.

20. In Re FAI Leasing Pty Ltd (Supreme Court of Queensland, unreported 13 September 1993) a company which was the owner of a building subject to the heritage provisions of the Town Plan for the City of Brisbane had granted a registered first mortgage to FAI Leasing Pty Ltd ("FAI"), and had subsequently granted a registered mortgage debenture to another financier, AGC, over "all the right title and interest of (the owner) in and to the transferable site area". AGC gave notice of assignment by way of charge of the transferable site area to the Brisbane City Council. FAI, on default occurring under the mortgage, claimed to be entitled to nominate the site to which the transferable site area could be transferred; AGC claimed that the transferable site area was unaffected by FAI's mortgage and it was the party entitled to the transferable site area by reason of the charge.

21. Clause 22 of the Town Plan, which unlike the Sydney City Council Codes and policy statements had legislative force as law, created "a transferable site area" which the owner of a heritage building could seek to have attached to another site to enhance the development capacity of that other site. Moynihan J, at p.9, described a transferable site area as being, in substance, the right (on the part of the owner of the heritage building) to have the Brisbane City Council consider, according to law, including the context of the relevant town planning provisions, whether to exercise a power in the holder's favour. If the discretion is exercised in the holder's favour, the transferable site area may be attached to another site. Having regard to s.24(6) of the City of Adelaide Development Control Act, Moynihan J's description of transferable site area accords with the nature of the right under Principles 17(1) and 17(3) in the owner of a conservation site to be granted permission to have TFA transferred to another site.

22. Moynihan J followed Uniting Church and Halwood Corporation Ltd. His Honour held that whilst the transferable site area did not form part of the fee simple of the land the subject of FAI's registered mortgage, the building owner's entitlement to transferable site area was a proprietary right capable of assignment and transfer, and capable of being acquired by AGC under the fixed charge held by it. A declaration was made in AGC's favour that it was entitled under the charge to the transferable site area.

23. The decision in Re FAI Leasing Pty Ltd appears at first sight to go further than Uniting Church. In Uniting Church Meagher JA considered that the owner of the historic site did not gain proprietary rights in "air space" merely when the Council notified the owner that notification had been placed in the Council's register that "air space" may be transferred for the historic site. The right arose by reason of later resolutions of the Council giving permission for the sale of "air space" subject to the fulfilment of conditions and confirming satisfaction of those conditions. In Re FAI Leasing Pty Ltd however, the owner of a heritage building was held to own proprietary rights to transferable site area before resolutions of the Brisbane City Council of a similar nature were passed. The explanation for the apparent difference in the decisions as to the timing of the creation of those rights is, I think, to be explained by the fact that the Brisbane Town Plan, which has legislative force, creates transferable site area rights by specifying in a table of Heritage Buildings the transferable site area of each building to which the scheme applies. Under the TFA Scheme in Adelaide there is no comparable table contained in the Principles.

24. It is not suggested in the present case that the applicant became the holder of TFA before the resolution of the Council under Principle 17(1) on 11 October 1988 which approved the transfer of TFA. The applicant contends that TFA, was acquired on 11 October 1988 when that approval was granted. I agree with that submission.

25. Clause 4 of the Agreement entered into on 5 October 1988 provided:

"4. This Agreement shall be subject to the Council agreeing to

permit the transfer of the transferable floor area in pursuance

of Principle 17."

The terms of the Agreement went on to require that the parties forthwith upon execution apply to the Council for approval, and acknowledged that the Council would be required under Principle 17(10), as a condition of approving the transfer of TFA pursuant to Principle 17(1), to require the owners of the donor site and the recipient site to enter into an agreement with the Council in accordance with s.39d of the City of Adelaide Development Control Act. The Agreement did not by its terms purport to create TFA. It provided for the transfer of TFA subject to, and following, approval of the Council under Principle 17(1). At 5 October 1988 when the Agreement was made the applicant had a mere expectancy that upon application to the Council TFA rights would be granted to it by approval given under Principle 17(1). If the application to the Council for approval were successful, the TFA rights thereby granted would be the subject of the transfer intended by the Agreement for the monetary consideration of $338,750. If not, the Agreement would lapse.

26. The application of Uniting Church and Halwood Corporation leads to the conclusion that the TFA rights granted by the Council's approval on 11 October 1988 were proprietary rights. Those rights were initially rights owned by the applicant. Upon transfer of those rights pursuant to the Agreement of 5 October 1988, which took effect on the registration of the memorial on 30 November 1988, the applicant ceased to be entitled to the benefits arising from 1355 square metres of TFA, and BHAS became entitled to those benefits. The commercial reality is that the applicant transferred the beneficial ownership of the proprietary rights constituted by the TFA to BHAS in exchange for the payment of a substantial money sum.

27. I do not consider it is helpful to emphasise that in the enjoyment of those rights BHAS will do so in relation to the recipient site whereas before the transaction those rights could only be enjoyed by the applicant in relation to the donor site. Where personal proprietary rights are transferred, for example goodwill in a business, or intellectual property rights, it is to be expected that those rights will be enjoyed in a different way, and in all likelihood at a different place, and at different times, from the way in which they would have been enjoyed had they be retained by the donor. I am unable to agree that in the present case what BHAS received was not anything which the applicant ever had. Before the transaction the applicant was entitled to have an application for development of the donor site considered by the Council on the footing that the plot ratio of the donor site would be calculated according to a floor area which included the subject TFA. After the transaction the applicant could only have such an application considered on the footing that the plot ratio calculation excluded that TFA. BHAS by the transaction gained the benefit of an exactly corresponding floor area for the purposes of an application for development on the recipient site. In my opinion that which the applicant transferred away by the transaction, BHAS gained. This is the reality of the situation which is reflected in the way in which the Principles are drawn, and in the way in which the transaction was implemented, including the payment of $12,382 stamp duty assessment on the conveyance.

28. For the purposes of Part IIIA, I consider the TFA rights transferred fall within the definition of "asset" in s.160A. I consider there was a disposal of that asset to BHAS within the ordinary meaning of that word: see Commissioner of Taxation v Cooling (1990) 22 FCR 42 per Hill J at 59-60. The TFA was owned by the applicant before the transfer took effect under the Principles. The disposal of the TFA by that transfer was matched by a corresponding acquisition by BHAS from the applicant. Relevantly, s.160M(1) provides:

"Subject to this Part, where a change has occurred in the

ownership of an asset, the change shall be deemed, for the

purposes of this Part, to have effected a disposal of the asset

by the person who owned it immediately before the change and an

acquisition of the asset by the person who owned it immediately

after the change."

Within the meaning of that subsection a change in the ownership of the asset occurred and that change is deemed to have effected a disposal of the asset for the purposes of Part IIIA by the applicant who owned it immediately before the change, and an acquisition of the asset by BHAS who owned it immediately afterwards.

29. I consider s.160M(6) has no application because the asset existed before the disposal, and was not created by the disposal. The TFA was not created by the Agreement of 5 October 1988. It was created by the approval granted by the Council under Principle 17(1) on 11 October 1988.

30. If there were a disposal of an asset for the purposes of Part IIIA, it is common ground that under 160ZD(1) the "consideration" for the disposal was the sum of $338,750.

31. No consideration was paid by the applicant for the grant of approval under Principle 17(1) by the Council. In these circumstances, for the purpose of determining the "cost base" or the "indexed cost base" s.160ZH(9) relevantly provided in 1988:

"For the purposes of the application of sub-section (1), (2) or

(3) in determining the cost base, the indexed cost base or the

reduced cost base to a taxpayer of an asset, if-

(a) the taxpayer acquired the asset from another person and did not

pay or give any consideration in respect of the acquisition;

(b) ...

(c) ...

the taxpayer shall be deemed to have paid or given as

consideration in respect of the acquisition of the asset an

amount equal to the market value of the asset at the time of

the acquisition."

32. In my opinion the applicant acquired the TFA "from another person" within the meaning of paragraph (a). The TFA was created by the resolution of the Council which granted approval under Principle 17(1). By that approval the applicant acquired the asset. There being no other provision of s.160ZH which would appear to cover the circumstances of this case more appropriately than s.160ZH(9), and as it is not suggested that there is any other provision of Part IIIA which deems no consideration to have been paid or given by the applicant in respect of the acquisition (see s.160ZH(10)), in my opinion it should be held that the applicant acquired the asset "from" the Council so as to bring the acquisition within s.160ZH(9). By analogy, just as an allottee of shares from a company acquires those shares from the company, so here the applicant acquired its TFA rights from the Council: Allina Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1991] FCA 78; (1991) 28 FCR 203. The applicant is therefore deemed to have paid or given as consideration in respect of the acquisition an amount equal to the market value of the asset at the "time of the acquisition".

33. Section 160U deals with the time of disposal and acquisition of an asset. The provisions of this section as they stood in 1988 do not appear to cover the circumstances of the acquisition in this case. Subsections (3), (4) and (5) provided:

"(3) Where the asset was acquired or disposed of under a

contract, the time of acquisition or disposal shall be taken to

have been the time of the making of the contract.

(4) Where the asset was acquired or disposed of otherwise than

under a contract, the time of acquisition or disposal shall be

taken to have been the time when the change in the ownership of

the asset that constituted or gave rise to the acquisition or

disposal occurred.

(5) Where the asset was constructed by a person otherwise than

pursuant to a contract under which the person constructed the

asset for another person, the time of acquisition of the asset

by the first-mentioned person shall be taken to have been the

time when the construction commenced."

The TFA was not acquired "under a contract" so as to bring the acquisition within s.160U(3). Although the Agreement of 5 October 1988 was placed before the Council as evidence of the transaction proposed between the applicant and BHAS, the TFA was not acquired by the applicant under that contract. It was acquired by the applicant independently of the contract by the exercise of the discretionary powers vested in the Council under the City of Adelaide Development Control Act and the Principles.

34. The fact that the asset was acquired otherwise than under a contract would bring the situation within the opening words of s.160U(4), but the balance of the provisions of that subsection are incapable of sensible application as there has been no change of ownership of the asset in connection with the acquisition of the asset by the applicant.

35. The asset was not "constructed" so as to bring the matter within s.160U(5).

36. The parties directed attention to s.160U(6) which provided:

"Where the asset was created by a person otherwise than

pursuant to a contract under which the person created the asset

for another person, the asset shall be taken to have been

acquired by the first-mentioned person-

(a) if the asset did not exist (either by itself or as part of

another asset) before the disposal - immediately before the

asset was disposed of; or

(b) in any other case - at the time of commencement of work on, or

of work that resulted in, the creation of the asset."

It is arguable that this subsection is confined in its operation to the creation of physical assets: cf Hepples v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1992] HCA 3; (1991) 173 CLR 492 per McHugh J at 545. Even if that were not so, the deeming provision is incapable of application as the asset was never owned by the person who created it, and never acquired by that person, namely the Council. Sections 160U(7), 160U(8), and 160U(9) have no application.

37. Whilst s.160U(1) provides that the time of acquisition or disposal for the purposes of Part IIIA "shall be ascertained in accordance with this section", the terms of the section do not cover the present situation. In these circumstances to make sense of the legislative scheme it is necessary to import ordinary principles to determine the time when the asset was acquired. I consider it should be held that the TFA rights were acquired when they were created by the grant of approval of the Council on 11 October 1988. I so hold, notwithstanding the unusual result which follows in this case from the application of the provisions of s.160U(3) to ascertain the time of disposal of the asset to BHAS. The disposal by the applicant was "under a contract" so, the time of the disposal is taken to have been the time of the making of the contract, that is on 5 October 1988, a date before the date of acquisition. Although the result may be unusual, it is one that may occur in ordinary commercial dealings. It is unexceptional for a vendor to agree to sell something to another before acquiring ownership of that thing for the purpose of completing the sale.

38. Upon the conclusions that the asset is to be treated as having been acquired by the applicant on 11 October 1988, and as having been disposed of by it on 5 October 1988, the submission of the applicant that the market value of the asset at the time of the acquisition was the same as the consideration received by the applicant on disposal should be accepted. Pursuant to s.160ZH(9) the relevant cost base is the same as the consideration received on disposal, and no capital gain accrued to the taxpayer.

39. I agree with the criticism made by Jenkinson J of Questions 1 and 2 in the Special Case. Counsel were agreed that it will be sufficient if the Court answer Question 3. Section 160ZO(1) to which Question 3(a) makes reference, provides that:

"Where a net capital gain accrued to a taxpayer in respect of

the year of income, the assessable income of the taxpayer of

the year of income includes that net capital gain."

The question assumes that the applicant incurred neither a capital loss nor a capital gain from other sources which are to be taken into account in arriving at a "net capital gain". It is apparent from the documents attached to the Special Case that this assumption is justified. In the circumstances I would therefore answer Question 3(a): No. The applicant's costs of the proceedings in this Court should be ordered to be paid by the respondent.
