FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

FRANCESCO GASPARIN v. COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

DECISION

JENKINSON J I agree in the judgment of von Doussa J.

SPENDER J I also agree in the judgment of von Doussa J.

VON DOUSSA This is an appeal from a single judge of this Court (O'Loughlin J) who dismissed an appeal from a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal which had affirmed an objection decision of the Commissioner of Taxation. The decision concerned an adjustment made to the returned income of the appellant for the year ended 30 June 1985.

2. The appellant since 1971 had carried on business as a builder and land developer. At material times he was one of the ultimate beneficiaries in a series of three trusts the leader of which was a participant in a joint venture with another company to purchase and subdivide land. In late 1984 land was purchased at Happy Valley. A plan for the establishment and approval of a residential subdivision of 88 allotments was prepared and lodged for approval. By the time the plan of subdivision was approved on 25 June 1985 the joint venturers had already entered into contracts of sale with various purchasers in respect of 73 of the allotments. Each contract was expressed to be subject to and conditional upon "the acceptance, deposit and registration of the plan of subdivision in the Land Titles Office on or before 30 July 1985". Each contract provided that settlement was to be effected within 14 days of the issue of a Certificate of Title by the Registrar-General for the relevant allotment. Certificates of Title were issued on 27 June 1985. It is common ground that all the conditions precedent in the 73 contracts of sale had been satisfied and that each of the contracts had become unconditional before 30 June 1985.

3. On or before 30 June 1985 contracts in respect of 9 of the allotments had been settled. The contracts in respect of the remaining 64 of the 73 allotments were settled in the following financial year. The joint venturers treated these 64 allotments in their books of account for the 1985 financial year as "closing stock on hand" for both accounting and taxation purposes. The Commissioner took a different view. The Commissioner formed the opinion that because the contracts in respect of these allotments had become unconditional before 30 June 1985, they had ceased to be trading stock on hand and had become "sales", and that the joint venturers had derived income from those sales in the 1985 financial year. This led the Commissioner to adjust upwards the income of the appellant as a beneficiary under the series of trusts. Having regard to the terms of resolutions of the different trusts in respect of the income of the 1985 financial year it was common ground that if the contentions of the Commissioner were correct, the adjustment was correctly made.

4. The Tribunal, and on appeal O'Loughlin J, agreed with the Commissioner.

5. The issue for determination is whether the profits from the proceeds of sale of each of the 64 allotments of land which undoubtedly formed assessable income in the hands of the joint venturers was assessable income derived by them in the year in which each of the contracts of sale became unconditional or in the year in which settlement took place.

6. The Tribunal found, and the parties agree, that the allotments of land in the 1985 financial year became part of the trading stock of the joint venturers. That land may comprise trading stock for the purposes of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") was settled by the High Court in The Commissioner of Taxation v St Hubert's Island Pty Ltd (In liquidation) (1977-1978) 138 CLR 210. The Tribunal held that when the contracts of sale became unconditional, the allotments to which the contracts related ceased to be trading stock as the vendors had lost their "dispositive power" over those allotments. The sale prices of those allotments were therefore to be brought to account as assessable income in the same financial year to balance the reduction of trading stock.

7. Mr Robertson QC who appeared with Mr S. O'Loughlin repeated before this Court the arguments which had been advanced below on the appellant's behalf. He contended that for the assessment under challenge to be correct a debt must have arisen between each purchaser and the vendors pursuant to the contracts of sale before 30 June 1985; that if no debt had arisen by that date under the unsettled contracts no income had been derived in respect of those contracts for the purposes of s.25 of the Act; and that in the sale of land where the purchase price is to be paid at completion no debt between the purchaser and the vendor is created until the vendor has completed his obligations to effect a transfer of the land on the completion date. Mr Robertson contended that the determinative question was whether a debt had arisen between the vendors and purchaser under each contract of sale rather than whether the subject allotment had ceased to be part of the vendors' trading stock on hand, but in any event he contended that the allotments to which the 64 unsettled contracts related remained trading stock on hand until completion.

8. In rejecting these arguments O'Loughlin J referred to the remarks of Gummow J in Barratt and Others v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1992) 107 ALR 385 at 394:

"But it is well to bear in mind that what is being sought is a

method giving, for each year of income, a substantially correct

reflex of the true income of the taxpayers, having regard to what

Fullagar J called the truth and reality of the situation: Ballarat

Brewing Co Ltd v FCT [1951] HCA 35; (1951) 82 CLR 364 at 369."

His Honour referred also to the decision of this Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Suttons Motors (Chullora) Wholesale Pty Ltd (1983) 83 ATC 4304 and All States Frozen Foods Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1990) 90 ATC 4175 as illustrating that the function of the Court is to seek out the truth and reality of the situation. His Honour considered that the realities of the present situation were that the 64 allotments the subject of the unsettled contracts had been disposed of when the contracts became unconditional and that a matching derivation of assessable income therefore arose for taxation purposes at that time. His Honour said:

"The allotments of land were undoubtedly "trading stock". They

had become the subjects of unconditional contracts of sale;

specific performance was available to the purchasers who had

become the equitable owners of the allotments. Although legal

title and possession remained with the vendor, he had lost his

"dispositive power"...

Notwithstanding the gap between contract and settlement and

notwithstanding the consequential delay in the right to recover

the unpaid purchase price I do not consider those matters are

sufficient to sustain the appellant's arguments. The event of

unconditional contracts constitutes, in my opinion, a disposal of

trading stock and a matching derivation of assessable income."

In substance, his Honour accepted the arguments of the Commissioner.

9. It is convenient first to consider the contention of the appellant that under the unsettled contracts of sale no debt had arisen by 30 June 1985. In McDonald and Another v Dennys Lascelles Limited [1933] HCA 25; (1933) 48 CLR 457 at 475-476 Dixon J, with whom Rich and McTiernan JJ agreed, cited with approval the following statements of principle by Salmond J in Ruddenklau v Charlesworth (1925) NZLR 161:

"As a general rule, on the failure or refusal of a purchaser to

complete an executory contract for the purchase of land the vendor

is not entitled to sue for the purchase money as a debt. He is

entitled merely to sue for specific performance or for damages for

the loss of his bargain. It is only when the contract has been

completed by the execution and acceptance of a conveyance that

unpaid purchase money may become a debt and can be recovered

accordingly. This general rule is sufficiently illustrated and

established by the case of Laird v. Pim [1841] EngR 237; (1841) 7 M. and W. 474; 151

ER 852. The sale of land is in this respect similar to the sale

of goods. In the case of goods sold and delivered, and of goods

bargained and sold, the property in each case having passed to the

buyer, the seller's remedy is to sue for the price. But if under

any executory contract the buyer wrongfully refuses to accept the

goods the seller's only remedy is an action for damages. The

general rule, however, that in an executory contract for the sale

of land the vendor cannot sue for the price is excluded whenever a

contrary intention is shown by the express terms of the contract.

And it seems established by authority that a contrary intention is

sufficiently shown in all cases in which by the express terms of

the contract the purchase money or any part thereof is made

payable on a fixed day, not being the agreed day for the

completion of the contract by conveyance. In all such cases the

purchase money or such part thereof becomes, on the day so fixed

for its payment, a debt immediately recoverable by the vendor

irrespective of the question whether a conveyance has been

executed and notwithstanding the fact that the purchaser may have

repudiated his contract. Notwithstanding such repudiation the

vendor is not bound to sue for damages or specific performance,

but may recover the agreed purchase money."

These principles were applied by the High Court in Sunbird Plaza Proprietary Limited v Maloney and Another [1988] HCA 11; (1987-1988) 166 CLR 245. In that case a contract of sale of a home unit provided for a 10 per cent deposit on the exchange of contracts and that the balance of the purchase price was payable "upon settlement" which was to occur within 14 days after notice from the vendor to the purchaser of the registration of a building unit plan. The terms of that contract bear similarity to the terms of the contracts of sale in the present case. In the events which happened settlement had not taken place and there had been no conveyance when the matter came before the Court. It was held that the balance of the purchase price had not become a debt payable by the purchaser. Mason CJ, with whom Deane, Dawson and Toohey JJ agreed, said at 253-254:

"Here the balance of the purchase price was payable 'upon

settlement'. Settlement has not taken place and there has been no

conveyance of the property sold. Once this is accepted, the

appellant is faced with a daunting task in making good the

submission that the respondent guarantors are liable under their

joint and several guarantee to pay the balance of the purchase

price and interest thereon, notwithstanding the absence of an

accrued liability on the part of the purchaser to make the

payment."

In my opinion the application of the general rule approved in these cases, and also in Automatic Fire Sprinklers Pty Ltd and Another v Watson [1946] HCA 25; (1946) 72 CLR 435 at 463-464, to the unsettled contracts of sale in the present case leads to the conclusion that at 30 June 1985 there were no debts owing by the purchasers to the joint venturers. In each case the promise to pay the balance of the purchase price "at settlement" remained executory until settlement occurred at which time a conveyance was executed, accepted and delivered to the purchaser. Until these events there was no accrued liability on the part of the purchaser to pay the balance of the purchase price.

10. Mr Robertson's submission that the validity of the assessment under challenge depends on there having arisen debts under the unsettled contracts at 30 June 1985 commenced with Carden's case: The Commissioner of Taxes (South Australia) v The Executor Trustee and Agency Company of South Australia Ltd [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 CLR 108. The imposition of the tax under consideration in Carden was on "all incomes arising or accruing in or derived from the State ..." in the year of tax, and the question was whether a medical practitioner should return only his actual receipts in the relevant year, or whether he should include unpaid fees earned in that period, that is whether his return should be made upon a cash basis as distinct from an earnings basis. The Court held, with Latham CJ dissenting, that in past years Dr Carden's professional income had been properly assessed upon actual receipts. Although the decision of the Court distinguished between the considerations which apply to taxpayers like Dr Carden who practised a profession, and traders who acquired or manufactured or carried a stock of vendible articles, the position of such traders was considered. Dixon J, with whom Rich and McTiernan JJ agreed, said at 155:

"Speaking generally, in the assessment of income the object is to

discover what gains have during the period of account come home to

the taxpayer in a realized or immediately realizable form."

and later at 155-156 he said:

"The computation of profits from manufacture and trading has

always proceeded upon the principle that the profit may be

contained in stock-in-trade and 'outstandings.' Whether this is

to be explained on some view that the purpose is to ascertain what

is the detachable increase in circulating capital, or more simply

on the ground of common sense and the teachings of experience, the

result for the purposes of taxation is the same. The result is

that a tax upon the profits or income of such a business must be

understood as a tax upon the profits or income computed according

to the system, because, according to common understanding and

commercial principles, that is the method of determining the

profits. The basis of a trading account is stock on hand at the

beginning and end of the period and sales and purchases. In such

an account book debts represent what before sale was trading stock

and it is almost inevitable that they should be taken into

consideration upon an accrual and not a cash basis."

Latham CJ who considered Dr Carden should be treated as a trader said at 123:

"In the case of traders, where tax is imposed upon the profits of

a trade, profits are calculated both in Australia and in England

on an earnings basis; that is to say, the trade debts which fall

due to the taxpayer during the year are credited and allowance is

made for bad debts."

Later, the Chief Justice said at 124:

"In the case of a trader it is well established that he must take

into account book debts owed to him as part of his income, at

least where those book debts fall due during the year in respect

of which he is making his return. An allowance may be made under

statutory provisions for bad or doubtful debts, but, subject to

such an allowance, the book debts must be returned as part of a

trader's income."

And at 125 he said:

"But trade debts which have accrued due in the relevant year but

which have not been paid must be included for the purpose of

ascertaining whether or not the business has earned a profit for

the year, just as stock in trade at the beginning and end of the

year must be taken into account for the same purpose."

Carden's case was applied in J Rowe and Son Pty Ltd v The Commissioner of Taxation [1970] HCA 57; (1970-1971) 124 CLR 421. The taxpayer conducted a retail store selling household goods either for cash, on payment within 30 days, or on extended terms of up to five years which provided for payment by instalments, interest, and security over the goods by bill of sale. The Court was required to determine the proper method of calculating the income derived in each year of trading by the sales on extended terms. The provisions of the Act relating to stock-in-trade applied to the taxpayer. The taxpayer contended that the amounts to be included in its assessable income were only such amounts as were received or receivable in the year of income. The Court rejected that contention and upheld assessments which included the full amount to be received under the agreements for sale on extended terms, except to the extent which that amount included interest on instalments payable in future years.

11. Menzies J with whom Barwick CJ agreed said at 448:

"It is implicit in the foregoing provisions that the proceeds of

any sale of stock in the ordinary course of business will be

brought into account in the year in which it is sold. The problem

then is to determine what are the proceeds of sales in the year in

which the taxpayer sells items of trading stock to customers upon

terms and takes a bill of sale to secure payment in the manner

already stated.

In a system of annual accounting, ordinary business considerations

would indicate that what becomes owing to a company for trading

stock sold during a year should, in some way, be brought into

account to balance the reduction of trading stock which the

transaction effects. Any other method of accounting would lead to

a misrepresentation of the trader's financial position."

and later at 448-449:

"It is accepted that, for taxation, as well as for business

purposes, the income of such a business is derived when it is

earned and the receipt of what is earned is not necessary to bring

the proceeds of sale into account. The acceptance of this basis

of accounting is recognized by the provisions of the Act relating

to the writing off of bad debts which 'have been brought to

account by the taxpayer as assessable income of any year': see

s.63."

His Honour then cited passages from Carden, and expressed his conclusion at 450:

"I consider income from the sale of stock is derived when the

stock is sold and a debt is created. It need not be payable in

the year of income."

12. As the above passages indicate the decision in a particular case must be tempered by ordinary business considerations. In determining the reality of the situation assistance may in some cases be afforded by accounting evidence and practice: see Carden at 151-153 and Arthur Murray (NSW) Pty Ltd v The Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 58; (1965) 114 CLR 314 at 318. But the accounting evidence in this case, contained in a letter tendered by consent before the Tribunal from a chartered accountant Mr B W Ellery, is to the effect that current accounting standards offer no guidance as to when income should be treated as being derived under an unconditional contract for the sale of land where the transaction remains to be completed. Mr Ellery concluded his letter by saying:

"I confirm that my research has revealed that neither the date of

unconditional contract, nor the date of settlement, are generally

accepted as the proper method in Australia of recognising revenues

from sale of real estate. It appears that each method has been

adopted."

The Court was also referred to "The Anatomy of Accounting" by A D Benton 2nd Edit. University of Queensland but that text merely confirms Mr Ellery's opinion saying that there is no uniform opinion in the accounting profession about when to recognise revenue from a selling transaction.

13. Absent assistance from the evidence about business or accounting practice, counsel for the appellant was in my opinion correct to lay stress upon the prominence given in Carden and Rowe to the importance of there being a debt in conjunction with a sale of trading stock before income is derived.

14. In expressing its conclusions upon the present case the Tribunal said:

"I have therefore concluded that, if (the joint venturers) were

permitted to include in (their) assessable income for the 1985

year only those sales for which payment had been received in that

year, this would constitute a 'misrepresentations of (their)

financial position'. (cf Menzies J at 448 in Rowe's case cited

above). Stripped of its own peculiar features, this case is, in

reality, no different from that of a Department store which

permits account customers 30 days before payment becomes due. Can

it be argued that sales effected between 25 June and the end of

the financial year are not to be brought into account in that year

because it left the store with 'no dispositive powers over the

goods', or because there is no 'debt due' until the following

year? Once the case is reduced to these basic terms, the answer

becomes at once obvious; cf Rowe (supra) at 448)."

With respect to the Tribunal, I am unable to agree that there is no difference in the present case from a Department store sale of articles on 30 days terms. In that example there has been a delivery of the articles sold. The contract of sale is no longer executory. A debt has accrued due; the debtor has become subject to an obligation to pay a sum certain in money even though the debt may not be payable forthwith: cf Carden at 127.

15. Further, I do not find the reliance which the Commissioner's contentions place on the need to match disposals from the trading stock to the derivation of income helpful in this case. Usually there must be such a matching to ensure a correct representation of a trader's financial position, and to ensure that the method of accounting adopted to ascertain that position is one calculated to give a substantially correct reflex of the taxpayer's true income. In the circumstances of Rowe where cash receipts could occur over up to five years after trading stock was sold and delivered to the purchaser, the need to match the disposal of trading stock to revenue earned was an important consideration. But in the present case such a matching will occur if it is held that that trading stock is not disposed of for the purposes of the Act until there is under a transaction for the sale of land both a loss of "dispositive power" and the accrual to the trader of a debt due by the purchaser. In the present case, reliance on the requirements of matching does not help to determine when in point of time the matching should occur. The requirement of matching is met satisfactorily either by treating the 64 allotments the subject of unsettled contracts of sale as trading stock on hand at 30 June 1985 so that the transactions are brought to account in the following financial year (the situation for which the appellant contends) or by treating the allotments as being disposed of before that date and bringing the proceeds of sale to account in the 1985 financial year (the situation for which the Commissioner contends).

16. The references to loss of "dispositive powers" in the reasons of the Tribunal and the Judge below emanate from the judgments of Rich J and Dixon J in Farnsworth v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 27; (1949) 78 CLR 504. In my opinion three points emerge from this case which strongly support the arguments of the appellant. In Farnsworth, the taxpayer, a fruitgrower, had delivered fruits to a packing company where it became intermingled with the fruits of other growers so that it became impossible to identify the fruit of any particular grower. The fruit was processed, packed and sold by the packing company which operated under the rules of a marketing association of which the taxpayer was a member. Under the rules of the association the proceeds of sale were distributed to members when the fruit was sold, less marketing expenses. By the end of the financial year in question various progress payments had been made, and the packing company estimated that in addition the taxpayer would receive net proceeds of sale from unsold fruit supplied during the financial year of 648 pounds. The Commissioner included the sum of 648 pounds as well as the progress payments actually received in the taxpayer's assessable income. It was held that this sum was wrongly included. The interest of the taxpayer in the unsold fruit in the pool was not, within the meaning of s.28 of the Act "trading stock on hand" of the taxpayer at the end of the financial year. The sum of 648 pounds was not the value of "trading stock" within s.36 of the Act and it was not on any other ground part of the taxpayer's assessable income in that year.

17. The first point from the decision which I consider supports the appellant arises from the discussion of the facts by Rich J and Dixon J. Rich J said at 515:

"A review of the facts leads me to the conclusion that after a

fruit grower has delivered his fruit to the packing company and it

has been processed he has lost all control and power of

disposition of it and his fruit has become so inextricably mixed

with the fruit of other growers as to be incapable of

identification. The property in the fruit passed to the packing

company and became converted into a claim for an account. The

grower's interest is not an equitable interest but an equity to an

account enforceable by action."

Dixon J said at 518:

"In my opinion it is not possible to treat the sum as the value of

the taxpayer's stock in trade on hand at the end of the accounting

period. The taxpayer's dried fruit had ceased to be 'on hand'

within the meaning of s.28 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1943

when she delivered it to the packing shed. The packing house

cannot be treated as a mere agent of the taxpayer holding her

stock in trade for sale. The taxpayer's dried fruit had been

delivered irrevocably to the packing house upon terms, complicated

no doubt, which entitled her to a money sum and left her with no

dispositive power over the fruit and no power to direct or control

the disposal by the packing house of the fruit either alone or in

combination with other suppliers. Of course the packing house was

contractually bound to process, pack and sell the fruit in the

appointed manner. The obligations it incurred have, I imagine, a

fiduciary character. For the packing house is bound to account

for the proceeds of the fruit pooled and to make a proper

distribution."

Both judgments stress that the taxpayer had lost all dispositive power over her fruit, and this fact led to the conclusion that the fruit was no longer trading stock on hand. The fruit was personal property capable of physical delivery and in the circumstances of the case property in the fruit had passed by the act of physical delivery and the subsequent intermingling and processing activities. In the present case the "trading stock" was real property. The allotments in question remained registered in the name of the vendors until settlement. Until then the vendors had not lost all dispositive power, and had not ceased to have any proprietary interest in the land. If, for any reason a purchaser under an unsettled contract of sale did not proceed, the vendors could elect to treat the contract at an end, and resell the allotment. Prior to settlement, under the contracts of sale the purchasers undoubtedly acquired interests in equity and rights to specific performance, but the vendors did not become bare trustees for the purchasers. The vendors retained substantial interests in the allotments which they enjoyed as beneficial owners: see Kern Corporation Ltd v Walter Reid Trading Pty Ltd and Others [1987] HCA 20; (1987) 163 CLR 164 at 191 per Deane J, and Stern and Anor v McArthur and Anor [1988] HCA 51; (1988) 165 CLR 489 at 521-522 per Deane and Dawson JJ. In the latter case Deane and Dawson JJ observed that:

"Any right to equitable ownership on the part of the purchaser is

contingent only, being subject to the payment of the purchase

money and being said to exist only so long as the contract remains

specifically enforceable at his suit."

The element of contingency is an important one. In Barratt and Others v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1992) 107 ALR 385 at 393-394 Gummow J, with whom the other members of the Court agreed said:

"No doubt a debt that is presently recoverable by action generally

will be an amount 'derived' in the relevant sense by the creditor.

The creditor will have a present right to receive the amount in

question, something both earned and quantified, without the

presence of any element of contingency or defeasibility. At the

other end of the scale, where the right of the taxpayer is

contingent, there will be no derivation before the contingency is

satisfied: see R W Parsons, Income Taxation in Australia:

Principles of Income, Deductibility and Tax Accounting, Law Book

Co, 1985,

para. 11.49. Nor will there be derivation

if the debt is yet

to be quantified: Farnsworth v FCT (1949)

[1949] HCA 27; 78 CLR 504 at 513 per

Latham CJ."

This leads on to the second point arising from the decision in Farnsworth which I consider supports the appellant's arguments. The contentions of the Commissioner do not satisfactorily address the situation which would arise in the event that a contract of sale did not proceed to settlement. If the appellant's approach is correct, the allotments remained trading stock on hand until each transaction progressed to the point where a debt accrued due from the purchaser i.e. at settlement. If, before that point were reached, a contract of sale came to an end, the allotment would remain as stock on hand and no accounting or taxation consequences would arise, save in respect of the deposit payment made by the purchaser which might, depending on the circumstances in which the contract of sale came to an end, be forfeited to the vendors and thereupon become an actual receipt at that time. On the other hand, if the Commissioner's approach is correct so that the income is taken to be derived from a sale as soon as a contract of sale becomes unconditional, the Act does not readily provide a means for reversing the imputed derivation of income from the contract which later failed. The difficulty arises from the fact that there is until settlement no debt which can be brought to account as a bad debt under s.63. In Rowe's case Menzies J at 448-449 referred to the recognition which the Act gives in the provisions in relation to bad debts to the basis of accounting that requires that there be a debt which is brought to account as derived income. The point was more fully explained by Dixon J in Farnsworth at 519 when he said:

"A third ground was taken for the commissioner in support of the

assessment. It is said that the taxpayer carried on a business in

which she produced a commodity and turned it into money. The

proceeds of such a business should be assessed, it is urged, on an

earnings basis, that is by a comparison of the position of the

business at the beginning and end of an accounting period, taking

into account all accruals and outstandings. Trading stock is

defined in the Act, s.6, to include anything produced. From this

it may be seen, it was said, that the Act contemplates such a

basis of assessment for primary production. Therefore, so it was

contended, the sum of

648 pounds should be

taken into the accounts of

the business as an outstanding for the year of income ending 30th

June 1943. The argument has, I think, weight; but it involves

difficulties. The sum expected to arise from the pool cannot be

described as a debt. If the sum was not realized in the ensuing

accounting period, the deficiency could not be brought within

s.63. If the deficiency could be brought in as a loss or debit it

must be on general principles of accounting. To bring it in

somehow would be essential to a fair assessment. Yet the

structure of the Act is hardly consistent with a debit which will

not fall within any category of authorized deduction. It could

not readily be brought within s.51. These are some of the

difficulties."

18. The third point in support of the appellant's argument arises, in my opinion, from the decision itself. Although Latham CJ reached the conclusion that the unsold fruit was not trading stock on hand for reasons which differed from those of Rich and Dixon JJ (he considered there was no evidence to support this conclusion), the whole Court held that no part of the fruit was trading stock on hand. It must follow that the stock had been disposed of during the financial year: cf All States Frozen Foods Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1990) 90 ATC 4175 at 4176-4177. Yet the decision of the Court was that the taxpayer had not derived income in the financial year in which the disposal of the trading stock occurred. The decision, in my opinion, does not support the Commissioner's argument that notions of matching require a loss of dispositive power by the joint venturers over the relevant allotments before 30 June 1985, to be balanced at the same time by treating as derived income profits expected to be received at a later date under the unsettled contracts of sale. On the contrary I think the decision is against the argument.

19. In Farnsworth the amount of 648 pounds was an estimate. There was uncertainty whether that precise amount would be realised net of expenses by future sales. Latham CJ at 513 contrasted such an estimate with a debt, which is a sum certain. See also Barratt's case at 393-394. The Tribunal sought to distinguish Farnsworth and Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Australian Gas Light Co and Anor [1983] FCA 341; (1983) 52 ALR 691 upon the ground that the anticipated profits of the transactions under consideration were merely estimates whereas the anticipated proceeds of sale in the present case were sufficiently quantified by the stated purchase price in each contract of sale. In my opinion this is not a satisfactory ground of distinction. The reason for the proposition stated in the judgments of the High Court that income is derived when a debt has accrued due, is to eliminate the element of contingency and uncertainty in the exercise of determining when income is derived for the purpose of levying taxation. The basis of the decisions was the absence of a debt, an accrued liability which superseded the estimate with a sum certain.

20. It is not clear from the papers that the assumption can properly be made that the statement of a purchase price in each unsettled contract of sale enabled the anticipated profits from that contract of sale to be reliably fixed at 30 June 1985. Until settlement occurred the duration of holding costs, and unexpected costs associated with settlement (e.g. due to delays by a purchaser) could not be known. However, this point was not raised before this Court, and does not appear to have been raised below.

21. In my opinion it should be held that the joint venturers derived income from the sale of the allotments of land which comprised their trading stock not when the contracts became unconditional, but at settlement when a debt accrued due from each purchaser to the joint venturers. The critical consideration is the time when the debt arose. It should also be held that each allotment remained trading stock on hand until settlement, that being the point of time in a transaction for the sale of land under a contract of sale in the terms of those before the Court when the vendor finally loses all dispositive power, and the contingency that the sale will not proceed to completion disappears.

22. I consider this conclusion is in accordance with the guidance or "signposts" (see Australian Gas Light Co at 698) given by the other decisions on which the appellant also relies, Henderson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1969] HCA 14; (1969-1970) 119 CLR 612 at 650-651, Barratt at 393-395 and Australian Gas Light Co both at first instance at 83 ATC 4220 at 4232-4322 and on appeal at [1983] FCA 341; 52 ALR 691. The conclusion is also consistent with the decision in Benwerrin Developments Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1981) 39 ALR 229 where it was held that for the purpose of s.36(8) of the Act trading stock comprising real estate was disposed of at the date of completion under a contract of sale. McGarvie J at 234 said:

"I considered that in the ordinary commercial sense the date of

completion ... was in this case the date of disposal. On that

day, the vendors became entitled to and received the balance of

the purchase price, and then held the property free of any

vendors' lien as bare trustees for the purchaser. The purchaser

at that stage was both beneficial owner and entitled to

possession. The transfer of the possession is a consideration of

importance. Rose v FCT [1951] HCA 68; (1951) 5 AITR 197 at 200-201; [1951] HCA 68; 84 CLR 118

at 123-124. The purchaser was placed in a position to acquire the

legal freehold estate in the land by registering the transfer.

The word 'sale' is apt to refer to a completed sale: R v Canadian

Pacific Railway Co (1911) AC 328 at 333-334. No one has suggested

that the date of registration of the transfer, which fell in the

next income year, was the day of disposal."

23. I would allow the appeal. There should be consequential orders that the appeal from the decision of the Tribunal be allowed; that the decision of the Tribunal be set aside; that the decision of the Commissioner disallowing the objection by the taxpayer be set aside; and that the objection be allowed insofar as the assessment under challenge depends on the issue of derivation argued before the Tribunal and before this Court. The Commissioner should pay the costs of the appellant of the appeal before O'Loughlin J and before this Court.
