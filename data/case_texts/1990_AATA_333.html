REASONS FOR DECISION

19 January 1990 Mr. Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

  1. These reasons relate to the affairs of three taxpayers and the years of income ended 30 June 1984 to 1986 inclusive. Seven assessments are in dispute. At least these taxpayers could afford the fees of $2,100 required at the present time to secure a single hearing. The taxpayers are all bodies corporate. They were three of a number of companies and other entities in a large group of commercial interests. Unless otherwise indicated, I shall refer to the three Applicants as "the Group". The affairs of the Group were under the complete control of Alexander (who for convenience I shall refer to as "Alex") until he chose to pass control and responsibility to a successor whom he had groomed to assume that responsibility. That period of transition was in place within the relevant period and it was possibly complete before it ended. But to appreciate the events of the relevant period, those years need to be placed in historical context.

THE BACKGROUND

  1. In 1967, Alex, already married, but for all that a very young man, was suddenly faced with the challenge of assuming responsibility for the family business following the death of his father. The business was in a precarious condition financially. He responded to the challenge. The challenge was to prove to be far greater than might have been expected because a revolution in trading conditions and in organisation was to overwhelm many in the industry during the 1970's and thereafter. Under the guidance of Alex, not only did the organisation survive the challenge, it actually thrived. In a period when most of its competitors abandoned the field, the Group prospered. In a period when Australian production was nearly halved, Alex increased production. Where once he had been producing only some 150 units a week, he came to produce 4,500 units per week.
  2. One unusual feature of his story, perhaps surprising to some, although it should not be so, was that Alex had had a very limited education. That did not mean that he was not highly intelligent. Moreover, he had the qualities of personality and intelligence which mark a gifted leader; gifts which do not invariably attend appointments to positions of authority and responsibility. He respected his subordinates and their efforts and made known his appreciation of them. He demonstrated his capacity by working alongside them, thereby showing his understanding of their problems. As he said, in his early days his duties included, those of machinist and mechanic on the factory floor. I have no doubt that his personal mastery of the skills of many of his subordinates, not only in relation to machinery, gained him respect in their eyes. He was not contemptuous of his subordinates and did not denigrate their efforts. Rather he selected those whom he considered to be worthy of being trusted with responsibility and thereafter left them to discharge that responsibility on their own initiative, but always subject to his overriding direction. I am satisfied that he led his subordinates, not by simply issuing orders at them and by blunt assertions of authority, but by gaining their respect and co-operation. He was a leader.
  3. That is not to say that he was not a man of strong views or that he was tolerant of low standards or of indiscipline. That was clear from the testimony of a senior employee - a man whose family lost the place in the industry that it had held over some generations. His evidence made plain the respect in which Alex was held, particularly for the circumstance that Alex, having placed responsibility in subordinates, then demonstrated his trust and confidence in them by leaving to them the discharge of those responsibilities. But he also made it clear that, once Alex had made a decision or given a direction, it was expected to be obeyed.
  4. The influence of the personality of Alex was not confined to dealings with his own staff. It extended to the retail trade. Throughout the Mainland States, he instituted and thereafter maintained a program of product promotion which was so effective that with time he came to be accepted as the person identifying future market trends to the retail trade, rather than merely performing the role of a manufacturer responding only to the demands of the trade as expressed by retailers.
  5. Alex also came to play a substantial part in the design of products. That involvement was of critical importance to financial success in the highly volatile and ever changing fashion industry in which the Group was engaged. Thereby he contributed very substantially to the success of the Group. But it was not invariably so. From time to time he failed to accurately predict how demand would change and for such misjudgments both he and the Group suffered. Such errors were expensive. That is not surprising for a Group involved in the fickle fashion industry. To predict or, a fortiori to create, a change in fashion could generate handsome rewards. To fail in the attempt could cause substantial losses.
  6. There is a further dimension to be mentioned. Commencing as early as 1968, Alex became a frequent visitor to the international markets; surveying the newest trends in public demand; and the latest developments in equipment. For a manufacturer which at the time only occupied a small place in the Australian market, that was a remarkably bold initiative, but it was to be one which contributed in large measure to the growth and success which was to come.
  7. I accept that initially Alex worked extraordinary hours and that his work for the Group was his life. However, I also find that, as the years progressed, his hours of work reduced although still remaining quite exceptionally high. Furthermore, I find that, as the Group expanded its activities - from some 30 employees in 1967 to about 360 by 1986 - his responsibilities changed in character. He moved from being a mechanic and machinist directly contributing to the production of units to a person organising through his subordinates every aspect of the internal affairs of the Group and also planning for the future in terms of design; of marketing; of equipment acquisition; arranging financing for equipment and materials acquisition, particularly in overseas markets; and also heavily involved in marketing. Marketing was to play a very substantial part in the development of the Group. It operated at two levels. The first was a level in which Alex was very much personally involved, travelling frequently to deal directly with persons of influence throughout the Mainland States in all major retailing organisations. In that way he was very much the personal image of the Group. The person of Alex was of critical importance to the success of those marketing endeavours, and thereby he came to exercise very considerable influence in the creation of demand. The second level of marketing activity did not involve him personally to the same extent, although here too he played a part in the devising of media projects. I refer to marketing campaigns directed to the public at large through the print and electronic media.
  8. The intense commitment of earlier days involving seven days per week, with work commonly commencing in the factory before 4.00 a.m. and extending well into the evening, was not maintained- permanently. In later years the pattern changed to five days per week for up to thirteen hours per day plus Saturday mornings. Despite the circumstance that from the early 1970's massive industrial change was under way, by 1972 Alex was able to indulge a personal interest - he acquired on lease a cattle grazing property not far from a capital city. By 1979 he had acquired a freehold of a more substantial property by buying nearly 250 hectares (c. 600 acres) somewhat closer to that city. From that point he extended his interests to the breeding and training of race horses on a substantial scale. He attended to those interests quite closely and, in some periods when he was not interstate or overseas, he would visit daily. But he surrendered responsibility for those projects to employees to a far greater extent than he had in manufacturing and in consequence he exercised less personal control. It is also to be noted that, unlike many who undertake such primary production ventures, he employed the labour of quite a number of other persons in those projects.
  9. His primary production activities generated substantial losses. For a person developing a new primary production enterprise, particularly one having the character of that undertaken by Alex, that is not surprising. That is not to say that such an undertaking is commercially unsound. It is a fact of economic life in this country that the development of rural land by bringing it to a state of greater productivity ordinarily generates revenue losses and capital gains. Such undertakings are particularly attractive to persons who, by reason of the derivation of substantial income from other sources, are not only capable of bearing the revenue losses involved in the development of rural land, but also have the advantage of being able to claim deductions for the primary production losses against other income in the determination of taxable income. So it was that, until 19 September 1985, the Parliament saw fit to allow such capital gains to be non-taxable, and at all times the outlays on revenue account which contributed to the capital gain to be deductible in the determination of taxable income.
  10. Some further generalisations need to be made by way of introduction. When Alex took over the business, it was the business of a small manufacturer in the highly volatile fashion industry. Alex changed that. The Group so developed that before the commencement of the relevant period and throughout it, one company in the Group became one of Australia's largest manufacturers in the field. That company is the Applicant I shall refer to as "Make-Co". Make-Co supplied product to two marketing companies; one, which I shall refer to as "Boy-Co", concentrated on the market in men's fashions; the other, "Girl-Co", concentrated on the market in ladies' fashions. Manufacture and marketing of ladies' fashions had formed no part of the activities of the business in 1967. The three companies are the Applicants.

THE ISSUES

  1. Under the leadership of Alex, the Group not only substantially developed its activities, in time it also came to be highly profitable. That came to be to the very substantial advantage (inter alia) of Alex. The rewards he received came to him principally in the character of an employee and largely by way of substantial bonuses paid over and above a small salary. The first issue in these references is to determine whether the companies paying those bonuses are entitled to claim the entirety of the amounts so paid as deductions to be brought to account in the determination of their taxable income.
  2. A second issue relates to some of the expenses of advertising. In the ordinary course of carrying on business, both Boy-Co and Girl-Co incurred very substantial expenses in advertising throught the print and electronic media. The issue to be determined is whether, as a result of action taken in the months of June 1985 and June 19B6, the companies concerned were entitled to claim certain deductions in the assessment of taxable income for those years.
  3. The third issue relates to the cost of raw materials. By reason of action taken in the months of June 1985 and June 1986, Make-Co claims to be entitled to an income tax deduction in relation to raw materials which were actually supplied, used and paid for in the following year of income.
  4. The fourth issue relates to liability to, and the remission of, additional tax for incorrect returns.
  5. The final issue, but the over-riding issue when it occurs, is whether the Commissioner had power to issue the amendments which he did.

THE ASSESSMENTS

  1. The Commissioner by his officers conducted an audit of the affairs of all companies concerned. That work was carried out in the latter part of the calendar year 1986; following the close of the year of income ended 30 June 1986; and prior to the assessment of income tax for that year of income. As a result of that investigation the Commissioner issued amended assessments increasing the taxable incomes of the companies by reason of the factors previously mentioned. In the case of Make-Co, in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1985, it was also increased by four other factors and, in the case of Boy-Co for the same year, by one other factor. Those additional factors are no longer in dispute. In consequence, according to the view formed by the Commissioner each company became liable pursuant to Section 223 of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act") to penalties by way of additional tax equal to 200% of the tax avoided. Having so determined, the Commissioner thereon discharged his duty to consider the remission of that additional tax and did so in accordance with his then standard practices whereby additional tax was reduced to a sum equal to 20% per annum of "tax avoided" during the period of evasion (as he calculated it) plus 40% of tax "avoided".

THE BONUS ISSUE

  1. It has been acknowledged by Counsel for the Commissioner that the bonuses paid would have been deductible but for the provisions of Section 109 of the Act. The Applicants contend that, notwithstanding Section 109 of the Act, the deductions claimed are wholly allowable or, alternatively, are allowable in greater sums that have been considered reasonable and have been allowed by the Commissioner.
  2. Section 109 of the Act at the material times was in the following terms:

"So much of a sum paid or credited by a private company to a person who is or has been a shareholder or director of the company...., being, or purporting to be -

(a) remuneration for services rendered by that person; or

(b) ....

as exceeds an amount which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, is reasonable, shall not be an allowable deduction

  1. In making the decision which is called for I particularly bear in mind the purpose of Section 109 of the Act as most recently explained in Ferrst v F.C.T. (88 A.T.C. 4755). With that in mind, I have regard to a number of considerations, some of which favour the claims of the Applicants; some of which favour the argument of the Commissioner. Considerations supporting the claims of the Applicants are:
(a) the quite exceptional, but not unique, skills and efforts of Alex; the long period in which those talents have been applied in the interests of the Group; and the very high level of success which ultimately flowed from those efforts;

(b) the circumstance that, as the expert witnesses agreed, it was reasonable that a person such as Alex should be rewarded by a remuneration package comprising a fixed base salary and a variable performance - bonus factor;

(c) that in his case, the fixed base factor was always remarkably low, and was also remarkable in that it had constantly remained low at $23,400;

(d) that it was reasonable that the lower the fixed base factor, the higher should be the potential result of any bonus factor;
  • (e) that measured over the years from 1979 - the earliest point which figures were available - the remuneration paid to Alex had been substantially less than in the years under reference - particularly in the 1981 and 1982 years;
  • (f) that the Group was entirely dependent upon the goodwill of Alex who had a capacity to destroy what he had so painstakingly built up;
  • (g) that it is not unreasonable that employees should share in the prosperity of employers, particularly in such remarkable profitability as this and, especially so, as that profitability was so closely tied to the performance of Alex; and
  • (h) that, over many years whether Alex would ever be fully paid would depend upon the companies having continued success. That being so the deferrment of payment placed Alex at risk of never being fully paid. So too in earlier years any attempt on the part of Alex to recover sums such as were later paid could have destroyed the viability of the companies to the detriment of both shareholders qua shareholders and Alex as an employee.
  1. I reject the argument for the Commissioner that one should only have regard to the moneys paid as a reward for services rendered to the Group during that financial year. On the other hand, I also reject as an over-simplification the argument advanced for the Applicants that, so long as shareholders were obtaining a "good return" or a "reasonable return" upon their investments that the distribution by way of bonus could not have been unreasonable.
  2. However, there are other factors which also have to be considered which are said to favour the contentions of the Commissioner. It was contended that, coincident with the transition of authority and responsibility from Alex to his successor, there had been a reduction in the efforts of, and the responsibilities borne by, Alex. I accept that to some degree that is so, but its significance should not be exaggerated. I am satisfied that the successor did not actually have all the authority, or all the responsibility, which it had pleased Alex to let him think that he had. I accept the evidence of Alex that he allowed his successor to make mistakes as part of his successor's learning experience. What is significant in that finding is that it follows that Alex permitted his successor to follow his own judgment. Implicit in that is acceptance of the proposition that Alex might have prevented that. I am satisfied that in the relevant period Alex never shed responsibility or authority to the point where he could not have restrained his successor, should he choose to do so.
  3. I do not find a great deal of help in trying to determine the answer by reference to the positions of others. There are many in the community who bring an exceptionally high level of skill to the discharge of most serious responsibilities; persons who are never likely to enjoy rewards such as those proposed as having been reasonable rewards for Alex, or acknowledged to be such. On the other hand, the evidence presented before me by experts in the field confirms that there are many persons who basically are no more than gifted salesmen, persons who by reason of that particular talent coupled with the characteristics of the field in which they are marketing, would have earned even more than Alex in the period with which we are concerned. Furthermore, they would have done so by reason of their efforts of only that period. Some guidance is to be had by considering the statistical information available as to salaries in the market place for "Managing Directors" (as described) of companies. But even that is of limited value, because in my view there is a very substantial difference between the management of a stable organisation as administered by a chief executive with the assistance of a strong team of able and experienced subordinates consistently generating moderate profits; and the situation faced by Alex. The competitors of the Group which had been managed by persons remunerated by such standards are probably no longer in business. I am satisfied that for Alex to be average was to fail.
  4. There are many ways the problem can be approached. THE ADVERTISING CLAIMS

  1. The Group advertised extensively and at very considerable expense. Over many years it had utilised the services of a particular advertising agent through whatever firm he was associated with. The agent found the Group to be such a major and important client that, contrary to usual practice in his office and throughout the industry, his fee for services was 5% of cost rather than the usual 7.5% of cost. Over many years the practice had been for the agent to advise; for the companies to authorise; for the agent to negotiate; for the services of the advertiser to be provided; and for the claims of the advertiser to be met by the Group after the service had been rendered. As was to be expected the companies maintained their accounts on an accruals basis and in that way accounts received for services rendered but which were unpaid were brought to account as sundry creditors. In June 1985 the agent had control of a program of newspaper and magazine advertising for Boy-Co and magazine advertising for Girl-Co. INTENTION TO CREATE LEGAL RELATIONS

  1. Having heard the evidence of Alex and of the agent as to the circumstances surrounding the execution of the agreement and the intentions and expectations of the parties, I am not persuaded that it was intended that the execution of the agreement would bring into existence any legally enforceable contractual relationship over and above the existing relationships under the parol agreements already in force between the parties. In short, I am not persuaded that any of the documents constituted a legally enforceable contract. That is so because one of the essential ingredients necessary to bring into existence a contractual relationship was missing: there was no intention to create legal relations by the execution of the documents.

THE SHAM ARGUMENT

  1. The submission for the Commissioner that the agreement was a sham can be dealt with shortly. Isaacs J. said long ago in Jaques v F.C.T. ((1924) [1923] HCA 70; 34 C.L.R. 328 at 358):

"A sham transaction is inherently worthless and needs to enactment to nullify it".

  1. The agreements here in question were inherently worthless in that they did not give rise to any contractual relationship such as they purported to effect. Each was a sham in the sense that it was not in substance what its form represented it to be. The finding adds nothing to the finding already made as to the absence of an intention to create a legally enforceable relationship.

WHEN "INCURRED"?

  1. Determination of this question requires that one should put aside the findings which have been made as to the lack of intention to create legal relationships and accept that the documents were effective to bring into existence legally enforceable contracts whereby the Applicants were obliged to pay the moneys stipulated forthwith. On that basis I accept that had the moneys in question been paid in accordance with the stipulation, whether voluntarily on the initiative of the Applicant or at the instance of the payees, the outgoings would have been "incurred" (Raymor (N.S.W.) Pty. Limited v F.C.T. 89 A.T.C 5173). But that is not the question. The question is whether, without payment, losses or outgoings were so "incurred" as to constitute them as losses proper to be brought to account in the determination of the taxable income of the year in which the obligation was so assumed. As to that I find upon the evidence that there was a mutually held expectation that payment would not be offered by the Applicants or called for by the payees immediately, and that payments would be called for and effected only as and when the relevant services were provided.
  2. In the face of those findings it is not difficult to cite expressions in decided cases supportive of the contentions of both parties. Given the findings on which the determination of this issue is based, there is a "presently existing liability", (Nilsen Development Laboratories Pty. Limited and Ors v F.C.T. [1981] HCA 6; (1981) 144 C.L.R. 616 @) but, although "it is not necessary that these should have been any actual disbursement" (Nilsen ante), the expenditure in the circumstances was "no more than impending, threatened or expected" (New Zealand Flax Investments Limited v F.C.T. [1938] HCA 60; (1931) 61 C.L.R. 179 @ 207). The outlay might never eventuate but that is not of the essence, because "all, or almost all, unpaid liabilities are in a sense defeasible, because they could in the future be forgiven by the creditor, or cancelled by Act of Parliament or barred by any applicable statute of limitations (Commonwealth Aluminium Corp. Limited v F.C.T. 77 A.T.C. 4151 @ 4161).
  3. In seeking a solution to the problem, I have sought guidance in the reasons for decision of Dixon J. (as he then was) in Commissioner of Taxes (S.A.) v Executor Trustee and Agency Co. of South Australia Limited (Carden's case [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 C.L.R. 108 @ 143 and following). The issue in that case was whether a medical practitioner who had practiced alone on his own account should have had his income for taxation purposes determined by the "cash receipts" or "accruals" method. What his Honour there had to say about the subject of properly accounting for revenue is also appropriate to matters of losses and outgoings.
  4. His Honour said (at page 154):

"In the present case we are concerned with rival methods of accounting directed to the same purpose, namely, the purpose of ascertaining the true income. Unless in the statute itself some definite direction is discoverable, I think that the admissibility of the method which in fact has been pursued must depend upon its actual appropriateness. In other words, the enquiry should be whether in the circumstances of the case it is calculated to give a substantially correct reflex of the taxpayer's true income ... Speaking generally, in the assessment of income the object is to discover what gains have during the period of account come home to the taxpayer in a realised or immediately realisable form. Clearly enough, the objective is to assess the taxable income of a given period: speaking generally, in the assessment of income, the object is to discover what gains have during the period of account come home to the taxpayer in a realised or immediately realisable form."

  1. For trading concerns such as the concerns of the Applicant, what has been sold but has not yet been paid for by-the purchaser needs to be brought to account as income. On the other hand, what has been availed of in goods or services during the income period, but not yet paid for, needs to be brought to account as a loss or outgoing in measuring the profit for that period. Further, as is illustrated by the decision in Raymor, what has been paid for in the ordinary course of business is appropriate to be taken into account in assessing the profits of the year of account, even though the goods or services will not be provided until the following year of income. But in my view, to allow that moneys not yet paid, and not intended to be paid within the year, should be brought to account in assessing the profits of the year when the goods or services to which the expenditure will relate will belong to a later year of income is not to achieve the principal objective; to obtain "a substantially correct reflex of the taxpayer's true income" (ibid). In my view the losses or outgoings were not such as to have been "incurred" during the year of income in which the contractual obligation to make the payments was assumed.

Part IVA

  1. In addressing the questions which have been argued as to the application of Part IVA of the Act, I do so on the basis that, contrary to the findings I have made, the liabilities in question were "incurred" and that the amounts claimed would have been deductible unless the provisions of Part IVA of the Act apply. Since a consideration of the application of Part IVA of the Act will prove to be unnecessary unless it happens that I am in error on both of the previous points, I shall only deal briefly with the subject.
  2. Given the premises, the essence of the submission for the Commissioner would seem to be that a taxpayer may forfeit a tax deduction to which he is entitled in the absence of Part IVA of the Act, if the "tax benefit" flowing from the deduction is of greater advantage to the taxpayer than the "tax benefit" which would be available to him if he qualified for the deduction only in the following year. Put that way, because entitlement to the deduction in the following year is not disputed in the present case, the only "tax benefit" to be considered must be in the circumstance that the deduction is claimed in the earlier year; and, but for Part IVA of the Act, would be allowable in the earlier year. That being so, the issue is not so much about the incurring of a liability of a loss or outgoing giving rise to an income tax deduction, so much as it is about the timing of the incurring of that liability. That is an issue which can commonly arise in the most ordinary of circumstances: as when a donor makes his tax deductible donation to a charity in June rather than July; or an employer pays a bonus in June rather than July; or a person in business replenishes his consumable stocks in June rather than July.
  3. The essence of Part IVA of the Act is that when a "scheme" (as defined) generates "a tax benefit" (as defined), the Part applies if, upon consideration of the relevant evidence "it would be concluded that ... (the scheme was) for the purpose of enabling the relevant taxpayer to obtain a tax benefit". Assessable income as determined by the Commissioner is attributed to the taxpayer; and deductions as determined by the Commissioner, but otherwise allowable, are denied him. Appropriate consequential adjustments may be made to the assessments of others. But there does not appear to be any provision authorising, or requiring, consequential adjustments to the other assessments of the taxpayer - a matter potentially of great significance as these proceedings indicate. Section.177C provides:

"(1) Subject to this Section, a reference in this part to the obtaining by a taxpayer of a tax benefit in connection with the scheme shall be read as a reference to -

  1. an amount not being included in the assessable income of the taxpayer of the year of income, when that amount would have been included, or might reasonably be expected to have been included, in the assessable income of the taxpayer of that year of income if the scheme had not been entered into or carried out; or
  2. a deduction being allowable to the taxpayer in relation to a year of income where the whole or a part of that deduction would not have been allowable, or might reasonably be expected not to have been allowable, to the taxpayer in relation to that year of income if the scheme had not been entered into or carried out,

and, for the purposes of this Part, the amount of the tax benefit shall be taken to be -

  1. in a case to which paragraph (a) applies - the amount referred to at that paragraph; and
  1. in a case to which paragraph (b) applies - the amount of the whole of the deduction or of the part of the deduction as the case may be, referred to in that paragraph."
  1. "Scheme" is most broadly defined. Unless the contrary intention appears, it means:
    1. any agreement, arrangement, understanding, promise or undertaking, whether express or implied and whether or not enforceable, or intended to be enforceable by legal proceedings; and
    2. any scheme, plan, proposal, action, course of action or course of conduct (Section 177A (1)).

It may be unilateral (Section 177A(3)) and includes schemes carried out "by a person together with another person or other persons" (Section 177A(4)).

A reference to a scheme

"being entered into or carried out by a person for a particular purpose shall be read as including a reference to the scheme or the part of the scheme being entered into or carried out by the person for two or more purposes of which that particular purpose is the dominant purpose". (S.177A(5)).

  1. The first observation to be made is that there is no need for the application of Part IVA of the Act unless the claim of the taxpayer is soundly based in the other provisions of the Act. It follows then that the role of Part IVA of the Act may be to ensure that the other provisions of the Act are not denied their intended effect by reason of subversions of form (as, for example, where by a chain of transactions, income comes to the hands of the taxpayer in the guise of capital (cf. Jaques [1923] HCA 70; (1924) 34 C.L.R. 328; Clarke [1932] HCA 46; (1932) 48 C.L.R. 56; Bell [1952] HCA 34; (1953) 87 C.L.R. 548; Newton [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1958) 98 C.L.R. 1; Hancock [1961] HCA 90; (1961) 108 C.L.R. 258; Mayfield (No. 1) [1961] HCA 57; (1961) 108 C.L.R. 303; Mayfield No. 2) [1961] HCA 58; (1961) 108 C.L.R. 323; and Ellers Motor Sales Pty. Ltd. 72 A.T.C. 4033); or that it may have a broader operation which enables the Commissioner to refuse to assess in accordance with the principles proclaimed by the Parliament in other provisions of the Act and, instead, to substitute his own perceptions of what ought to have been taxable income in substitution for the taxable income determined by the application of the other provisions of the Act.
  2. Secondly, it is to be noted that there are many decisions by taxpayers which lead to a reduction in taxable income. Some have already been noted. Others are decisions such as a decision to sell a business: a decision which commonly results in the taxpayer not deriving assessable income that would have been his but for the sale. Others are decisions such as to purchase new equipment: purchases which commonly result in claims to deductions which would not have been allowable had it not been for the purchase. Furthermore, the timing of decisions to purchase equipment qualifying the purchaser for tax benefits, such as investment allowance, will commonly be made for the purpose, and often the dominant purpose, of taking advantage of the tax benefits offered by the Act for those who undertake the risk of such investments. In making such decisions, tax considerations are commonly in the mind of the taxpayer. It would be a strange interpretation of Part IVA of the Act to hold that, whenever such "benefits" were availed of, or even sought, as provided for by the principal provisions of the Act, Part IVA of the Act should operate to deny taxpayers those benefits.
  3. Thirdly, there arises a question as to whether evidence that the taxpayer took advice before acting is relevant to the questions to whether there was a "scheme". In the present case the issue arose as to whether the Applicants had taken the advice of Counsel or solicitors before acting. It was not suggested that that evidentiary issue could canvass questions as to the advice given or the communications made in the course of seeking that advice. However, in my view the question so raised goes beyond a question of legal professional privilege. That is because the same question would arise as to whether, in determining whether or not there was any scheme, it was relevant whether to consider the Applicant had consulted with others than barristers and solicitors: persons such as accountants experienced in advising in the tax field; officers of the Commissioner who are held out as willing to advise the community; and other persons claiming expertise in tax matters, such persons ranging from merchant bankers to equipment salesmen. Furthermore, such evidence could extend to questions as to whether the taxpayer had applied his own mind to the availability of the tax consequences claimed. In my view such matters are not relevant and that the appraisal of the "scheme" is to be made on an objective basis "by looking at the overt acts (as defined by Section 177D(b) of the Act) by which it was implemented" (cf. Newton v F.C.T. [1958] UKPCHCA 1; (1958) 98 C.L.R. 1 per Lord Denning @ 8-9).
  4. Against that background I proceed to a consideration of Section 177D of the Act which defines the scope of the Part. Section 177D of the Act provides:

"This Part applies to any scheme that has been or is entered into after 27 May 1981, and to any scheme that has been or is carried out or commenced to be carried out after that date (other than a scheme that was entered into on or before that date), whether the scheme, has been or is entered into or carried out in Australia or outside Australia or partly in Australia and partly outside Australia, where -

(a) a taxpayer (in this section referred to as the "relevant taxpayer") has obtained, or would but for section 177F obtain, a tax benefit in connection with the scheme; and

(b) having regard to -

(i) the manner in which the scheme was entered into or carried out;

(ii) the form and substance of the scheme;

(iii) the time at which the scheme was entered into and the length of the period during which the scheme was carried out;

(iv) the result in relation to the operation of this Act that, but for this Part, would be achieved by the scheme;

(v) any change in the financial position of the relevant taxpayer that has resulted, will result, or may reasonably be expected to result, from the scheme;

(vi) any change in the financial position of any person who has, or has had, any connection (whether of a business, family or other nature) with the relevant taxpayer, being a change that has resulted, will result or may reasonably be expected to result, from the scheme;

(vii) any other consequence for the relevant taxpayer, or for any person referred to in sub-paragraph (vi), of the scheme having been entered into or carried out; and

(viii) the nature of any connection (whether of a business, family or other nature) between the relevant taxpayer and any person referred to in sub-paragraph (vi),

it would be concluded that the person, or one of the persons, who entered into or carried out the scheme or any part of the scheme did so for the purpose of enabling the relevant taxpayer to obtain a tax benefit in connection with the scheme or of enabling the relevant taxpayer and another taxpayer or other taxpayers each to obtain a tax benefit in connection with the scheme (whether or not that person who entered into or carried out the scheme or any part of the scheme is the relevant taxpayer or is the other taxpayer or one of the other taxpayers)

  1. It is significant, and consistent with the views I have already expressed, that the Act does not make provision for a consideration of the state of mind of individuals except in so far as "purpose" can be discerned from their actions and the circumstances attending them.
  2. Bearing in mind the premise underlying a consideration of the application of Part IVA of the Act, namely, that the liabilities in question were incurred in the relevant years of income and the amounts claimed as deductions would have been allowable as deductions in those years of income unless the claims are to be denied by the provisions of Part IVA of the Act, I proceed to findings as to the application of Section 177D of the Act. I am satisfied that in each instance a "purpose" in entering into the agreements was to enter into binding commitments with the provider of the relevant services for the-provision of the services contracted for and, in consideration of that, for the Applicants to assume an immediate liability to pay the amounts contracted for. I am satisfied that by entering into the agreements when it did, as it did and in the terms adopted, in each instance the Applicant had as a purpose the objective of satisfying the requirements of the provisions of the Act other than Part IVA which would give rise to an entitlement under those other provisions of the Act to an income tax deduction. Bearing in mind the premise of the exercise, I find that, by reference to the other provisions of the Act, the Applicants were entitled to the deductions claimed unless their claims are to be denied by force of Part IVA of the Act. I find that in the very broad sense provided for by Section 177A of the Act, there was a "scheme" and that it was (inter-alia) "for the purpose of enabling the relevant taxpayer to obtain a tax benefit", but that it did not seek to obtain for the taxpayer any tax benefits to which it was not entitled by reason of the other provisions of the Act. I find that the dominant purpose in entering into the scheme as and when and how it did was the obtaining of that tax benefit in the sense that, but for the prospect of the tax benefit, the "scheme" would not have been entered into. I also find that all of the foregoing findings are findings proper to be made having regard only to the "overt acts" by which the scheme was implemented. I set aside as irrelevant the acknowledgments of Alex that the purpose in entering into the agreements was for the Applicants to become eligible for tax deductions considered to be available upon the application of other provisions of the Act. Taking the view I have, the substantially significant matters are the major change in the relevant commercial practices whereby the Applicant made forward commitments such as it had not previously made; and, secondly, the circumstance that it proceeded to actually defray such liabilities as it had assumed in accordance with that former practice and not in accordance with the obligations assumed by the documented contract. In order to complete the analysis., I express the view that,- had payment been made in accordance with the agreements upon the execution of them, there would probably have been no scope for the application of Part IVA of the Act, In my view the need for much of the old learning as to Section 260 of the Act will remain. For those reasons I conclude that, even if the claims in question had proved to be allowable pursuant to the provisions of Section 51(1) of the Act, the Commissioner was entitled to cancel the tax benefits pursuant to provisions Section 177F(1) of the Act.

THE RAW MATERIALS CLAIM

  1. In order to carry on production, Make-Co was dependent upon a sufficient supply of suitable raw materials. Procuring them was an ongoing problem for the company. The task was made more difficult by the circumstance that in large part raw materials came from overseas sources. Its difficulties were compounded by the need to have sufficient lead time to meet market demands, but at the same time, to keep commitments sufficiently limited not to overstock and yet have supplies in sufficient volume to be able to meet the demands of the market as they became known. For the most part, Make-Co dealt with Australian agents and in large measure it was able to buy the materials required from stocks held locally. During the years of income ended 30 June 1986 and 1987 Make-Co paid $751,282 and $718,931 to a local agent. Payment was effected on receipt of accounts following delivery of the materials. The Commissioner does not contest the right of Make-Co to have income tax deductions in those amounts in those years. However, in each case, Make-Co claims an income tax deduction in the year of income preceding the year of payment. It rests its claim to do so upon an assertion that Make-Co had "incurred" liability for the payment upon the making of orders during the month of June in the year in which it seeks to sustain the claim.
  2. The evidence that was presented as to the arrangement was in the form of letters from the agent dated 25th June 1985 and 25 June 1986. It was conceded by the Commissioner that, despite the absence of evidence from the agent and from Make-Co as to the making of any agreement, the arrangements should be considered to have been as effective as the agreements entered into under seal in relation to advertising. The letters were in near identical terms. As addressed by the agent to Make-Co, they read as follows:

THE POWER TO AMEND

  1. Due to differing factual circumstances attending the issues as to the power of the Commissioner to amend the original assessments against the Applicants, and particularly having regard to amendments to the Act effecting changes to the power of the Commissioner to issue amended assessments, it is necessary to consider the issues in relation to each company and in relation to each year of assessment.
  2. Section 170(1) of the Act expresses the power of the Commissioner to amend assessments in very general terms by providing:

"The Commissioner may, subject to this Section, at any time amend any assessment by making such alterations therein or additions thereto as he thinks necessary, notwithstanding that tax may have been paid in respect to the assessment".

  1. But the succeeding provisions of the Section provide substantial limits upon the exercise of that power. There are two aspects to those limitations which are presently relevant. The first is as to time. The most restrictive provision as to time allowed the Commissioner only three years from the date upon which the tax became due and payable under the original assessment in which to amend (Section 170(3)). Since all of the amended assessments now under review issued on 28 September 1987, a date within three years of the date upon which tax became due and payable under the original assessments being amended - the earliest original assessment having been 26 April 1985 - it follows that there was no restriction as to time on the power to amend.
  2. The second limitation relates to the scope of the power to amend. The relevant provisions were amended by Act No. 46 of 1986 with effect in relation to the amendment of assessments which issued as original assessments, or as amendments of those original assessments, on or after the 1st July 1986. As a result, the amended assessments under review fall into two categories: those relating to the years of income ended 30 June 1984 and 1985; and those relating to the year of income ended 30 June 1986.
  3. As to the former. Section 170(3) provided that:

" . . . . no amendment of the assessment increasing the liability of the taxpayer in any particular shall be made except to correct an error in calculation or a mistake of fact;

That concept was expanded by Section 170(8) of the Act which as it provided:

"Where

(a) any provision of this Act is expressly made to depend in any particular upon a determination, opinion or judgment of the Commissioner; and

(b) any assessment is affected by in any particular by that determination, opinion or judgment,

then if, after the making of the assessment it appears to the Commissioner that the determination, opinion or judgment was erroneous, he may correct it and amend the assessment accordingly in the same circumstances as he could under this Section amend an assessment by reason of a mistake of fact."

  1. Applying those provisions I make the following findings:

(a) MAKE-CO: YEAR ENDED 30 JUNE 1984

The amended assessment only issued to give effect to a determination of the Commissioner made pursuant to the powers conferred by Section 109 of the Act, whereby he disallowed in part the claim of Make-Co to have a deduction for the entirety of the bonus paid. As the amendment sought to correct a determination or opinion of the Commissioner, the issue of the amended assessment was within power.

(b) MAKE-CO: YEAR ENDED 30 JUNE 1985

The amendments embraced a diversity of issues. As to some (foreign exchange; investment allowance; depreciation; stock write-down) the right to amend was not disputed. The only other issue was as to the right to amend to disallow the disputed claim in relation to raw materials. I am satisfied that even if there could be said to have been "true disclosure", there had not been "a full ... disclosure". Further, the Commissioner was acting to correct a mistake of fact which he had made as to that claim. In consequence the amendment was within power.

(c) BOY-CO: YEAR ENDED 30 JUNE 1985

The amendment disallowed two claims: legal expenses ($6,586) and advertising ($712,436). The right to amend in relation to the former item was not disputed. As to the latter I am satisfied that, as with the raw materials claim for Make-Co, there had not been a full and true disclosure and the Commissioner was acting to correct a mistake of fact in relation to the advertising claim. I am satisfied that the amendment was within power.

(d) GIRL-CO: YEAR ENDED 30 JUNE 1985

The amendment sought to disallow amounts previously allowed in relation to advertising ($113,054) and bonus ($391,466). For reasons expressed in relation to Make-Co (1985) as to the former item and in relation to Girl-Co as to the latter I am satisfied that the amendments were within power.

(e) GROUP: YEAR ENDED 30 JUNE 1986

The original assessments in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1986 in each instance issued after 30 June 1986. With effect from that date Section 170(3) was amended to read as follows:

"Where a taxpayer has made to the Commissioner a full and true disclosure of all material facts necessary for his assessment, and an assessment is made after that disclosure, no amendment of the assessment increasing the liability of the taxpayer at any particular shall be made after the expiration of three years from the date upon which the tax became due and payable under that assessment".

In short, the restrictions previously limiting the Commissioner to action to correct errors in calculation or mistakes of fact (including the correction of erroneous determinations, opinions, or judgments) were removed. It follows that the power of the Commissioner to amend was not reduced. That being so, I am satisfied that, because the amendments only sought to correct mistakes of fact in relation to raw materials (Make-Co, year ended 30 June 1986) and advertising (Boy-Co, year ended 30 June 1986), and to rectify an erroneous determination as to bonus, (Girl-Co, year ended 30 June 1986), the amendments were all within power.

  1. By reason of the amendments being effected within the three year period I consider it unncessary to rule on an argument as to whether want of full-time disclosure upon one matter empowers the Commissioner to amend as to other matters as to which three as been full-time disclosure. I merely note that the view acted on by his Honour, Windeyer J. in W. Thomas & Co. Ltd. v F.C.T. ((1965) [1965] HCA 54; 115 C.L.R. 58) stands unchallenged.

ADDITIONAL TAX FOR INCORRECT RETURNS

  1. When the Commissioner issued each of the assessments now under review, he levied additional tax for incorrect returns. In each instance, he identified precisely the amount so levied and he also identified the amount so levied as "additional tax for incorrect return". The Act provided that, if there was a liability to additional tax for incorrect return, the liability was to be 200% of "tax avoided" (S.223(1), unless remitted in whole or in part by the Commissioner (S.227(3)). Any person who was aware that the Act made such provision for remission would have suspected from the figures assessed that the Commissioner had allowed some remissions. But the Commissioner provided no information to the taxpayers which would have enabled them to determine how the figures levied had been calculated. In fact the Commissioner's officers had set out to fix upon penalties in accordance with the then normal practices of the Commissioner. That involved determinations as to which of the elements of tax avoided were penalisable; what was the amount of tax avoided; the period of avoidance; the measuring of the period in respect of which compensatory interest was to be levied at the rate of 20% per annum; and fixing upon one or more percentage rates which expressed an appropriate "culpability factor". At the time the "culpability factor" was commonly fixed upon at 40%, regardless of the gravity, or lack of gravity, of the circumstances giving rise to the liability for penalty. Those elements having been determined, it was then necessary that those concepts be applied with mathematical accuracy in order to correctly levy what was considered to be an appropriate amount of additional tax.
  2. By withholding from them information as to what had been done, the taxpayers were denied an opportunity to even check the mathematical accuracy of what had been done. That was unfortunate because, as it happened, the mathematical exercise had not been carried out correctly in all cases. It was discovered on the final day of hearing that there had been a significant error in the mathematical computation of the penalties.
  3. What the Commissioner had determined was that additional tax was leviable in relation to all of the items adjusted by the amended assessments; that the periods of evasion should be as measured with calculations ranging up to 407 days; and that the "culpability factor" should in all instances be 40%.

ADDITIONAL TAX: BONUSES

  1. For reasons which were unexplained no "additional tax" was levied against Make-Co in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1984. Yet additional tax was levied against Girl-Co in relation to the same issue for the years of income ended 30 June 1985 and 1986. The right to impose any additional tax was clearly conditioned upon a "false or misleading statement" having been made by the Applicant within the meaning of Section 223 of the Act. It was contended for the Applicants that there had been no "false or misleading statement", whether by act or omission, in relation to the matter of "bonuses", at least on the part of Girl-Co, because it had disclosed the fact of the payments to Alex. It was further argued that, since it was acknowledged that there was an entitlement to the deduction under Section 51(1) of the Act and that the deduction would have been allowable but for a determination of the Commissioner made under Section 109 of the Act, there could not have been any "false or misleading statement" by the Applicant. It had disclosed all matters correctly which were relevant to its claim to such a deduction under Section 51(1) of the Act as it had claimed. The argument was that the circumstance that the Applicants had been held not entitled to such a deduction depended wholly upon exercises of discretion by the Commissioner. Thus, it was argued, there could have been no "false or misleading statement" in any relevant sense. It could not have been a duty of any Applicant to advise the Commissioner of a decision which was wholly a decision of the Commissioner and which had yet to be made.
  2. In the event it is unnecessary to decide the point. Counsel for the Commissioner conceded that all penalties by way of additional tax in relation to "bonuses" should be remitted.

ADDITIONAL TAX: ADVERTISING AND RAW MATERIALS CONTRACTS

  1. Calculation of the maximum amount of liability by way of additional tax for incorrect return is essentially simple. It involves two factors: determination of the amount of tax avoided; and the doubling of the figure so determined. However, the calculation of additional tax to be levied after remissions according to the practice of the Commissioner is more complicated. That exercise involves several factors: determination of tax avoided; measurement of the period of avoidance; the application of a multiplier at the rate of 20% per annum; and the fixing of a culpability factor as a percentage of tax avoided. In his closing address. Counsel for the Commissioner advised that, having regard to Income Tax Ruling 2517, the contention to be advanced for the Commissioner was that the appropriate rate of "culpability factor" on the pre-commitment issues should vary according to the basis to be determined by the Tribunal as the appropriate basis for the disallowance of the claimed deductions. So it was argued that the "culpability factor" should be as follows:

If Section 51 applied 30%

If "sham" applied 35%

If Part IVA applied 40%

In so far as that approach reflects regard to the gravity of the conduct being penalised, I approve the approach without wishing to be thought to be adopting the weightings expressed in the table.

THE LOSS TO THE REVENUE

  1. But in my view the basic approach remains seriously-deficient in so far as it fails to have regard to the circumstance that there was no attempt on the part of these taxpayers in relation to these matters to avoid tax upon their taxable incomes. What they sought to do was gain an advantage to which they were not entitled, but it was to be a temporary advantage only. They were not seeking deductions for losses that they had not and would not incur. They were seeking to bring expenses of the business to account in the year of income preceding the year of income in which they were entitled to bring those expenses into account. For all that, the conduct is deserving of censure. However, the "misconduct" is far less serious than an attempt to forever avoid payment of tax. The significance will be clear to all who have lent money and known the difference between being repaid late and not being repaid at all.
  2. But as to the pre-commitment claims, there are also other factors of great importance which appear not to have been taken into account at all by the Commissioner. The first consideration is that the advantage gained by the pre-commitment was not permanent. It was to only defer payment of tax from the due date for payment under the original assessment until the due date for payment under the original assessment for the next succeeding year of income. For reasons of convenience in exposition only I shall assume that period of time to be one year: no more and no less. That being so, the loss to the Revenue in the first year is a loss of use of the "tax avoided" for one year. That loss can been appropriately measured as 20% of tax avoided (20% per annum for one year) - using the late payment rate provided for by Section 207 of the Act - or 14.026% per annum (14.026% per annum for one year) - the rate provided for as the rate of interest to be applied in favour of taxpayers entitled to refunds as provided for by the Taxation (Interest on Overpayments) Act 1983 and used by the Commissioner in calculating compensatory interest pursuant to Income Tax Ruling 2517.

CULPABILITY FACTOR - OTHER ITEMS

  1. No evidence or argument was placed to me on the question of the "culpability factor" in relation to the other items which touched upon the assessment of both Make-Co and Boy-Co in the year of income ended 30 June 1985. COMPENSATORY RATE - ALL YEARS

  1. On the evidence now before me I am satisfied that the period in relation to which a compensatory rate was calculated was one favourable to the taxpayers in that in each case the period measured was far less than it might have been. Therefore the taxpayer was favoured by the period of time which elapsed to assessment.
  2. As to the rate applied, I have already observed (cf. Case U36 87 A.T.C. 266) that, had the taxpayers been correctly assessed at the time of original assessment but been able to defer the payment of the relevant tax assessed until the due date for payment under the amended assessments, the rate of interest which would have applied against the taxpayer under the statute would have been 20% per annum (S.207). That rate in nearly all circumstances is itself a hard, and even harsh, rate to apply. But it is the rate chosen by the Parliament and there is a duty to apply it in the circumstances where it applies. For those reasons were the matter only to be considered on that basis, I would have upheld a compensatory rate of 20% per annum.
  3. But since the Commissioner has issued his Ruling 2517, he has declared a practice throughout the nation that in assessing additional tax for incorrect return the compensatory factor is to be calculated at 14.026% per annum; and since that time, in each case which has come to my knowledge, he has by Counsel and by his officers proposed or agreed that the Tribunal, in reviewing additional tax for incorrect returns, should substitute the rate of 14.026% per annum for the rate of 20% per annum whenever it was previously imposed. That being so, the Commissioner does not in these cases oppose the reduction of additional tax by a sufficient sum to reduce the compensatory rate from 20% per annum to 14.026% per annum. In my view it would be unjust to deny the Applicants the benefit of that concession, even though it is a concession which in my view is a liberal one.

  1. As has been indicated earlier, the position in relation to the assessments for the year ended 30 June 1986 is quite different. What lies at the heart of that difference is that in assessing as he did for the year of income 30 June 1986, the Commissioner failed to give effect to the view he had formed that amounts which he disallowed in the preceding year were to be disallowed because they were only allowable, and should have been allowed, in the year of income ended 30 June 1986. Had the amounts disallowed in the preceding year been allowed, and at the same time the amounts claimed for the 1986 year been disallowed, there would have been no loss to the Revenue.
  2. That being so the first question to consider is whether there can be any liability to additional tax. That must be determined by reference to Section 223(1) of the Act which provides:

"Where

(a) a taxpayer

(i) makes a statement to a taxation officer ... that is false or misleading in a material particular; or

(ii) omits from a statement made to a taxation officer any matter or thing without which the statement is misleading in a material particular; and

(b) the tax properly payable by the taxpayer exceeds the tax that would have been payable by the taxpayer if it were assessed on the basis that the statements were not false or misleading, as the case may be,

the taxpayer is liable to pay, by way of penalty, additional tax equal to double the amount of the excess."

(I shall continue to refer to that excess as "the tax avoided").

  1. As I have indicated in an earlier decision (Case V81 88 A.T.C. 557 at page 571):

"... regard must be had to each statement as a separate statement, in order to determine "the tax that would have been payable by the taxpayer if (the tax) were assessed on the basis that the statement were not false or misleading ...." (emphasis added).

On that basis, liability to additional tax is to be determined without having regard to the circumstance that claims to other deductions properly allowable were not made by the taxpayer or granted by the Commissioner; or to the circumstance that amounts disallowed in one year were allowable in another.

  1. But that is not to say that such considerations are not relevant to an exercise of the discretion to effect remissions. The fact is that, had the Commissioner given full effect to the views he had formed when issuing the assessments for the years of income ended 30 June 1985 and 1986, the assessments of Make-Co and Boy-Co would have fixed upon taxable incomes for the latter year less than those previously assessed. So viewed there never was any loss to the Revenue in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1986.
  2. For that reason it is not appropriate that there should be any additional tax by way of a compensatory factor to the Revenue. However, there remains a question of a "culpability factor" to be considered. In my view there was power to levy additional tax; and a duty to consider the remission in whole or in part of the additional tax so provided for. But when those statements are placed in context, in my view the remissions should be most substantial. In exercising that discretion, I do not find it helpful to consider any calculations placed upon a concept of "tax avoided" as determined by a comparison of the original assessment and the amended assessment. In my view justice will be done if additional tax is reduced for the year of income ended 30 June 1986 from $132,283 to $6,600 for Make-Co and from $124,936 to $6,200 for Boy-Co.
