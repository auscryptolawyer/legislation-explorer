REASONS FOR DECISION



10 March 1995 Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)



  1. This is an application to review a decision of a delegate of the respondent under s 16 of what was formerly known as the Occupational Superannuation Standards Act 1987. The decision was to affirm a primary decision that the applicant fund did not satisfy the superannuation fund conditions during the 5 financial years 1988 to 1992 inclusive, and that special circumstances did not exist within the meaning of s 13(1)(b) of that Act.
  2. The fund was established on 30 June 1986. Prior to that date, Mr G had retired after being employed as a mechanical fitter for 20 years by a leading public construction company. On his retirement, he received a lump sum. The fund and an associated family trust were set up on the advice of the applicant’s first accountant. The lump sum constituted the initial capital of the fund and was invested in a cash management trust.
  3. It was not clear to me whether the first accountant also established a set of account books. If he did, they do not appear to have been written up regularly at any time thereafter. At all relevant times the members of the Fund were, and are, Mr & Mrs G. Mr G gave evidence on the hearing before me. Although of Italian birth, his English was fluent and I have no doubt that he understood the general nature of the arrangements that had been set up for him. The suggestion made from time to time that as English was not his first language, he was confused about the nature of events and that this should be taken into account as a special circumstance was not established, even if such a condition could be so characterised.
  4. He told me, however, that he did not keep any books of account except an ordinary “school book”. He would write down entries in these books from time to time dealing with moneys taken out of the superannuation fund and moneys invested elsewhere. He would have discussions with his wife from time to time as to where they thought the best rate of interest could be achieved. No minutes were ever kept of any of these discussions, even if they could be regarded as meetings. They were the only directors of the trustee company. Mr G said that at the end of each year he would throw out each school book as it was filled up. He did not know the names of any books of account and denied that the fund ever had a journal or that he understood what a journal or minute book was. He agreed that at all relevant times he saw no difference between leaving money in the fund and taking the money out and investing it in his name, or in the name of himself and his wife, as those 2 persons were the only members of the fund. He agreed that he had no understanding of the need for separating fund assets and moneys from his own private affairs.
  5. He said that no taxation return or superannuation return was filed during the 5 years in question. In the early years, he said this was on the advice of the first accountant because there were losses. These appear to have been in the family trust rather than in the fund. It is not clear to me when he ceased to consult that accountant. He did not seek advice from his second accountant until 1993.
  6. In that year, the second accountant prepared annual accounts for the 5 years in question, prepared income tax returns for the fund and arranged for them to be lodged and prepared superannuation returns for presentation to the respondent. In accordance with the requirements of the regulations, each superannuation return was accompanied by a certificate from the second accountant in these words —

“We certify that we have audited the accounts and records of [Superannuation Fund] for the year of income ended [relevant date] in accordance with the Australian Auditing Standards. In our opinion the information provided by the trustee in the return of the fund for that year of income is consistent with that contained in those audited accounts and records in respect of which we made an unqualified audit report.”

  1. I can not understand how any qualified auditor could give such a certificate upon the basis of the records as described to me in evidence by Mr G. Nevertheless, such a certificate was given and a corresponding certificate was given by Mr G as managing director of a company that was trustee of the fund. That certificate again affirmed that the particulars contained in the return were correct and, with the exception mentioned in the return, the fund satisfied the superannuation fund conditions applicable to the fund in relation to the relevant year of income.
  2. In each year, the exception mentioned in the return was the disclosure of a loan balance to Mr G coupled with a request for exercise of discretion under s 13 and an undertaking that the breach would be rectified. All 5 returns were apparently lodged at the same time.
  3. In response to specific questions asked by the respondent, the second accountant replied on 16 July 1993 that the borrowing member was a director of the trustee company, that the loan commenced at an amount of $62,886 on 1 August 1987, that additional borrowings were made at irregular intervals, that interest was charged at a rate of 16.5 per cent per annum and that the final loan repayment was made on 1 April 1993. The second accountant went on to confirm that interest was journalised in full on 30 June in each relevant year. He also added that there was no formal loan agreement in existence but that the trustees and sponsoring employers had raised minutes to evidence the loan transaction.
  4. It was apparent from Mr G’s evidence that these were (to use a charitable word) reconstructions. There is no contemporary record that was made available to me indicating an agreement to pay interest. There is no indication that interest was journalised in full on 30 June in each relevant year. Indeed the evidence was that there was no such book of account as a journal. There was no minute put before me to evidence the loan transactions. Mr G’s evidence was that he did not know what a minute book was. Although the second accountant, who represented the applicant at the hearing, said that there were minutes, he did not have them with him and they were not tendered in evidence. I can only assume that they were not tendered because they would not have assisted the applicant’s case.
  5. On the basis of these returns, it was clear that there had been a breach of the investment standards, specifically in relation to the loans to members. In 1988 the loan to Mr G stood at $72,397 while the total assets of the Fund were $83,717. Balance date at each of the subsequent years showed loan balances varying from 63 per cent to 48 per cent of total assets. The final loan repayment of $59,219 was made on 1 April 1993, well after the end of the financial years presently under consideration.
  6. Following further communications from the respondent, the second accountant again wrote on 9 November 1993, setting out the basis for claiming a favourable exercise of discretion and raising for the first time this suggestion —

“Alternatively and without prejudice to the foregoing it could be agreed that the assets of the Fund were invested in arms-length securities at all times on a “tracing” of those funds.”

That suggestion was finally elaborated in a statement of facts and contentions received by the Tribunal, and by the respondent, only one week prior to the hearing.

  1. I am not sure even now that I understand what was intended to be demonstrated by this elaborate document. It purports to trace the use to which some of the loans were put and attempts to identify investments resulting from those advances. Notwithstanding the fact that the second accountant had given audit certificates to annual accounts showing the existence of loans, an argument was attempted in the statement of facts and contentions that this was an incorrect approach and that showing the amounts as loans did not, on an equitable tracing basis, show the true nature of the transactions. The statement then went on to identify a number of specific acquisitions including parcels of shares in companies that failed and other assets, such as a bank bill financed by contributions from several sources including the fund, and a commercial real estate property financed largely through external mortgage borrowings (in itself a breach of the Investment Standards if the asset was to be regarded as a fund asset.).
  2. Although the claim is made that in those cases where investments stood in the names of Mr G and his wife they were simply agents for the fund, there is not a shred of evidence to support such an assertion. There are no deeds of trust, no minutes, no correspondence, no contemporary records at all to indicate that the transactions were other than what they appear to be on the surface. They appear to be a free use by Mr G of the fund’s moneys for his own purposes. The second reconstruction attempted by the second accountant appears to be an attempt to charge the private losses of the family of Mr and Mrs G to the fund.
  3. Suggestions were made that contributions to the fund should not have been shown as contributions, but should have been shown as reductions of the loan accounts. As to that I again would say firstly that I find it impossible to understand how an auditor could give an audit certificate certifying that the accounts reflected a true and fair statement of the financial affairs of the fund, including a description of moneys received as members’ contributions and then later suggest that they could have been treated otherwise and that I should accept that this was a possible way of characterising the moneys. They were either contributions or loan repayments. They can not be both. Signing off the accounts is an assurance to all users of the accounts that the descriptions given in the accounts are the way the payments were intended by the payer and treated by the payee. Secondly, of course, the argument itself supports the fact that the audited accounts were correct in the first place and that the amounts were properly shown as loans.
  4. I find this a strange way of presenting accounting evidence. Accounts should be an accurate reflection of the financial transactions occurring within a fund. The second accountant has taken the audited accounts and then adopted a “what if” approach. In effect, he says that the breaches are not so serious as they appear, if one looks at the economic realities. The first answer to that of course is that audited accounts ought to reflect economic realities. Secondly, there is no evidence at all to support any contention that moneys taken from the fund’s accounts were invested for the benefit of the fund’s members. In 2 cases (the commercial property and the bank bill) investments were made in the name of the company which is the trustee of the fund. As that company is also the trustee of the employer family trust, it is impossible to tell on that ground alone whether the investments were made for the benefit of the fund or for the benefit of the trust.
  5. What the second accountant proposes is that the respondent should simply ignore accounting standards in an endeavour to conjure up special circumstances. This is not an appropriate procedure and I am surprised that it has been proposed.
  6. In any event, even if one assumes the validity of the “what if” approach, a residue of substantial breaches is left. In order to address them it will be necessary to consider whether special circumstances exist. The concept was considered fully in Case Z27 92 ATC 255 the reasoning in which was not disapproved on appeal in Tefonu Pty Ltd v Insurance and Superannuation Commissioner 93 ATC 4727. Case Z27 has been followed notably in Case 17/94 reported at 94 ATC 198 and Case 33/94 reported at 94 ATC 306. Whilst it is true that broad discretions should not “by the gradual deposition of judicial decisions become fossilised into rigidity” (Minister for Community Services and Health and Another v Chee Keong Thoo [1988] FCA 54; (1988) 78 ALR 307 at 324) nevertheless certain principles have emerged to give guidance as to whether there is “something unusual or different to take the matter out of the ordinary course”, to use the words of Burchett J in Chee Keong Thoo.
  7. There were 8 principal headings relied upon by the applicant as grounds for the exercise of the respondent’s discretion in its favour. The whole basis of so-called equitable tracing was put forward as the first of these grounds. In my view that submission is based on a misapprehension. The legal status of ownership of assets and the legal status of moneys advanced to members are matters to be established to determine whether or not a breach has occurred. Viewing them in a way different from their correct legal construction does not assist in determining whether the respondent’s discretion should be exercised.
  8. The second ground was related to the state of mind of Mr G. It was said that both directors had been honestly confused and believed that they were acting as agent for the fund. Since the decision in Case Z27, it is clear that ignorance of the standards is no excuse. In fact the directors consulted the first accountant when the fund was established and continued to consult him for some time afterwards. His advice may not have been the best. His reassurance to the directors that they need not file income tax returns may have been questionable. Nevertheless the directors had the opportunity to seek all relevant advice and should have taken advantage of those opportunities.
  9. The third ground is that in fact Mr G acted as agent for the fund in drawing moneys and investing them in other areas, even when investments were made in his own name or in the name of himself and his wife. This is a mere assertion. There is no evidence to substantiate such an assertion. Indeed I have the impression from Mr G’s evidence that he did not understand the distinction between his private funds and the property of the superannuation fund.
  10. The fourth ground suggested was that at all times the trustee did not put the members’ funds at risk and that it always intended to abide by the purpose and prescriptions of the legislation and regulations. An intention to act in accordance with one’s duty as trustee is not, of course, a special circumstance.
  11. The fifth ground put forward by the trustee was that it had made full disclosure and had since rectified the breach. As to that submission, it is clear that rectification did not take place until after the relevant period. Furthermore the fact that full disclosure is made is no more than would legitimately be expected of any trustee.
  12. The sixth ground related to the age of both members. Mr G is aged about 70 and his wife is aged about 62. They have my sympathy. They do not appear to have been well served in the advice they have received. Nevertheless erosion of the benefits of members is not a special circumstance. It happens every time there is a breach which is not overlooked by the Commissioner. Adverse financial consequences to members is not “something unusual or different to take the matter out of the ordinary course”. Indeed it is the ordinary and expected result of failure to comply with the superannuation conditions.
  13. It was claimed, seventhly, that the size of the breaches were immaterial. I do not share that view. I have already referred to the extent of the loans in relation to the assets of the Fund. Even on the reconstructed accounts, the size of the breaches would have been material.
  14. Finally the applicant submitted that because all outstanding loans and interest had been repaid in April 1993, this in itself would constitute a special circumstance. Section 13 of the Act requires the Commissioner to take into account special circumstances “that existed in relation to the Fund during the year of income”. Even if repayment of the loans is to be regarded as a special circumstance, it can not be regarded as one that existed in that period.
  15. In all the circumstances, I see no reason why the respondent’s decision should be disturbed. It will therefore be affirmed
