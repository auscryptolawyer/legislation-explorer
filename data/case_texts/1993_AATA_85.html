Re Martin John Innes Morgan and Commissioner of Superannuation [1993] AATA 85 (12 March 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: MARTIN JOHN INNES MORGAN

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. W92/200

AAT No. 8584

Number of pages - 9

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

P Gerber(1) (Deputy President), J.G Billings(1) (Member) and R.D Fayle(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Deferment of benefits - Election outside 21 days of retirement - Discretion to recognize election - Failure of employer to advise employee of rights - Whether lack of awareness cause of failure to exercise election to preserve superannuation benefits - Failure to make enquiries - Long delay - Public consideration - Whether prejudice to Fund and/or other employees - Whether "desirable" - Discretion not exercised - Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth), ss 137, 157.

HEARING

PERTH, 10 December 1992

12:3:1993

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

The applicant, now aged 42 years, is presently employed as a technician with Telecom Australia ("Telecom"). He joined Telecom in 1969 and became a contributor under the Superannuation Act 1922 as amended ("the Act").

2. In February 1976, the applicant undertook a period of employment with the Government of Papua New Guinea ("PNG"), taking leave without pay from Telecom. Shortly before his PNG contract was due to come to an end, he saw an opportunity to go into business in that country and thereupon sought further leave without pay with the intention of starting his new business venture. Telecom agreed and extended his leave without pay up to 30 June 1985. It is common ground that the applicant was engaged in business in PNG from the time his contract there came to an end to some time in mid-June 1985. Throughout his stay in PNG, the applicant preserved his superannuation rights.

3. On or about 28 June 1985, i.e. some two days before he was expected to return to Telecom, the applicant decided to terminate his employment and wrote a letter of resignation to his employer, the details of which are set out below. In due course, he was sent a form - "Form S2A" - which he filled out in PNG and returned to Telecom. (The form is dated "17/5/85". However, we are satisfied that this date is incorrect and is more likely to have been 17 July). Mr Morgan asserts that this was the only form sent to him. Form S2A provides for the three options available to Commonwealth employees intending to resign or retire from the service. It is headed:

APPLICATION FOR REFUND OF ACCUMULATED CONTRIBUTIONS

LUMP SUM BENEFIT, OR

ELECTION FOR PRESERVATION OF SUPERANNUATION RIGHTS.

Below the provision for name, address and occupation, the form states:-

*(i) apply for a refund of accumulated )

contributions ) provisions)

under the ) of the Act)

I ... hereby *(ii) elect for a payment of a lump sum )

*(iii) elect for the preservation of superannuation rights

NOTE: An election as in (ii) or (iii) is binding and cannot be revoked unless there are exceptional circumstances.

*Delete whichever inapplicable.

If you are going to be employed by the Commonwealth or a Commonwealth Authority in the near future, please advise name of Department.

4. Pausing here, the reference that "an election as in (ii) or (iii) is binding and cannot be revoked unless there are exceptional circumstances" is, to say the least, ambiguous with respect to option (i). Leaving aside the issue of causation - to which we will refer later - it is fair to say that someone wanting to retire and, weighing up the pros and cons of preserving his/her superannuation rights could well be confused by the wording of the Form.

5. In his evidence, Mr Morgan informed the Tribunal that just prior to his decision to resign, he happened to be in Perth for personal reasons and took advantage of the occasion to discuss his future with a Mr George Keenan, who was the District Manager of Telecom at Geraldton North West. On this occasion, Mr Keenan (who has since retired) offered the applicant a further extension of leave without pay. However, on his return to PNG, having considered and rejected that option, the applicant formalised his resignation by letter addressed to the State Manager of Telecom ("T13/51") as follows:

28th June, 1985

Dear Sir,

I refer to my recent conversation I had with Mr Keenan, North West

Division, regarding an extension of my leave without pay.

I have decided to forego that option and wish to resign from

Telecom as of 30th June 1985.

Moneys owing to me (i.e. Long Service and superannuation) can be

sent to the following address: PO Box 539, GOROKA, PAPUA NEW

GUINEA.

My reason for resigning is that there seems little point in

requesting an extension of leave without pay if I forfeit all

conditions except seniority.

Please accept my thanks and gratitude for the help and

consideration that has been extended to me while seconded to the

Post and Telegraph Corporation of P.N.G. I have enjoyed the work

with Telecom and P.T.C. of Papua New Guinea and found it rewarding

and may, at a later day, seek re-employment in Western Australia.

Yours sincerely

10. 10 In due course, Mr Morgan received the refund due to him under the provisions of the Superannuation Act - some $A10,566.99 in all.

12. Mr Morgan impressed us as a witness of truth who would go to great length to try and explain his state of mind at all relevant times, even where his evidence might not assist his claim. He informed us that he decided to return to Australia some time in 1986 (largely because of the education of his children who were then in their teens) and, having sold his share of the business and arriving back in Australia, he applied to rejoin Telecom and was promptly accepted and sent to Port Hedland.

13. The legal effect of having elected to apply for a refund of his accumulated contributions was that, for purposes of his superannuation, Mr Morgan started once again "from scratch" when he resumed his employment with Telecom, i.e. he lost the accruing equity which, had he preserved his superannuation rights, would have included his years of service prior to his resignation - a not inconsiderable future loss if he should contemplate retirement before attaining the age of 65 years (by which time the loss would be significantly reduced).

14. On the whole of the evidence, we are satisfied that at the time he resigned, Mr Morgan was given no verbal or written advice as to his various options. We are also satisfied that he was not consciously aware that he had the option of preserving his superannuation rights, albeit the wording of Form S2A, he now concedes, should have put him on notice. ("The question was asked, or pointed out in the statements, in reconsideration, yes, perhaps it should have. But at the time and my location and situation, I just didn't believe - I didn't know about any other options"; p 15 tr). We are also satisfied that whilst he became aware of the "preservation" option soon after rejoining Telecom, he only learnt of the provision of sec 157(1) (providing a discretion to "recognise" the election) half way through 1990. He thereupon sought to make a late election to preserve his superannuation rights in reliance on sec 157(1). Thus, on 24 July 1990, he claimed in his application that:-

"If I had been informed about my rights to preserve my

superannuation benefits, I would have done so as the benefit of

this to myself and family are obvious.

I have only recently found out that I can apply for a late

election to preserve my superannuation benefit under section

157(1) of the Act. I hereby formally request permission to avail

myself of a late election to preserve my rights."

15. This application was refused, first by Mr Vance, a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation on 20 June 1991, and subsequently confirmed by Mr Whithear, another delegate of the Commissioner, on a requested reconsideration on 3 July 1992.

16. It is the decision of 20 June 1991, viz not to direct that the election made by Mr Morgan on 24 July 1990 under subsec 137(1) be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed, which is the subject of the application before us.

17. Turning to the law, sec 137 and 138(1) of the Act provide:-

137. (1) A person who:

(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which

paragraph (b) does not apply and, upon ceasing, is not entitled to

pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with

section 69, 72 or 73; or

(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue

which the person is to be deemed, under subsection 58(3), to have

retired voluntarily;

may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible

employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner, that this

Division apply in relation to the person.

(2) If a person makes an election for the purposes of this

section within 1 month before he ceases to be an eligible

employee, the election has effect as if it had been made on the

day after he ceased to be an eligible employee.

(3) Subject to sub-section (4) an election under this section is

of no effect unless the person who made the election gives notice

in writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such

further period as the Commissioner allows, after the expiration of

the period that is the prescribed period in relation to him -

(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration

of the prescribed period and, if so, the name and

address of his employer; and

(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of

the prescribed period a member of the superannuation

scheme applicable in relation to that employment and, if

so, specifying the scheme concerned.

(4) ...

Section 138. (1) Subject to this Division, where -

(a) a person makes an election under section 137; and

(b) within the period that is the prescribed period in relation

to him, he -

(i) becomes employed in public employment; and

(ii) becomes a member of an eligible superannuation

scheme that is applicable in relation to persons

employed in that employment,

a transfer value in respect of the person is payable to the person administering that scheme.

18. Section 157 of the Act provides:

(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an

election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration

of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the

election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the

circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should

be recognized, the Commissioner may direct that the election be

treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the

election shall have effect accordingly.

19. Evidence was adduced by both parties for the purpose of informing the Tribunal of the advice available to intending retirees at the time Mr Morgan made his election. Thus, the applicant advised us that he was offered virtually no information re superannuation entitlements at the time he first joined Telecom and absolutely none when he indicated his intention to retire from the service. In support of that evidence, he called a Mr Smith, a technical officer with Telecom, who deposed that he, like the applicant, was given little information on joining Telecom with respect to superannuation. The respondent, in turn, called a Mr David Chamberlain, currently employed in the Consumer Business Unit of Telecom, an unit set up to look after staff conditions of service. It is part of this witness' duties to run seminars advising staff with respect to superannuation rights. Mr Chamberlain was asked specifically about employees on secondment to the PNG Government and their awareness of their right to preserve their superannuation benefits. The following took place in evidence-in-chief.

23. The delegate thereupon concluded that, on the information available to him, it was not desirable that Mr Morgan's election should be accepted, because, inter alia:

18. At the time of his resignation Mr Morgan had been employed in

Commonwealth employment for more than 15 years. That being the

case once alerted to the fact that preservation was a feature of

the CSS it was not unreasonable to have expected him to have

sought further information if he had been interested in pursuing

this matter. In this regard I have taken note of the fact that the

form S2A contained specific references to preservation of

superannuation rights and that this Office had made available to

Departments and Approved Authorities information on this subject

prior to Mr Morgan's resignation

24. The above reasoning is nothing if not robust. The evidence - unshaken in cross-examination - is to the effect that the applicant was at no stage advised by his employer as to his available options under the Superannuation Act, i.e. from the time he became a member of the Commonwealth Superannuation scheme in 1969 and only became aware of that option when he received a circular from his union, dated 30 April 1990, advising that members "who have broken periods of service and have withdrawn their own superannuation contributions at the time of resignation during the period 1 - 76' to early 1988 may have an entitlement to a `late' election to preserve their CSS benefits"; (exh "B"). To add insult to injury, Mr Chamberlain, when asked how many of the 80 to 100 members of the Human Resource Department "would have been knowledgable about superannuation" conceded that "you'd probably be looking at no more than half a dozen". Although not relevant to the outcome, we cannot refrain from noting that, contrary to what another delegate of the respondent concluded in his reconsideration of the decision under review, the duty to employees is not sufficiently discharged merely by issuing -

"Commonwealth Departments and Approved Authorities with a series

of information papers between 1976 and 1978 relating to various

aspects of the new superannuation scheme. In particular

Information Paper No 7, which was issued on 2 Aug 1978, contained

specific information in relation to the preservation of

superannuation rights in respect of persons leaving the CSS. This

information was subsequently incorporated into the Instructions

contained in the Superannuation Manual and would have been

available to Mr Morgan, through his Personnel Section, at the time

he ceased to be a member."

25. As already noted, Mr Morgan sought a reconsideration of Mr Vance's decision and, in due course, his decision was reconsidered by Mr R Withear who, after closely analysing an earlier case on the same point - re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 14 AAR 456, upheld Mr Vance's decision.

26. Although Mr Withear's reconsideration is, technically, not relevant to our determination (cf sec 154(6)), we do not propose to ignore it.

27. At the outset, it must be said that Mr Whithear undertook a more detailed and thorough examination of the facts than appear in the decision now under review. In his reasons, Mr Withear stated, inter alia,

18. In considering whether there are "objectively good reasons

which justify (Mr Morgan) not being required to comply with the

time limit, I note firstly (here followed the reference to the

distribution of the information papers referred to in para 24

above). ...

19. Mr Morgan's application form was signed by him and witnessed

by an officer of the Post and Telecommunication Corporation, Papua

New Guinea. Prior, at least, to submitting it, he had directed

enquiries about how to apply for a refund, but not about

preservation, to his former staff section in Perth and the RBO.

In Re Liddle [1991] AATA 191; (1990) 14 AAR 456), Deputy President Forgie stated:

"... The form is not as comprehensive in its explanations as

the form now used (S2R). ... However, what the form does do

is to alert the reader to the fact that he or she must choose

one of three things: ... Certainly, no information can be

gleaned from the form itself about these options but the

form, does, in my view, put the reader on notice that he or

she should make further enquiries as to whether these options

apply to his or her circumstances and generally find out more

information out them." (p 473)

The other members of the Tribunal expressed agreement with Deputy

President Forgie in this regard.) In this regard, in Re Liddle,

Deputy President Forgie further stated (and on this point the

other two Tribunal members concurred) that:

"It seems to me that Mr Liddle had, at the time of his

resignation, the opportunity to make enquires about his

entitlements. However inadequate the explanatory material in

the application form (i.e. form 2A) it would at least have

put him on notice that he should make enquiries. It seems to

me that he made his choice careless of his entitlements ...

."

20. Accepting this view and applying it to the present case, even

if, as Mr Morgan maintains, "the staff clerk I spoke to even

presumed I had no other choice but to get a refund of

contributions", and notwithstanding the incomplete information

contained in Telecom's handbook, which, of course, was not an

authorised publication sanctioned by the RBO, I am of the opinion

that Mr Morgan should have made "further enquiries" about his

resignation benefits, and in particular the meaning of

"preservation", once being alerted to the existence of the option

for preservation of superannuation rights by the reference in the

form S2A (our underlining), an option applicable to people who

resign. He telephoned his former staff section and the RBO to ask

for advice about how to apply for a refund, and clearly, having

noted the references to the preservation of rights section in the

application form - an option he was required to address in filling

out the form - could have made enquiries about the preservation

option in the same way. Mr Morgan does not, himself, claim to

have made any attempt to find out what the reference to the term

"preservation of rights" in the form meant, or more generally what

was the nature of the benefit options he was required to choose

between in completing the form. He states, in fact, "out of

ignorance, I would have presumed that options 2 and 3 did not

apply". I am of the view that in making choice of options without

doing so he made his choice, in the AAT's words, "careless of his

entitlements".

21. In the Liddle case, the view of the majority of the Tribunal

was that, although Mr Liddle would have been "put on notice" by

the references on the form to preservation of rights that he

should have made enquiries, at the time when he might have been

expected to initiate those enquiries he was suffering from a

diagnosed condition which adversely affected his mental capacity,

and that the consequent difficulties in obtaining advice were

further exacerbated by his geographical isolation from his agency.

In those circumstances the Tribunal considered the Commonwealth

had failed adequately to advise Mr Little of his rights. At the

time Mr Morgan applied for benefit he was in Papua New Guinea.

Nevertheless, his geographical isolation was not such that he was

unable to make enquiries, for in fact he telephoned both his

former staff section and the RBO to ask about claiming a refund.

In any case, in Liddle's case, geographical isolation was not a

reason in itself for Mr Liddle being unable to obtain advice. It

merely exacerbated the difficulty made by his mental state. At

the time of his resignation, I have no reason to believe Mr Morgan

was not in good mental state or was incapable of making rational

decisions, including on superannuation matters. Therefore, I do

not believe such an obligation upon the Commonwealth existed in Mr

Morgan's case. I do not consider it unreasonable to expect that

an officer, such as Mr Morgan, when completing an important form

such as an application for superannuation benefits, would read the

form carefully, not the references to preservation, consider

whether his existing belief as to his entitlements might have been

wrong or incomplete, and ask his personnel section or the RBO what

the reference to "preservation" meant.

28. The first thing to observe is that the form used in Liddle (Form 2A), as set out in DP Forgie's decision, differs significantly in one respect from Form S2A used in this case. Thus, Form 2A omits any warning as to the effect of electing to take a payment of a lump sum benefit or preserving one's superannuation rights, limiting itself to the following:

apply for a refund of accumulated contributions )

I... of... hereby apply for payment of a lump sum benefit )

elect for preservation of superannuation rights )

* Delete whichever inapplicable

whereas Form S2A could well convey the impression that whilst an election for a payment of a lump sum or the preservation of superannuation rights may - save in special circumstances - be irrevocable, an application for a lump sum may be withdrawn without penalty. Since the difference between these two forms was not adverted to in argument and because we considered that it was possible that the applicant may have been misled by the ambiguous wording of the form, we decided to recall the parties for further submissions. In the result, after formal submissions by both parties, the Tribunal asked the applicant whether he had considered the various options set out in the form before signing it; his reply is not without significance:

Yes, I did. At the time I was only aware that I could get my money

back. So, whilst I looked at the form, I formed the view that I

didn't have a choice.

In other words, the ambiguity in the form did not cause or contribute to Mr Morgan's lack of awareness of his entitlement under the Act.

29. It became clear during the hearing that Mr Morgan's real complaint was not so much that he lost the opportunity to remain in the Fund, but rather that the respondent failed to inform him of his options. Thus, after some questions from the Tribunal relevant to causation (i.e. had he been alerted to the superannuation preservation option, would he have elected to take it in preference to seeking a refund of his contributions), the following took place:

MR MORGAN: My point of view is that I had a right which I wasn't

told about, regardless of the outcome. I had a right, in my

opinion, to be informed properly of my options regardless of what

I intended or may or may not have done some time in the future."

(p 23 tr).

The D PRESIDENT: I am trying to put it in simple language. If, had

you had a full awareness of your entitlement in 1985, we come to

the conclusion that you would not have exercised that election in

favour of leaving your money in the fund, then your failure to be

informed is not a feature which looms large in our

consideration---I understand what you are saying and you put it

very well and I do understand. When I resigned, I didn't know

about the options. ... Okay, I maintain that I should have been

told, but also there were other considerations. ... I believe I

should have been told and, therefore, I can't read this the same

... what I may or may not have done after I resigned is all

conjecture - I would suggest that what's going to happen from

today, tomorrow, the next week. (pp 25 tr).

30. In the end, we have concluded that Mr Morgan fails on of causation in which the inadequacy of the "system" of advising employees of their superannuation entitlements then in operation and/or the ambiguity in the wording of Form S2A played no part. In other words, Mr Morgan has not persuaded us on the probabilities that if he had been fully informed as to his options, he would have elected to preserve his superannuation rights in preference to his lump sum entitlement. True it is that when he was asked by a member of the Tribunal: "If you had been informed of your option at that time...", he chimed in with the response: "I would have left the money there." However, although we have no doubt that this answer was given honestly, nothing can produce so much wisdom as hindsight. Our reason for coming to the above conclusion is that at the time of Mr Morgan's resignation, he was (i) in business in PNG, (ii) had no immediate plans to return to Australia, let alone rejoining Telecom, and (iii) used the proceeds of his payout to reduce the mortgage on the family home. Against that background, his evidence that an awareness of his options would have persuaded him to leave his money in the Fund simply makes no sense. There is nothing like hindsight to alter one's perceptions of what one would have done "if". Regrettably, only fortune tellers and pollsters can - or attempt to - predict the future.

31. Even if the applicant had been able to straddle the causation hurdle, there is a further reason why he cannot succeed. In Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation (published on 26 February 1993, as yet unreported), a case also dealing with the discretion to recognise an election under the Act outside the prescribed period, a Full Tribunal consisting of Deputy President McMahon, Dr DB Travers and Mr HG Julian DSC (Members) took the opportunity to analyse many of the authorities dealing with the vexed question of time limitations. After citing the well-known passage from Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; 7 ALD 315 at pp 319-320, the Tribunal found it helpful to apply the principles relevant to the exercise of discretion under sec 157(1) derived from that case to the case before it. Summarising Cohen, the Tribunal noted:

That there must be an acceptable explanation of the delay. In the

circumstances of late election cases, one should ask firstly

whether the applicant in fact knew of his rights to preserve

superannuation benefits. One should ask whether one is satisfied

that the employee was in fact appraised of the situation and

whether, through passage of time, or through supersedence (sic) of

other interests at the time, the information has simply been

forgotten. Secondly, if one is satisfied that specific information

was made available to the applicant, one should then ask whether

any enquiries were made of the applicant's personnel officer or at

the office of the Commissioner. ... Thirdly, one should consider

the opportunities given to the applicant to enquire. One should

look at the employee's education level, his health, any language

difficulty or impediment to information sharing, any family or

personal circumstances that might impinge upon the employee's

opportunity to enquire, the location and any general publicity

available at the time. ... His Honour considered one should take

account of any action taken by the applicant other than making an

application for review under the Act. In the circumstances of a s

157 application, one may ask what happened to the lump sum that

was paid to the applicant. In looking at the applicant's actions,

one may also ask whether the application for a refund is the only

record of an election or whether separate correspondence also

amounts to an election to take accumulated contributions. One

should also enquire about the actions of the employee when he or

she again entered the public sector and became eligible to renew

contributions.

His Honour considered that any prejudice to the respondent should

be taken into account. ... Again, the length of the delay will be

material in considering the extent of the prejudice. The longer

the interval between ceasing to be an eligible employee and

exercising a late election, the more difficult it will be to

establish the facts and the degree of knowledge available to the

applicant on resignation.

His Honour also considered that public consideration should be

taken into account. ... There is however a larger public

consideration in that if the discretion is freely granted in

favour of late election applicants, then the stability and

structure of the fund could be affected and furthermore those who

fail to make an election because of their perception of time

limits under the Act would be unfairly prejudiced.

32. The above amply demonstrates the substantial barriers in the path of those seeking to invoke the discretion to extend time for making an election. Whilst Mr Morgan has a sufficient and adequate explanation for the delay in seeking to invoke the discretion, he made no enquiries from his personnel officer or at the office of the Commissioner at the time he resigned, an omission made worse by the existence of a superannuation manual. The applicant is a man of considerable education, and although in New Guinea at the time he resigned, he suffered from none of the disabilities which the majority in Liddle (supra) found sufficient to treat the election as if it had been made within the period allowed. Again, the lump sum received was used to reduce the mortgage on the family home. For good measure, we are satisfied that the respondent has demonstrated a small prejudice to the fund were the application to succeed, and we can only echo the view expressed in Lander "that if the discretion is freely exercised in favour of late election applicants, then the stability and structure of the fund could be affected and furthermore those who fail to make an election because of their perception of time limits under the Act would be unfairly prejudiced".

33. For the above reasons, the Tribunal affirms the decision under review.
