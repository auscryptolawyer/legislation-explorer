Decision The Tribunal varies the decision under review by:

(a) remitting the matter to the respondent with a direction that he amend the assessment for the year ended 30 June 1991 to include assessable income in accordance with the findings and reasons for decision;

(b) by allowing the applicant’s objection to additional tax in part by reducing purported interest under section 170AA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”) to nil and remitting additional tax imposed under section 223 of the Act to 25% of the tax applicable to additional income included.

REASONS FOR DECISION

4 July 1997 Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member



  1. This is an application to review a decision of the respondent to disallow an objection against an amended assessment of income tax based on the income of the applicant for the year ended 30 June 1991. The amendment was issued to increase the assessable income of the applicant by $130,431 being an increase in the income derived from the F Family Trust. This trust had derived income in the same year from the P Unit Trust and the respondent had taken the view that the income from the unit trust was greater by the same $130,431.
  2. At the hearing the applicant was represented by Mr G Davies of counsel and the respondent by Ms J Davies of counsel. The hearing proceeded on the basis of an agreed statement of facts with a Mr F giving oral evidence in amplification of those facts.
  3. The P Unit Trust was established by deed dated 1 June 1989 and carried on business between 1 June 1989 and 31 March 1991. The F Family Trust held 30 units and three other unrelated family trusts held 50, 10 and 10 units respectively. Prior to 1 June 1989, the business had been carried on by another unit trust in which the same four family trusts held units in the same proportions. During 1990 a dispute arose between the unit holders which was resolved by a settlement agreement dated 5 December 1990. Under the terms of this agreement, the parties agreed that the P Unit Trust would prepare a balance sheet and profit and loss statement for the period 1 July 1990 to 17 November 1990 and make an interim distribution to unit holders of the profit for that period by credit to loan accounts. It was agreed further that the units held by the F Family Trust would be redeemed by the Trustee of the P Unit Trust for $24,000 and that the loan account of the F Family Trust would be repaid in instalments between January 1991 and December 1991. The units were redeemed on 21 December 1990.
  4. For the year ended 30 June 1990, the P Unit Trust showed a net profit in its accounts of $584,494 and a distribution to F Family Trust of $175,349. After various adjustments for income tax purposes totalling $369,100, the largest item of which was an expense prepaid in that year of $435,035, the P Unit Trust disclosed a net income for tax purposes of $215,394 and the share of F Family Trust of $64,619. The prepayment was treated in the accounts at 30 June 1990 as an asset and written off as an expense in the accounts of the following year.
  5. The initial profit and loss account of the P Unit Trust for the period to 17 November 1990 showed a net profit of $965,545 and an interim distribution to the F Family Trust of $289,663. A subsequent adjustment for a bad debt recovery increased the interim distribution to the F Family Trust to $291,484 and this amount was credited to the loan account in the books of the unit trust. The accounts of the unit trust for the year ended 30 June 1991 showed a net profit of $908,221 of which $291,484 had been distributed to the F Family Trust. However, the income tax return of the unit trust disclosed a net income of $1,335,554 after adjustments totalling $427,333 with, again, the largest item being the $435,035 prepaid expense claimed in the prior year and written back in the 1991 year for tax purposes. The share of net income for tax purposes attributable to the F Family Trust was shown in the return of income of the P Unit Trust at $421,994. The difference between the distribution to the F Family Trust shown in the financial statements and the income tax return was $130,510 being 30% of the $435,035 prepayment adjustment.
  6. The return of income of the F Family Trust for the year ended 30 June 1991 disclosed an income of $289,663 from the P Unit Trust and, after other income and business losses, a net income of $191,037. The resolution of the trustee of the F Family Trust resolved to distribute $5,000 to Mr F and the balance to the applicant. The return of income of the applicant disclosed income from the F Family Trust of $186,037. The respondent issued an amended assessment to the applicant increasing the income by $130,431. The applicant objected to the amended assessment and the objection was disallowed by the respondent.
  7. Prior to dealing with submissions in this matter it is appropriate to clarify the amounts involved. The respondent increased the income of the applicant by $130,431. This figure was said to be the difference between the share of accounting profit and the amount returned by the F Family Trust as income, $291,484, and its share of net income shown in the unit trust tax return of $421,915. Unfortunately, neither figure accords with the real facts shown on the documents lodged with the Tribunal. The first error is that the F Family Trust disclosed in its return of income a figure of $289,663, the original figure produced by the 17 November 1990 accounts, not the amended figure of $291,484 resulting from the bad debt recovery which was both credited to the F Family Trust loan account in P Unit Trust and shown in the financial statements for the year ended 30 June 1991. The second error is that the figure of $421,915 is the share of primary production income only and does not include a further $79 of non-primary production income. The result is that the actual difference between income returned by the F Family Trust and its share of net income disclosed in the P Unit Trust return is:

P Unit Trust Return $421,994

F Family Trust Return 289,663

Difference $132,331

  1. For the applicant, it was submitted that only the income to which it was entitled by way of actual distribution should be included in assessable income. Mr Davies placed reliance on Clause 41(f) of the trust deed governing the P Unit Trust. Clause 41 required the trustee in each accounting period to pay, apply or set aside the net income of the trust for the benefit of unit holders in proportion to the number of units held at the end of the accounting period. A discretion was given to the trustee to accumulate income and to make interim distributions. Paragraph (f) of Clause 41 provided:
“The Trustee shall hold so much of the net income of the Trust Fund for each Accounting Period as shall not be the subject of a determination effectively made at or prior to the end of such Accounting Period pursuant to the foregoing provisions of this sub-clause 41 in trust for the Unit Holders in proportion to the number of Units of which they are respectively registered as holders on the last day of such Accounting Period.”

It was said that, in the accounting period ended 30 June 1991, the trustee had determined to make an interim distribution and, as this was not sufficient to dispose of the whole of the net income, the balance must be apportioned to unit holders as at the end of the period. As the F Family Trust was not a unit holder at 30 June 1991, it was submitted that no part of any income beyond that received as an interim distribution could be considered as income to which that trust was entitled. It was argued that the F Family Trust was entitled only to the income received as an interim distribution. It was said that the purpose of the settlement agreement was to remove two unit holders and allow the remaining unit holders to continue and receive the benefit of future sales and the resulting profit. Income tax consequences were said to be taken into account only at year end and those consequences can only relate to unit holders who were holders at that year end. It was submitted that the income tax effect of the prepayment could not have been dealt with at 17 November 1990 as the settlement agreement required the calculation to be based on a profit and loss statement drawn up “on a normal monthly basis consistent with proper accounting practices”. It was denied that the acceptance of the applicant’s argument would lead to a windfall gain as against the respondent. Any windfall, it was said, would be against the other parties with whom the applicant was in dispute and who, by being unit holders at year end, should bear the whole tax cost of the adjustment between accounting and taxable income.

  1. For the respondent it was submitted that the basis of assessment adopted was correct. It was submitted that the provisions of section 97 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”) required the inclusion in the assessable income of the F Family Trust the same proportionate share of the net income, as defined by section 95, of the P Unit Trust for the year ended 30 June 1991 as its share of trust or accounting income for that same year. It was argued that this “proportionate” approach as adopted by Hill J in Davis v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 89 ATC 4377 was correct and was the approach adopted by the trustee and beneficiaries in respect to the income of the year ended 30 June 1990. It was said that to allow a different approach would be against policy and equity. Alternatively, it was submitted that the amount of the prepayment at 30 June 1990 had been exhausted in accounting terms by 17 November 1990 and it was appropriate to ascertain the respective shares of net income at that date. It was submitted also, that the provisions of section 95A of the Act apply to deem the F Family Trust to continue to be presently entitled until the year end even if the Tribunal was to find that the cessation as a unit holder prior to that date was relevant. The respondent further relied on the provisions of subsection 26(b) of the Act in the event that the Tribunal did not accept the submissions on the operation of section 97. It was said that, if the applicant was to be successful, there would be an inappropriate windfall gain as the share of income from the P Unit Trust in the prior year and which was included as assessable income of the unit holder was less than the actual cash entitlement. The primary cause of this reduction was a timing difference in relation to a deduction which the respondent believed should be reflected in an equivalent increase in income of the following period.
  2. This matter revolves primarily around subsection 97(1) of the Act, the relevant provisions of which state:
“97(1) Where a beneficiary of a trust estate who is not under any legal disability is presently entitled to a share of the income of the trust estate—

(a) the assessable income of the beneficiary shall include—

(i) so much of that share of the net income of the trust estate as is attributable to a period when the beneficiary was a resident; and

(ii) ...

...”

Section 95 defines ‘net income’ in the following terms:

“‘net income’, in relation to a trust estate, means the total assessable income of the trust estate calculated under this Act as if the trustee were a taxpayer in respect of that income and were a resident, less all allowable deductions, ...”

There follows some exclusions from allowable deductions which are not relevant to this matter.

  1. Although there is no reference to a year of income or any period within which the identification of beneficiaries, share of the income or net income is to be made, it is recognized that the scheme of the whole Act is a scheme of annual taxation based on a financial year. One of Ms Davies’ submissions was that, in relation to the P Unit Trust, the share of income of the F Family Trust could be calculated by reference to the period to 17 November 1990 so constituting two periods within the financial year ended 30 June 1991. In this submission the decision of the Federal Court in Rowe (B and HG) v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 82 ATC 4243 was seen as authority. That case involved a partnership and the Court said ( at page 4244):
“Net income must ordinarily be related to a period. For taxation purposes, in the case of a partnership, that period is the relevant tax year. That does not, however, necessarily mean that that is the only, or the critical, relevant period for taxation purposes. Where, for example, a partnership business has been carried on by one partnership for part of a tax year and by a different partnership for the residue of the tax year, there will, for taxation purposes, be two periods in respect of which net income will be required to be ascertained so as to divide overall income between the two partnerships. The fact that the accounts in relation to the first period are not drawn up until after the period has expired, will not alter the prima facie position that the persons who, as partners, derive the income during that period will be the persons in whose assessable incomes the net income of that period falls to be included, according to their respective interests, pursuant to sec. 92(1) of the Act.”

It is clear that in the absence of agreement to the contrary a partnership will cease on the change of constitution of that partnership and a new differently constituted partnership may commence. Here we are dealing with a trust estate in which the beneficial interest of the beneficiaries are determined by the ownership of units in that trust estate. A change of unit holders during a year does not put an end to one trust estate and the commencement of a new trust estate. The ‘trust estate’ here continued unchanged by the repurchase of the units held by the F Family Trust. Consequently, the questions to be answered here are: i) what was the share of income of the trust estate for the year ended 30 June 1991 to which the F Family Trust was entitled? And ii) what share of net income of that trust estate for the same year should be included in its assessable income?

  1. The principle thrust of the submission of Mr Davies was that the answer to the second question was governed by Clause 41(f) of the Trust Deed. To consider this submission it has to be recognized that there can be, and frequently is, a difference between net income for the purposes of the Act and net income for the purposes of the trust. Under the terms of the trust deed the trustee was obliged to “collect, receive and get in” all income from the investments of the trust fund and pay out all costs and other proper outgoings in respect of the investments and administration of the trust fund. A balance sheet and statement of income and expenditure at each 30 June was required to be prepared in accordance with normally accepted accounting period. The net income of the trust fund in each accounting period was to be dealt with under Clause 41 of the deed. It is clear that the ‘net income’ referred to in that clause, including paragraph (f), is the net income as arrived at by the statement of income and expenditure. For convenience I will describe that figure as the ‘distributable income’ and it is that figure which the trustee was required to deal with and is the only figure which the trust deed and the trustee can deal with. If the calculation of net income under section 95 of the Act comes to a different figure, it is section 97 which governs how that figure is dealt with not the trust deed or the trustee. As a result, I am unable to accept that Clause 41(f) of the trust deed provides any answer to the question of what was the share of the net income to which section 97 refers. It can relate only to the relevant shares of distributable income. Here the F Family Trust was presently entitled, in the year ended 30 June 1991, to $291,484 out of a distributable income of the P Unit Trust for that year of $908,221.
  2. Prior to considering the basis for calculation of the share of net income pursuant to section 97 it is appropriate to consider what is meant by “the income of the trust estate”. Mr Davies, in his submission conjectured that this phrase could be referring to gross income of the trust estate and it then may be necessary to consider, in arriving at a beneficiary’s share, the expenses incurred by the trustee in respect to the specific beneficial interest. In this case, that might be seen as expenditure to 17 November 1990 only. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Totledge Pty Ltd [1982] FCA 64; (1982) 60 FLR 149, Bowen CJ, Deane J and Fitzgerald J, in a joint judgement, said (at pages 159 and 160):
“...the preferable construction of s. 97(1) is to treat the requirement of present entitlement to a share of the income of the trust estate as not being concerned with distinctions between gross income as derived and ‘surplus income’ after payment of costs, expenses and outgoings but as referring to a present vested right to demand and receive payment of the whole or part of what has been received by the trustee as income and, retaining that character in his hands, is legally available to be distributed to those entitled to it as beneficiaries under the trusts of the relevant trust estate.”

Later (at page 161) their Honours said:

“In Taylor v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [[1970] HCA 10; (1970) 119 CLR 444] Kitto J. Examined what was said in the judgments in Whiting’s case and concluded that ‘the tenor of the judgements is, I think, that “presently entitled” refers to an interest in possession in an amount of income that is legally ready for distribution so that the beneficiary would have a right to obtain payment of it if he were not under a disability’ [at pages 449-452]. Kitto J.’s reference to ‘legally ready for distribution’ was, as is made clear both by his decision in the case and subsequently in his judgment, to income that was available for distribution regardless of whether the accounts necessary to enable its precise ascertainment had been completed at the end of the income year or whether it was actually held in a form ready for immediate payment.”

In this case the amount legally ready for distribution by the trustee of the P Unit Trust in respect of the year ended 30 June 1991 was $908,221 and the share of that income to which the F Family Trust was presently entitled was $291,484.

  1. The application of section 97 where the distributable income differs from the ‘net income’ as defined by section 95 which, for convenience, I will describe as ‘tax law income’ has been the subject of much debate. Surprisingly, the question does not appear to have been faced squarely by the Courts, although Boards of Review have given the matter attention in cases such as Case E69, 5 TBRD 431, Case C36, 71 ATC 156 and Case R32, 84 ATC 298 prior to the decision of Hill J in Davis’ case (supra). Although the major part of the decision in that case related to the application of section 260, his Honour discussed what he described as the “proportionate view” and the “alternative view” of dealing with the difference between the two amounts. He said (at pages 4403 and 4404):
“The different views are best understood by considering the terms of sec. 97 as it stood prior to its amendment in 1979. It will be recalled that these amendments followed upon the Taxation Review Committee, Full Report, 1975 (Asprey Committee Report) and were largely concerned to overcome the problem exposed by the decision of the High Court in Union Fidelity Trustee Co. of Australia Ltd. v F.C. of T. 69 ATC 4084; [1969] HCA 36; (1969) 119 CLR 177. Although these amendments do not affect the construction of sec. 97 for present purposes they obscure the simplicity of the section by what for the present analysis is unnecessary verbiage.

Section 97 read as follows:

‘(1) Where any beneficiary is presently entitled to a share of the income of a trust estate and is not under any legal disability, his assessable income shall include that share of the net income of the trust estate.’

Under the ‘proportionate view’ the opening words of sec. 97(1) require a calculation of the share or proportion of the trust law income to which each beneficiary of a trust estate is presently entitled. Once that share or proportion is calculated then the second half of the subsection operates to include in assessable income that same share or proportion of the ‘net income’. Under this view, provided that there is a beneficiary or there are beneficiaries who are alone or together presently entitled to the whole of the trust law income of a trust estate the net income for tax purposes will be distributed among those same beneficiaries proportionately and there will be no amount upon which the trustee will be liable to pay tax under the provisions of sec. 99 or 99A of the Act.

The alternative view, while accepting as it must that present entitlement can relate only to trust law income and not to tax law income for the simple reason that the composition of ‘net income’ is merely a matter of computation and may include amounts which have no correlation with assets at all (e.g. an amount that might be included in the net income under sec. 36 of the Act) would construe sec. 97 as directing specific attention to a taxpayer’s share of trust law income and requiring the inclusion in assessable income only of that part of the net income of the trust estate as is represented by the proportionate part of trust law income. Thus if one assumes that trust law income is to be divided equally between two beneficiaries A and B and the trust law income is $100, where the net income is $200 of which only $100 is represented by the trust law income only that $100 is distributed equally between A and B under sec. 97 leaving there to be a balance undistributed in respect of which the trustee is to be assessed and liable to pay tax under sec. 99 or 99A.

It is quite clear that neither interpretation of sec. 97 produces a desirable result as a matter of tax policy and the scheme of Div. 6 calls out for legislative clarification, especially since the insertion into the Act of provisions taxing capital gains as assessable income. On the proportionate view a taxpayer may be assessed on amounts he neither did nor could receive; on the alternative view a taxpayer could be taxed on less than he received if the share of trust law income exceeded that part of the net income as is represented by trust law income, and the maximum rate of the tax under sec. 99A would be applicable to the balance. However, the proportionate view does seem to me, as a matter of language, to be the better construction of the section and in the absence of any authority compelling me to adopt the alternative method, I propose to accept it. It was not argued by either side that the proportionate method was incorrect.”

With respect, his Honour’s acceptance of the proportionate view would appear to be the proper construction of section 97 notwithstanding the possible anomalies or inequity which that construction might produce. It must be assumed that the difference between the words “share of the income” at the commencement of section 97(1) and “that share of the net income” in paragraph (1)(i) were chosen deliberately by the legislators. As discussed previously the share of the income is the share of the distributable income and it is that proportionate share of the tax law income which is to be included in the assessable income of the beneficiary. Here the proportionate share of the tax law income, $1,335,554, to be included in the assessable income of the F Family Trust is the same proportion as $291,484 is of $908,221. That figure is $428,632. It is different to the figure shown as the taxable amount in the return of income of the P Unit Trust and used by the respondent in his calculations. As indicated earlier, the return of income calculated the increase from distributable income to taxable income relevant to the F Family Trust as being $130,510 solely on the basis of 30% of the prepayment. On any analysis there are no grounds for limiting the calculation to one item only of the adjustments to arrive at the taxable income of the P Unit Trust. Section 97 is concerned with two figures only, the share of distributable income and that share of the tax law income of the same year.

  1. Having determined the assessable income of the F Family Trust it is then necessary to determine what proportion of the net income of that trust estate should be included in the assessable income of the applicant. As stated earlier, the return of income of the trust disclosed a net income of $191.037 for the year ended 30 June 1991. This was made up of:

Primary production loss $(48,141)

Share of partnership loss (16,179)

Income from P Unit Trust 289,663

Interest received 889

Prior year losses carried forward (35,195)

$191,037

Adjusting for the increase in assessable income from the P Unit Trust increases this net income to $330,006. A resolution of the directors of the trustee company dated 24 June 1991 stated:

“The estimate of the income of the Trust for the year ended 30th June 1991 was considered and it was resolved that the following distribution of the net income be made:-

‘Mr F’ $ 5,000

‘the applicant’ The balance

It was further resolved that the distributions be effected by placing the relevant amounts to the credit of the parties in the books of the Trust.”

Under the terms of the trust deed, the trustee had a discretion to distribute income within the class of beneficiaries. Clause 3 of the deed relating to income also stated:

“AND PROVIDED FURTHER that the application or setting aside of any part of the income of the trust estate to or for the benefit of any beneficiary may be effectually made by a resolution of the Trustee that a sum out of or portion of the net income of the Trust Fund for the accounting period or a sum out of or portion of the net income as defined in Section 95 of the Income Tax Assessment Act of the Trust Fund for the accounting period be allocated to such beneficiary or otherwise dealt with for the benefit of such beneficiary AND any resolution of the Trustee allocating income as hereinbefore provided shall be irrevocable and the income of the Trust Fund shall be dealt with as required by such resolution.”

It is difficult to see how a trustee can by resolution apply or set aside to or for the benefit of a beneficiary any amount included in net income as defined in section 95 of the Act which is not also reflected in the calculation of net income or distributable income of the trust fund. However, in the foregoing resolution no such attempt was made. It may be argued that where a similar style resolution is made by a trustee allocating a fixed sum to one beneficiary and the balance of income to another, and resolves that any additional amounts, which are included by section 95 so that taxable income exceeds distributable income, are to be allocated to that residuary beneficiary, that resolution determines the amounts to be included in assessable incomes under section 97. However, that was not the position here. It might be seen as useful to refer to the basis of distribution in a prior year to ascertain which beneficiary obtained the benefit of a lower assessable amount than the share of distributable income which would have arisen from the assessable income from the P Unit Trust for the year ended 30 June 1990. This approach, even if seen as a valid method of applying section 97, which is very doubtful, can provide no assistance in this case as the F Family Trust disclosed a net loss for the year ended 30 June 1990. Whilst there may well have been a positive distributable income with the inclusion of distributable income of the P Unit Trust, any resolution of the trustee in respect of that year was not in evidence. As a consequence, and to be consistent in the interpretation of section 97, the amount to be included in the assessable income of the applicant for the year ended 30 June 1991 is the same proportion of the net taxable income of the F Family Trust as the applicant’s proportion of the trust’s distributable income for that year. The financial statements of the trust for the year ended 30 June 1991 were not provided in evidence at the hearing and a direction was issued subsequent to the hearing for such statements to be filed with the Tribunal with a copy to be provided to the respondent. These statements disclosed a net profit for the year of $226,232. The difference between that and the taxable income disclosed in the return of income of the same year was $35,195 being a loss carried forward from the prior year. The financial statements showed a distribution from the P Unit Trust of $289,663 not the $291,484 shown in the financial statements of the unit trust for that same year. It can only be assumed that the higher figure which was advised to the trustee of the F Family Trust on 27 December 1991 was not known at the time of preparation of the financial statements of that trust. The correct result is that the distributable income of the trust for the year was $1,821 more that disclosed or $228,053. The resolution of the trustee as reflected in the financial statements allocated distributable income as to $5,000 to Mr F and the balance to the applicant. This means that the applicant’s proportion of the net taxable income of the F Family Trust is to be calculated as:

223,053

228,053 of 330,006 = $322,771

As a result of using some incorrect figures and relying on the allocation of net income in the P Unit Trust return of income, the respondent had included $316,468 in the amended assessment of the applicant as the share of net income of the F Family Trust. This is $6,303 less than the foregoing calculation and assessable income of the applicant for the year ended 30 June 1991 should include that further amount.

  1. The applicant had objected to the additional tax imposed in the amended assessment for the year ended 30 June 1991. The amended assessment showed:
“Additional Tax for Incorrect Return 15,260

Section 170AA Interest 14,627 ”

The additional tax for incorrect return represented 30% of the additional income tax levied by the amended assessment. It was submitted for the applicant that the applicant could not have known or been expected to know of the end of year adjustment to produce the net income of the P Unit Trust. It was said that the applicant had ceased to be a unit holder and to have any involvement with the P Unit Trust after the settlement agreement in November 1990 and it was inappropriate to suggest that any enquiries should be made in relation to matters which were no longer the applicant’s concern. It was further submitted that the respondent had no power to levy interest under section 170AA where additional tax was levied under section 223. For the respondent it was submitted that it was relevant to consider that Mr F had been a director of the trustee of the P Unit Trust until December 1990, had been aware of the adjustment at 30 June 1990 which decreased assessable income but had made no attempt to consider the other side of the adjustment in the year ended 30 June 1991. It was argued that, at least, the applicant had been inadvertent or guilty of minor carelessness in relation to a potentially contentious item. Ms Davies was unable to say why the amended assessment made reference to section 170AA and she understood that the additional tax was imposed pursuant to section 223.

  1. Section 223 was repealed by Act No. 101 of 1992 but continued to apply to assessments in respect of the year ended 30 June 1992 or prior years. This section was effectively replaced in the same amending Act by sections 226A to 226ZB. The former section 223 applies to this matter and provided for additional tax for a false or misleading statement or the omission from a statement any matter or thing without which the statement is misleading in a material particular. Additional tax of up to 200% of the resulting further income tax was provided for. Section 227 provides a discretion to the respondent to remit the whole or any part of such additional tax and it was so remitted to 30%. Section 170AA provides for interest on the amount of any increased liability remitting from an amended assessment. However, as it applied for the year in question, subsection 170AA(2) provided that interest is not payable where the taxpayer is liable to pay a penalty under section 223 or any other provision of Part VII, other than section 222. That section relates to penalty for failure to keep or furnish documents or information. This is not alleged here. The result is that the applicant cannot be liable to interest under section 170AA where a penalty is imposed by section 223. Given the circumstances of this case and the inability to levy interest on income tax payable on an amended assessment issued in February 1995 in relation to the year ended 30 June 1991, it is appropriate that some additional tax be imposed under section 223. The rate of additional tax is currently imposed by section 226G for failure to take reasonable care and although not applicable to the year in question, could, nevertheless, be seen as indicative of an appropriate rate for circumstances of this case. I find, therefore, that the additional tax for incorrect return should be remitted to an amount equal to 25% of the tax relevant to the additional income included. Mr F was a director of the applicant, the trustee of the F Family Trust and the trustee of the P Unit Trust, for part of the relevant year. The same tax agent prepared and lodged income tax returns for the applicant and the F Family Trust for the years ended 30 June 1990 and 1991. There must have been an awareness of both the likelihood of differences between assessable income and distributable income and the substantial difference at 30 June 1990 in respect of the P Unit Trust which would have to be reflected in the June 1991 assessable income.
  2. This is a case which highlights the difficulties and potential inequities posed by the wording of section 97 of the Act. Although in this case the result might not be seen as unduly inequitable as the difference between tax law income and distributable income arose primarily as a result of a timing difference in deductibility of an expense. However, many cases involve expenditure properly incurred by a trustee but non-allowable for income tax purposes. As pointed out by Hill J, under the proportionate view of interpreting section 97 which I have followed here, a taxpayer may be assessed on income which he did not and could not receive. In extreme cases the income tax payable could exceed the amount of the actual entitlement. Equally a taxpayer may be assessed on an amount considerably less than the amount of the entitlement. If the alternative view was adopted the maximum rate of income tax pursuant to section 99A would apply to any excess of tax law income over distributable income. Apart from the possible inequity of the penal rates applicable to income assessed under 99A in circumstances quite unrelated to tax avoidance, the result will be that it is the beneficiaries as to the corpus of the trust estate who suffer the diminution of the corpus. I support Hill J in his plea for legislative clarification.
  3. The result of the foregoing analysis is that the decision under review should be varied by the allowance in part of the applicant’s objection to additional tax by reducing purported interest under section 170AA to nil and by remitting additional tax levied under section 223 to 25% of the tax on additional income included. The decision should be further varied by remitting the matter to the respondent with a direction that he amend the assessment to include assessable income in accordance with the findings and reasons for decision.
