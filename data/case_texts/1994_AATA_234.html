Beth Marion Miller and Commissioner of Superannuation [1994] AATA 234 (11 August 1994)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

BETH MARION MILLER v COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. Q92/510

AAT No. 9666

Number of pages - 9

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

D.W. MULLER, (Senior Member), H.M. PAVLIN, (Member) AND A.M. BRENNAN, (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Preservation of superannuation benefits by a resigning eligible employee - election outside of prescribed period - discretion to recognise late election

Superannuation Act 1976: Ss137(1), 157

Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation No. G161 of 1993

Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman No. SG87 of 1993

HEARING

BRISBANE, 17 August and 1 December 1993

11:8:1994

Solicitor for the Applicant: Mr. Watling, Watling Roche

And Assoc

Solicitor for the Respondent: Ms. Jolly, Advocate

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision of the delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation not to direct that the election made by Beth Marion Miller on 14 November 1990 under subsection 137(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that subsection of the Act.

DECISION

D.W. MULLER, H.M. PAVLIN AND A.M. BRENNAN This is an application for review of a decision by the delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation dated 21 August 1992 confirming the decision of a delegate of the Commissioner of 27 March 1992 not to direct that the election made by Beth Marion Miller, the applicant, on 14 November 1990 under subsection 137(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (the Act) be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed.

2. It is the applicant's contention that when she resigned from the Department of Social Security (DSS) in 1981 she was not given any information about the options available to her in respect of preservation of superannuation rights and that if she had been given the information she would have preserved her rights instead of seeking a refund of contributions.

3. The matter was heard in Brisbane on 17 August 1993 and 1 December 1993. At the hearing the applicant was represented by Mr. Watling, solicitor, and the respondent was represented by Ms. Jolly.

4. Oral evidence was given at the hearing by the applicant, her husband, Ms. Carol Sheffield an employee at the Public Sector Management Commission and Mr. John Edwards an employee of the Department of Social Security.

5. The Tribunal had before it the following documents:

(a) Documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative

Appeals Tribunal Act 1975.

(b) Letter dated 5 March 1993 from the Holy Spirit Hospital to

Watling Roche and Associates.

(c) Letter dated 4 September 1993 from Dr. John Herron.

(d) Letter dated 25 May 1993 from St. Stephen's Private Hospital

to Watling Roche and Associates.

(e) Letter dated 6 May 1980 from Drs. Rudolph, Cooper and Stark.

(f) Letter dated 28 April 1980 from Drs. Rudolph, Cooper and Stark.

(g) Letter dated 18 August 1992 from ANZ Bank to Mrs. Miller.

(h) Original Form S2A.

(i) Letter dated 6 May 1980 from Mrs. Miller to Pay Clerk, Staff

Section, Brisbane.

(j) Letter dated 18 February 1980 from Mrs. Miller to Personnel

Officer, Staff Section, Brisbane.

(k) Letter dated 5 March 1980 from DSS to Mrs. Miller.

(l) Superannuation circular dated 2 August 1978.

6. The following facts are not in dispute and the Tribunal finds as follows:

(a) Mrs. Miller was born on 17 May 1951.

(b) She commenced employment as a data processing officer in the then

Department of Health on 29 October 1973.

(c) In February 1975 she married John Gary Miller who was employed

with the Department of Customs and Excise.

(d) In November 1976 John Miller was promoted to a position in

Mackay. Mrs. Miller went with her husband to Mackay and obtained

a transfer to DSS in Mackay.

(e) In 1979 John Miller was promoted to a position in Maryborough.

Mrs. Miller was able to obtain a transfer to a position in DSS,

Maryborough.

(f) On 21 February 1979 Mrs. Miller wrote to the DSS staff section

stating (insofar as it is relevant) -

"Would you please advise me on the new superannuation scheme. I need

the information for an insurance firm.

What, if anything, am I entitled to upon disablement re pension or

other benefit from the Superannuation scheme?"

On 28 March 1979 DSS replied as follows:

"In reply to your questions on superannuation please find enclosed

2 leaflets containing information on invalidity and age retirement.

From these you may be able to get an idea of your approximate return

if you are retired out for either reason at some future time."

(g) In November of 1979 Mrs. Miller had a malignant melanoma excised

at the Holy Spirit Hospital in Brisbane.

(h) On 18 January 1980 Mrs. Miller submitted her resignation with DSS

with effect from 15 February 1980.

(i) In January 1980 Mrs. Miller took some recreation leave and then

on 24 January 1980 she withdrew her notice of resignation.

(j) Between 6 May 1980 and 6 August 1980 Mrs. Miller was absent from

work on sick leave.

(k) She was granted maternity leave from 6 August 1980 to 4 August

1981.

(l) Mrs. Miller's second son was born on 27 September 1980. He was

not well for some time after his birth and needed special care.

(m) Whilst Mrs. Miller was on maternity leave she received a letter

from DSS dated 3 July 1981 requesting that it receive

notification of her decision to resume work or to resign no later

than 5 August 1981.

(n) By letter dated 28 July 1981, Mrs. Miller wrote to her employer

and gave notification of her resignation with effect from 4

August 1981. A memorandum from her regional manager to the

personnel section advising of her resignation, dated 30 July 1981

states "Unfortunately Mrs. Miller feels she cannot return to work

as her family commitments are too demanding."

(o) On 25 August 1981 a letter was sent by the Department to Mrs.

Miller which confirmed acceptance of her resignation, advised her

in relation to payment for various entitlements and stated

further as follows:-

"Enclosed please find two S2A forms for refund of superannuation

contributions. Please complete both forms where indicated and return

them to Personnel Section as soon as possible."

The S2A forms were headed:

"Application for Refund of Accumulated Contributions/Lump Sum Benefit

or Election for Preservation of Superannuation Rights."

They also contained the following:

"I............. hereby

* apply for a refund of accumulated contributions )

apply for a lump sum benefit )

elect for preservation of superannuation rights )

* Delete whichever is inapplicable."

(p) On 7 September 1981 Mrs. Miller completed the application forms

(S2A). She signed the form and her signature was witnessed by her

husband. The words "apply for a lump sum benefit" and "elect for

preservation of superannuation rights" had lines through them.

The lines consist of carbon lines and ball-point lines. The

ball-point lines are identical in colour, texture and thickness

to the ball-point writing used to fill in the other parts of the

form. No witness was able to tell the Tribunal whether or not the

carbon deletions were on the form before Mrs. Miller filled it

in. In any event the completed form was treated as an application

for a refund of accumulated contributions.

(q) At the time of filling in the S2A form described above, neither

Mrs. Miller nor her husband gave any thought to the second and

third options set out therein. They did not think that those

options were available to Mrs. Miller. They had only ever heard

of people receiving a refund of contributions upon resignation.

(r) On 16 October 1981, a payment of accumulated contributions of

$2724.06 was authorised by the RBO to be made to Mrs. Miller.

(s) Mrs. Miller had no immediate or pressing need for the money. She

deposited it in an ANZ account. It was subsequently used as part

of a deposit on the purchase of a house.

(t) On 23 April 1990 Mrs. Miller resumed full-time employment as an

Administrative Services Officer, Class 1, with the Department of

Employment, Education and Training (DEET) in Caboolture.

(u) In August of 1990, Mrs. Miller attended a superannuation seminar.

At that time a new public service scheme was coming into effect

and the seminars were run to inform staff of their options. Mrs.

Miller discovered that she could have preserved her

superannuation rights in 1981.

7. On 14 November 1990, Messrs Watling Roche and Associates, solicitors, wrote on the applicant's behalf to the RBO, stating, among other things:

"....

Our client indicates that at the time of her resignation in 1981 no

information was given to her with regards to the preservation of any

benefits. The only information our client received from the

Department was the letter dated 25th August 1981.

Our client was not advised by the personnel section or any member

from the Superannuation department of her availability to preserve

any benefits.

It is for this reason that our client requests and hereby applies for

a Late Election to preserve her superannuation benefits and in this

regard she is prepared to repay any amount required to the Department

for the preservation of her benefits.

....

A criteria that the Commissioner should take into account would be

the length of service by our client at the time of her resignation

and you will note that this in fact was not a lengthy period in all

the circumstances considering that she had leave of absence on two

occasions by way of maternity leave. Our client also was not in a

position in the Department where she would have acquired any

knowledge with regards to the Superannuation Act and Scheme. You will

note that at the time of her resignation from the Department by our

client she was on maternity leave and had been on such leave from

approximately September of 1980 until the date of her resignation in

July of 1981 and all communication to her was by way of a letter

dated 25th August 1981.

We would suggest in the circumstances that the Department owed a

special duty to our client with regards to advising her of her

options in particular to preserve her benefits and we await your

reply in relation to this matter."

8. The solicitor's letter of 14 November 1990 was treated as a late election to preserve superannuation rights. Over the following year there was much correspondence back and forth. Eventually on 27 March 1992 an agent of the Commissioner for Superannuation determined that Mrs. Miller's late election should not be accepted. On 21 August a delegate of the Commissioner confirmed the decision of 27 March 1992.

9. The relevant legislation is contained in sections 137 and 157 of the Superannuation Act 1976:

"137. (1) A person who -

(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which

paragraph (b) does not apply and, upon so ceasing, is not

entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in

accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or

(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue of

which the person is to be deemed, under sub-section 58(3), to

have retired involuntarily,

may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an

eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner,

that this Division apply in relation to the person.

(2) If a person makes an election for the purposes of this section

within 1 month before he ceases to be an eligible employee, the

election has effect as if it had been made on the day after he ceased

to be an eligible employee.

(3) Subject to sub-section (4) an election under this section is of

no effect unless the person who made the election gives notice in

writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such further

period as the Commissioner allows, after the expiration of the period

that is the prescribed period in relation to him -

(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of the

prescribed period and, if so, the name and address of his

employer; and

(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the prescribed period

a member of the superannuation scheme applicable in relation to

that employment and, if so, specifying the scheme concerned.

157.(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an

election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of

the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the

election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the

circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be

recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated

as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election

shall have effect accordingly."

The Division referred to in section 137 is Division 3 of Part IX and

refers to "preservation of rights of persons ceasing to be eligible

employees".

10. Miss Sheffield gave evidence that she was the person who sent the two S2A forms to Mrs. Miller in August 1981. She said that in 1981 she may not have been aware of the three options available to people who resigned. She treated the forms that she sent out as "for refund of superannuation" forms. She told the Tribunal that in the 1980's not one person who resigned asked about the other options. She dealt with a large number of resignations in that period.

11. Mr. Edwards has been a clerk in DSS since 1978. In 1981 he was designated a "team leader". He has been processing staff resignations since 1978 to the present day. He told the Tribunal that in the early 1980's he had all the available information about superannuation rights and options in pamphlet form which he stored in a drawer in his office. No information was circulated unless there was a specific request for it. In the early 1980's nobody ever asked him about preservation of superannuation rights. People always sought a refund of contributions. He now gets a few enquiries about preservation but they are rare. He has only ever had one person elect for preservation from 1978 to the present day. People who resign still seek the lump sum refund.

12. At the hearing Mrs. Miller was asked what she would have done in 1981 if she had been given all of the information about superannuation that she now possesses. She gave the following answers:

"MR. MULLER: Q. Just try to cast your mind back to 1981. I mean, you

might know now which one might be the one to take, but if you had all

the information available to you in 1981, and think about the climate

then and how you were feeling, and the reasons for resignation, what

would you have done then, had you had all the information at your

fingertips?

A. I possibly would have elected to preserve my benefits. It was

never my intention to stay home for ever. I always intended going

back to work at some stage, not necessarily with a government

department, but I always wanted to go back to work.

Q. And notwithstanding the evidence that you have given regarding the

stress that you may have been under at the time or the sickness of

your children, you said that you possibly would have preserved your

benefit?

A. Had I known about it, yes.

MISS JOLLY: Q. You intended to have a lengthy period away?

A. Yes.

Q. And did you think about when you finally went back to work how

long you would like to work for?

A. For ever.

Q. Until retirement age?

A. Until I retired, yes, until retirement.

Q. Now if at the time, someone had sat you down and said, okay, you

can get your refund of contributions which is $2,700, which is your

contributions plus interest, and which in 1981 terms is a quarter of

your annual salary?

A. Yes.

Q. Your gross annual salary, so it is about three months full pay; or

you can preserve. Now, you intended not to go back to work for quite

a while, so you would have - as you will today if this is accepted -

you will get a deferred benefit, which means that your money stays in

the fund and your employer's contributions stay in the fund. And they

stay, there until you reach retirement age?

A. Right.

Q. At 55, 60 or 65. You can choose to retire. And when you do so, you

can get a lump sum from these - that money back in 1981 - you can get

a lump sum payment of that in this time still - which is still a

considerable way down the track?

A. Yes.

Q. Or you can get a pension from that as well. Bearing in mind that

you did use the money less than six months later to buy - to put a

deposit on a house--?

A. Yes.

Q. ----are you able to say with any degree of certainty, what you

would have done at that time?

A. Well, as I said before, knowing what I know now, I more than

likely would have preserved it.

Q. You said before that you possibly may have preserved it?

A. Yes, well, I don't know what I would have done 20 years ago. I

would have had to have had that information there, and then

reconsidered my options then.

Q. So you cannot say - realistically you cannot say what you would

have done in 1981?

A. I believe that I would have preserved, but no, for certain I could

not say unless I was back in the situation itself."

13. The Tribunal has had the advantage of the comments made by Davies J and Cooper J in Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation No. G.161 of 1993 in a judgment handed down in Brisbane on 4 May 1994, and those of Von Doussa J in Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman No. SG87 of 1993, Adelaide, 17 June 1994.

Davies J:

"Many such provisions, including s.157, are not concerned with

rights. If an act is not performed within the prescribed time, there

is no right to have it performed. But there is a discretion to permit

time to be extended should it be appropriate in the circumstances to

do so. In the exercise of this discretion, it may be more important

to consider the consequences of extending or refusing to extend time

than to debate the reasons why the act was not done in time. Section

157 merely requires that the Commissioner 'be satisfied that in all

the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election

should be recognised'. 'All the circumstances' include all relevant

circumstances existing at the time the discretion is to be exercised.

In the application of s.157, it may be of little consequence that the

employee did not enquire as to his or her right to elect under s.137

or even that the employee positively decided not to elect. Section

157 confers a discretion which operates in either such case.

Frequently, the discretion is exercised favourably because the facts

which the employee took into account, when he or she decided not to

elect, subsequently turned out differently. Oftentimes, the

discretion is favourably exercised when the employee has encountered

health problems of which he or she was unaware at the time of

retirement. One of the objects of s.157 is to alleviate the

consequences of such a change in circumstances. The circumstances

need not be of a medical character. In Mr. Chalk's case, the change

in circumstance was that he resigned from Telecom intending to

establish himself in an entirely new career as a farmer but was

unsuccessful in doing so. And so he again took up employment with

Telecom, after a lapse of two years.

The Superannuation Scheme operates in the context of Commonwealth

employment. The rights which the Act confers are statutory rights.

But the entitlement to participate in the Scheme and to receive the

benefits of the Scheme do not merely have a statutory base. They form

an incident of the employment. Thus, a decision-maker exercising the

s.157 discretion could think it fair to the employer, to the

employee, and to the Scheme that the benefit should be calculated by

reference to the totality of the employee's service and to the

totality of his contributions, including any sums which must be

refunded."

Cooper J:

"The conduct of the Commissioner is only relevant to the exercise of

the discretion to treat the election as within time under section 157

if it helps to explain why the appellant did or did not make a timely

decision in a particular way if the conduct of the appellant is

itself a relevant consideration. In the instant case the Tribunal

found that the appellant did not know of the choices open to him

under the Act and that neither Telecom nor the Commissioner advised

him of the available choices. The Tribunal correctly treated as a

fact relevant to its deliberations that the appellant was ignorant of

the choices available and that ignorance was partly explicable by the

failure of Telecom and the Commissioner to correctly inform the

appellant of the available choices. However, section 157 does not

require that the Commissioner determine whether there exists

attributable fault on the part of the appellant or any other person

giving rise to the need to extend time; nor that the discretion be

exercised on the basis of the existence or absence of attributable

fault. Rather, as explained by Davies J. in his reasons, the section

merely requires that the Commissioner 'be satisfied that in all the

circumstances it is desirable that the election should be

recognised'. Those circumstances are all the relevant circumstances

existing at the time the exercise of the discretion falls for

consideration and will include circumstances of the type identified

by Davies J as relevant to the statutory purpose for which the

discretionary power in section 157 to treat an election as within

time was enacted."

Von Doussa J.

"In s.157(1) 'desirable' is not referable to a state of mind -

whether of the elector or of the Commissioner. Whether a late

election is 'desirable' is to be judged objectively having regard to

all the circumstances of the case. The suggested meaning 'something

worthy of achievement' does not imply other than an objective

evaluation of the circumstances of the case; but in the end, whether

the recognition of a late election is 'something worthy of

achievement' must depend on what is fair in order to do justice

between the person who seeks to make the late election and the

requirements of the administration of the Fund."

15. In Mrs. Miller's case the Tribunal takes the following matters into account:

(a) Mrs. Miller worked for the Department of Health and the

Department of Social Security from 29 October 1973 until 4 August

1981, a period of just under eight years. During that time she

was absent from work on sick leave for three months (6 May 1980

to 6 August 1980) and absent from work on maternity leave for 12

months (6 August 1980 to 4 August 1981).

(b) Upon resignation Mrs. Miller intended to remain away from work

for many years to raise her family. She intended to return to

work in the future but she was uncertain as to whether or not it

would be with the Government.

(c) Mrs. Miller received a refund of her contributions. Although she

had no pressing need for the money at the time she received it

she has had the use of it for about ten years.

(d) Mrs. Miller rejoined the Public Service on 23 April 1990 when she

was nearly 39 years of age.

(e) Mrs. Miller made a request to allow for the election to preserve

her superannuation rights on 14 November 1990.

(f) If Mrs. Miller remains an eligible contributor to the fund until

she reaches the normal retiring age of at least 55 years she will

receive a not insignificant retiring benefit.

(g) Prima facie the rules should be followed for the requirements of

the administration of the fund.

16. The Tribunal can see nothing in the circumstances of Mrs. Miller which suggests that there is anything unfair in applying the normal rules relating to the time limit within which the election should have been made. This is not a case where an exercise of the discretion allowed for under section 157 is necessary to do justice between the parties.

17. The decision not to treat the election made by the applicant as if it had been made within the period allowed will be affirmed.
