NT94/42 and Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1995] AATA 484; (1995) 30 ATR 1238; 95 ATC 243 (3 April 1995)

Last Updated: 13 May 2009



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



TAXATION - Applicant and partners received amount from lessor as incentive to rent office - contingent liability of any partner to other partners arising upon retirement within five years of receiving incentive amount - whether incentive amount constituted receipt on account of income - whether incentive amount was income in one financial year - penalty for late lodgement of tax return - decision affirmed



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (as amended) - s.25(1)

Taxation Administration Act 1953 - s.14ZS, 14ZQ, 14ZZA



Commissioner of Taxation v Cooling 90 ATC 4472

Hayes v FC of T [1956] HCA 21; (1956) 96 CLR 47

FC of T v Whitfords Beach Pty Limited 82 ATC 4031

FC of T v The Myer Emporium Limited 87 ATC 4363

Rotherwood Pty Limited v FC of T 94 ATC 4514

Arthur Murray (NSW) Pty Limited v FC of T [1965] HCA 58; 114 CLR 314

REASONS FOR DECISION



3 April 1995 The Hon. Justice Rodney N. PURVIS

(Presidential Member)



FACTUAL SITUATION AND THE ISSUES



1. The applicant is a Chartered Accountant carrying on his practice in association with a number of partners. Office services are and were provided to the partnership by a trust, the trustee of which is and was a company. The directors of the service company were partners of the partnership.



2. The partnership had for some years been carrying on its activities at the one leasehold premises in Sydney. At a time nearby to the expiry of the then lease, inquiries were made as to the rent likely to be payable if the partners renewed their then current lease. The amount was found to be much in excess of that then being paid, and so alternate business premises were sought in the city. A new building had been completed, or was nearing completion, and the owners were anxious to acquire an “anchor” tenant, a prestigious occupant, who would, by being in occupation, entice others to acquire space in the building. The owners also sought to capitalise the value of their investment by reference to rentals.



3. The partners were interested in occupying a number of floors of the new building. The owner endeavoured to set a rental with the partners which would be a benchmark for other tenants. The rental stipulated was in excess of that currently being paid, and as an incentive to the partners to take up the space, the owner, whilst not reducing the rent, offered to pay a lump sum, an incentive amount, to the partners.



4. The partners decided to accept the offer and relocate. The tenant was to be the trustee of the services trust. In consideration of payment of the incentive amount to the partners, they, by deed dated 9 July 1990 between themselves and the owner/lessor of the new building, agreed to procure the trustee to enter into the lease and inter alia to guarantee the performance by the trustee of its obligations under the lease.



5. The amount payable, the incentive amount, was in the sum of $2,416,134, and was to be apportioned amongst the partners as detailed in an annexure to the deed of 9 July 1990. The amount applicable to the applicant taxpayer was $234,756.



6. Prior to their entering into the deed, the partners had, according to the applicant, together with the directors of the trustee company, held a meeting at which:



“ ..... the question of the receipt of an incentive amount for the entering into of a new lease of new premises by [the trustee company] was discussed and in particular, it was recognised by all partners that substantial costs would be incurred in paying for the fitting out of the leased premises. At that meeting it was agreed that it would be inappropriate to accept a share of an incentive amount and then resign from the partnership leaving the remaining partners to bear the ongoing commitment for the costs of the fit-out. It was accepted that a partner who resigned from the partnership should not retain the whole of his share of the incentive amount.



Accordingly, it was agreed by the partners as a condition of the partners accepting receipt of the incentive amount, that each partner would agree that in the event of the partner resigning from the partnership at any time up until 30 June 1995, he would pay to the other partners an amount calculated in accordance with an agreed formula which was based upon [sic] in principle at the meeting and subsequently reduced to a formal agreement. It was further agreed that adjustments would also be required on changes in equity shares in the partnership. At a meeting of the partners held on 14 October 1991, one of the partners ....... presented a finalised “claw back agreement” and requested that all partners sign it.”



7. The lease agreement was executed , the incentive amount paid to and received by each of the designated equity partners and the partnership began to occupy the new space.



8. The agreement said to have been reached amongst the partners as to adjustment on resignation from the partnership, described as the “claw back agreement”, in effect provided for payment by the retiring partner to the remaining equity partners of an amount calculated by reference to the incentive sum received by that partner (but subject to various adjustments) if the retirement took place during the five years following receipt of the incentive amount.



9. It was said by the applicant taxpayer that:



“The effect of the “claw back agreement” is that a partner would retain the whole of the benefit of the incentive payment only if he completed five years service as a partner subsequent to the date of receipt of the incentive amount. In the event of a partner resigning before the expiration of that time, there would be a pro rata adjustment of the kind specified in the agreement ...... “.



10. Prior to the date on which the “claw back agreement” was signed by the partners and on 30 September 1991, a partner did resign and the principles embodied in the agreement were applied in determining the amount to be the subject of adjustment. The amount so determined was not in fact paid back by the retiring partner, but the amount was deducted from the share of profits otherwise due to such retiring partner.



11. There was an issue raised during the hearing of this application as to whether the “claw back agreement” was in fact arrived at prior to receipt of the inducement moneys or some time later. The Tribunal is satisfied on the whole of the evidence that even if the claw back agreement was not signed by the partners until September 1991, the claw back agreement was in fact part of the arrangements entered into by the partners when they were discussing the basis upon which the total of the incentive moneys would be dispersed amongst them. They were also at that time debating the cost of fitting out the new premises and the extent to which this cost would be borne by the remaining partners in the event of a retirement. As was stated by one of the partners during the course of his evidence at the hearing, “the claw back agreement is a mechanism to ensure that on retirement a partner would bear a share of the costs of the leasing of equipment then to be carried by the remaining partners”.



12. The lease was taken up, the new office premises equipped and fitted out and the partners occupied the leased space. No part of the incentive amount was used to this end. The incentive amount was paid to the partners for their own use and enjoyment. The cost of equipping and fitting out was funded by the service trust from its own funds, by way of lease arrangement, or otherwise.



13. The respondent seeks to bring to tax pursuant to the provisions of s.25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act the proportion of the incentive amount received by the applicant taxpayer and to include this amount as assessable income in the one financial year. The taxpayer disputes that the amount received was income, but says that if it is to be assessable to tax, then the figure subject to tax should be apportioned over five years, having in mind the “contingent liability” under the claw back agreement.



14. Additional tax for late lodgement of the relevant return was imposed. The taxpayer seeks to have the Tribunal review the imposition of the penalty.



ASSESSABILITY OF THE INCENTIVE AMOUNT



15. The respondent contends that the receipt by the taxpayer of $234,756 from the owner/lessor represents assessable income in his hands within the meaning of s.25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. The taxpayer in effect submits that the same was a receipt on account of capital.



16. The taxpayer and his partners were engaged in the practice of Chartered Accountants. They had been so engaged when occupying their previous premises. They continued so after moving to the new address. On the evidence, the Tribunal is satisfied that the acquisition of the new lease was a transaction carried out as an integral part of arrangements put in place for the conduct of the practice. The receipt of the incentive amount was by agreement expressed as being consideration for the partners procuring the service company to become party to the lease of the business premises, a car parking licence (for the use of partners) and a deed. The trustee company and the service trust were structures put in place to better facilitate the conduct of the business. The Tribunal finds that the arrangement entered into by the partners, the trustee company and the trust formed a part of the business activity of the partners and “a not insignificant purpose of it was the obtaining of a commercial profit by way of the incentive payment” (see Commissioner of Taxation v Cooling 90 ATC 4472, 4484).



17. Even be it that there was an advantage accruing to the lessor in being able to capitalise the value of its building on the basis of showing a higher rental income than in fact was the case, the partners did have two alternatives. One of those alternatives was to cause the trust to pay a lesser rent and thence obtain a smaller tax deduction for its outgoings and charge the partners a lesser amount by way of service fees. The other alternative was to cause the trust to pay a higher rent, and so obtain a larger tax deduction and charge a higher amount as service fees to the partners, with the equity partners, however, receiving the incentive amount.



18. The test to be applied in drawing a distinction between a receipt on account of income and one on account of capital has been said to be objective rather than subjective (Hayes v FC of T [1956] HCA 21; (1956) 96 CLR 47 at 55). Whether an amount is income in ordinary concepts depends upon its quality in the hands of the recipient (Cooling (supra) at 4479).



“Although it is well settled that a profit or gain made in the ordinary course of carrying on a business constitutes income, it does not follow that a profit or gain made in a transaction entered into otherwise than in the ordinary course of carrying on the taxpayer’s business is not income. Because a business is carried on with a view to profit, a gain made in the ordinary course of carrying on the business is invested with the profit making purpose thereby stamping the profit with the character of income. But a gain made otherwise than in the ordinary course of carrying on the business which nevertheless arises from a transaction entered into by the taxpayer with the intention or purpose of making a profit or gain may well constitute income. Whether it does depends very much on the circumstances of the case “. (FC of T v The Myer Emporium Limited 87 ATC 4363 at 4366).



“Generally speaking, however, it may be said that if the circumstances are such as to give rise to the inference that the taxpayer’s intention or purpose in entering the transaction was to make a profit or gain, the profit or gain will be income notwithstanding that the transaction was extraordinary judged by reference to the ordinary course of the taxpayer’s business.”



19. The fact that a profit or gain results from an isolated venture or a “one-off” transaction does not preclude it from being properly characterised as income (FC of T v Whitfords Beach Pty Limited 82 ATC 4031 at 4036-4037). A profit or gain so made will constitute income if the property generating the profit or gain was acquired in a business operation or commercial transaction for the purpose of profit making by the means giving rise to the profit (Myer (supra) 4367).



“If the profit be made in the course of carrying on a business, that in itself is a fact of telling significance. It does not detract from its ordinary significance that the particular transaction is unusual or extraordinary judged by reference to the transactions in which the taxpayer usually engages if it be entered into in the course of carrying on the taxpayer’s business“. (Myer (supra) 4369).



20. However, the purpose of profit-making must exist and relate to the particular operation (FC of T v Spedley Securities 88 ATC 4126 at 4130).



21. In FC of T v Cooling (supra), the Court was called upon to consider the assessability or otherwise of an amount received from a lessor as an incentive to move to new premises. At 4484, in the course of his reasons for decision, Hill J. said:



“Where a taxpayer operates from leased premises, the move from one premises to another and the leasing of the premises occupied are acts of the taxpayer in the course of its business activity just as much as the trading activities that give rise more directly to the taxpayer’s assessable income. Once this is accepted, the evidence established that in Queensland in 1985 it was an ordinary incident of leasing premises in a new city building, at least where the premises occupied were of substantial size, to receive incentive payments of the kind in question. Why then should a profit received during the course of business where the making of such a profit was an ordinary incident of part of the business activity of the firm not be seen to be income in ordinary concepts?



Another way of analysing the facts of the present case is to consider whether the transaction giving rise to the incentive payment can properly be characterised as a profit-making scheme.”



22. As in Cooling, so in the present case, the applicant contends that his business was the performance of professional services and not the receipt of incentive payments. This contention does not fit comfortably with the observations made by his Honour in Cooling with which the Tribunal concurs. The transaction entered into by the firm in Cooling was held to be a commercial transaction. “It formed part of the business activity of the firm and a not insignificant purpose of it was the obtaining of a commercial profit by way of the incentive payment”. The Tribunal is satisfied that the receipt of the inducement money in consideration of causing the trust to enter into the lease was all part of a commercial transaction carried out in the course of the business/professional activity of the applicant taxpayer. The decisions, acts, negotiation and agreement were all performed, and intended so to be, as an integral part of the conduct by the applicant and his partners of their practice.



23. The attention of the Tribunal was directed to a recent decision of Carr J. in Rotherwood Pty Limited v FC of T 94 ATC 4514 where at 4530, 4531, it was suggested his Honour “seemed to be retreating from what was said in Cooling”. More particularly, it was suggested that where his Honour at 4531 said:



“However, the decision of the Full Court of this court in FC of T v Hyteco Hiring Pty Limited 92 ATC 4694 has caused me to pause. I have come to the conclusion that if, contrary to my view, the Old Lease can be regarded as an asset then its disposal in the present matter was not an ordinary incident of Chancery Services’ business. I fully appreciate that these are factual matters where sometimes subtle factual distinctions lead to different conclusions.”,



his Honour was there seeking to read down what had been expressed by Hill J. in the earlier decision. In the present matter, the Tribunal does not have before it a factual situation where there is a disposal of an asset, rather is it the gaining of an incentive payment in the circumstances earlier indicated. The Tribunal does not read the decision in Rotherwood as a reading down of the reasons in Cooling.



24. The Tribunal is satisfied that in this matter, the applicant taxpayer entered into the transaction with the requisite intent. The incentive amount received is a receipt on account of income and within the meaning of s.25(1) of the Act is assessable to tax.



YEAR IN WHICH INCOME DERIVED



25. It is contended by the respondent that the lease incentive amount was derived by the taxpayer in the year of income in which it was received, that is, the year ended 30 June 1991.



26. The taxpayer says that the claw back agreement was not unrelated to the receipt of the incentive amount since it was agreed amongst the equity partners (being the only partners who received a share of the amount paid by the lessor) that it was a precondition to the receipt of the moneys. Thus it is said the incentive amount was not wholly derived until the expiration of the five year period. In order to avoid an obligation arising whereby an ex-partner was required to pay to the other partners a prescribed amount, each partner, it is contended by the taxpayer, was required to remain in the partnership for the five years.



27. It is to be noted, however, that the requirement to pay a sum of money consequent on retirement arises from the fact of an additional burden then falling upon the remaining partners to fund the costs of equipping and fitting out the new office space and the perceived need to be assisted in this regard by the retiring partner, the burden then otherwise being spread over a fewer number of persons. The requirement to pay does not arise from a partner being considered ineligible to have received the whole of a share of the incentive amount. This amount had been received by a partner, and such partner had the property in the money and the right to utilise it as considered by the partner apposite.



28. There was not at the time of receipt of the incentive amount any obligation on the part of any one of the equity partners to pay an amount to the other partners. Whether such an obligation came into existence depended upon a partner making a personal decision to retire from the partnership, a decision not related to the receipt of a portion of the incentive amount. A consequence of the decision of a partner to retire would have been the imposition on the remaining partners of a greater share of the costs associated with the moving. It was thought that these costs would be absorbed or fully funded over a period of five years. Hence, even though the lease was for a period of ten years - a period accepted by the partners and the trust and trustee company, in part by reason of the payment of the incentive amount - at the expiration of five years no obligation to pay an amount would arise.



29. The taxpayer had done all that was required of him to earn the income in the year when it was paid to him. The amount received became the beneficial property of the partner. It was not an amount paid in advance (see Arthur Murray (NSW) Pty Limited v FC of T [1965] HCA 58; 114 CLR 314). It was not affected by any trust or charge; there was no legal impediment in the way of the partner dealing with the money as the partner willed. A liability to pay a designated sum, even be it calculated on the basis of the amount of the incentive received, less deductions, would arise, if it did so, from circumstances unassociated with the receipt of the money or the reasons for its receipt.



30. The taxpayer contended that no partner could be regarded as having derived the whole of the incentive payment until the expiration of the five year period. This is not consistent with the factual situation where each partner received an amount without any restriction being placed on its use. The partner retained the whole of the benefit of the payment. If the partner retired, then and only then did an obligation arise to pay an amount to the remaining partners.



31. It was further submitted that the amount could not be regarded as earned until the five years had passed. That as a matter of “business good sense”, the partners and hence the respondent should “treat each amount of fees [incentive amount] received but not yet earned as subject to the contingency that the whole or some part of it may have in effect to be paid back ....... should the agreed quid pro quo not be rendered in due course” (Arthur Murray (supra) 319).



32. What then as to the “business good sense”? The applicant in his own evidence, when asked how a transaction the like of that being discussed in these reasons would be dealt with from an accounting and commercial point of view, spoke of taking up the “full income and liability that existed under the claw back agreement”. “I would”, he said, “take up the full incentive of $234,756 and take out a contingent liability over the four years”. A past partner in the firm of Chartered Accountants, indeed the one time senior audit partner, one who had been involved with accounting standards for the Australian Accounting Research Foundation, now retained as a consultant, when asked how he would treat an incentive receipt in an income statement and balance sheet, spoke of, in the first year, including one-fifth of the net incentive as income, that is, $31,241 (being the incentive receipt less various adjustments) as an “extraordinary item”. He would have placed the full net incentive less the first year’s “income” in the balance sheet as a liability. He was of the view that one could not have the assurance that income had been earned other than for the one-fifth amount. Revenue should only be recorded as income when there was an assurance as to complete entitlement. “As with the Arthur Murray case, present day accounting standards would require the subject amount to be handled in a like manner”. Thus the Tribunal was entreated to apply business and accounting standards “ordinary business and commercial principles “ as to the time of derivation of income. The respondent submitted that accounting principles should not replace what is laid down in the Income Tax legislation itself. As was also stated in Arthur Murray (NSW) Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra) at 320, “insofar as the Act lays down a test for the inclusion of particular kinds of receipts in assessable income, it is likewise true that commercial and accountancy practice cannot be substituted for the test”.



33. Even be it that partners of the applicant and the applicant spoke of the manner in which they would treat the receipt of this income, there was no evidence to the effect that any one of the recipients of the incentive payment had in fact so treated the incentive amount or the contingent liability under the claw back agreement. One would have thought that the best evidence as to the appropriate accounting treatment of such an item would have been for the Chartered Accountants to have demonstrated how they had dealt with the item in their own records.



34. There was not any evidence as to any of the partners “as a matter of business good sense” having so treated the amount received or having put an amount aside as “not yet earned”. There was no contingency of having to “pay back” the payer of the incentive, that is, the lessor. The payees under the claw back would be the remaining partners. There was no quid pro quo.



35. The Tribunal is satisfied that the whole of the incentive amount was earned during the 1991 financial year and was income derived in that year for the purposes of s.25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act.



- Additional tax



36. The respondent contends that the imposition of additional tax for the late lodgment of the return is an ineligible income tax remission decision within the meaning of s.14ZS of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 being a penalty imposed at a rate not exceeding 20 per cent and hence not a reviewable objection decision under s.14ZQ of the Act. As the decision of the Tribunal is to affirm the objection decision under review as to assessability and incidence, the contention of the respondent is maintained. The decision as to penalty is not subject to review by the Tribunal. (s.14ZZA of the Act).



37. Even if the additional tax decision had been reviewable, the circumstances advanced by the taxpayer would not warrant the decision being disturbed. The only reason given for the delay in filing his return was that the taxpayer “is a busy tax practitioner responsible for lodgment of numerous clients’ returns”. Because of pressure to lodge clients’ returns, his own preparation was delayed. It was contended that the imposition of additional tax calculated at a rate of 20 per cent “is most unfair bearing in mind that the whole of the amount is non tax deductible”. It is contended that the Commissioner should have allowed a further period in which the taxpayer could have lodged his return and should have calculated the additional tax from a date later than shown in the calculation attached to the Commissioner’s letter.



38. A person in the position of the taxpayer should ensure that his own business affairs, including his tax affairs, are in order ahead of those of his clients, this as an example to his clients of knowledge, competence, efficiency and diligence. Even if the Tribunal had jurisdiction as to penalty, the decision would be affirmed.



DECISION



39. The decisions under review are affirmed.
