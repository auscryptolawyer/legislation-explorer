Re CC Fryar and Commissioner of the Australian Federal Police [1988] AATA 334 (25 November 1988)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: C.C. FRYAR

And: COMMISSIONER OF THE AUSTRALIAN FEDERAL POLICE

No. A88/57

AAT No. 4766

Freedom of Information

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.K. Todd(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Freedom of information - whether exempt documents - information communicated by foreign government in confidence to State Government - information then communicated by State Government to Commonwealth Government in confidence - whether release would have adverse effect on conduct of agency

Freedom of information - effect of confidential release of information to third party

Freedom of Information Act 1982, ss.14, 33(1)(a)(iii), (1)(b); 33A(1)(b); 36(1)(b); 40(1)(d); 41(1); 43(1)(c)(i); 45

Australian Federal Police Act 1979, s.8

Re Reithmuller and Australian Federal Police (1985) 8 ALN N92

Re Hazan and Australian Federal Police (Decision No.2889, 19 September 1986)

Re Robinson and Department of Foreign Affairs (1986) 11 ALN N48

Re Downie and Department of Territories (1985) 8 ALD 496

Re Fewster and Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet (1986) 11 ALN N266

Re Fewster and Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet (No 2) (1987) 13 ALD 139

Re Howard and Treasurer of Commonwealth of Australia (1985) 7 ALD 626

Re State of Queensland and Australian National Parks and Wildlife

Service (Australians for Animals, party joined) [1986] AATA 224; (1986) 13 ALD 158

Re Mann and Australian Taxation Office (1985) 3 AAR 261

HEARING

CANBERRA

25:11:1988

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

By letter dated 14 December 1987 an application was made pursuant to the Freedom of Information Act 1982 ("the FOI Act") by the abovenamed applicant in the following terms:

"...I would like to be supplied photocopies of all

material, subsequent to 1/1/87, relating to (1) the

licence to operate the Christmas Island casino,

including the AFP report on Federal Hotels (2)

Federal Hotels (3) Dr Stanley Ho.

Would you please transfer this request to the

Department of Territories and the offices of the

Minister for Justice and Minister for Territories,

for the same material specified, including all

Ministerial documents, but excluding press

releases, media clipping and the Heads of

Agreement."

By letter dated 30 December 1987, the applicant provided further explanation of his request as follows:

"Although covered within the ambit of my FOI

request currently with you, it occurred to me I

should specify that I would like to be supplied a

photocopy of the relevant sections of the NSW

police report on Federal Hotels and the HKMS

syndicate's applications for the Darling Harbour

Casino licence, presuming it is held on file by the

AFP or another agency.

Also I neglected to mention that I would like you

to transfer my request to the Attorney-General's

Department for any relevant documents they may

hold, as well as the other agencies I mentioned."

General

2. By the time the matter came on for hearing the matters remaining in dispute were certain passages appearing in two documents, referred to as Document 1 and Document 2. Document 1 was a letter from the then (9 September 1987) Commissioner of the Australian Federal Police ("the AFP") to the then Secretary of the Department of the Arts, Sport, the Environment, Tourism and Territories, and paragraphs 5, 6 and 7 thereof were in issue. Document 2 was described as a Ministerial Brief, from the Deputy Commissioner Operations, AFP, on behalf of the then Commissioner, for the Parliamentary Secretary for Justice, Senator M. Tate. Paragraphs 6 and 7 thereof were, save in the most minor respects, in the same terms as paragraphs 5, 6 and 7 of Document 1.

3. The claims of exemption that were relied upon by the respondent were based upon ss.33(1)(a)(iii); 33(1)(b); 33A(1)(b); 40(1)(d); 41(1); 43(1)(c)(i); and 45 of the FOI Act.

4. The documents in question, that is to say Document 1 and Document 2, including the portions thereof claimed to be exempt, were, commendably, provided to the Tribunal as a matter of course. Evidence was given on affidavit (Exhibits 2, 4 and 5) and the deponents thereto attended for cross-examination which appears in the transcript. Significant portions of the evidence were contained in an affidavit (Ex.5) in respect of which a confidentiality order was requested and made, and in cross-examination of deponents from portion of which it was necessary to exclude the applicant. In consequence it is not possible fully to set out the facts given in evidence in relation to the claims of exemption, nor is it possible to set out submissions that were made as to the conclusions to which the Tribunal should come to the extent that they were contained in a confidential submission that was tendered (Ex.11) by Miss Vardanega who appeared on behalf of the respondent. Not for the first time, I propose to proceed by referring to specific questions raised by the applicant and to aspects of principle that are of particular significance in this case, and by then making findings accordingly.

5. I agree with the well established propositions of law contained in an open written submission tendered by Miss Vardanega (Ex.3) that was provided to the applicant. I need however to refer to certain matters relating to the concept of the public interest; to public discussion, or to knowledge through other sources, of the contents of the material claimed to be exempt; and to release by mistake thereof. Before doing so however it is necessary to give some further background to the matter and to refer to the applicant's submissions.

The Answer to Parliament

6. The applicant's submission was that the paragraphs the subject of the claims for exemption "... have to do with the probity of an answer to a question without notice, given by the Minister for Justice, Senator Tate, to the Senate on 14 December 1987." The Minister had been asked: "Was the clearance by the Australian Federal Police (AFP) of Federal Hotels Ltd for the Christmas Island casino licence conditional on Dr Stanley Ho dive(st)ing himself of his interests in Tal Holdings Ltd, Federals' owners? Have the Australian Federal Police ever had reservations about Dr Ho's involvement with Federal Hotels? Given that Dr Ho's shares were still on Tal's register on 18 November, will the Minister recommend new inquiries to ensure that there is no continuing connection between Dr Ho and Federals?" (Hansard, Senate, 14 December 1987, p 2981).

7. The applicant's submission then went on to refer to the Minister's answer, which I now set out. I do so in full, as any attempt to summarise or shorten it could lead to unintended changes of emphasis or even meaning:

"The question asked was in three parts and the

answer to each of them is no. But I think the

Senate deserves a fuller answer. It just so

happens that I have a fairly extensive brief to

fill out those simple 'no' answers to the three

parts of the question. I could do no better than

to read from the brief. On 14 September 1987 the

Federal Police provided me with the following

advice on its investigations into Federal Hotels

Ltd - that is in relation to its proposed

development of a casino on Christmas Island. I

quote directly from the advice:

'On 4 June 1987 the then Minister for

Territories, on behalf of the Commonwealth,

entered into an agreement concerning the

development of a hotel and casino on Christmas

Island. The agreement requires that the

casino licence be issued to a reputable casino

operator, approved in writing by the

Commonwealth. The developer has proposed

Federal Hotels Ltd as the operator and has

sought the Commonwealth's approval.

The Minister for Territories had indicated

that before Federal Hotels' application be

approved, the Commonwealth should ensure that

it does not have undesirable financial

connections. The AFP agreed to undertake this

investigation with Federal Hotels Ltd meeting

the costs.

The inquiry was conducted by a team from the

AFP Bureau of Criminal Intelligence and

included consultation with State and Territory

police forces, licensing authorities which have

had experience with Federal Hotels Ltd and

certain overseas authorities. The

investigation focused not only on the

reputation and character of Federal Hotels Ltd,

but also on companies and individuals who may

have an interest in the company, as well as

directors, major shareholders and senior

employees identified as having a direct

association with the proposed Christmas Island

casino.'

That was on 14 September. The advice goes on to

say:

'On the basis of information available, the

inquiry did not reveal any matter which would

cause the AFP to recommend against the company

being granted a casino licence. Should the

company's application proceed, however, and

persons not currently connected with the

application become directly involved in the

casino establishment, the AFP would need to be

advised so that this advice can be reviewed.'

That is the end of the advice to me on 14

September. On 30 November the Federal Police

provided me with further advice following

allegations in Parliament on 27 October regarding a

director of Federal Hotels Ltd and his association

with persons and companies of alleged ill repute.

The AFP informed me that as a result of these

allegations a number of Federal Hotels' directors

were re-interviewed. Those inquiries did not,

however, alter the findings of the earlier advice

given to me.

To get this matter in chronological perspective

a little further - it may be in the back of the

honourable senator's mind - the New South Wales

police report on Federal Hotels was made in

mid-year. The AFP investigation commenced in

mid-July. I understand that on 14 May 1987 his

fellow directors of Federal Hotels Ltd voted Dr Ho

off the board. At the time of AFP advice of 14

September, Dr Ho had resigned as a director of Tal

Holdings Ltd - the parent company of Federal Hotels

- and of Federal Hotels Ltd although he still held

5.65 per cent of Tal shares. I was advised late

last week that Dr Ho has since sold all his shares

and no longer holds any interest in Tal Holdings or

Federal Hotels."

8. On 16 February 1988 the Minister gave the following answer, which again I must set out in full, to a question inviting his response to allegations that he had misled the Parliament (Hansard, Senate, 16 February 1988, pp 24-25):

"Of course I am aware of the allegations which

have been made, and I would like to take this

opportunity to give some sort of reply to Senator

Jones, and thence to the chamber. Allegations

were made that I had misled the Parliament in

answer to a question from Senator Tambling which I

gave to the Senate on 14 December 1987. I think

honourable senators will already have received a

copy of my media release of 3 February. Before I

continue, I table that release. When I answered

Senator Tambling's questions on 14 December I had

two written briefs, dated 14 September and 30

November, and an oral briefing of 10 December to

draw on. The brief of 14 September contained four

paragraphs, which were the substantial advice to me

concerning Federal Hotels Ltd, suitably to hold a

licence in relation to the Christmas Island casino.

I read those paragraphs to the Senate. Then

followed two paragraphs inviting me to the (sic)

note certain matters concerning Dr Ho's association

with Tal Holdings.

At all stages, and most emphatically by the

Australian Federal Police officer who briefed me

orally on 10 December, I was advised not to release

those paragraphs which the Australian Federal

Police regarded as confidential. I accepted that

advice, as have other Ministers when provided with

certain information by law enforcement agencies.

I therefore refrained from reading those paragraphs

verbatim. Nevertheless, I drew on those

paragraphs, both in my general answers no to

Senator Tambling's questions and specifically to

inform the Senate of Ho's relationship to Tal

Holdings as at 14 September. This relationship

had been superseded by further disposition of his

shares - the subject of the oral briefing on 10

December. I informed the Senate of this sequence.

By the end of the answer, I had given as succinct,

full and up to date account of Dr Ho's position as

was consistent with maintaining the recommended

confidentiality as to certain details in the two

paragraphs.

It was always my intention to give the fullest

possible information to the chamber. I table the

brief given to me on 30 November, as returned by me

to the AFP, with a side notation which indicates I

was anxious to quote as much as possible from the

brief of September 14. It reads:

'Urgent that I have more for Parliamentary

reply: surely most of the memo of 14/9 could be

used.'

It was in response to this notation and the new

situation on the share register which led the AFP

officer, Chief Superintendent Whiddett, to call on

me on 10 December for an oral briefing. Chief

Superintendent Whiddett, in consultation with my

Senior Private Secretary, Daryl Smeaton, composed a

summary of the latter part of the brief of 14

September and advised that I should use those words

as they were consistent with the substance of the

brief and yet did no damage to reputation. I

table that summary. Those words, drafted as a

substitute for those portions of the brief of 14

September I did not read out verbatim, are the

exact words to be found in my reply to Senator

Tambling. That was why I was confident that I had

given Senator Tambling the fullest possible answer

drawing on the written and oral briefs to me and it

was that which I had intended to assert in answer

to his supplementary question.

In so far as my words conveyed the impression

that I had quoted every word from every part of the

briefs of 14 September and 30 November, I regret

that choice of words and apologise to Senator

Tambling and the Senate if that impression was

conveyed. I emphasise that those paragraphs, upon

which I drew but did not directly quote in whole,

do not contain anything which undermines or

contradicts any part of the answer given by me to

Senator Tambling. I most certainly did not

mislead the Senate as to Dr Ho's relationship with

Tal Holdings Ltd and the AFP's assessment of its

significance to the application by Federal Hotels

for the Christmas Island casino licence. I stand

by the answer no to each of Senator Tambling's

questions, which were:

'Was the clearance by the Australian Federal

Police (AFP) of Federal Hotels Ltd for the

Christmas Island casino licence conditional on

Dr Stanley Ho divesting himself of his

interests in Tal Holdings Ltd, Federal's

owners? Have the Australian Federal Police

ever had reservations about Dr Ho's involvement

with Federal Hotels? Given that Dr Ho's

Shares were still on Tal's register on 18

November, will the Minister recommend new

inquiries to ensure that there is no continuing

connections between Dr Ho and Federals?'

It is well known to honourable senators that I

provided the full briefs of 14 September and 30

November to the shadow Attorney-General, Mr Peter

Reith, on a confidential basis, but without

prejudice to Mr Braithwaite's pursuing of such

freedom of information (FOI) rights as he may

possess. I completely reject Mr Reith's assertion

that those paragraphs reveal that I misled the

Senate as to the substance of my answer. It would

be easy, and personally satisfying, to authorise

the release of those two paragraphs. I could

over-rule both the earlier advice of the AFP to me

and also the AFP's own absolutely independent

judgment that these paragraphs not be disclosed in

response to an FOI request. I have been invited

by the media to do so ever since this story broke.

However it would be quite improper for me to

release those paragraphs for my own political

reasons. This is the invidious position I find

myself in, but I rely on this chamber's acceptance

of the long standing practice of Ministers with

responsibility for law enforcement not to publish

allegations which, being without foundation after

investigation, could nevertheless harm the

reputation of an individual who is the subject of

the allegation. I intend to abide by that

long-standing practice."

9. The applicant in his submissions made much of a claim that access to the information in question was necessary in the public interest for "the clearing up of a lingering question about the veracity of Senator Tate's answer to the Senate and subsequent statements". Without referring at this stage to the precise references to the concept of the public interest in the sections of the FOI Act that are here relevant, it seems to me that any public interest in the problem as so stated, that is to say in relation to criticism of the Minister's answer, is one that is in general terms now exhausted in so far as the FOI Act is concerned. The Tribunal should of course be extremely reluctant to make any comment about the course of the proceedings of Parliament, but having as it were been forced into some consideration of the problem it seems appropriate to say that, if there were thought to be any problem about the original answer given on 14 December 1988, such problem lay simply in the possibility of the Senator's having given the impression that, in his own words, he "... had quoted every word from every part of the briefs of 14 September and 30 November". In respect of this the Senator said in the course of his answer set out in paragraph 8 above: "... I regret that choice of words and apologise to Senator Tambling and the Senate if that impression was conveyed". That having been said, it seems to me that, in respect of the allegation made, there is no public interest remaining to be satisfied by further disclosure. The explanation has been given to Parliament and it is for Parliament to consider it, as indeed it did in the course of a censure motion which was debated immediately after the Minister's statement on 16 February 1988. The question of the Minister's answer is accordingly simply not relevant to the question now before me, and I say no more about it. In so far however as any question of public interest relates not to the question of the alleged possible misleading of Parliament by the relevant passages being omitted to be read, but rather to the question of the alleged need for their actual content to be disclosed, that consideration falls to be considered within the normal parameters of an FOI matter. It owes nothing to the rather unusual, and indeed tender, circumstances in which the present case has arisen.

The Public Record

10. The applicant presented a case that a "summary" tabled by the Minister in the Senate on 16 February 1988 of the paragraphs in question had placed significant information on the public record and that the paragraphs, at least to the extent to which their contents overlap the information already thus on the public record, should be released.

11. The applicant also said that he had been provided, in documents released pursuant to an initial FOI request, with the names of two countries claimed to be identified as the sources of information.

12. Again, the applicant referred to the fact that a document making a statement about Dr. Ho had been released by the AFP. The statement may have been made either by the then Department of Territories (as the respondent claimed the relevant passage inferred) or by the AFP, depending upon how it was read, but it had been released and, the applicant said, any "similar remarks" should be released.

13. Finally under this heading, the documents had been released to Mr. Reith, the then shadow Attorney-General, on a confidential basis, and the applicant contended that, contrary to what the respondent argued, this represented a release to the public.

14. On this topic generally see Re Reithmuller and Australian Federal Police (1985) 8 ALN N92 at N93; and Re Hazan and Australian Federal Police (Decision No.2889, 19 September 1986). In my opinion too much should not be made of the fact that the contents of a document are already known publicly or, at least, to an applicant. The force of the claim will depend to some degree on the kind of exemption claimed. For instance, if a claim of exemption is based on the proposition that disclosure would divulge information communicated in confidence by a foreign government to the Government of the Commonwealth, and that claim is found to be ultimately justified as such, it would be nothing to the point that the information were found to have been already disclosed by some disreputable person, or as a result of accident or mistake (see Re Robinson and Department of Foreign Affairs (1986) 11 ALN N48 at N50-51). That would not permit the Commonwealth to breach its trust by making its own disclosure. It should keep the faith which has been reposed in it by the foreign government. Mutatis mutandis, that principle in my opinion applies in the present case in relation to disclosure of information communicated to it by a State police authority. It follows that whatever form the release of information has taken, no ground is displayed in the present case for supporting denial of an exemption otherwise properly based.

15. In saying this I should make it clear that I do not depart from, but rather distinguish, what I said in Re Downie and Department of Territories (1985) 8 ALD 496. There the question of prior partial release had occurred in a context where such release had been effected by the person from whom further access was sought, the respondent to the application for review. In that case it was thought to be not open to maintain a claim of exemption where the partial release that had occurred could represent a distorted presentation of the information in question. At 502 the Tribunal said:

"(21)... Whereas there would in our opinion have

been a case for contending that it would be

contrary to the public interest to disclose any of

the material the subject of the request, the

respondent's release of portions of documents that

form part of a series of documents reflecting a

course of administration may lead to a situation in

which the assessment of the public interest is

coloured by the release that has occurred. ..."

"(22)... In these circumstances there is a

public interest in ensuring that the material

already released does not represent a distorted

selection of the totality of the material on the

issue. ..."

16. When all is said and done, while the information that has been withheld is not susceptible of the description "innocuous", it is nearly so having regard to the various public releases to which the applicant referred in his submission. The point however in relation to the AFP is that where claims of exemption such as those in s.33, s.33A and s.45, and probably also s.40(1)(d), are concerned, it is not the disclosure of the particular information in question that constitutes the vice of such disclosure. It lies rather in the breach of confidential proprieties, a breach which in truth can lead to the cessation of, or diminution in, the future flow of information from the source in question. So to say represents no inconsistency with what the Tribunal has said in cases such as Re Fewster and Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet (1986) 11 ALN N266 and Re Fewster and Department of Prime Minister and Cabinet (No 2) (1987) 13 ALD 139 when it has emphasised the importance of a consideration of the information contained in a document. That emphasis was so placed in cases arising under s.36(1)(b), where the notion of public interest has primacy in the evaluation of the question whether a document is exempt, and is not a mere adjunct of a ground of exemption based primarily on the source, general characteristics, or specific likely effect of disclosure of a document.

17. The question of the confidential release to Mr. Reith should be mentioned. I see that release as an example of the application of s.14 of the FOI Act:

"14. Nothing in this Act is intended to prevent

or discourage Ministers and agencies from

publishing or giving access to documents (including

exempt documents), otherwise than as required by

this Act, where they can properly do so or are

required by law to do so."

This section has perhaps not been sufficiently discussed in the cases. The Administrative Appeals Tribunal does not have power to order that access to a document be granted. Essentially it decides, save where a certificate has been issued, whether a document is exempt. An understanding of the Tribunal's role, and of the operation of s.14, is, for example, needed for an understanding of some aspects of Re Howard and Treasurer of Commonwealth of Australia (1985) 7 ALD 626. There the question for the Tribunal was not whether access to the documents in question should have, as it had in fact, been given to the ACTU. The grant of that access was open under s.14. Nor was the question before the Tribunal whether access should be granted to Mr. Howard. The question was simply whether the claim of exemption should be upheld in respect of Mr. Howard's application. The bottom line in the present case is that access was properly granted to Mr. Reith, he having apparently accepted the limitation that was imposed as a condition of the grant of such access. What happened in this respect has no further impact on the present case.

Other Matters

18. I have taken into account all of the contentions put by the applicant in his written submission (Ex.A), in particular his submissions that

(a) under s.33(1)(a)(iii)

. a mere allegation that disclosure could

reasonably be expected to cause damage to the

international relations of the Commonwealth is

not enough (O'Donovan and Attorney-General's

Department (1985) 8 ALD 528);

. disclosure of the fact that the AFP has lines of

communication throughout the world is not of

itself alone likely to prejudice the

effectiveness of its procedures;

(b) under s.33A(1)(b) that a mere allegation that

disclosure will damage Commonwealth/State relations

is insufficient (Re State of Queensland and

Australian National Parks and Wildlife Service

(Australians for Animals, party joined) (1986) 13

ALD 158.

19. While I have already referred to the problem under the heading of "the Public Record", it is necessary to emphasise in general terms that the consequences for the AFP of its losing the confidence, of police forces in other States of the Commonwealth or in other countries, that information given to the AFP by such a source, and/or the identity of the source itself, will be treated confidentially, are very great. The desire, once aroused, of persons to have access to information contained in documents is often very great, and such persons often find it hard to understand why it could be damaging for information obtained from such sources to be released. The need for the information is seen as paramount. The decision maker however, and on external review this Tribunal, each bear a heavy responsibility to have regard to the serious consequences that might follow should the AFP become an object of suspicion in the eyes of its sources. Should any belief be there formed that the confidence may be broken where the particular information in question seems of minor importance, or where it or much of it has somehow been elsewhere released, then the sources can be expected to live in a state of constant suspicion that an erroneous decision as to release of information obtained from them may be made.

20. It should finally be noted that the question of public interest effectively arises in the present case only in relation to s.33A and s.40, since the reference to the public interest in s.33 does not establish any independent criterion. Once the primary criteria are satisfied, the content of the notion of contrariety to the public interest is automatically satisfied: See Re Mann and Australian Taxation Office (1985) 3 AAR 261 at 273; Re O'Donovan at 533; Re Fewster; Re Fewster (No 2).

Conclusion

21. In summary, having considered the evidence, the documents themselves, and the submissions of the parties, I find as follows:

(a) In relation to paragraph 5 of Document 1 (Ex.6) and the

first three sentences of paragraph 6 of Document 2 (Ex.7):

(i) I uphold the claim of exemption under s.40(1)(d) for

the reason that the relevant disclosure would or

could reasonably be expected to have a substantial

adverse effect on the proper and efficient conduct

of the operations of the AFP having regard to its

functions as specified in s.8 of the Australian

Federal Police Act 1979. I find that in all the

circumstances there is no matter in the portion of

the document in question the disclosure of which

would, on balance, be in the public interest.

(ii) I uphold the claim of exemption under s.45 for the

reason that the relevant disclosure would constitute

a breach of confidence.

(iii)I make no finding in respect of the claim under

s.43(1)(c)(i), it being unnecessary in any event to

make any finding in relation thereto, and there

being in any event doubts in my mind as to the

sufficiency of the evidence in relation to this

claim.

(b)In relation to paragraph 6 of Document 1 and the last

four sentences of paragraph 6 of Document 2:

(i) I uphold the claim of exemption under s.40(1)(d) in

the same terms as under (a)(i) above;

(ii) I uphold the claim of exemption under s.45 as under

(a)(ii) above;

(iii)I make no finding in respect of the claim of

exemption under s.43(1)(c)(i) - see (a)(iv) above.

(c)In relation to paragraph 7 of Document 1 and paragraph 7

of Document 2:

(i) I uphold the claim of exemption under

s.33(1)(a)(iii) for the reason that the relevant

disclosure would be contrary to the public interest

for the reason that it could reasonably be expected

to cause damage to the international relations of

the Commonwealth.

(ii) I uphold the claim of exemption under s.33(1)(b)

for the reason that the relevant disclosure would

be contrary to the public interest for the reason

that it would divulge matter communicated in

confidence on behalf of a foreign government.

(iii) I uphold the claim of exemption under s.33A(1)(b)

for the reason that the relevant disclosure would

divulge matter communicated in confidence by or on

behalf of an authority of a State, being a State

law enforcement agency, to an authority of the

Commonwealth, being the AFP, and that it would not

on balance be in the public interest for such

disclosure to be made.

(iv) I uphold the claim of exemption under s.40(1)(d)in

the same terms as under (a)(i) above.

(v) I uphold the claim of exemption under s.45 for the

reason that the relevant disclosure would

constitute a breach of confidence.

(vi) I make no finding in relation to the claim of

exemption under s.41(1) it being unnecessary to do

so.

(vii) I make no finding in relation to the claim of

exemption under s.43(1)(c)(i) it being unnecessary

to make any finding in relation thereto and there

being in any event doubts in my mind as to the

sufficiency of the evidence in relation to the

claim.

22. In the result the Tribunal will affirm the decision under review.
