CORPORATIONS - INTENTIONS OF CORPORATIONS - RELEVANCE OF DIRECTORS AND SHAREHOLDERS INTENTIONS - RELEVANCE OF DIRECTORS AND SHAREHOLDERS OTHER ACTIVITIES - RELEVANCE OF ACTIVITIES OF OTHER COMPANIES CONTROLLED BY SAME SHAREHOLDERS AND DIRECTORS -principal shareholder of applicant controlled other companies engaged in proximate activities

EVIDENCE - AAT - EVIDENCE OF CORPORATE INTENTIONS - MINUTES OF MEETINGS OF DIRECTORS - EVIDENCE OF TRUSTEE INTENTIONS - TRUST RECORDS - IDENTITY OF CONTROLLING PARTIES - CORROBORATION - NEED FOR CORROBORATION WHERE WITNESS CREDIT IMPUGNED - no documentary evidence of intentions - neither company minutes nor trust records - witness credit impugned - no satisfactory corroboration

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT87/2178 TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re: Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

1 September 1989 Mr B J McMahon (Deputy President)

1. The applicant, as trustee, filed a return of income for the year ended 30 June 1984. In it was disclosed a sale of 5 acres of land in the year of income resulting in a "capital profit" of $402,055-52. The "profit" was arrived at by deducting from the gross sale price of $500,337-48, amounts representing selling commission, legal fees, and the cost of the land, calculated pro rata to the overall holding of 45 acres which the applicant had. It was contended in the return that the amount of $402,055 was a capital profit and was not taxable. This submission was not accepted. The applicant was assessed to tax as if the profit shown in the return was assessable income. An objection to this assessment was disallowed in full. This application is brought to review that objection decision.

  1. If the profit is assessable income, it will be brought to tax pursuant to s 25(1) or the former subsection 26(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 which was applicable at the time of the events in question.
  2. Section 25(1) deals with moneys commonly regarded as income in accordance with prevailing accounting concepts. The relevant provisions of s 26(a) brought to tax "profit arising from the sale by the taxpayer of any property acquired by him for the purpose of profit making by sale, or from the carrying on or carrying out of any profit making undertaking or scheme". The burden of proving that the assessment is excessive lies upon the taxpayer, pursuant to the terms of s190(b) of the Act.
  3. In considering the application of the first limb of s26(a), it is necessary to determine the purpose of acquisition. It is well settled that the relevant purpose is the subjective purpose of the taxpayer and is to be determined at the time of acquisition of the subject property. The applicant is a company. It is therefore necessary to establish the purpose of those who control it. Gibbs CJ said in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Whitfords Beach Pty Ltd [1982] HCA 8; 150 CLR 355 at 370 -

"Counsel for the taxpayer submitted that it was not permissible to blur the distinction between the company and its shareholders. That of course is true, but in deciding whether what was done was an operation of business, it is relevant to consider the purpose with which the taxpayer acted and, since the taxpayer is a company, the purposes of those who control it are its purposes. In Ruhamah Property Co Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (77) the majority of the Court regarded as important, if not decisive, the purposes with which the shareholders and directors of the company acted, although Isaacs J, who dissented, thought it erroneous to consider a company merely as machinery for carrying out individual purposes(78). However, in my opinion Isaacs J took too rigid a view of the effect of Salomon v Salomon & Co (79) if he thought that in determining the purpose with which a company acted it was not permissible to have regard to the intentions of the directors who controlled it. In the present case, the three companies which became the shareholders, or the two which became the managers (it matters not which), represented the directing mind and will of the taxpayer and controlled what it did, and their state of mind was the state of mind of the taxpayer : HL Bolton (Engineering) Co Ltd v TJ Graham & Sons Ltd (80), cited in Tesco Supermarkets Ltd v Nattrass (81); and see Bernard Elsey Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (82)."

5. There is some doubt as to who controlled the applicant de jure at the relevant time, although there is no doubt as to the manner in which that control was exercised de facto. The company is a trustee of a discretionary trust. Apart from the tender of the trust deed, which is silent on the point, there was no evidence as to the beneficiaries of the trust at the relevant time. There was evidence, which will be dealt with in greater detail later, that at the time of the acquisition, the general interests in the venture (however formally arranged) were agreed to be divided as to 31 per cent to Mr R as to 20 per cent to Mr B and as to 49 per cent to a solicitor. The solicitor's share was (according to Mr R and Mr B) to be paid for entirely by Mr R and (they said) the solicitor agreed to pay interest on his proportion of the funds at the rate of 20 per cent per annum until such time as he was able to repay the loan. According to the solicitor, who gave evidence, he undertook no financial obligations whatsoever According to his evidence, he was simply a passive trustee for another discretionary trust in which no specific beneficiaries had been appointed. Provision was made in the deed only for distribution of the trust estate to certain charities in the event of default of appointment. It is possible that beneficiaries had then been appointed, but that the solicitor was unwilling to name them. Accordingly it is not possible to find with any degree of certainty the identity of the beneficial owners of the whole purchase undertaking. Nevertheless, as I have said, in fact it was controlled by Mr R and it is to his evidence that I turn first to establish the relevant facts.

  1. He said in evidence that in 1977 while in St Vincent's Hospital, he was approached by an executive of a failed property development company which owned a large area of land in a northern suburb of Sydney. This development company had planted poplar trees on the property. More will be said of them later. The executive told Mr R that the land was presently under cultivation of these trees but in the future it would be suitable for development and was well located. Mr R eventually told him that he was willing to buy only a small area. He concluded ultimately by buying 2 1/2 acres on which a house was built. That land was purchased by a company HF Pty Ltd, wholly owned by Mr R's family and undoubtedly controlled by him. It still remains in the ownership of that company. It is coloured light green on Exhibit E, a plan of the area.
  2. On a second occasion when the applicant was in hospital, he was visited by the solicitor and Mr B. The solicitor denies that he was acting on behalf of the failed development company, although on occasions Mr R has asserted that this was the case. The solicitor told Mr R at any event that the development company was in financial difficulties and that this was a golden opportunity for him to acquire the land. He reminded Mr R that he already held land in the vicinity and that it would suit him to purchase this land.
  3. Mr R had in addition to the green land, previously acquired land adjoining it and coloured blue in Exhibit E in the name of a company owned as to 50 per cent by Mr R 10 per cent by Mr B and 40 per cent by a local agent. That company (which will be referred to as X) had been endeavouring for some time to obtain re-zoning of its land. It sold land coloured black on Exhibit E to a well known development company at arm's length. That development company had succeeded in obtaining appropriate re-zoning of the black land and had proceeded to build a shopping centre on the land. Mr R said that the success thus achieved in obtaining re-zoning was one of the factors that prompted him to agree eventually to the proposals by the solicitor and Mr B.
  4. It was agreed eventually that the 45 acres of land, which adjoined the other 2 portions to which I have referred, should be acquired in a legal structure to be arranged by the solicitor and should be owned in the proportions to which I have referred. This structure is now the present applicant.

10. The solicitor was instructed to act on the purchase and to arrange for the acquisition of a suitable purchasing vehicle. During the conversations preceding these instructions, it was agreed that the applicant should seek re-zoning of the land. Until this was achieved, profits from the poplar trees would pay for outgoings. The fact that the land was under cultivation would also be useful in minimising land tax.

  1. Notwithstanding the oral agreement alleged by Mr R the solicitor paid neither interest on the value of his 49 per cent share nor any capital in reduction of the cost of this share. He contended that he was under no obligation to pay either. In evidence before this Tribunal, he denied that he had been unable to meet these commitments, as was alleged by Mr R. He agreed that he had surrendered his interest to Mr R but said that this was as a result of a general falling out and an inability on his part to find his share of funds for holding charges and other ongoing demands. There seems to have been no documentation of the passing of the solicitor's interest to Mr R. At any rate none was tendered in evidence. Mr R said however that it occurred some 2 years after the land was originally acquired.
  2. From the time that he became the 80 per cent owner of the venture, he pursued the re-zoning of the land with the utmost vigour. In his view, the land would have been suitable for re-zoning for industrial or residential purposes. Mr B thought that only industrial purposes had been envisaged. The solicitor said he gave no thought to the desirable form of re-zoning, so long as it was ultimately not its then zoning of non-urban, which virtually precluded any development. Mr R had many meetings with the local shire councillors. He engaged town planners and engaged solicitors to make representations to the Department of Planning and Environment on behalf of the group. The 40 per cent shareholder in X, the proprietor of the blue land, who was the agent, worked in conjunction with him in pursuing these submissions. He was unsuccessful in achieving his goal. The land remaining after the sale which gave rise to the subject assessment is still zoned non-urban.
  3. In 1982, the agent was told by a shire councillor that a church had been looking for suitable land in the area. The agent contacted the church and offered them part of the blue land held by X in which he had a 40 per cent interest. Officials from the church inspected the land but indicated that they were not interested. He then told them of the subject adjoining land and told them that if they made an offer he would try to secure the land for them. After the church had made many enquiries, an offer was made which the agent then conveyed to Mr R. Their evidence agrees that there was then a discussion between them concerning the continuing holding charges, relief from which would be effected from the proceeds of sale. Eventually (according to the agent) Mr R said he had conferred with his partners and came back and said that he would accept the offer. The church was willing to pay $100,000 per acre for up to 17 acres. The applicant accepted an offer at this price for 5 acres. An option for a further 7 acres was granted and was exercised in a subsequent year of income. The property was sold on the basis that the church would pursue its own development application. All the parties who gave evidence on the hearing agreed that they took no part in negotiations with council. There was however some equivocal evidence concerning a petition of local residents that was certainly signed by the agent and by Mr R as principals, and may indeed even have been organised by them. The petition was in favour of re-zoning of the proposed church land for the sake of amenities which it would bring to the area.
  4. In cross-examination, Mr R agreed that after the sale of the black land to the development company for the shopping centre, it was sought to eliminate liability for taxation on the profit, whether or not such a profit could have been regarded as a capital profit. The company that had originally acquired the land, X, sold the remainder of the land to another company in identical ownership, Y, and was then manipulated in a way that is commonly known as the bottom-of-the-harbour technique. The exercise was unsuccessful. However Mr R agreed that all the directors of X and Y believed that the profit on the sale of the shopping centre land was taxable and they had been so advised by the solicitor.
  5. Y has since sold a second portion of its land to an arm's length company for development as a retirement village. The company owned entirely by Mr R's family (HF Pty Ltd) applied for a liquor licence in respect of premises to be erected on the green land. His evidence was that the company was successful in obtaining the licence, but was unsuccessful in obtaining re-zoning of the land. The licence was then sold to Coles for use in conjunction with their store in the shopping centre.
  6. The oral evidence of Mr R is in marked contrast, in relation to the poplar trees, to representations made on behalf of the applicant from 3 sources. The return of income was prepared by Mr R's accountant. He has been associated with Mr R's interests since Che early 1940 s and undoubtedly has the confidence of Mr R. The return contains information in Schedule 16 under the heading "sale of property" in these words -

"The property consisted of a poplar tree plantation growing for supply to matchstick manufacturers and was purchased for this purpose. There was also a house on the property which was let most of the time. The property was let to various people from time to time to agist livestock thereon.

The property has been exempt from NSW land tax in recognition of its use for primary production.

In early 1983 the company was approached by the [church] to sell portion of the land to them for use as a [church establishment].

Due to the fact that use of poplar wood in matchstick manufacture was superseded so that the companies were no longer interested in purchasing poplar trees for that purpose and the occurrence of a "blight" of some description which had attacked the poplar trees it was decided to abandon the venture and to sell 5 acres to the [Church] and an option was granted on a further 7 acres."

17. This is a formal statement in a formal communication, a return of income which was signed by Mr R as public officer. It is quite inconsistent with the oral evidence he gave before this Tribunal. He insisted that the purpose of the applicant in acquiring the property was to have it re-zoned and then to hold it as an investment. The poplar trees counted hardly at all in the formulation of this purpose. They were an element only in that they might have provided a source of income for current charges. He gave evidence that at no time has the applicant cultivated the trees. It has not made any significant enquiries as to how they should be managed. It has never directed any person to tend to those trees that are in worse condition than others. It has never arranged for pruning lower branches in order to provide smooth trunks for veneers. Mr R said that when the property was acquired there was a caretaker on the premises. The evidence was however that he took no part in any cultivation work. None of the trees has been felled or sold. The match company failed to respond to a single invitation to inspect them. On the evidence put before me, it is clear that the property was not purchased for the purpose of a poplar tree plantation.

18. Having received an assessment for income tax on the profits from the first sale to the church, Mr R then consulted a well-known tax specialist firm of chartered accountants. A letter written by that firm, on behalf of the applicant, was tendered as Exhibit 2. The relevant parts of that letter are in the following terms -

"The adjustment sheet accompanying the 1984 assessment on the trustee discloses that the profit of $402,055-52 shown in the income tax return as being attributable to the sale of 5 acres in July 1983 was deducted from the net loss shown in the return and treated as being assessable. This calculation depends for its accuracy on the proposition that [the applicant] as trustee purchased the land for resale at a profit. It is submitted that it did not. The land in question was acquired from the Receiver in [failed property developer] following failure of that company in 1977. This company acquired the land for the purpose of growing poplar trees for match making and a recent discussion with one of its former directors has confirmed that it had conducted feasibility studies to achieve that result. The solicitor acting for the Receiver of [failed property developer] approached Mr [R] advising him of the feasibility studies conducted by [failed property developer] and of the possibility of planting and cultivating poplar trees which are fast growing trees suitable for match making. At the time the Receiver was appointed the poplar tree plantation at [location] and another out of Sydney conducted by [failed property developer] were under the supervision of an employee of the company who lived at [location] and after [applicant] as trustee acquired the land in December 1977 this person continued to be employed to maintain the poplar trees.

However, it subsequently transpired that due to the introduction of the more popular disposal lighters matches requiring the use of poplar wood diminished in popularity and, therefore, the opportunity to plant and cultivate and replant was lost. However, the trees at [location] were maintained by the employee until July 1981 when he was dismissed for not carrying out his duties properly although, by this time, it had been ascertained that the cost of removing the poplar trees would be greater than they were worth after removal."

  1. Once again, the information in this letter is quite inconsistent with the evidence given by Mr R. The solicitor denied that he was acting for the receiver of the failed property company and insisted that his involvement was a personal one. Mr R, as I have pointed out, has evinced no interest in planting or cultivating poplar trees. The evidence that he gave is inconsistent with the accountants' statements (presumably on instructions) that the company acquired the land for the purpose of growing poplar trees.
  2. The applicant also consulted a large firm of solicitors and instructed a partner in that firm who has a widespread reputation as an expert in the field of taxation. He also wrote to the respondent on behalf of the applicant. The relevant parts of his letter are as follows -

"Growing upon the land at the time were about 2,000 poplar trees which had been established by the vendor and which were approaching maturity. A director of the vendor [executive] represented to Mr [R] that farming of the poplar trees would yield a profit of about 20% on monies invested. Such a profit would have paid the carrying costs of the land (ie rates and taxes etc) and also would have yielded a profit. There was also a dwelling house erected upon the property which was tenanted and yielding income. Mr [R] had no time to make his own assessment of the commercial viability of the poplar trees but reasoned that even if the poplar tree farm was not successful commercially the land had intrinsic worth as investment land. Mr [R] had acquired through other entities, land in the [location] and the price for that other land was substantially in excess of that being asked by [vendor]. The land was zoned non-urban at the time of the acquisition and therefore the prospects of any development at that time or shortly thereafter were minimal.

After acquisition the person who had supervised the poplar trees for the vendor was engaged by the taxpayer and continued to maintain the trees. Several hundred new trees were also planted. However shortly after the purchase of the land and before the trees had reached full maturity the market for matches made from the wood of poplar trees effectively collapsed. This was as a result of the advent of matches made from cardboard and disposable cigarette lighters. The price of poplar tree wood fell sharply and did not recover during the time of the taxpayer's ownership of the property. The harvesting of the trees growing upon the property no longer became a commercial proposition and effectively the poplar tree growing activities came to a halt.

Due to the failure of the poplar tree growing the taxpayer, through Mr [R], proceeded to examine whether there were alternative uses to which the land could be put."

  1. It clear from the evidence given before me that "several hundred new trees" were not planted. Mr R made what can only be described as a feeble effort to justify this statement by saying that suckers from existing trees must have been counted among the several hundred. The statement that there was not an examination of alternative uses until such time as the poplar market collapsed is also inconsistent with Mr R's present version of-events. He now says that at all times his purpose was to develop and hold the land and that the presence of the poplars was simply a factor in inducing him to acquire the land.
  2. I can not accept, as Mr R suggested, that all 3 advisers were in error and had misunderstood him. There is a certain consistency and coherence of interpretation in the submissions put forward by each of them that can be accounted for only by the instructions of Mr R. It is beyond belief that all 3 experts could have failed to understand him. The conclusion I draw is that he has told one version of his purpose to the respondent, through his expert advisers, and now tells another version to this Tribunal.
  3. The evidence of Mr B was vague except in matters vital to the applicant's case. For example he did not know whether he was a director of the applicant company. He knew that one of his own companies had a 20 per cent interest in the venture. Apart from the discussions that took place at the hospital with the solicitor, he has taken no part in the history of events. He relied entirely on his memory for details of this hospital conversation which took place some 12 years ago. He took no part in the negotiations for re-zoning or (he said) in negotiations for the sale of part of the land to the church. He believed that Mr R had informed him of that sale after the event. To this day, neither Mr B nor the solicitor has seen the land.
  4. Notwithstanding this, Mr B was insistent that the original intention of the joint venturers was to develop the whole of the land as an industrial estate and to hold it as an investment. He agreed that no plans for such a venture had ever been drawn up.

25. The applicant submitted through its counsel that its dominant purpose in acquiring the whole property was not profit making by sale and that the evidence compelled a finding that any use or dealing with the land beyond re-zoning had not been articulated in the minds of Mr R, Mr B or the solicitor. The applicant (it was submitted) was in the same position as that referred to by Mason J (as he then was) in Admin Exploration Pty Ltd (in liquidation) v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 72 ATC 4253 at pages 4259 and 4260 -

"That profit-making by sale was not the sole or dominant purpose for acquisition in a given case may be demonstrated by showing that the dominant purpose actuating the acquisition was another and inconsistent purpose, as for example, retention as an investment. So also the taxpayer may show that, although profit making by sale was a purpose, or a consideration, which actuated the acquisition, it was not the dominant purpose, either because another and inconsistent purpose actuated the acquisition in an equal degree or because before and at the time of acquisition the taxpayer held no firm view as to what he would do with the property and the prospect of its sale at a profit was recognised as no more than a possibility to be later considered in common with other possibilities which at or before the time of acquisition appeared no less remote." [Emphasis added].

  1. The respondent agreed that if the facts demonstrated such a purpose, then the profit would not fall within the terms of the first limb of s26(a). He submitted however that the facts did not support such an interpretation.
  2. In written submissions, the applicant said that "the-evidence does not leave it open to the Tribunal to find that the sole or dominant purpose of the applicant's acquisition of the land was profit-making by sale and that is what the Tribunal has to find for the first limb of s 26(a) to be applicable". In my view, this is not a correct way of stating the issue. The applicant bears the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive. So as to negative support for the assessment under the first limb, it seems to me that the applicant must satisfy this Tribunal that its dominant purpose when acquiring the property was not profit making by sale. Profit making by sale can include profit making by sale after improvement of the value by re-zoning, as much as it can mean profit from sale after improvement by building, as was referred to in Moruben Gardens Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 72 ATC 4147 at 4157. Re-zoning does not diminish the identity of that sold with that acquired.

28. For 7 reasons, I consider that the applicant has failed to discharge the onus cast upon it by the Act. Firstly, I consider that I should take into account actions by associated parties who are related to the principals of the applicant and who hold land in the area. This course of action was approved by Lockhart J in Cole v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 89 ATC 4792. At page 4796 His Honour said -

"In view of the land dealings of Mr Cole over a number of years I do not regard it as irrelevant to take into account his involvement in any land acquisitions or sales by his wife. They are part of the background of the matter and matrix of facts which it was proper for the Tribunal to take into consideration. Indeed, the Tribunal, in stating the matters found in paragraph 15, did so on this very basis. The dealings with the land acquired by Mrs Cole in 1974 were closely related to the dealings of Mr Cole with land acquired by him. I do not regard the Tribunal as having relied upon those matters for any impermissible purpose."

29. The present "matrix of facts" includes the following items -

a) the applicant acquired the land after part of the adjoining land had been re-zoned for use as a shopping centre and was partly motivated in its purchase by this successful endeavour

  1. the conduct of the directors of X and Y (which include the principal director of the applicant) indicates that they feared the profit from the sale of part of their land for use as a shopping centre would be taxable
  1. Y continued its selling activities by selling another portion for a retirement village
  1. Mr R's family company improved the value of its holding in the green land by investing in the acquisition of a liquor licence which it then proceeded to sell
  2. negotiations with the church were initiated by the agent who was a 40 per cent shareholder in Y with Mr R
  3. both Mr R and the agent played a part in the zoning political process through the petition favouring re-zoning of the church land
  4. since the sale which gave rise to the present assessment, the applicant has sold a second parcel of land to the church

This matrix of facts militates against the applicant's argument. Taken together they render less credible a submission that the land was not acquired for resale at a profit.

  1. Secondly, I find as a fact that the applicant's purpose in acquiring the land was not the purpose of growing poplars. All its subsequent actions (except for the instructions given by Mr R to the experts) are inconsistent with such an alleged purpose. For all practical purposes, the poplars have been ignored since acquisition.
  2. Thirdly, it follows that the principal of the applicant has embarked upon an attempt to deceive the respondent. This must impugn his credit. It leaves me in a position where I can not accept his word as to his purpose 12 years ago, unless that word is independently corroborated in some way. There was no written corroboration. The verbal corroboration of Mr B and the solicitor is, for reasons I have outlined, quite unsatisfactory.
  3. My fourth reason relates to the difficulty of establishing the purpose of the applicant because of the unsatisfactory evidence in establishing all the relevant beneficial participants in the enterprise. If as was alleged by 2 of the witnesses, the solicitor beneficially held 49 per cent of the enterprise then his purpose would have been relevant. However his evidence was that he did not hold it beneficially, that his purpose was quite vague, except that the land would one day be "a good investment" and that the persons that he represented and to whom he was accountable were and still are unknown at the date of the acquisition or later.
  4. The fifth reason concerns the stated purposes of Mr R. In the version of events given in this Tribunal, he said that he wished to achieve re-zoning of the land as industrial or residential. A submission to this effect was made by planners on behalf of the applicant in a letter, which forms part of Exhibit I. A suggested plan accompanies that letter and indicates that the consultants contended for a considerable area to be re-zoned as residential, an area to be zoned as open space and the remaining area to be zoned as light industrial. It is not credible to accept that the applicant would have retained ownership of land deliberately re-zoned as open space. It is also, in ray view, quite unlikely that the residential land, as such, would have been developed by the erection of houses that would have remained in the ownership of the applicant, as Mr R would have it. At the very least, any purpose involving a re-zoning in accordance with these plans must also include a purpose of conveying any open space land to an appropriate authority and, more probably than not, of conveying individual residential lots to developers or to end-users.
    1. The sixth matter that I have taken into consideration is the fact that there is no contemporary evidence and no documentary evidence to corroborate the stated purpose of the applicant in 1977. Although the applicant is a company, no minutes of meetings of directors were tendered. The applicant is also a trustee. No records of the trust were put in evidence showing discussions that might have been held at the time and indicating reasons for the investment of trust funds. Mr R said that he started to pursue re-zoning not earlier than 2 years after the applicant acquired the land. It was only when he became an 80 per cent shareholder after a "general falling out" with the solicitor that this intention seems to have been adopted or at least pursued. This delay does not corroborate a stated purpose at the date of acquisition to achieve rezoning and to develop the land as an investment.
    2. The seventh reason for my finding that more probably than not, the applicant has not demonstrated that it did not acquire the land for the purpose of profit making by sale, is the inherent improbability of such a submission. The fact that the applicant has been left with the land and has been unsuccessful in having it re-zoned has no doubt dampened the optimism with which Mr R first approached the project. He must demonstrate, however, that it was not his dominant purpose at that time to acquire the land for improvement by way of re-zoning and for subsequent resale. I find it inherently improbable that the prospects of sale would not have been discussed between the original joint venturers. All gave evidence that they considered the land would be re-zoned one day and that it would be thereby made valuable. Enhanced value is useless unless it is realised in whole or in part. In the absence of any corroboration, I find it impossible to accept that the ultimate purpose was not discussed and that this ultimate purpose was not the immediate purpose.

36. This being so it is not necessary to deal with the second limb of s 26(a). The assessment has been justified by the respondent under the first limb and the applicant, in my view, has not succeeded in showing that the assessment is excessive. Accordingly the objection decision is affirmed.
