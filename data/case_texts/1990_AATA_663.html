AT88/58 and Commissioner of Taxation [1990] AATA 663; (1990) 21 ATR 3691; 90 ATC 580 (14 September 1990)

Last Updated: 3 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL No. AT88/58



TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION



Re: AT88/58

APPLICANT



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



RESPONDENT

AAT Decision No 6225



Before: D F O'Connor J (President)

At: Canberra

On: 14 September 1990



REASONS FOR DECISION



1. This matter was heard before the former President of the Tribunal, the Hon. Justice Trevor Hartigan. A decision however had not been given prior to his death in April this year. The parties have agreed that the matter be determined by me on the transcript.



2. This is an application to the Tribunal under the Australian Capital Territory Stamp Duty Act 1969 (the Stamp Act). It relates to an assessment by the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation who was at the relevant time an administrator of the Act's provisions. The assessment is an assessment of stamp duty on an agreement for the sale of an interest under a Crown lease of land situated in the Australian Capital Territory. A shopping centre is erected upon the land. There existed at all relevant times a management agreement in respect of the shopping centre.



3. The applicant company, A Co. Ltd was the purchaser under an Agreement for Sale (Crown Leasehold) of the interest of B Ltd under a Crown lease and goods for a total purchase price of $51,000,000. The agreement is document T1 of exhibit 1.



4. It appears from special condition 9 of the Agreement for Sale that it was the intention of the parties that the relevant property be sold subject to the management agreement. The management agreement referred to in special condition 9 is defined to mean the Management Agreement made on 22 December 1972 between B Ltd as owner, C Investments Pty Ltd as manager and C Corporation Ltd as guarantor. C Retail Pty Ltd replaced C Investments Ltd as manager pursuant to the provisions of a deed made on 4 November 1985. The management agreement is document T3 of exhibit 1.



5. Counsel for both parties placed before the Tribunal a document entitled "Statement of Agreed Facts". That document became exhibit 2. Its terms are as follows:



"1. If, in determining the 'value of the interest in the land...agreed to be transferred' for the purposes of Column 3, Item 5 of Schedule 1 to the Stamp Duty Act, 1969, it is not permissible to take into account the interest of [C Retail Pty Ltd] pursuant to the Deed of Agreement for Management and Profit Sharing dated 22 December 1972 the value of the interest agreed to be transferred was $71 million. That is to say, the unencumbered value of the Crown Lease was $71 million.



2. If, in determining the 'value of the interest in the land...agreed to be transferred' for the purposes of Column 3, Item 5 of Schedule 1 to the Stamp Duty Act 1969, it is permissible to take into account the interest of [C Retail Pty Limited] pursuant to the Deed of Agreement for Management and Profit Sharing dated 22 December 1972, the value of the interest agreed to be transferred was $51 million. That is to say, the value of the Crown Lease subject to the Management Agreement was $51 million."



6. The respondent, the Commissioner of Taxation, is empowered to administer the Stamp Act by virtue of the Australian Capital Territory Taxation (Administration) Act 1969 (Cth); the Taxation Laws Amendment Act (No. 2) 1987 (Cth) and the Australian Capital Territory Taxation (Administration) Ordinance 1987 (ACT).



7. The Deputy Commissioner of Taxation had adopted a value of $71,000,000 as the value of the property transferred under the agreement for sale. He went on in accordance with the provisions of s.71(1) of the Australian Capital Territory Taxation (Administration) Act 1969 to assess the stamp duty payable as $1,774,515. If the value of the property had been $51,000,000 the stamp duty payable would have been $1,274,515.



8. As the applicant had paid duty of $1,274,515 an amended assessment for $500,000 issued on 10 June 1986. The respondent accepted a proposal that half of the amended assessment be paid pending resolution of this matter. That part payment was made on 17 July 1986.



9. The submissions for the applicant may be stated as follows. The interest of the manager C Retail Pty Ltd in the property is of a proprietary nature which detracts from the value of the seller's (B Ltd's) interest in the property. The seller could not dispose of the lease except on terms which protected the Manager's interest under the management agreement. A Court of Chancery would intervene to restrain the seller from dealing with the leasehold interest in a way which would operate to defeat the manager's interest under the management agreement. The value of the seller's interest in the land which is the subject of the lease must be regarded as diminished by the value of the manager's interest under the management agreement. The manager's interest has a commercial value of $20,000,000. Accordingly, the value of the property should be assessed at $51,000,000.



10. Section 45 of the Australian Capital Territory Taxation (Administration) Act 1969 (Cth) (the Administration Act) contemplates that the duty imposed on the transfer of a Crown lease for a term exceeding 5 years or an agreement for such a transfer is payable by the transferee. Section 45 provides:



"45. (1) Duty imposed on a transfer of an estate in fee simple in land or of a Crown lease for a term exceeding five years, or an agreement for such a transfer, is payable by the transferee or the person to whom the estate in fee or the lease is to be transferred under the agreement, as the case may be.



(2) Duty imposed on a lease is payable by the lessee.



(3) Duty imposed on a transfer or assignment, or agreement for a transfer or assignment, of a lease other than a Crown lease for a term exceeding five years is payable by the transferee or assignee, or the person to whom the lease is to be transferred or assigned under the agreement, as the case may be."



11. Section 4 of the Stamp Act provides:



"4. Subject to this Act, stamp duty is imposed on the instruments included in the classes of instruments specified in Schedule 1."



12. Section 5 of the Stamp Act provides:



"5. The amount of stamp duty on an instrument included in a class of instruments specified in column 2 of Schedule 1 is the amount set out in, or calculated in the manner set out in, column 3 of that Schedule opposite to the reference to that class of instruments."



13. At the time of execution of the agreement for sale (28 February 1986), Item 5 of Schedule 1 of the Stamp Act provided that the duty payable on a "Transfer, or an agreement for a transfer, of a Crown lease for a term exceeding 5 years of land situated in the Territory" was:



"(f) $2.50 for every $100, and for any fractional part of $100 of the part of the value of the interest in the land transferred or agreed to be transferred that exceeds $250,000."



14. In order to examine what interest, if any, the manager may have had in the Crown lease it is necessary first to examine the history of this matter. On 21 May 1971 a Deed of Agreement (exhibit 1 document T2) was entered into between B Limited, C Development Pty Ltd (as developer), C Investments Pty Limited (as Manager) and C Corporation Ltd (as guarantor). Put shortly, that deed contemplated that B Ltd would advance certain monies known as "project costs" to the developer to enable it to build a shopping centre on land the subject of a Crown lease in the Australian Capital Territory. The developer had acquired the land as a result of an agreement with the Commonwealth. Once the development was complete the developer was then to assign the benefits and rights under the Crown lease to B Ltd for a consideration equal to the project costs. On acquiring the leasehold interest B Ltd was to enter into the management agreement with the manager, C Investments Pty Ltd.



15. This subsequently occurred and on 22 December 1972 a Deed of Agreement for Management and Profit Sharing (the management agreement) was entered into between B Ltd (as owner), C Investments Pty Limited (as manager) and C Corporation Limited (as guarantor).



16. Part 1 of the management agreement sets out "Definitions and Interpretations". Clause 1.1 defines the "Principal Deed" in terms which acknowledge that the management agreement has been entered into as a consequence of the deed of agreement described above. Part 2 of the management agreement provides for the appointment of a manager. By clause 2.1 the owner appointed the manager and the manager agreed to serve the owner as manager of the land and building as defined in Part 1 (i.e. the shopping centre in the Australian Capital Territory) for a period commencing on 19 September 1972 and expiring on 31 March 2020 or the termination of the Crown lease, whichever being the earlier. Clause 2.2 then conferred wide powers on the manager to do whatever was necessary to manage the shopping centre. Part 3 of the management agreement sets out the duties of the manager in respect to lettings. Those duties were to let at best rents (clause 3.1), that the owner's consent was required in relation to certain lettings (clause 3.2) and that the manager was not to "assign, transfer or otherwise deal with its rights or interests" arising under the management agreement, save to a subsidiary of the guarantor (clause 3.3).



17. Part 4 of the management agreement sets out the duties of the manager in relation to the management of the building. Those duties were to effect general insurance (clause 4.1(a)), to effect public risk insurance (clause 4.1(b)), to effect loss of rents and consequential loss insurance (clause 4.1(c)), not to act in a way that would cause the insurance on the building to be rendered void or voidable or whereby the rate of premium on such insurance would be increased (clause 4.1(d)), to pay rates and taxes (clause 4.1(e)), to make repairs (clause 4.1(f)), to yield up the building in good repair on the expiration or early termination of the management agreement (clause 4.1(g)), to effect maintenance contracts (clause 4.1(h)), to paint the building regularly (clause 4.1(i)), not to alter the building without the consent of the owner (clause 4.1(j)), to ensure that the tenants and other occupiers observe and perform their obligations (clause 4.1(k)), to take proper steps to keep the building free of rodents and other pests (clause 4.1(1)), to allow the owner to enter from time to time in order to view the state of repair (clause 4.1(m)), to carry out requirements of public authorities (clause 4.1(n)), not to permit undue noise (clause 4.1(0)), to pay water, gas, electricity and other service charges (clause 4.1(p)), not to allow the building to be used except for the purpose for which it was constructed (clause 4.1(q)), to serve on the owner copies of all notices received in relation to the shopping centre (clause 4.1(r)), and to observe the conditions of the Crown lease (clause 4.1(s)).



18. Part 5 of the management agreement sets out the obligations of the owner. They included allowing the manager to manage the shopping centre free from interruption (clause 5.1(a)), to take all necessary action to enable the manager to comply with the manager's obligations (clause 5.1(b)), and not to remove certain fixtures (clause 5.1(d)). Importantly clause 5.1(c) provided:



"The Owner will not transfer mortgage charge or otherwise deal with or dispose of the Land or the Building in such a way as may terminate or prejudice the Manager's rights hereunder for the then unexpired period of this Agreement. PROVIDED THAT nothing in this sub-clause shall be deemed to prevent or prohibit the Owner from so dealing with the Land or the Building if the Owner secures continuance or procures a substitution of this Agreement for the Manager for such unexpired period on terms no less favourable than those of this Agreement."



19. Part 6 of the management agreement sets out provisions in relation to a sinking fund. Part 7 of the management agreement concerns accounts and remuneration of the owner and manager. Clause 7.1 sets out a regime for the distribution of "gross rents" as defined in clause 7.8(b). They were to be applied in the following manner. First, in payment of "management expenses" as defined by clause 7.1(d). Second, in payment to the owner of a "basic return" calculated at 8 1/2% of the "project costs" as defined in clause 7.1(d). Third, in payment to the owner of 50% of "management profits" as defined in clause 7.8(c), namely, gross rents less the basic return and all management expenses. Fourth, retention of the balance of management profits by the manager as remuneration for its services. The remaining sub-clauses of Part 7 deal with various contingencies relating to the amount of gross rents received and rights and duties of the owner and manager in relation to Part 7.



20. Clause 7.1 is set out in full:



"All gross rents of the Land and the Building whether received by the Manager or the Owner in any accounting year shall be brought into account and applied in order of priority as follows:



(a) Firstly, in payment of all management expenses (as hereinafter defined);



(b) Secondly, in payment to the Owner of a return (herein called 'the Basic Return') calculated from day to day at the rate of eight and one half per centum (8 1/2%) per annum on the Project Costs (increased as hereinafter provided relating to a Stage of the Project for which a Certificate of Practical Completion has issued (hereinafter called 'a completed Stage' whether incurred before or after the commencement of this Agreement as agreed between the Owner and the Manager or failing agreement as certified by the Owner's auditors (whose certificate shall be final and conclusive), the basic return to be paid by monthly instalments on the first day of each month during the term of this Agreement (except the first and last payment which if necessary shall be proportionate), the first payment to be made on the first day of the month next following the date of this Agreement. For the purpose only of assessing the Basic Return, the Project Costs relating to a completed Stage shall be increased by an amount equal to the return which the Owner would have received, allowing for credits pursuant to Clause 6.2(a) of the Principal Agreement, had the Owner invested such part of the Project Costs at the times and in the amounts of the actual expenditure of such part of the Project Costs and received a return on such investment at the rate of seven per centum (7%) per annum simple on the daily balances until the date of issue of the Certificate of Practical Completion relating to that Stage;



(c) Thirdly, in payment to the Owner within three calendar months after the expiration of each accounting year of fifty per centum (50%) of management profits (as hereinafter defined);



(d) Fourthly, by retention of the balance of management profits by the Manager as remuneration for its services as such."



21. Part 8 of the management agreement deals with termination. Of relevance is clause 8.4 which provides that termination is to be:



"without prejudice to all other rights powers and obligations of the parties subsisting at the time of such termination other than in respect of the continuation of this Agreement."



22. Part 9 of the management agreement deals with general matters. Clause 9.3 provides that:



"Nothing herein contained shall be construed to create or confer a lease or tenancy of any kind or any estate or interest upon or in favour of the Manager in respect of the Land and Building and the Owner and the Manager hereby expressly negative any creation of any such leasehold or tenancy rights and declare that other than as herein contained upon the termination of this Agreement the Manager shall cease to have any right in respect to the said Land and Building."



23. Mr Emmett QC for the applicant submitted that clause 9.3 was:



"Acknowledging in effect that so long as this agreement is in force the owner recognises that the manager has a right in relation to the land and building". (Transcript at p.21).



That seems, at least at first sight, to be drawing a long bow as to the effect of clause 9.3.



24. Finally, Part 10 of the management agreement deals with the guarantee. It does not need examination in any further detail.



25. Mr Emmett QC also made reference to a Deed of Arrangement for Management and Profit Sharing entered into on 4 November, 1985 between B Limited as owner, C Retail Pty Limited as manager and C Corporation Ltd as guarantor. As noted above, under that deed C Retail Pty Ltd replaced C Investments Pty Ltd as the manager of the shopping centre. With some minor amendments to the management agreement the various parties to the Deed of 14 November, 1985 adopted all the rights, duties and obligations that existed under the management agreement.



26. It is useful to refer to two other documents that Mr Sullivan for the respondent tendered. First, a Deed of Novation entered into on 28 February 1986 between B Ltd, A Co. Ltd, C Retail Pty Ltd, and C Corporation Ltd. By that deed it was agreed that B Ltd was released from its obligations under the management agreement. It was further agreed that A Co. Ltd would be bound as owner and C Retail Pty Ltd would be bound as manager under the management agreement. That deed also provided that the deed of novation was interdependent with the agreement for sale of 28 February 1986. A Co Ltd covenanted with C Retail Pty Ltd to perform the management agreement and to be bound by its terms in every way as if A Co Ltd were a party to the management agreement in lieu of B Ltd. C Retail Pty Ltd accepted the liability of A Co Ltd upon the management agreement in lieu of that of B Ltd and agreed with A Co. Ltd to be bound by the terms of the management agreement in every way as if A Co. Ltd were named as a party to the management agreement in place of B Ltd. As between B Ltd, C Retail Pty Ltd and C Corporation certain releases were also given which need not be considered.



27. The second document that Mr Sullivan tendered was a Deed of Termination entered into on 28 February 1986 between A Co. Ltd, C Retail Pty Limited, and C Corporation Limited. That deed made reference to the agreement for sale of 28 February 1986, the management agreement, the deed of 4 November 1985 and the deed of novation. Recitals F and G of the deed of termination provided that:



"F. As a condition of it completing its purchase of [the shopping centre A Co. Ltd] requires:



a) the Management Agreement to be terminated;



b) [C Retail Pty Ltd to withdraw the caveat lodged against the title to [the shopping centre] to protect [C Retail Pty Ltd's] interests under the Management Agreement; and



c) [C Retail Pty Ltd] to covenant with [A Co. Ltd] in the manner set forth in clause 4 of this Deed.



G. [C Retail Pty Ltd] has agreed to meet [A Co. Ltd's] requirements as described in Recital F on condition that [A Co. Ltd] pays [C Retail Pty Ltd] a Management Agreement termination payment of $20 million dollars as hereinafter provided."



28. Clause 1 of the deed of termination set out the interdependent nature of that deed. By clause 2(a) of the deed the method by which the $20,000,000 was to be paid was set out. By clause 2(b) the effect of making the payment was to terminate the management agreement "with prospective effect from the date of completion of the Sale Agreement". That sub-clause then provided that A Co. Ltd was deemed to have been released from its obligations under the management agreement and that A Co. Ltd was deemed to have released C Retail Pty Ltd and C Corporation Ltd from their respective obligations under the management agreement. Clause 3 was a saving clause so far as rights which arose prior to the date of termination were concerned. Clause 4(a) set out numerous definitions. By clause 4(b) C Retail Pty Ltd undertook not to enter into any sort of arrangement by which it would compete with A Co. Ltd's interests in the shopping centre. In the event of a breach of that undertaking C Retail Pty Ltd would pay pre-estimated liquidated damages to A Co. Ltd. The remaining parts of clause 4 then regulated the rights of the parties in the event of such a breach of undertaking by C Retail Pty Ltd. The other clauses of the deed of termination regulated the rights of the parties in so far as the entry and execution of the deed of termination was concerned.



29. Other relevant material was tendered at the hearing. First, a handwritten note in the Commissioner's file (Exhibit 5). It was produced by the respondent but tendered by the applicant. That note sets out various figures. It says "Contract $51,000,000", "$20,000,000 Management Contract" and "$71 M - TOTAL VALUE FOR GPT [sic] to acquire total interest in site". It then sets out some particulars about the management. Mr Sullivan for the respondent, when objecting to the note being tendered, indicated that it was not created in the respondent's office but rather was created by an unknown officer in the Australian Valuations Office from whom advice had been sought by the respondent at the time when the assessment was made (see transcript at p.6). The agreed facts however do not give rise to any problem in relation to evidence of value. A further handwritten note headed "Note for file" was tendered by the respondent (exhibit 8). It is a document from the file of the Australian Valuations Office 18, 21 and 23 April 1986. It basically records a conversation between its author and an employee of A Co. Ltd relating to the value of the land. Finally, Mr Sullivan tendered documents which constituted a title search of the relevant land (exhibit 9).



30. The case for the applicant was that by virtue of the management agreement, the manager (C Retail Pty Ltd) had acquired a proprietary interest in the land the subject of the Crown lease. That interest was the right to receive half of the net profits after the events contemplated by clause 7.1 of the management agreement had occurred. It was further argued that a Court of Chancery would intervene to restrain the owner (B Ltd) from dealing with its leasehold interest in any way which would operate to defeat C Retail Pty Ltd's interest under the management agreement. As a result the value of B Ltd's interest in the land the subject of the lease was diminished by the value of C Retail Pty Ltd's interest under the management agreement. As noted earlier, it was common ground between the parties that if the interest of the manager was to be taken into account then the value of the interest in the land agreed to transferred by the agreement for sale was only $51,000,000.



31. Counsel for the respondent submitted:



l) B Ltd was the registered proprietor of the Crown lease and was the only person who had any legal or equitable interest in that lease save for any other registered interests;



2) C Retail Pty Ltd did not have any registered interest in the Crown lease nor did have any legal or equitable interest;



3) Consequently A Co. Ltd purchased the entirety of the Crown lease from B Ltd;



4) The unencumbered value of the Crown lease was $71,000,000;



5) The rights of C Retail Pty Ltd under the management agreement were mere personal contractual rights and were not capable in any way of reducing the value of B Ltd's interest in the Crown lease;



6) Even if C Retail Pty Ltd did have some sort of proprietary interest in the Crown lease the interest that B Ltd retained in the Crown lease remained the totality of the Crown lease and the value of that was the full unencumbered value of $71,000,000.(See Transcript at p.32.)



32. Counsel for the respondent saw the initial issue also as being whether the nature of the interest of C Retail Pty Ltd in the Crown lease was merely contractual or was in some way proprietary. The Management agreement did not give rise to any proprietary interest in the land. Here the fundamental right conferred on the manager was the right to manage with the right to enter upon the property being purely ancillary to that right.



33. Counsel for the respondent submitted that on its true construction the management agreement appointed C Retail Pty Ltd to serve B Ltd as manager (clauses 2.1 and 5.1(b)(i)) and that C Retail Pty Ltd was merely B Ltd's agent (clause 2.2). Counsel then further submitted that clause 7.1 merely confers an entitlement to some surplus of the rent after certain calculations and the management agreement does not confer a right to share in the rents alone (clause 7.2). That right is only a contractual right and would not support a caveat as it is not a proprietary right (cf. Shepherd v Houston [1927] SASR 144; section 104 of the Real Property Ordinance 1925 (ACT); and Woodman & Nettle, The Torrens System in New South Wales (at p.524/21). At most, Counsel for the respondent submits, the management agreement confers an interest in a fund of money and not in the property itself. Finally, counsel submits that the management agreement itself acknowledges that it was not intended to create in C Retail Pty Ltd any interest in the land (clauses 5.1(c) and 9.3).



34. Whether or not something in the nature of a proprietary interest was created in C Retail Pty Ltd's favour depends on the proper construction of the management agreement (see Isaacs J. in Commissioner of Stamp Duties v Yeend [1929] HCA 39; (1929) 43 CLR 235 at 246; Codelfa Construction Pty Ltd v State Rail Authority of NSW [1982] HCA 24; (1982) 149 CLR 337).



35. Meagher, Gummow and Lehane in Equity: Doctrines and Remedies (2nd edition, p.98) say:



"As a starting point, any system of proprietary interests may usefully be valued by reference to at least four criteria. These are: (a) the power to recover the property, the subject of the interest or the income thereof (ie a 'property right') compared with the recovery of compensation from the defendant payable from no specific fund, (b) the power to transfer the benefit of the interest to another; (c) the persistence of remedies in respect of the interest against third parties who thus assume the burden thereof; and (d) the extent to which the interest may be displaced in favour of competing dealings by the grantor or others with interests in the subject matter (i.e. priorities). These characteristics are present in varying degrees in the hierarchy of equitable estates and interests.... It is incorrect to assume that unless all these characteristics are present there cannot be 'property'."



36. In asserting the proprietary nature of the applicant's interest, counsel relied principally on Cowell v Rosehill Racecourse Co. Limited [1937] HCA 17; (1936) 56 CLR 605. Counsel referred to passages in that case which analysed the circumstances in which a contractual licence may be coupled with a proprietary interest and the extent to which such a licence was therefore not revocable. Counsel for the applicant also made an ancillary submission that if in fact the interest transferred was the interest of B Ltd subject to the management agreement then the agreement for sale itself simply did not fall within the head of charge (Item 5 of Schedule 1 of the Stamp Act) at all because the subject matter of the agreement for sale was something less than the full leasehold. Counsel did not press that submission at any length.



37. So far as the first submission of Counsel is concerned, Megarry & Wade in The Law of Real Property (5th Edition) list three main varieties of licence (at p.799). The first is a bare licence which is the simplest form of licence and is not supported by any contract. They give as an example the gratuitous permission to enter a house or to cross a field. It can be revoked at any time provided there is no covenant not to revoke it. Second, a contractual licence is a licence that is granted under the terms of a contract which often restricts the licensor's right to revoke it. Megarry & Wade note that it "is licences of this class which have grown from mere contracts into a new kind of interest in land, at least in certain situations" (at p.800). Such contractual licences are capable of binding successors in title as equitable interests. While Megarry and Wade note that in this area "the law is now very confused and difficult to fit in with established equitable principles" they state that the courts appear to be well on their way to "creating a new and highly versatile interest in land which will rescue many informal and unbusinesslike transactions, particularly within families, from the penalties of disregarding legal forms". The third type of licence is a licence coupled with a recognised interest in property. An example is the right to enter another's land to hunt and take away the deer killed. It involves two things, a licence to enter the land and the grant of an interest (a profit a prendre) in the deer. As Dixon J. (as he then was) said in Cowell, supra, at p.630:



"The licence to go upon the land is incident to the grant and depends upon it. But the purpose must be to take away something which is upon the land or forms part of the soil or otherwise deal with an ascertainable subject of property".



38. Counsel for the applicant argued that the management agreement granted to C Retail Pty Ltd a right to share in the rents and profits from the building. A licence to enter the building was conferred by various other provisions of the management agreement to enable the manager to gain the benefit of that right. Counsel submitted that this right to share in the rents and profits was a proprietary right and was the same thing as taking something out of the land or soil. It therefore belonged to the third category listed above.



39. The following passage in Cowell v Rosehill Racecourse Co. Ltd., supra, were relied upon by the applicant (at 615 per Sir John Latham):



"If a man creates a proprietary right in another and gives him a licence to go upon certain land in order that he may use or enjoy that right, the grantor cannot divest the grantee of his proprietary right and revest it in the grantor, or simply determine it, by breaking the agreement under which the licence was given."



40. Counsel categorised the right given to the manager in this case as being something akin to a profit a prendre. He submitted that Latham CJ used this very concept as an example of the right to which he was referring. Starke J. said (at 626):



"Grants of easements, or of profits a prendre or the right of taking natural produce or profits from the land of others, are well-known instances of licences coupled with grants or interests in realty, and are irrevocable."



41. Megarry and Wade (5th edition at p.850) define a profit as "a right to take something off another person's land". The thing taken must be part of the land (see Manning v Wasdale [1836] EngR 1065; (1836) 5 Ad. & E 758) and the thing taken must, at the time it is taken, be susceptible of ownership (see Lowe v J.W. Ashmore Ltd [1971] Ch. 545). Mason J. in Australian Softwood Forests Pty Ltd v Attorney-General (NSW) ex rel Corporate Affairs Commission [1981] HCA 49; (1981) 148 CLR 121 at 130 put forward a similar definition and after considering the provisions of the documents in that case concluded that the right under consideration was something in the nature of a profit, even if not one in the strict sense of the word - perhaps a licence coupled with an interest.



42. Megarry and Wade identify four types of profits:



1) a profit appurtenant - this is a profit which, by the acts of the parties, is annexed to some nearby dominant tenement and runs with it;



2) a profit appendant - this is a profit annexed to land by operation of law - it probably only exists in the form of a common of pasture;



3) a profit pur cause de vicinage - this only exists in the form of a common of pasture. If two adjoining commons are open to each other, there is a common pur cause de vicinage if the cattle put on one common by the commoners have always been allowed to stray to the other common and vice versa;



4) a profit in gross - this is a profit exercised by the owner independently of his ownership of land; there is no dominant tenement. The authors say such a profit is an interest in land which will pass under a will or intestacy or can be sold or dealt with in "any of the usual ways".



43. It is difficult to imagine a basis for placing the manager's interest into the fourth of the above categories let alone any of the others. In Frank Warr and Co Ltd v London County Council [1904] 1 KB 713 at 720 Collins MR said:



".......but I think the plaintiff's counsel failed to support the main proposition which they put forward in their argument, namely, that a licence to make a profit on land by trading upon it stood on the same footing for this purpose as a profit a prendre. In the case of a profit a prendre the profit arises out of the land itself, as in the case of the right to take animals ferae naturae on the land, and that is the reason why a contract with reference to such a right has been held to come within the 4th section of the Statute of Frauds. There is no authority to shew that a licence to carry on a trade on land can be regarded for this purpose as analogous to a profit aprendre."



44. The Tribunal does not accept that a right akin to a profit a prendre was created under this management agreement.



45. Counsel for the respondent submitted that the owner was the registered proprietor of the Crown lease and was the only person who had any legal or equitable interest in the lease apart from the sub-lessees. The manager had no registered interest in the lease so the applicant purchased the entirety of the lease from the owner. The rights of the manager under the management agreement were merely personal contractual rights.



46. The right to enter the property was purely ancillary to the main right conferred by the agreement, which was the right to manage. Counsel referred to Commissioner of Stamp Duties v Yeend (supra) where at pp.244-245 Isaacs J. said:



"The crucial question then is: Is that subject matter 'property' within the meaning of the Act? The definition is very wide, since it 'includes real and personal property, and any estate or interest in any property real or personal, and any debt, and any thing in action, and any other right or interest'. From the fact that 'property' is the matter defined, that the last word is 'interest', and from a careful inspection of the terms of the Second Schedule under the heading 'Conveyances of any Property', and with the additional light given by the use of the word 'right' in sec.71, it is clear to me that the word 'right' - which is the word relied on by the Commissioner in this case - must be confined to a right in the nature of property as ordinarily understood...The test in every such case must be whether the 'right' which is either 'transferred to' or 'vested in' or 'accrues to' the alleged taxpayer, is a personal right or a property right. It is not, in my opinion, material whether it is created by the agreement or not. That is not the standard. Sec.71 confirms that view. The standard is the inherent nature of the right that is the immediate subject matter of the agreement. That subject may in a given case pre-exist or it may not, the only essentials are that it is 'property' and that it is tranferred to, vested in or accrues to some person."



47. Clause 2.1 of the management agreement showed that the agreement was one for the provision of services; clause 2.2 empowered the manager to act as agent for the owner. Clause 7.1 only shows how the rents are to be dealt with by the agent and clause 7.2 shows that the manager's entitlement is only to a certain amount which is left over and if there is none left over then his entitlement ceases. Reference was made to Shepherd v Houston [1927] SASR 144, where a man agreed to allow the respondent an equal third share of the "net profits" made on the resale of the land. The land was sold and the buyers gave a mortgage to the appellant which purported to be a loan but was really part of the purchase money which the appellant was supposed to hold on trust. It was held that the most the appellant had was a claim under contract law.



48. In Woodman and Nettle, The Torrens System in NSW (at p.524/20) a number of examples are given where it has been held that the interest under consideration did not support a caveat and the right to profits from the sale of land is listed as an example.



49. Clause 9.3 makes it clear that no intention existed to create a property interest for the manager. So far as the agreement for sale was concerned it says the sale takes place subject to the management agreement. This intention was, and indeed could only be reflected through the deed of novation. In the absence of the deed the manager's rights would have been terminated by the sale.



50. The management agreement is a document which sets out the nature of the relationship that was to exist between several large commercial bodies. The parties were faced with the management of a large commercial property. The agreement ensured that the manager had a great deal of autonomy and was remunerated accordingly. Such remuneration was calculated according to the terms of clause 7 of the management agreement. Inevitably in order to complete the managing of the property and to guarantee the autonomy of C Retail Pty Ltd it was given wide rights of entry and management. However, that autonomy was not such as to give to the manager what could be called a "proprietary interest" in the land and building. Rather, it was to guarantee the commercial success of the venture.



51. Properly construed, therefore, the management agreement did not create a proprietary interest in C Retail Pty Ltd's favour. Further, that seems to have been the intention of the parties when one considers the effect of clauses 5.1(c) and 9.3 of the management agreement.



52. Accordingly, I find that when determining the value of the interest in the land and building the subject of the agreement for sale the rights of the C Retail Pty Ltd should be disregarded and the value so determined was $71,000,000.



53. The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.
