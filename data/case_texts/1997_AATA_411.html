Anthony Joseph and the Commissioner of Superannuation [1997] AATA 411 (23 October 1997)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

ANTHONY JOSEPH v. THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V97/538

AAT No.

12333

Number of pages - 18

Superannuation



COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

J DWYER (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme - preservation of superannuation rights - Election to preserve benefits more than 21 days after resigning - discretion to recognise late election - insufficient information given to applicant regarding options available - belief that preservation of rights had occurred - whether in the interests of justice that time be extended - weigh benefit to applicant against prejudice to respondent - consideration of Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme Board approved guidelines

S 137 (1), 157 (1)Comcare v A'Hearn [1993] FCA 498; (1993) 119 ALR 85

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 11 September 1997 (hearing), 23 October 1997 (decision)

23:10:1997

Appearances

Solicitor for the Applicant: Mr G Butler, Union Representative

Solicitor for the Respondent: Miss T Munoz, Advocate of ComSuper

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and remits the matter to the Commissioner for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the election made by Mr Joseph on 27 October 1993 be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by s 137(1) of the Act.

DECISION

J DWYER

1. This is an application for review of a reviewable decision made on 16 June 1994 not to direct under ss 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") that the election made by Mr Joseph on 27 October 1993 be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by s 137(1). That decision was confirmed on 22 April 1997.

2. Mr G Butler, a union representative, appeared for Mr Joseph. Miss T Munoz, an advocate with Commonwealth Superannuation Administration ("ComSuper"), appeared for the respondent. Mr Joseph gave evidence. Evidence on his behalf was also given by Mr Farr who formerly worked with the Department of Finance in the Retirement Benefits and Commonwealth Superannuation Branches. The Tribunal received into evidence the documents ("the T documents") lodged pursuant to s 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 and the other exhibits tendered during the hearing.

3. The relevant legislative provisions are as follows:

"137. (1) A person who:

(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which paragraph (b) does not apply and, upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or

(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtues of which the person is to be deemed, under subsection 58 (3), to have retired involuntarily;

may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner, that this Division apply in relation to the person.

157. (1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognized, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly.

4. Mr Joseph who was born on 7 April 1950 became a member of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme established under the Superannuation Act 1922 on 13 February 1974. He worked with the Commonwealth in the Department Community Services in the Commonwealth Rehabilitation Service from 1973 until 1987 when he resigned in order to take up a similar position with the Victorian Transport Accident Commission. On 11 October 1993 Mr Joseph rejoined his former department which was then known as the Department of Health, Housing, Local Government and Community Services. On 27 October 1993 Mr Joseph applied to have a late election to preserve his Commonwealth Superannuation benefits (T20/68) treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by s 137(1) of the Act.

5. On 17 March 1987 Mr Joseph wrote a letter resigning from the Department of Community Services with effect from 3 April 1987. His letter of resignation advised:

"With regards to my Commonwealth Superannuation fund I would like to "rollover" my benefit into ANZ ADF fund. I enclose an application to be forwarded to the Superannuation Board."

6. The T documents do not contain a copy of the application to the Superannuation Board which Mr Joseph enclosed with his letter of resignation. However they do contain a form S2A entitled "Application For Refund Of Accumulated Contributions/Lump Sum Benefit Or Election For Preservation Of Superannuation Rights" completed by Mr Joseph on 30 March 1987 and received by the Australian Government Retirement Benefits Office ("AGRBO") on 21 April 1987. That form states:

"I ... hereby

(i) apply for a refund of accumulated contributions)

(ii) elect for a payment of a lump sum benefit) under the provisions of the

(iii) elect for the preservation of superannuation rights. )Superannuation Act.

Note an election as in (ii) or (iii) is binding and cannot be revoked unless there are exceptional circumstances."

Mr Joseph struck out options (i) and (ii) and elected option (iii) the preservation of superannuation rights under the Act.

7. The T documents include (at T9/31) the letter from Personnel Services, Department of Community Services forwarding the form S2A completed by Mr Joseph to the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner"). That letter shows that the Personnel Services officer handling the matter was confused about the options available to a person who was resigning from Commonwealth employment. The letter states:

"Please find enclosed completed form S2A for the abovenamed officer who has resigned at the close of business on 3 April 1987.

...

It would be appreciated if calculation of his refund could be commenced immediately." (emphasis added)

8. There is an inconsistency between the form S2A completed by Mr Joseph in which he indicated that he wished to preserve his superannuation rights under the Act, and the letter forwarding that form to the Commissioner, in which the Personnel Services officer asked that the calculation of Mr Joseph's refund be commenced immediately. That inconsistency does not seem to have alerted any officer within AGRBO to the fact that the Personnel Services staff of the Department of Community Services did not appear to understand the operation of preservation rights under the Act. The fact that the staff of the Personnel Services Division did not themselves understand the effect of an election to preserve rights, could be seen as an indication that it was possible that Mr Joseph might also be under a misunderstanding as to the difference between the preservation of his superannuation rights under the Act and a refund of his accumulated contributions.

9. As Mr Joseph in his form S2A had elected to preserve his superannuation rights he was forwarded a letter from the Commissioner dated 29 May 1987, which provided him with information about the benefits to which he was entitled as a result of his election for preservation of superannuation rights (T14 p52). A number of enclosures were sent with that letter, but they did not include copies of calculations as to Mr Joseph's entitlements in certain circumstances which had been made before the letter was sent. They are included in the T documents (at T13 pages 42-51).

10. The letter to Mr Joseph dated 29 May 1987 stated at paragraph 3:

"3. In order for this Office to determine the benefit to which you will be entitled (i.e. Transfer Value, Deferred Benefit or a refund of accumulated contributions) it would be appreciated if you could complete the enclosed form entitled "Preservation of Superannuation Rights - Notice". This form should be completed at a date three months after ceasing to be an eligible employee and returned to this Office not later than 24 July 1987.

Please note that an election for preservation of superannuation rights will be ineffective, and a refund of accumulated contributions will result, if this form is not returned to this Office by the due date."

11. Mr Joseph returned that form without completing it, together with a letter dated 20 July 1987 (T15/61) which read as follows:

"In reference to my superannuation, I would now like to transfer my gross accumulated contribution into a "rolled-over" State Supersave ADF (State Bank of Victoria).

Please provide a "statement of Termination notice and the completion of the "Roll Over Payment Notification" form."

On 30 July 1987 that letter was annotated within AGRBO "Failed to comply with Section 137(3) of the Superannuation Act 1976. Election invalid". After 30 July 1987 Mr Joseph was treated as if he had revoked the election for preservation of superannuation rights which he had made in the form S2A, and had instead decided to apply for a refund of accumulated contributions.

12. Mr Butler sought to rely on the fact that the form S2A had stated that an election under option (iii) was binding, and could not be revoked except in exceptional circumstances. He claimed that this was ignored within AGRBO. Mr Butler seemed to be asking the Tribunal to regard that action by AGRBO as wrong and as a factor in favour of allowing the late election. There is no merit in that argument. Section 137(3) provides:

"(3) Subject to sub-section (4) an election under this section is of no effect unless the person who made the election gives notice in writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, after the expiration of the period that is the prescribed period in relation to him-:

(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of the prescribed period and, if so, the name and address of his employer; and

(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the prescribed period a member of the superannuation scheme applicable in relation to that employment and, if so, specifying the scheme concerned." (emphasis added)

As Mr Joseph did not give the Commissioner the required notice in writing his election was "of no effect".

13. As stated in paragraph 4 of these reasons Mr Joseph returned to Commonwealth employment on 11 October 1993 when he again commenced work with the Commonwealth Rehabilitation Service. On 27 October 1993 he wrote requesting approval of a late election to preserve his Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme benefits (T20 p68). With that letter he enclosed documents showing that the whole amount of his accumulated contributions which he had received as a refund in 1987 had been deposited in State Bank Approved Deposit Funds throughout the time he was not in Commonwealth employment. The evidence is that he was in public employment with the Transport Accident Commission for the whole period when he was not in Commonwealth employment.

14. On 21 June 1994 Mr Joseph was advised that a decision had been made not to accept his late election to preserve his superannuation rights. On 7 July 1994 he sought a reconsideration of that decision and on 22 April 1997 Mr Joseph was advised that a delegate of the Commissioner had confirmed on reconsideration the delegate's decision not to recognise his late election. That is the decision under review in these proceedings.

15. Mr Joseph gave evidence. He said that when he decided to leave the Commonwealth Public Service in order to take up a position with the Victorian Transport Accident Commission he asked somebody in Personnel Services at the Commonwealth Department of Community Services about his superannuation. The person to whom he spoke did not have much knowledge about superannuation and told him to ring AGRBO in Canberra. Mr Joseph said he telephoned and told the woman to whom he spoke that he wanted to keep his superannuation money until retirement. She suggested that he could preserve it or roll it over into an Approved Deposit Fund ("ADF") such as the ANZ ADF. The only information she gave Mr Joseph to help him choose between the two options was that the interest in an ADF would be better.

16. Mr Joseph said that he was not told that he would be preserving his pension entitlements if he preserved his superannuation rights under the Act. Nor was he told about the benefits of retaining the employer component of his superannuation, by preserving his rights and keeping his contributions within the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme. Mr Joseph said that when he wrote his letter of 17 March 1987 he did not understand what he would be giving up if he "rolled over" his refund into an ADF fund instead of preserving his rights under the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme.

17. Mr Joseph said that he did not have any knowledge about ADF's and that it was the person from AGRBO who suggested to him that he roll the money over into the ANZ ADF, as he wrote in his letter of 17 March 1987. He pointed out that he had always banked with the State Bank and in his letter of 17 March 1987 he had referred to the ANZ ADF simply because that had been suggested to him by the person from AGRBO.

18. In his letter of 7 July 1994 seeking a reconsideration of the decision not to accept a late election Mr Joseph wrote (T27 p87):

"my sole aim was to preserve my benefit until retirement."

He said in evidence that was why he selected option (iii) the preservation of superannuation rights when he filled in the Form S2A on 30 March 1987.

19. Ms Munoz placed reliance, as did the decision-makers within Comsuper, on the letter dated 29 May 1987 sent to Mr Joseph by the Commissioner (T14 p52). Mr Joseph said in evidence that he did not fully understand that letter but that when it arrived he rang the person to whom he had previously spoken in AGRBO and asked about the form he had to complete. She said something like, "don't worry about it you don't need to complete the form because you have already decided to roll your refund over." He then wrote his letter of 20 July 1987 (T15/61) advising that he wished to transfer his gross accumulated contribution into a "rolled-over" State Supersave ADF, changing the chosen rollover fund to the State Bank in order to keep all his banking with his preferred bank.

20. It seems that the person to whom Mr Joseph spoke on the telephone did not realise that he had elected to preserve his superannuation rights on the Form S2A. If she had realised that he had selected option (iii) she would probably have given him different advice, or at least would have realised that he did not understand the difference between preserving his rights and obtaining a refund of contributions.

21. Mr Joseph's evidence as to the lack of information he was given about the difference between a preservation of superannuation rights and a refund of contributions, and as to his own confusion on the issue was supported by the evidence of Mr Farr. The Tribunal received in evidence a brief statement from Mr Farr and he gave evidence. In his statement he wrote:

"The preservation arrangements of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme are particularly complex. Even now, some 21 years after the CSS was established, the benefit of preserving entitlements is not generally understood, as is evidenced in the numbers of resigning members who do not exercise this option."

22. In his evidence Mr Farr explained that he had been in Commonwealth employment from 1963 to 1997 and had spent most of that time in work which involved superannuation issues. Since 1982 he had been with the Department of Finance in the Retirement Benefits and Commonwealth Superannuation branches. He had been concerned with policy development and costing relating to the Commonwealth Superannuation. He is now a consultant on Commonwealth superannuation. Mr Farr said that two to three years before Mr Joseph's resignation in 1987 changes had been introduced to encourage the use of rollover funds and reduce reliance on old age pension. He said that at that time there was little understanding of the changes. He said that since 1972 there had been complex preservation rights under the Superannuation Act 1922, but they were very poorly understood. Mr Farr said that unless it was clearly explained people did not understand the difference between preservation of rights and preservation of money. Mr Farr said that given the general low level of understanding of preservation rights within the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme he thought there could well have been confusion on the part of the person to whom Mr Joseph spoke, as well as on Mr Joseph's part. He said there were manuals but the level of understanding was quite low and there were gaps in training, even within ComSuper. Mr Farr was of the opinion that contributors did not understood much about their preservation options. He commented:

"It is not until one approaches cessation [of employment] that one becomes interested in what options are available."

23. Mr Farr said that in 1987 no pamphlet had been available pre-1976 to contributors detailing their preservation rights. The Tribunal received in evidence a number of explanatory pamphlets. One was entitled "New Contributors - General information for new contributors since 1 July 1976" (Ex A6). It provided the following information:

"RESIGNATION BENEFITS

15. Contributors who resign before minimum retiring age become entitled to a refund of accumulated contributions. They also have the alternative of electing to preserve their superannuation rights. These could be a transfer value to be paid to some other eligible scheme, or a deferred benefit in the Commonwealth Scheme. Both the transfer value and the deferred benefit include an employer financed element."

That paragraph is not very informative as to the nature of a "deferred benefit," but it is more information than was made available to those like Mr Joseph who were contributors before 1976. Although Mr Farr gave evidence that the same provisions applied to contributors who joined the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme prior to 1 July 1976 that information was not contained in any of the pamphlets received in evidence.

24. Mr Farr said that the number of contributors who resign and elect the preservation of rights option is still quite low. The gist of his evidence was that unless one requires the refund of contributions for a specific purpose, a contributor's best option would usually be to preserve rights under the Act. He said that because of the general lack of understanding of the preservation option:

"Most people simply take their own contributions and forfeit the employer contributions."

Mr Farr acknowledged that in some cases that might be because the person wants to use the lump sum refund of contributions, but he said it was his opinion that many people did not realise the benefits of the deferred benefit provisions and failed to preserve their rights because of a lack of understanding of those rights. He said that it is not clearly explained that if rights under the Act are preserved:

"at the end of the day final benefits are calculated on employer contributions of 2 times the employee contributions".

25. Mr Farr has expertise in the field. We accept his evidence which we found very helpful. We also accept the evidence of Mr Joseph. We find that the person to whom Mr Joseph spoke in AGRBO did not explain to him the real difference between preserving his rights under the Act and obtaining a refund of contributions. We do not need to make any finding as to whether she was herself not clear of the difference, or whether she had the knowledge but did not succeed in conveying it to Mr Joseph over the telephone. As Mr Farr said preservation rights are complex and are often not clearly explained.

26. The Tribunal finds that Mr Joseph did not understand that by requesting a transfer of his "gross accumulated contribution" into a "roll-over" State Supersave ADF he was losing the benefit of the Commonwealth contributions in respect of his 13 years of eligible service. Nor did he understand that if he had adhered to his choice of option (iii) and preserved his rights under the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme he would on retirement have been entitled to a Commonwealth funded pension in respect of his 13 years of Commonwealth Service, as well as to pension or a lump sum representing his own contributions.

27. Our finding that Mr Joseph did not understand about the difference between a refund of contributions and preserving his rights under the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme is shown by his correspondence with ComSuper:

(i) First he wrote on 17 March 1987 that he wanted to "rollover" his benefit;

(ii) Then he elected on 30 March 1987 to preserve his superannuation rights;

(iii) Finally he wrote on 20 July 1987 that he wanted to transfer his contribution into a "rolled over" State Supersave ADF.

28. We also find that Mr Joseph was influenced by the advice given to him by the person to whom he spoke when he telephoned AGRBO the first time. That finding is supported by the fact that in his first letter Mr Joseph nominated an ANZ deposit fund, although he had no commitment to the ANZ Bank. As his letter of 20 July 1987 shows, he preferred to bank with the State Bank. A file note signed by S de Bear on 6 September 1995 (Ex A8 p17) is consistent with Mr Joseph's evidence that he was advised that a rollover fund would give him higher interest than a preservation of rights. The file note states:

"As preservation was a specialised subject in the office few people in the office would have felt confident about giving advice and nearly all calls were directed to his section. There were, however, some members of the scheme who were unwilling to leave their funds in a scheme which discouraged withdrawal of those funds (either in full or in part) prior to the retirement event: in this case a person might well have been advised to place their funds in a Roll-over fund in order to maintain accessibility to their funds - should they preserve and then withdraw before the retirement event they will only receive a refund of their contribution plus interest. In this scenario, the ADF may well have given a higher benefit in terms of higher interest which in the long term may compensate for the loss of the employer component, especially if the (then) high interest rates were maintained."

29. We find that Mr Joseph would not have been unwilling to have left his funds in the Commonwealth scheme, but maybe the person to whom he spoke did not understand that. We find that Mr Joseph at all times wanted to preserve his superannuation rights and that so far as he understood, the roll-over of his refund of accumulated contributions into an ADF was the best means of preserving his superannuation rights. We accept Mr Joseph's evidence that he did not understand that by doing so he would lose the possibility of maintaining the benefit of employer contributions in respect of his 13 years of Commonwealth service, whether or not he returned to Commonwealth employment. We find that it was only when Mr Joseph did return to Commonwealth employment and made enquiries about returning the proceeds of his ADF to the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund that he learnt that he could not do so because he had taken a refund of accumulated contributions rather than preserve his rights under the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme. We find also that Mr Joseph did not use any of the funds and never had any interest in having access to his money prior to retirement. As T49/119 shows, the total amount of his refund of contribution was kept in State Bank ADF's throughout the whole time he was not in Commonwealth Employment.

30. The Tribunal recognises the difficulty facing the Commissioner in trying to explain the complex provisions of the Act by letter. Although the letter of 29 May 1987 from the Commissioner to Mr Joseph was no doubt carefully drawn to try to summarise the legislation, it does not give a clear picture of the comparative benefits and disadvantages of the three different courses set out at the top of the letter. Because it is a standard form letter and does not meet the particular circumstances of Mr Joseph, it contains a lot of detail which was irrelevant to him. He had already advised that he was going to work for the Transport Accident Commission. As there was no "eligible superannuation scheme" for employees of the Transport Accident Commission it would have been more helpful to Mr Joseph if the letter had advised him that option (i) was not available to him for that reason. The letter could also have advised Mr Joseph that as he could not transfer the employer contributions to an eligible superannuation scheme he would lose the benefit of the $45,000 employer contribution unless he left his contributions within the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme. Another important piece of advice for Mr Joseph would have been that if he did preserve his rights under the Act and later returned to Commonwealth employment he would be able to resume contributions and add to his then current 13 years of service, so as to have his ultimate retirement pension calculated on the basis of his full period of Commonwealth contributing service and on his final salary.

31. The most important information which Mr Joseph needed in order to decide whether he wanted to preserve his superannuation rights or to take a refund of his own contributions was probably a concise statement of the Age and Invalidity Pension deferred benefits to which he would become entitled if he elected to preserve his rights. We find that had Mr Joseph understood the effect of the two options which were available to him, he would have elected to preserve his superannuation rights under the Act. We find that Mr Joseph did make telephone calls seeking an explanation of the options available to him. We find that the telephone conversations did not give him that knowledge. It would also have been of assistance to Mr Joseph if he had been told prior to making his election that if he opted for deferred benefits he would have become entitled to a pension on retirement at age 60, even if he never returned to Commonwealth employment.

32. Both Mr Butler and Ms Munoz relied on three Federal Court decisions, Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 33 ALD 420 a decision of the Full Court, Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 33 ALD 569 and the more recent decision of Commissioner for Superannuation v Adams (Federal Court, 18 August 1992, unreported). They also referred the Tribunal to guidelines as to the exercise of the discretion to treat a late election as if made within the period allowed in s 137(1) of the Act.

33. Mr Chalk left his employment with the Postmaster General's Department, which later became Telecom, because of the ill-health of his daughter. At first he had taken leave without pay, but when he received a letter advising him that he would have to continue his superannuation contributions if he remained on leave without pay, he decided to resign from his Commonwealth employment. He did not want to pay the contributions because he had purchased a farm after he gave up work with Telecom. When the farm proved unsuccessful Mr Chalk rejoined Telecom. It was not until three years after he rejoined that he became aware of the right to make a late election. A delegate of the Commissioner refused to exercise the s 157 discretion favourably to Mr Chalk. It was claimed on behalf of Mr Chalk that he had not been advised of his rights to elect to preserve his superannuation right when he left Telecom in 1976, and if he had been so advised, he would have made the election to preserve in 1976.

34. Mr Chalk's application to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal was unsuccessful. The Tribunal accepted his evidence that he was not advised of his right to leave his moneys in the scheme in 1976, but it rejected his evidence that if he had been advised of that right he would have elected to retain his funds in the scheme. The Tribunal came to that conclusion because Mr Chalk had not made any enquiries about continuing in the scheme shortly prior to his retirement and because he had used the superannuation refund towards the purchase of the farm.

35. Mr Chalk's appeal to the Full Court of the Federal Court was dismissed. Davies J, with whom Black CJ agreed, considered Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd and Others v Minister for Home Affairs and Environment (1984) 58 ALR 305 where Wilcox J set out the principles on which time for the performance of an act may be extended. Davies J stated that it is important that a view be formed as to whether it is in the interests of justice that time be extended. He explained that in the exercise of the discretion to extend time in provisions such as s 157 of the Act, it may be more important to consider the consequences of extending or refusing to extend time, than to debate the reasons why the act was not done in time. He said at p425:

"Section 157 merely requires that the Commissioner "be satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised". "All the circumstance[s]" include all relevant circumstances existing at the time the discretion is to be exercised."

His Honour then went on at p426:

"In the application of s.157, it may be of little consequence that the employee did not enquire as to his or her right to elect under s.137 or even that the employee positively decided not to elect. Section 157 confers a discretion which operates in either such case. Frequently, the discretion is exercised favourably because the facts which the employee took into account, when he or she decided not to elect, subsequently turned out differently. Oftentimes, the discretion is favourably exercised when the employee has encountered health problems of which he or she was unaware at the time of retirement. One of the objects of s.157 is to alleviate the consequences of such a change in circumstances. The circumstances need not be of a medical character. In Mr Chalk's case, the change in circumstance was that he resigned from Telecom intending to establish himself in an entirely new career as a farmer but was unsuccessful in doing so. And so he again took up employment with Telecom, after a lapse of two years.

The Superannuation Scheme operates in the context of Commonwealth employment. The rights which the Act confers are statutory rights. But the entitlement to participate in the Scheme and to receive the benefits of the Scheme do not merely have a statutory base. They form an incident of the employment. Thus, a decision-maker exercising the s.157 discretion could think it fair to the employer, to the employee, and to the Scheme that the benefit should be calculated by reference to the totality of the employee's service and to the totality of his contributions, including any sums which must be refunded." (emphasis added)

36. Davies J explained how s 55(1) of the Act takes into account the employee's years of contributing service in calculating the benefit to which an employee is entitled. He said:

"Mr Chalk has in fact served approximately 35 years going back to 1957. If his service between 1957 and 1976 were not recognised, the employer could have an undue gain. The late return of the contributions may not be a matter of concern. In his reasons for decision, the Commissioner's delegate, Mr R.C. Whithear, who has had long experience in these matters, explained:-

"If a late election is allowed, Mr Chalk will have had the use of his refunded contributions (or, at least, the net amount if any tax was paid on them) from 1976, and the Superannuation Fund will have been deprived of their use; and to that extent there is an element of advantage to him and of disadvantage to the fund in such a course. On the other hand, he will not have the advantage of receiving interest on his contributions over the period during which they were not in the fund."

Thus, because the moneys which would have to be repaid under s.138(11)(b) would not commence to accrue interest until repaid, it may be that no unfair advantage would result from a late election."

Unfortunately for Mr Chalk, as those matters had not been raised in the original hearing nor in the amended notice of appeal, the Court decided that they provided no ground for allowing the appeal.

37. In Chalk, Cooper J agreed with the reasons for decision of Davies J but added some short additional reasons. He commented at p428:

"The conduct of the Commissioner is only relevant to the exercise of the discretion to treat the election as within time under section 157 if it helps to explain why the appellant did or did not make a timely decision in a particular way if the conduct of the appellant is itself a relevant consideration. In the instant case the Tribunal found that the appellant did not know of the choices open to him under the Act and that neither Telecom nor the Commissioner advised him of the available choices. The Tribunal correctly treated as a fact relevant to its deliberations that the appellant was ignorant of the choices available and that ignorance was partly explicable by the failure of Telecom and the Commissioner to correctly inform the appellant of the available choices. However, section 157 does not require that the Commissioner determine whether there exists attributable fault on the part of the appellant or any other person giving rise to the need to extend time; nor that the discretion be exercised on the basis of the existence or absence of attributable fault. Rather, as explained by Davies J in his reasons, the section merely requires that the Commissioner "be satisfied that in all the circumstances it is desirable that the election should be recognised". Those circumstances are all the relevant circumstances existing at the time the exercise of the discretion falls for consideration and will include circumstances of the type identified by Davies J as relevant to the statutory purpose for which the discretionary power in section 157 to treat an election as within time was enacted."

38. Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman concerned a similar fact situation to Chalk. Mr Boardman had also left his public service employment, because of a child's health problem. Mr Boardman gave evidence that although he was asked to sign the same form as Mr Joseph, which contained three options as to what he wished to do in respect of his superannuation entitlements, he was told that the only option available to him was to apply for a refund of accumulated contributions. Mr Boardman gave evidence that his father had been a Commonwealth Public Servant and that he knew of the good benefits available to him on retirement, and thus would have elected to preserve his entitlement had he known that it was an option open to him. The Tribunal found that preservation of superannuation benefits on resignation was virtually unknown among personnel officers in the Adelaide headquarters of the Department of Transport in 1979.

39. Von Doussa J, stated that the discretion in s 157(1) is unconfined. He quoted from Gummow J's observations in Bond Corporation Holdings Limited v Australian Broadcasting Tribunal [1988] FCA 433; (1988) 84 ALR 669 at 680:

"Where a statute confers a discretion which is in its terms unconfined, the factors that may be taken into account in the exercise of the discretion are similarly unconfined, except insofar as there may be found in the subject matter, scope and purpose of the statute some implied limitation on the factors to which the decision-maker may legitimately have regard."

His Honour then explained that the purpose of the Act, as its long title indicates, is to make provision for and in relation to an occupational superannuation scheme for eligible persons. He continued at p576:

"The benefits payable under that scheme reflect, among many considerations, the length of service of, and contributions by, the eligible employee. On the one hand an election recognised under s.137(1) will extend to a person who has periods of broken service, benefits which fully reflect the total period of service as an eligible employee. That would be in accordance with the general policy of the Act. On the other hand the orderly administration of the superannuation scheme requires that time limits be applied, generally speaking, so that decisions which impinge on investment and accounting procedures are made soon after an event such as resignation occurs. Matters of this kind set the context within which the discretion under s.157(1) is conferred, and the context in which the discretion must be exercised, but in the abstract they do not elucidate the meaning of "desirable" or dictate criteria by which the Commissioner is to be "satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised"."

Von Doussa J held that the word "desirable" in the context of s 157(1) means "fair in order to do justice between the person who seeks to make the late election and the requirements of the administration of the fund".

40. In Boardman Von Doussa J acknowledged that there is a distinction between a time limit under rules governing the time within which action should be brought, and s 157(1) of the Act, but he suggested that the distinction would be unlikely to make any practical difference. He agreed that it is appropriate to have regard to the brief period of 21 days prescribed in s 137(1) for making an election, and the period of extension requested.

41. In Commissioner for Superannuation v Adams Finn J emphasised the importance of deciding the issue in accordance with the principles explained by the Full Court in Chalk, and applied in Boardman so as to take into account all the material relevant to "the breadth of the discretion conferred by s 157(1)". His Honour also endorsed the Tribunal's suggestion that guidelines were needed to provide guidance as to the exercise of the discretion having regard to the effect of Chalk and Boardman and the breadth of the discretion conferred by s 157(1). Finn J stated that a resumption of Commonwealth employment is an additional consideration beyond the fact of resignation itself, that will be relevant to the exercise of the discretion under s 157(1). He held that the reasons why, and circumstances in which an employee does not elect the preservation option within the prescribed time, are also relevant.

42. In Hunter Valley, Wilcox J pointed out that the applicant bears the onus of rebutting the prima-facie rule that the relevant action should be taken within a prescribed period. The Full Court in Comcare v A'Hearn [1993] FCA 498; (1993) 119 ALR 85 has explained that it is no precondition for success that an acceptable explanation for delay must be given. The important issue is as Wilcox J said "whether it is fair and equitable in the circumstances to extend the time". In order to decide what is fair and equitable it is relevant to consider the benefit to Mr Joseph of the late election and any prejudice to the respondent which may result from the delay.

43. Applying the test laid down in Chalk and Boardman we must decide what is desirable in order to do justice between Mr Joseph and the requirements of the administration of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme Fund. To do so we must weigh the need of Mr Joseph for an extension of time against any prejudice to the Fund if the extension is granted. What is desirable will be what is fair in the interests of justice. Van Doussa J said in Boardman at p577:

"... in the end, whether the recognition of a late election is "something worthy of achievement" must depend on what is fair in order to do justice between the person who seeks to make the late election and the requirements of the administration of the Fund."

(i) benefit to Mr Joseph

44. Mr Farr explained that the impact for Mr Joseph will be quite significant. He said that Mr Joseph lost the benefit of $45,000 of employer contributions which would have accumulated interest which would have been available to pay him pension on retirement. He said that the cost currently to replace those lost contributions would be about $90,000. To allow Mr Joseph to make a late election would benefit him in a manner envisaged by the Act. It would enable him to make better provision for his superannuation. That is particularly relevant because he is now again a person employed by the Commonwealth. If he is allowed to make a late election he can add further years of contributory service to improve his superannuation benefits.

45. In both Chalk and Boardman the Federal Court made the point that to grant an employee the benefit of years of service actually performed is consistent with the policy and objects of the Act. As set out in paragraphs 35 and 36 of these reasons, Davies J said in Chalk (at p426):

"... a decision-maker exercising the s.157 discretion could think it fair to the employer, to the employee, and to the Scheme that the benefit should be calculated by reference to the totality of the employee's service and to the totality of his contributions, ..."

...

"Mr Chalk has in fact served approximately 35 years going back to 1957. If his service between 1957 and 1976 were not recognised, the employer could have an undue gain."

Von Doussa J said in Boardman (at p576):

"The benefits payable under that Scheme reflect, among many considerations, the length of service of, and contributions by, the eligible employee. On the one hand an election recognised under s.137(1) will extend to a person who has periods of broken service, benefits which fully reflect the total period of service as an eligible employee. That would be in accordance with the general policy of the Act."

(ii) prejudice to the respondent

46. Mr Farr gave evidence on this point. He said:

"If Mr Joseph is successful the impact on the CSS would be quite clearly negligible."

The Tribunal accepts Mr Farr's evidence that the prejudice to the fund if Mr Joseph's service is recognised will be negligible. We note that evidence is consistent with what was said by Davies J of the evidence as to prejudice in Chalk (at p426-427):

"The late return of the contributions may not be a matter of concern. In his reasons for decision, the Commissioner's delegate, Mr R.C. Whithear, who has had long experience in these matters, explained:-

"If a late election is allowed, Mr Chalk will have had the use of his refunded contributions (or, at least, the net amount if any tax was paid on them) from 1976, and the Superannuation Fund will have been deprived of their use; and to that extent there is an element of advantage to him and of disadvantage to the fund in such a course. On the other hand, he will not have the advantage of receiving interest on his contributions over the period during which they were not in the fund."

Thus, because the moneys which would have to be repaid under s.138(11)(b) would not commence to accrue interest until repaid, it may be that no unfair advantage would result from a late election."

47. Similarly in Boardman Von Doussa J described as dangerous the argument that the prejudice to the respondent and the public interest is the risk of encouraging other people to make applications to have late elections recognised. He said at p581:

"There is in the argument a risk of "double counting" the administrative disruption to the orderly running of the fund caused by late elections - first in requiring reasons why the prima facie time limit should be extended, and then in adding as a separate hurdle the risk of administrative disruption from too many applications. The time limit has its purpose, but the Act recognises that there will be deserving cases where fairness to the applicant requires that the time limit be relaxed. In such cases it is the policy of the Act, and the legal right of the applicant, to a favourable exercise of discretion under s.157(1). If the proper application of s.157(1) to individual cases results in many people obtaining recognition of late elections, so be it, that is the law."

48. We find that the significant benefit to Mr Joseph of having his late election recognised is more weighty than the negligible prejudice to the fund of allowing the late election.

(iii) reasons for the failure to make [or adhere to] the election within the time prescribed

49. Although the Full Court held in Comcare v A'Hearn that it is no precondition for success that an acceptable explanation for the delay must be given, the reasons for the delay in making an election are relevant. Finn J in Adams held that the reasons why and circumstances in which the employee failed to make the election within the prescribed time are relevant.

50. This matter is distinguishable from all the other late election cases in that Mr Joseph did in fact make an election to preserve his rights under the Act. The question is not why did he not make the election in the prescribed period, but why did he not continue with the election he had made and complete the "Preservation of Superannuation Rights - Notice" (T15 p62) sent to him with the Commissioner's letter of 29 May 1987, (T14). By failing to complete that form Mr Joseph gave AGRBO the impression that he had changed his mind and his election became invalid.

51. Mr Joseph said that he did not ever change his mind because he did not understand the difference between preservation of rights and rolling over his accumulated contributions to an ADF. We accept his evidence and find that had he realised the consequences of seeking a refund of his contributions to deposit them in the State Supersave ADF he would not have sought a refund.

52. We have already found it was only because he did not understand the difference between a preservation of rights and a refund of accumulated contributions that Mr Joseph wrote his letter of 20 July 1987 (T15/61) instead of completing the Notice sent with the Commissioner's letter of 29 May 1987 and thus maintaining his election for preservation of rights. We have set out in paragraphs 25-29 our findings and the reasons for those findings. Mr Joseph's evidence is also supported by the fact that there is no reason suggested as to why he would have chosen the option which was less advantageous to him than the preservation of his superannuation rights, if he had understood the difference. The evidence of Mr Joseph was that he at all times intended to preserve his superannuation rights as fully as he could and that he was surprised to learn that he could not return his refunded contributions plus accumulated interest to the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme when he returned to Commonwealth employment in October 1993.

53. There is no evidence to suggest a different finding. Mr Joseph did not use any of the refunded contributions to purchase a home or set up a new business or for any similar purpose. He left the refund in State Bank ADF's. Thus this matter is distinguishable from Re Singleton and Commissioner for Superannuation (1993) 32 ALD 347 on which Ms Munoz relied. In that matter Mr Singleton used the money to extend his house, to render it more suitable for the church ministry for which he had left his Commonwealth employment.

54. It is relevant that Mr Joseph made application for a late election as soon as he learnt that he could not rejoin the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme (and transfer his State Superannuation and ADF proceeds to that scheme) on rejoining Commonwealth employment. He applied to make a late election on 27 October 1993 two weeks after returning to Commonwealth employment. Those facts support his evidence that he did not know that he had not preserved his rights under the Act until he tried to rejoin the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme.

55. The Tribunal accepts that there was a reasonable explanation for Mr Joseph not pursuing his decision to make the appropriate election in 1987. We find that he did not realise that he was opting for a different option because he did not understand the distinction between the two options. We find:

(1) That Mr Joseph was at all times anxious to preserve his superannuation fund so as to provide for his retirement.

(2) That he never used any of the contributions refunded to him for any purpose other than to keep it as a fund to provide for his retirement.

(3) That although Mr Joseph was told that one option available to him was to elect to preserve his superannuation rights he did not understand what that meant.

(4) That when, having elected to preserve his rights, Mr Joseph wrote and asked for a refund of his accumulated contributions to be paid to the State Supersave fund, he did not realise that in doing so he was choosing an option different to preserving his rights under the Act.

(5) Nor did he realise that he was depriving himself of the benefit of his 13 years of contributory service as a member of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme and that if he should return to Commonwealth employment he would not be able to use those 13 years towards the 20 years of contributory service which, because he was a pre 1976 member of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme, would give him an entitlement to the maximum rate of pension on age retirement.

(6) That Mr Joseph did not realise that by not maintaining the election to preserve his rights he was depriving himself of the benefit of the Commonwealth employer contributions.

(7) That if Mr Joseph had understood these matters he would have maintained the election he made to preserve his superannuation rights in the form S2A which he completed on 30 March 1987.

56. We find that this is not a case where a deliberate decision had been made to reject the option of preservation in favour of receiving the immediate benefit of the accumulated contributions. All the evidence indicates that Mr Joseph at all times intended to preserve his superannuation until his retirement but that through ignorance he failed to give effect to that intention.

57. Guidelines such as those recommended by the Tribunal in Re Adams were approved by the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme Board on 5 September 1996. They are also used in matters where the decision is made by a delegate of the Commissioner. They are contained in the T documents at T2 pp16-17. While they are not binding on the Tribunal and cannot restrict the Tribunal's discretion under s 157(1), nor modify the principles explained by the Federal Court in Chalk, Boardman and Adams, we agree with Ms Munoz that it is appropriate for us to have regard to the guidelines.

58. The first guideline states:

"1. The Board is of the view that, notwithstanding any current views about the adequacy of forms S2A and S2R, it is not desirable for a late application to be granted solely or largely on the grounds that the applicant now claims not to have properly understood the form at the relevant time;

- in the absence of contrary evidence, completion of form S2A/S2R will be taken to signify an informed decision to select a refund and forgo preservation.

- the expectation is that an applicant who may not have understood the choices available should have made inquiries prior to completing the form S2A/S2R. Failure to have made such inquiries without good reason will be taken as signifying an informed choice unless, had they sought advice on the options available from the appropriate official source relevant to their workplace or circumstances, they would have received materially erroneous or misleading advice that would have caused them to misunderstand their options."

We find that the fact that Mr Joseph elected the preservation option in the Form S2A he completed on 30 March 1987 after having written requesting a rollover on 17 March 1987, and then did not complete the "Preservation of Superannuation Rights - Notice" sent to him, but instead again requested a rollover by letter dated 20 July 1987 shows that Mr Joseph was confused. Thus we find that he did not make "an informed decision to select a refund and forgo preservation".

59. The second guideline states:

"2. The Board is also of the view that it is generally desirable to grant a late election in cases where an application has been lodged promptly after an applicant has learned that a case for a late election might exist, and where the applicant can provide evidence, where appropriate, that:

...

(ii) they were materially misinformed at a relevant time by an authoritative source upon which it was reasonable for them to rely concerning either the options available to them in their circumstances, or the substance of any particular relevant option. Corroboration of receipt of incorrect advice would lend support to an application. Such corroboration could be by way of evidence of prevailing practice, supporting statement from others with contemporary experience, evidence from parties directly involved, or other relevant evidence;

- an authoritative source would include ComSuper (or its predecessors) or the personnel area of the applicant's employer

- the incorrect advice could have been received on an individual basis or provided to all relevant employees, eg by way of a "handout" provided to all employees on termination.

...

...

(v) they did not select any option on the application form but the form was signed by the applicant, or an option was selected but the form was not signed by the applicant;

(v) they established an interest, through the use to which the refund was put when received by the applicant, in making provision for his or her retirement, eg: where the applicant maintained the money through action such as a deposit in a bank account, purchase of shares or rolled the money over to a roll-over facility or another Superannuation Fund or Scheme.

..."

60. We find in respect of Guideline 2 that Mr Joseph lodged his application for recognition of a late election very promptly after he learned of that possibility. We also find that Mr Joseph was at the relevant time given inadequate information by someone in the ComSuper office as to the option he should choose in order to best "preserve his benefit until retirement". In particular we find that he was not informed as to what benefits he would be giving up in choosing a refund and roll-over option. We find as we have set out earlier in these reasons that Mr Joseph's evidence on this issue is corroborated by Mr Farr, by the confusion shown in the letter from Personnel Services, Department of Community Services (T9 p31) and by Mr Joseph's own correspondence. As to paragraph (v) of Guideline 2 we consider that Mr Joseph's conduct in completing the preservation option on the Form S2A and then not completing the form "Preservation of Superannuation Rights - Notice" is analogous to signing the form but not selecting any option. It shows that Mr Joseph did not understand the effect of the election he made on the Form S2A. As to paragraph (vi), Mr Joseph's use of the refund of accumulated contributions was such as to show that he was interested in making provision for his retirement.

61. Guideline 3 states:

"3. The Board would not usually consider it desirable to grant a late election -

(v) where the applicant, in making his or her late election, has allowed more than three months to elapse since he or she first became aware that a late election for preservation could be made; or

(vi) if the applicant had made enquires and received correct advice at any time prior to resignation/termination."

62. We have already stated that Mr Joseph made his application as soon as he learned that a late election for preservation could be made. We have set out in our reasons why we find that Mr Joseph had not received adequate information and advice prior to his resignation in 1987.

63. In Boardman, Von Doussa J pointed out that the time limit specified in the statute gives a prima facie rule as to when the election should have been made and that before favourable exercise of discretion can be made, it must appear that it is fair and just in all the circumstances to extend time. The Tribunal is satisfied in this matter that it is fair and just to extend time. We find that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election to preserve his superannuation rights made by Mr Joseph on 27 October 1993 should be treated as if it had been made within time.

64. The decision under review will be set aside. In substitution the Tribunal will direct that the election made by Mr Joseph on 27 October 1993 be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by s137(1) of the Act.
