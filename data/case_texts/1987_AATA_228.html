QT85/7099; QT84/8 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 228; 87 ATC 886 (12 June 1987)

Last Updated: 21 July 2009

Income tax - company recoupment tax - nominated shareholder of class of vendor shareholders - company interposed in purchase of land - whether company liable is tax on profit from sale of land - whether land acquired for profit making undertaking or scheme.

Income Tax Assessment Act (1936) ss 25, 26(a) Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Act (1982) sl8.

FC of T v Myer Emporium 1987 87 ATC 4363

FC of T v Whitfords Beach Pty Ltd [1982] HCA 8; 1982 150 CLR 355

McClelland v FC of T (1970) 70 ATC 4115

Scottish Australian Mining Co Ltd v FC of T [1950] HCA 16; (1950) 81 CLR 188

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) NOS. TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

APPLICANT

And:

RESPONDENT

REASONS FOR DECISION

1. The applicant in this case is one John Everyman who is the person upon whom the Commissioner has served a Notice of Assessment of a company known as Harbour Proprietary Limited, pursuant to section 18 of the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Act, being the person nominated for that purpose by the nominating vendor shareholders.

  1. The relevant Act - acronymously referred to as the TUCT Act - is aimed at the collection of money found to be irrecoverable, one way or another, by way of company tax from companies which have been stripped of their assets, including accumulated profits before tax, and any assets representing provisions for tax on which payment of the tax might otherwise have been recovered.
  2. The aim of the legislation is to impose liability upon the vendors of shares where the sale of the shares was followed by a stripping operation, as well as upon those who promoted the scheme of which the sale forms part. In essence, the present application effectively asks me to determine whether or not, had there been no sale of the shares, the company would have been liable to tax with respect to the profit made on the sale of land at Surfers Paradise, acquired by the company some time in either July or August of 1978 and sold in 1980.
  3. The facts which I have been asked to find by Mr Fraser, of learned counsel for the applicant, are in the main not in dispute. Mr and Mrs Everyman are a married couple. Mr Everyman had been an airline pilot who was retired prematurely at age 56. He and his wife, who was somewhat older, then were faced with the problem of what to do with their lives. Mr Everyman had received a relatively modest lump sum by way of retirement benefit of approximately $165,000 (less 5 per cent tax) which, with some additional savings, enabled the couple to have a disposable capital of the order of, roughly, $190,000.
  4. In addition, they had a small farm which, although it qualified as "primary production" for tax purposes, resulted yearly in modest losses. Effectively then, the source of income at the Everyman's disposed could be derived only from investments achieved from the capital at their disposal.
  5. The couple came to the Gold Coast with the aim of, hopefully, settling there, acquiring a block of units one of which they would use as their matrimonial home and the others for the derivation of income as well as providing a caretaker-like occupation for Mr Everyman. They looked at one particular block of units in Labrador, six units in all, priced at $100,000, and, needing some advice and having very good friends, the Heeps, who lived on the coast, they rang them shortly thereafter. This would have been approximately 15 or 16 July 1978. The Heeps were most hospitable and invited them to come over and stay with them. The Everymans informed them what their plans were and asked them to look at the units in Labrador, which the Heeps did. They were unimpressed and advised the Everymans that they could probably do better. At some stage during that discussion, it appears that the conversation turned to a joint venture that is the Heeps, who had been living on a nursery for many years, were anxious to leave the nursery and move to the Gold Coast. The families discussed, somewhat informally, that they might combine forces and possibly acquire a block of units jointly, thus providing a home for both the Everymans' and the Heeps', as well as providing additional units for letting purposes.
  6. It was, as I said, a somewhat informal arrangement. It so happened that at around the same time, Mrs Heep had seen an advertisement for an auction, to be held at Surfers Paradise on July 21, 1978. The property to be auctioned was a large vacant block of land in Smith Street of substantial size, some 5321 square metres, or 1 acre, 1 rood and 7.4 perches. Both the ladies were familiar with the area; both had lived there and both evinced a desire to return to that locality.
  7. On the other hand it was quite clear to all the parties that the land, because of its location and size, would have a reserve price on it, which they estimated to be of the order of $600,000 or $700,000 - certainly vastly beyond what then was contemplated by either party. Indeed, Mr Heep, who had been a builder and had kept up some degree of familiarity with local prices, concluded, after inspecting the land that this was "out of our ball game".
  8. Whether or not the Everymans inspected the site prior to the auction I am unable to determine. It is not surprising that nine years after the event, recollections should be somewhat hazy as to the minutiae relating to and leading up to the sale. In the result, I do not believe that anything turns on this. At all events, the parties set off that morning, that is 21 July, a Friday, to attend the auction. Recollections vary as to the reason why they did so. Doing the best I can with the somewhat diverse evidence, I accept the proposition advanced by Mr Heep that they had nothing better to do; they wanted to keep up with local values; that it was an outing. In any case, on the principle, "you never know your luck in a big city" - it was after all an mortgagee's sale - who knows, perhaps the land might well be knocked down at bargain basement rates. In the event, that exactly occurred. Who bid what, and when, and what the final bid was, is clouded in considerable obscurity.
  9. Mr Heep's evidence is to the effect that the bidding started at some $800,000 or $700,000 and was gradually reduced in $100,000 lots till, at $300,000, someone started the bidding. Mrs Everyman thought it was Mr Heep. He, on the other hand, was sure it was someone else. According to Mr Heep, he came in at $320,000 and again at $340,000; that being the final bid, the auction ceased and the parties were informed that this was below the reserve. It was stated by Mr Heep that at all steps of the various steps in the bidding, he had discussions with Mr Everyman and his wife, and that they had agreed there and then between themselves that Mr Heep should continue the bidding on behalf of all the parties. This is contradicted by the evidence of Mr and Mrs Everyman. I am satisfied that their recollection is to be preferred. Bidding for land at well over $300,000 was, after all, a major event in their lives and I am satisfied that, had this tete-a-tete really taken place, they would almost certainly have recalled it. In fact, both were surprised to discover that the final bid had been made by Mr Heep. I find as the most likely explanation of the events that followed that at or around $340,000 or $360,000, Mr Heep became keenly alive to the fact that there was a distinct possibility that this land could be acquired for a substantially lower figure than its market value.
  10. I accept Mr Heep's evidence that his final offer of $360,000 was not accepted and that the parties then went home. Thereafter, I find it very difficult to make any finding of fact with any degree of confidence; I am very sceptical of the evidence that there were any major - or indeed any - discussions about jointly building walk-up units from this point onwards. Indeed, given the limited financial resources of the Everymans, it is difficult to visualise how they could have participated in such a venture - if they sold their Sydney property, they would have committed their entire resources without any prospect of immediate return. In other words, they would have been left without any income for a substantial period of time.
  11. I accept the evidence of the Everymans that they left for Maroochydore the next day, that is July 22, 1978, in a somewhat bewildered state, but with the clear impression that the land was still being negotiated and that they would be included in any successful outcome to these negotiations.
  12. The Everymans' struck me as an honest couple; the kind of couple one frequently encounters who suddenly get a largish lump sum payment and do not know what to do with it. Many invest it unwisely and lose it. The Everymans, more by good luck than good management, entrusted it to Mr Heep who virtually carried them piggyback onwards onto fortune.
  13. What probably happened after 21 July, 1978 goes something like this: Mr Heep, having made an intelligent guess that the amount owed to the mortgagee was $380,000, hoped that increasing his offer by a further $20,000 might well clinch the deal. He made one quite significant comment to the effect that he was prepared "to carry the extra $20,000"; that is, he would not seek half the additional amount from the Everymans. In fact, of course, he carried the whole financial burden thereafter, the Everymans making no further monetary contribution of any kind.
  14. Whether at the point in time when Mr Heep decided to increase his offer to $380,000, a specific purpose had formed in his mind is not, in my opinion, critical, because I am satisfied that by the time the taxpayer company had become the nominated party to the transfer - and this occurred on 3 August 1978 - Mr Heep had resolved to embark upon a high rise development on the subject land. I can think of no other plausible explanation as to why the interposition of a company would be required if the sole purpose of the parties had been to erect walk up units for their own accommodation and for letting.
  15. It should be noted that the interposition of the company had not been Mr Heep's idea, but rather the proposal of Mr McTavish, a somewhat murky grey eminence in the background. The explanation McTavish gave in the witness box, to the effect that the interposition would be "neater" in case of the death of one of the partners, is about as implausible as the explanation he advanced to the effect that, having advised the shareholders to sell their shares in the company - "to get rid of the company" -he was nevertheless confident that the purchaser would either pay any company tax owing or, somehow, absorb the profit in some larger tax loss.
  16. I find, as a fact, that Mr McTavish was fully aware at all times what he was about. To what extent Mr Heep was alive and to the consequence of what was going on, I am unable to determine, nor is it relevant for my consideration.
  17. On 3 August, 1978, that is, the day on which the nomination was exercised, a great deal of activity took place. On that day, discussions were held with the architect, and it was quite apparent that Mr Heep was by then keenly alive to the fact that he was sitting on a gold mine which could be exploited for substantial gain by the construction - be it by joint venture or otherwise - of a high rise unit development.
  18. There is some confusion as to the date when the property finally passed from mortgagee to purchaser, a confusion not helped by the fact that the taxpayer company was interposed for fiscal considerations and the company's minutes falsified in order to avoid stamp duty. By way of "mitigation", it was urged upon me that the Queensland Stamp Act had by that time been amended and that no stamp duty was in fact payable. It is an interesting submission, but quite irrelevant. For present purposes, the punctual temporis is the 3rd of August, 1978 - the date upon which the taxpayer company became the owner of the subject land. I find as a fact that on that date, Mr Heep had decided upon using the land for high rise development, and that his intention was, for all practical purposes, the intention of the company. It may well be - indeed it is likely - than on the day of the auction, the parties attended the sale more out of curiosity and for something to do, and that the initial bidding was done on impulse. However, a lot of water has flown under the bridge between the tentative offer of $360,000 on July 21 and the 3rd of August, the day the taxpayer company was interposed as the ultimate purchaser of the Smith Street site. It is not without significance that at that time, or shortly thereafter, a feasibility study for the development of the site, prepared on behalf of the previous owner, had materialised. The reference to it in the sales brochure read: "Preliminary plans for a 16 storey block of 90 units and restaurant have been considered by the executive committee of the Gold Coast City Council for approval subject to certain requirements." It was tendered through McTavish, the builder, who believed it most likely that he had been given this study by Heep at their first meeting. Heep, on the other hand, claims never to have seen it before.
  19. I repeat, on the whole of the evidence, I am satisfied that by 3rd August, 1978, Mr Heep had decided to embark upon a high rise development upon the site. All the evidence points in that direction. McTavish confirms it, although conceding that at their first meeting - that is between McTavish and Heep - there had been "some minor reference" to the construction of walk up units. Nevertheless, the thrust of the meeting had been the discussion of utilizing the site for its advertised purpose - the construction of high rise development.
  20. In case the matter goes on appeal, I will deal briefly with the evidence of the other principal actors. I have already said that the evidence of the Everymans was credible; they impressed as witnesses of truth. Their intention at all times had been to erect a modest walk up block of units on the Gold Coast. It was ultimately their good fortune that they became involved with the Heeps and, once involved, they were simply carried along, willy nilly, into a highly successful venture without any participation or involvement on their part. It was Mr Heep, and he alone, who conceived the project and carried it through to its ultimate conclusion, the other members of the original "partnership" just going along for the ride.
  21. Turning to Mrs Heep, she was a reluctant witness. She was obviously ill at ease in the witness box, due to her age and an underlying illness. I refused to accept her statutory declaration, sought to be tendered in reliance on a medical certificate. I held the certificate to be somewhat "lightweight" and, since it indicated that the witness would be sufficiently recovered within a couple of weeks to appear as a witness, I informed counsel that I would grant an adjournment at the end of his case, if he sought one, to enable him to call Mrs Heep if he were so minded. In the events which occurred, Mrs Heep appeared the next day to give evidence. However, her oral testimony did little to shed any light on the events. She was an honest witness doing her best. However, to the extent that she sought to affirm her statutory declaration, I attach no weight to it since this never went any further than a constant reiteration of "if it's in my statement, it must be true". Her statutory declaration had all the hallmarks of special pleading; it was obviously drawn by a skilled draftsman. In the circumstances, I find that something more is required to lend it weight than a repetitive re-affirmation by an obviously reluctant witness.
  22. I have less faith in the evidence of Mr Heep. He would, at times, reconstruct the events of July 1978 to suit the occasion. I reject the evidence of the various alleged conversations with the Everymans during and after the auction. He added gratuitously that the Everymans had at their disposal some $400,000 because their Sydney property was on the market. This did not receive support from the evidence of the Everymans, nor the documentary evidence, which indicated that their Sydney property was not placed on the market until October 1978. I find that Mr Heep was initially bidding and negotiating for the property on a frolic of his own and independently of any ability of the Everymans to match his contribution, let alone contribute to any subsequent building development. The Everymans' subsequent participation in the project was a stroke of luck for them, but it can have no bearing on the tax position of the company.
  23. When the bargain was finally struck (the agreed price being $380,000), allegedly on behalf of the Everymans and the Heeps, it certainly came as a matter of complete surprise to the latter, who learnt the good news over the telephone.
  24. Turning to the law, the findings of the fact that I have made leads me to the conclusion that the land - when it was acquired by the taxpayer company - was acquired for the purpose of erecting high rise units. The exact concatenation of how this was to be achieved had not been worked out in detail. However, for tax purposes, that does not matter. I find that the land was acquired for a profit-making undertaking or scheme and falls classically within the second limb of section 26(a) of the Tax Act.
  25. In the circumstances, it is unnecessary to consider whether the gain is also "income" pursuant to section 25(1). That matter fell for consideration in FC of T v Whitfords Beach Pty Ltd [1982] HCA 8; (1982) 150 CLR 355. The facts in that case were as follows: The taxpayer company acquired some 1600 acres of land adjacent to Whitfords Beach, 24 km north of Perth in 1954. The land was acquired to give shareholders of the company access to beach-front shacks and not for profit-making by sale or for business purposes. In December 1967 (by which time the market value of the land had increased), all of the shares in the company were acquired by three other companies with the purpose of gaining control over the land and with the intention that the taxpayer company would cause the land to be subdivided, developed and sold at a profit. New Articles of Association were adopted by the taxpayer company to permit it to achieve this purpose. Two of the prospective companies were appointed by the taxpayer company as managers to carry out the work, including the procuring of the necessary re-zoning of the land. The project was carried through on a massive scale, involving provision of roads and parklands. Eventually, substantial profits from the sale of these allotments were received by the taxpayer company, which was assessed for income tax in the years 1972, 1973, 1974 and 1975. Whilst the whole Court was unanimous in the result, namely that the profits were assessable income, there was little unanimity in their Honours' reasoning. Thus Gibbs CJ. Mason and Wilson JJ concluded that after December 1967, the taxpayer had become a company which existed for the sole purpose of carrying out the operation on which its new shareholders had decided when they acquired their shares. In the light of these circumstances, the development, subdivision and sale were therefore more than the mere realisation of an existing asset (such as was found, for example, in the Scottish Australian Mining Co. case [1950] HCA 16; (1950) 81 CLR 188) and amounted to the carrying on of the business of land development. In the result, the profits constituted income within ordinary concepts, assessable under section 25(1). Wilson J took the view that on such a finding, no concluded decision on the application of section 26(a) was required. Gibbs CJ and Mason J on the other hand, concluded that section 26(a) would only operate where section 25(1) does not (a view which, sits uneasily with the majority decision of the Privy Council in McClelland v FC of T 70 ATC 4115). Murphy J concluded that the profit constituted assessable income pursuant to the second limb of section 26(a), viz. that what was done constituted a profit-making undertaking or scheme.

26. Both counsel referred me to a recent decision - FC of T v The Myer Emporium (87 ATC 4363) - in which a unanimous High Court concluded that the impugned amount in question formed part of the taxpayer/respondent's income under section 25(1) of the Act. However, their Honours went on to observe (at p 4372): "A similar chain of reasoning would have led to the conclusion that the amount constituted assessable income under the second limb of section 26(a). The relationship between ss.25(1) and 26(a) has been the subject of much previous discussion involving considerable differences of opinion about the extent (if any) to which the provision of the second limb of s26(a) supplemented the provision of s25(1) (see e.g. Whitfords Beach, at pp.379-381). It is, however, unnecessary that we examine that question here since, as we have indicated, we consider that the amount in question in the present appeal constituted income of the taxpayer both pursuant to ss.25(1) and 26(a)".

27. In the circumstances, I do not propose to embark on the academic exercise whether, in the instant case, the gain from the sale of the company to the entrepreneurs who developed the land and built the units (including, among others, both the Everymans' and the Heeps') also constitutes income under ordinary concepts; it is sufficient for present purposes to have identified a profit-making undertaking or scheme which falls classically into the ambit of s26(a).

28. It was urged upon me by Mr Fraser that the Crown had not sufficiently "identified" the undertaking or scheme in this case. I find this submission has no merit. I find that once it is found that the taxpayer company had acquired the subject land for purposes of erecting high rise units as a commercial enterprise - and no suggestion was ever made that these units were to be held for investment by the joint entrepreneurs - that is sufficient "identification". It follows that, once having embarked upon such an undertaking, it cannot be circumvented for tax purposes by the deft device of "selling the company" which was used to acquire the land.

29. I have therefore concluded that the Commissioner correctly assessed the company on the gain made on the sale of the land (which was sold for $800,000 in January, 1980) and that this applicant is stuck with the fiscal consequences which flow from that sale.

30. The decision of the Tribunal is to affirm the decision under review.

This is to certify that this and the preceding fourteen (14) pages are a true and correct copy of the Decision and Reasons for Decision of Dr P. Gerber, Senior Member in matter No. QT85/7099 and QT84/8.
