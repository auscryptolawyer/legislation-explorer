Re Gkn Kwikform Services Pty Limited v Commissioner of Taxation [1990] FCA 341 (5 September 1990)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: GKN KWIKFORM SERVICES PTY LIMITED

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No. G146-8 of 1989

FED No. 476

Taxation

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Foster J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - hiring out scaffolding equipment - compensation for lost or damaged equipment - whether profit so derived capital or trading profit - distinction between capital and income.

Income Taxation Assessment Act 1936

F.C. of T. v Cyclone Scaffolding Pty Limited 18 FCR 183

Gloucester Railway Carriage and Wagon Co v IRC (1925) AC 469

Van den Berghs v Clark 1935 19 Tax Cases 390

Federal Coke Co Pty Limited v F. C. of T. [1977] FCA 3; 34 FLR 375

F.C. of T. v Myer Emporium Limited [1987] HCA 18; (1986-87) 163 CLR 199

F.C. of T. v Spedley Securities Limited 88 ATC 4126

Statham and Anor v F.C. of T. 89 ATC 4070

F.C. of T. v Cainero 88 ATC 4427

Cooling v F.C. of T. 89 ATC 4731

GP International Pipecoaters Pty Limited v F.C. of T. (Unreported, 20 June 1990)

HEARING

SYDNEY

5:9:1990

Counsel for the applicant: A. H. SLATER

Instructed by: Mallesons Stephen Jaques

Counsel for the respondent: Sir Maurice Byers QC with Ms McMillan

Instructed by: Australian Government Solocitor

ORDER

The appeal be upheld.

The decision of the respondent in relation to the applicant's objection be set aside.

There be substituted a decision allowing that objection.

The relevant assessment be amended accordingly.

The respondent pay the applicant's costs of the proceedings.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with by Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

The applicant appeals from two amended assessments issued by the respondent Commissioner of Taxation ("the Commissioner") and one assessment. The amended assessments are dated 19 April 1988 and the assessment is dated 4 March 1988. The income periods involved are the applicant's financial years 1 May 1984 to 30 April 1985, 1 May 1985 to 30 April 1986 and the period 1 May 1986 to 31 December 1986, the applicant's financial year then having been altered to correspond with the ordinary calendar year. Objections to the three assessments were lodged and disallowed by the Commissioner. The applicant has appealed to this court from the Commissioner's disallowance. The appeals are therefore appeals ab initio. The same questions are raised in each appeal and the parties have agreed that the decision in one, namely appeal G146, shall determine the result in all three appeals.

2. A great deal of material has been placed before the court indicating the position occupied by the applicant in the structure of the Kwikform Group of companies prior to April 1985 and thereafter. Prior to that date it was a wholly owned subsidiary of Kwikform Industries Limited, under the name "Kwikform Services Pty Limited". An associated company Kwikform Sales Pty Limited was also a wholly owned subsidiary. In April 1985 Kwikform Industries Limited became a subsidiary of GKN Australia Limited and thereafter changed its name to "GKN Kwikform Industries Limited". The applicant remained a subsidiary and changed its name to "GKN Kwikform Services Pty Limited". Likewise, the associated company remained a subsidiary and changed its name to GKN Kwikform Sales Pty Limited ("Sales"). It is not necessary to mention other changes which then, and later, occurred in the overall structure of the group of companies.

3. The evidence clearly establishes that the work of the group is organised into divisions. So far as the applicant and Sales are concerned, it appears that the same division of function has been maintained since the restructuring in 1985 as obtained prior thereto. They divide between themselves a substantial area of the group's operations in relation to the provision of scaffolding for use in buildings. At all relevant times, including the financial years under consideration, Sales handled the sale of scaffolding components to purchasers in the building and related industries. It also sold to companies in the group, when required. The applicant, on the other hand, was, at all relevant times, engaged in the business of hiring out the same sort of equipment to users in the building industry. It had and has a large client base including Governments, major construction companies and smaller tradesmen. The type of scaffolding concerned is readily observable at major city construction sights. It is used for essential support purposes both inside and outside buildings under construction or renovation. Under the division of function obtaining between the two companies, a customer wishing to acquire scaffolding as part of its own equipment for present and future use would do so by ordering and obtaining it from Sales. A customer who wished to hire it for temporary purposes would enter into the necessary hiring arrangements with the applicant.

4. It follows, of course, that at all relevant times the applicant, having regard to the extensive nature of its business, had in stock a very large number of the component parts of scaffolding. It appears that the applicant provided two modular systems of scaffolding, the Kwikstage Scaffold and the Milsystem Scaffold, the first system having been invented in 1966 and the latter having been introduced into Australia in 1979. The evidence indicates the differences between the two systems but they are not relevant for present purposes. The component parts of both systems are made of steel and are long lasting. The evidence establishes, indeed, that a substantial quantity of equipment which was first used in the 1960s is still in stock in the applicant's business and being used for the purposes of that business. Its ultimate lifespan remains unknown. The stock is maintained from purchases from Sales which produces approximately 50% of the applicant's hiring stock. Sales buys in the balance from independent steel merchants, fabricators, or through second-hand purchases.

5. I am satisfied that it is not a practical possibility for the applicant to keep track of the individual items of scaffolding equipment which it keeps for hiring out to customers. The components are interchangeable and, currently, the age of individual components has no bearing upon their suitability for use by customers. I accept the evidence of Mr Gore, a director of the applicant, that "the only practical way of keeping a record of the applicant's hire stock is to treat as equivalent all items of the same kind, whenever they were manufactured. Thus the applicant's fixed asset register draws no distinction on account of date of acquisitions". Orders for scaffolding from customers are filled from stock, according to the number of parts ordered, and without reference to the age of those parts. Orders are placed in accordance with written forms which contain conditions of hire, to which reference will be made later.

6. It is established by the evidence that there is a regular "leakage" of items of scaffolding from the amounts ordered by customers for particular jobs. This term is used by the applicant to denote short returns of equipment from hiring. Such short returns occur in three ways. Firstly, on fairly rare occasions, the applicant will make sales of stock on hire to a customer, at the request of the customer. Stock sold in this way is, of course, retained by the customer at the end of the job and not returned with the hire stock. Such selling is an exception to the general rule of the group that sales of scaffolding equipment are the province of Sales and not the applicant. Transactions of this kind result from a policy decision to satisfy the requirement of a customer and, at the same time, "lock in" the customer to future dealings with the applicant. If the customer owns a certain amount of Kwikform or Milsystem Scaffolding, it will, as a matter of necessity, return to the applicant to hire more such equipment when the need arises, as only the applicant's equipment would be compatible with that which has thus been retained by the customer. Secondly, where comparatively insignificant amounts of equipment are not returned from a building site, the applicant, at its discretion, and no doubt having regard to the importance of the customer, will simply disregard the loss and write it off. Thirdly, where there is a significant amount of equipment not returned at the completion of a hiring, the customer is charged for the loss of this equipment in accordance with conditions of the contract to which I shall make reference later. Most of the "leakage" falls into this category.

7. Equipment in the first category is referred to as "sales off hire", whilst equipment in the third category is referred to as "chargeable losses". This case is concerned with the appropriate treatment for taxation purposes of monies received in the relevant income years in respect of these "chargeable losses". In the applicant's 1985 financial year the amount of these losses was $837,781, being 5.71% of the total receipts on the applicant's business. In its 1986 financial year the relevant amount was $1,133,697, being 3.26% of the total business receipts. In the 1986 year (ending December 1986) the relevant amount was $828,688, being 3.25% of the total receipts. In the same years the amounts received for "sales off hire" were $451,113, $331,850 and $417,317 respectively.

8. The evidence demonstrates clearly that, although these amounts were not insignificant in the accounts of the applicant, they did not represent a commercial result from the items of equipment which was in any way comparable to the result achieved from the hiring out of the equipment. A new piece of equipment, apparently, recovered its cost to the applicant during its first year of hiring. Thereafter, its life being of indefinite duration, it continued to earn hiring charges for many years into the future. It was worth far more to the applicant as an item of hiring stock than could be realised for it if it were sold to a customer or lost by the customer in circumstances where the customer was obliged to compensate the applicant for the loss under the hiring contract. As will be seen, the amount required to be paid by a customer where it failed to return a piece of equipment was, in fact, the current price of that item as charged by Sales. This was seen by the applicant as a positive encouragement to the customer to care for the item and return it at the end of the hire. It also went some distance, but by no means the full distance, towards compensating the applicant for the loss of the item as a piece of income earning equipment.

9. Additionally, it was established that the applicant actively sought to prevent the loss of items of scaffolding equipment by an incentive scheme designed to encourage the managers of its branches to ensure, so far as possible, that all equipment was returned to it from hire.

10. For accounting and taxation purposes the applicant, at all relevant times, treated its stock of hiring equipment as plant used by it for the purpose of producing assessable income and, therefore, as being subject to the depreciation provisions of the Income Taxation Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") (sections 54 - 62). For depreciation purposes, it appears that a period of 15 years was accepted as appropriate. Items of stock that fell into the categories of "chargeable losses" and "sales off hire" were, thus, stock which had been subject to depreciation.

11. In respect of both categories of stock, as the actual sale price in the case of sales and the amount of compensation received in the case of non-returns was, under the hiring agreement, the current retail list price, the situation necessarily obtained that there was a full recoupment of the amount of depreciation which had been allowed. Consequently this recouped amount was always brought to tax under s 59 of the Act. Over and above the amount of this recoupment, however, the applicant received an amount in respect of each item which constituted a profit. This profit consisted, in relation to "chargeable losses", in the difference between the amount received by way of compensation for loss under the contract and the original cost of the item against which depreciation had been charged. It appears that the cost was arrived at on a basis described as a "weighted average basis", acceptable to the applicant and the Commissioner in circumstances where, as already discussed, it was not practicable to keep records relating to each item of equipment.

12. The profits on chargeable losses, the liability to taxation of which is the subject of the dispute in these proceedings, are set out in exhibit E, and amount to $442,088 for the year ending 30 April 1985, $749,049 for the year ending 30 April 1986 and $586,460 for the period ending 31 December 1986. It may be noted, from the same exhibit, that the amount of profit on "sales off hire" for the same periods was $243,125, $159,204, and $209,382 respectively.

13. It is not, and never has been, disputed that the profits from "sales off hire" are profits on capital account and therefore not taxable under the Act. It is accepted that they are profits arising from the sale of fixed assets or plant of the company, being hire stock and not trading stock. It is the applicant's contention that the profits received on the amounts of compensation paid for loss of hire equipment should be treated in the same way, as being, in effect, profits received on disposal of capital assets. It is submitted that the fact that the disposal is involuntary rather than by way of voluntary sale cannot be of any significance in determining the appropriate category into which the receipt should be placed. It may be noted that, in correspondence, the Commissioner originally accepted the view that these profit items were on capital and not revenue account. A change of heart led to amended assessments and this litigation.

14. Before coming to the submissions of the applicant and the respondent, it is convenient to deal with certain specific matters.

15. Firstly, because of the division of function between Sales and the applicant, I am satisfied that the items of scaffolding, whose non-return in the relevant income years has led to emergence of the "profits" the subject of this dispute, were never acquired by the applicant as trading stock. The possibility that a very small proportion of the components acquired by the applicant might, in fact, be sold to a hirer rather than returned at the completion of the hire does not, in my view, affect the validity of this general proposition. This is simply not a case where it could be said that the taxpayer operated a business in which there was a pool of goods available, alternatively, for hire or sale with the result that any item could take on the characteristic of trading stock or hiring stock depending upon the use to which the taxpayer, at any point of time, wished to put it. In this regard, the case is factually different from Memorex Pty Limited v F C of T (1987) 77 ALR 299, F C of T v Cyclone Scaffolding Pty Limited 18 FCR 183, and Gloucester Railway Carriage and Wagon Co v IRC (1925) AC 469. As a matter of broad description, without consideration of the details of contractual arrangements, the applicant was in the business of hiring out equipment, and not in the business of selling it. It acquired its stock for the purpose of retaining it and hiring it to customers for reward and not for the purpose of selling it at a profit.

16. Secondly, the applicant acquired and dealt with its stock as "plant" or fixed assets. In its accounting and taxation treatment of its stock it was not contemplated that it would be available, for business purposes, other than for hire. It became subject to depreciation under the Act from the moment of its acquisition. This situation was at all times accepted by the respondent who, as already indicated, has not challenged the appropriateness of treating the occasional sales of the equipment as producing receipts on capital account. After recoupment of depreciation pursuant to the Act, the resulting profit has been accepted as a non-taxable capital profit. It has also been accepted that no relevant intention of resale for profit existed at the time of acquisition of any of the equipment, with the result that no claim to levy tax has been made under s 25A(1) of the Act.

17. Thirdly, it is established by the decision of the Full Court of this court in Memorex that the depreciation provisions of the Act do not provide an exclusive code for the taxation of monies received upon the sale or other disposal of a depreciated asset. In circumstances where the "consideration receivable" (s 59(3)) exceeds the relevant cost price of the item, the tax payable is not limited to the full recoupment of depreciation previously allowed. The additional profit is taxable under s 25(1) if it can properly be characterised as a revenue profit. If it is properly characterised as having been received on capital account, then it is not taxable. In this regard it must be kept in mind that the Act clearly preserves the common law distinctions between capital and income (see e.g. s 51(1)). It is also clear that a profit can form a part of a taxpayer's gross income under s 25(1) of the Act (Memorex p 303, and cases there cited).

18. Fourthly, in determining whether a particular amount of profit is received by a taxpayer on capital or revenue account, close regard must be paid to the nature of the taxpayer's business. This admonition receives due emphasis in the often quoted passage from the opinion of the Lord Justice-Clerk in Californian Copper Syndicate (Limited and Reduced) v Harris 1904 5 Tax Cases 159 at 165 where his Lordship said:

"It is quite a well settled principle in dealing with

questions of assessment of Income Tax, that where the

owner of an ordinary investment chooses to realise

it, and obtains a greater price for it than he

originally acquired it at, the enhanced price is not

profit in the sense of Schedule D of the Income Tax

Act of 1842 assessable to Income Tax. But it is

equally well established that enhanced values

obtained from realisation or conversion of securities

may be so assessable, where what is done is not

merely a realisation or change of investment, but an

act done in what is truly the carrying on, or

carrying out, of a business."

19. This passage was considered by Barwick C.J. in London Australia Investment Co Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1977] HCA 50; 1976-77 138 CLR 106 at 112, in the following passage:

"The oft citation of the truism there expressed has

given it a delphic significance. But, in truth, what

was there said furnishes no criterion for determining

such a question as is now before this Court in this

case. Of course, what is produced by a business will

in general be income. But whether it is or not must

depend on the nature of the business, precisely

defined, and the relationship of the source of the

profit or gain to that business. Everything received

by a taxpayer who conducts a business will not

necessarily be income. As I have said, it must depend

on the essential nature of his business and the

relationship of the gain to that business and its

conduct."

20. His Honour was in dissent in this case, but I do not understand anything in the majority judgments to cast doubt upon the accuracy of these observations.

21. Fifthly, in considering the nature of the applicant's business, it is, obviously, necessary to have regard to the terms under which it hired the items of scaffolding. Reliance is placed upon these terms by the Commissioner in a way to which I shall make reference later. The term of the hiring contract which applied in respect of hirings prior to 1985 read as follows:

"The Hirer shall be responsible for the equipment

from the time the Hirer collects it from the

Company's depot or from the time the Company delivers

it to the Hirer's site (as the case may be) until the

time the Hirer returns it to the Company's depot or

until the time the Company collects it from the

Hirer's site (as the case may be). An Inventory of

equipment prepared by the Company shall be binding

and conclusive between the Company and the Hirer and

in the event of any discrepancy in items of equipment

being disclosed by such Inventory on termination of

hire the Hirer shall forthwith pay to the Company an

amount sufficient to cover all losses at the then

current list price for such lost items and any other

costs and expenses which the Company may incur as a

result thereof. The Hirer shall be responsible for

repairing and maintaining the equipment in good order

and condition having regard to the condition thereof

at the date of commencement of hire and excepting

only fair wear and tear shall forthwith pay to the

Company an amount sufficient to cover any and all

losses costs and expenses the Company may incur as a

result of the Hirer failing or omitting so to do."

The corresponding paragraph in use in hiring contracts after 1985 was as follows:

"At the termination of the hiring all goods shall be

returned to the Company in good condition excepting

only fair wear and tear. All goods shall be deemed

in good condition unless hirer notifies Company to

the contrary within 24 hour of delivery of goods.

The Hirer shall on demand pay in respect of any goods

damages or not so returned the then current selling

price for the goods. Until such sum is paid hire

charges shall continue to accrue and any and all

other costs and expenses incurred by the Company as a

result of such shortages or losses. The Hirer's

responsibility for the preservation and safekeeping

of the goods shall not be determined until the goods

are physically handed over to the Hirer by the Company."

It may be noted that this term contains some obvious misprints which were corrected when the document was reprinted later. The word "damages" is obviously intended to be "damaged"; also the words "Hirer" and "Company" at the conclusion of the last sentence are obviously transposed.

22. It is convenient to deal with one aspect of the respondent's argument at this point. The respondent submits, for reasons to which I shall make reference later, that the whole of the amount payable by the Hirer under either of these clauses is a receipt on revenue account. Indeed, this submission is put as a main submission, the alternative submission that the profit element in the payment should be so treated being a secondary or subsidiary submission. I am satisfied that the first submission cannot, in any circumstances, be sustained. To treat the whole of the monies received under these terms as forming part of the gross income of the applicant under s 25(1) of the Act would be inconsistent with the express provisions of s 59 dealing with the loss of depreciated property. Section 59(2) provides that where the consideration receivable in respect of the loss of depreciated property exceeds the depreciated value of that property at the time of the loss, "the excess, to the extent of the sum of the amounts allowed and allowable in assessments for income tax under this Act...in respect of depreciation, shall...be included in (the taxpayer's) assessable income..." "Consideration receivable" is defined in s 59(3) to mean: "(b) in the case of loss or destruction of the property - the amount or value received or receivable under a policy of insurance or otherwise in respect of the loss or destruction". Clearly the amounts payable under these contractual terms would fall within the wording of this sub-section with the result that such part of the payments under the contract as represented the amount necessary to recoup previous allowances for depreciation would be brought to tax under s 59(2), with the result that only what has been described as the "profit" element would remain as potentially taxable under s 25(1).

23. With these observations in mind I turn to a consideration of the submissions made by the parties.

24. The applicant's primary submission is that the profits made on the monies received from hirers in respect of "chargeable losses" should receive the same taxation treatment as the profits made on monies received for "sales off hire": they should be regarded as being received on the capital account of the applicant. This is so because they are paid in respect of the loss of items which form part of the fixed, not circulating, capital of the applicant. Each item lost through its non-return at the end of the hiring is an item of "plant", a capital asset, used for the purpose of generating income through hiring charges. It is part of the applicant's "profit making apparatus" (per Lord Macmillan Van den Berghs Limited v Clark 1935 19 TC 390, 431/2). It is part of the tree of which the hiring revenue is the fruit.

25. Furthermore, it is put that there was an absence of any relevant profit-making intention. The items of equipment were not acquired with any intention of gaining a profit from their disposal; on the contrary, there was no intention to dispose of them at all, the intention being to retain them as income earning assets. A fortiori there was no intention to make a profit at the time of their loss. There was in fact no intention to lose them, their disposal by this means being entirely involuntary.

26. The amounts received by way of compensation for loss under the contract were, as a matter of construction, and commonsense, intended to stand in the place of the lost goods. As such they had the same capital quality as did the goods themselves, a result which was recognised by the fact that a significant part of the amount received was necessarily devoted to recouping the depreciation in capital value of the goods previously allowed to the applicant as a deduction under the depreciation provisions of the Act.

27. Reliance was also placed for the characterisation of the profit element as a receipt on capital account, upon the passage from the judgment of Brennan J in Federal Coke Co Pty Limited v F C T [1977] FCA 3; 34 FLR 375 at 401 where his Honour said:

"When a recipient of moneys provides consideration

for the payment, the consideration will ordinarily

supply the touchstone for ascertaining whether the

receipt is on revenue account or not. The character

of an asset which is sold for a price, or the

character of a cause of action discharged by a

payment will ordinarily determine, unless it be a

sham transaction, the character of the receipt of the

price or payment. The consideration establishes the

matter in respect of which the moneys are received.

The character of the receipt may then be determined

by the character, in the recipient's hands, of the

matter in respect of which moneys are received. Thus,

when moneys are received in consideration of

surrendering a benefit to which the recipient is

entitled under a contract, it is relevant to inquire

whether or not that benefit was a capital asset in

his hands. To adapt the words of Lord Macmillan in

Van den Berghs Ltd v Clark (1935) AC at p 443, and of

Williams J in Bennett v Federal Commissioner of

Taxation [1947] HCA 25; (1947) 75 CLR 480 at p 485, the inquiry is

whether the congeries of the rights which the

recipient enjoyed under the contract and which for a

price he surrendered was a capital asset."

28. It was put on behalf of the applicant that the application of this analysis to the present case produces the result that the receipt of the compensatory amount, including the profit element, was a capital receipt in the hands of the applicant, in that what it provided in return for it was a capital asset, namely part of its income earning plant. This approach involved treating the transaction of loss of the applicant's goods by the hirer and recompense by the hirer to the applicant by payment of the current retail price for the item as being, in effect, a notional sale of the capital asset for a contractual price. A similar approach appears to have been taken in the case of Commissioner of Taxation v Cyclone Scaffolding Pty Limited 18 FCR 183, to which I shall make reference later. The same result is achieved by regarding the payment under the contract as being, in effect, a pre-estimate of damage for the conversion by the hirer of the applicant's goods, a judgment in conversion having the effect of transferring title in the goods converted to the convertor. The consideration for the payment, in either case, would be the transfer of title in goods owned by the applicant, which goods were relevantly an income-producing and, therefore, capital asset. The receipt of the compensation money would therefore be on capital account.

29. It was also submitted on behalf of the applicant that the decision of the majority in Cyclone necessarily bound me to hold that the amounts in issue in these proceedings were received on capital account. I shall return to this matter later.

30. The applicant, in his initial submissions, also dealt with anticipated arguments on the part of the respondent based upon the decision of the High Court of Australia in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Myer Emporium Limited [1987] HCA 18; (1986-87) 163 CLR 199. In fact arguments based upon statements of principle in that decision were not at the forefront of the respondent's submissions. However, as I understand it, they played some part in those submissions. Accordingly I think it appropriate to deal with the questions raised by the applicant in relation to that case.

31. The passage in the case which is the subject of constant reference is at p 209. It reads as follows:

"Although it is well settled that a profit or gain

made in the ordinary course of carrying on a business

constitutes income, it does not follow that a profit

or gain made in a transaction entered into otherwise

than in the ordinary course of carrying on the

taxpayer's business is not income. Because a

business is carried on with a view to profit, a gain

made in the ordinary course of carrying on the

business is invested with the profit-making purpose,

thereby stamping the profit with the character of

income. But a gain made otherwise than in the

ordinary course of carrying on the business which

nevertheless arises from a transaction entered into

by the taxpayer with the intention or purpose of

making a profit or gain may well constitute income.

Whether it does depends very much on the

circumstances of the case. Generally speaking,

however, it may be said that if the circumstances are

such as to give rise to the inference that the

taxpayer's intention or purpose in entering into the

transaction was to make a profit or gain, the profit

or gain will be income, notwithstanding that the

transaction was extraordinary judged by reference to

the ordinary course of the taxpayer's business. Nor

does the fact that a profit or gain is made as the

result of an isolated venture or a "one-off"

transaction preclude it from being properly

characterized as income: Federal Commissioner of

Taxation v Whitfords Beach Pty Ltd [1982] HCA 8; (1982) 150 CLR 355

at p 366-367, 376. The authorities establish that a

profit or gain so made will constitute income if the

property generating the profit or gain was acquired

in a business operation or commercial transaction for

the purpose of profit-making by the means giving rise

to profit."

32. It may be noted that immediately after this passage the judgment proceeds by stating that "The celebrated decision in Californian Copper Syndicate v Harris makes the point". The court further emphasised that in that case a distinction was drawn "between a mere realization or change of investment" and "an act done in what is truly the carrying on, or carrying out, of a business", the distinction being further expressed in the case in the following passage:- "Is the sum of gain that has been made a mere enhancement of value by realising a security, or is it a gain made in an operation of business in carrying out a scheme for profit-making?"

33. Although the passage from Myer has sometimes been cited as the basis for a submission that it establishes a new category of income according to the ordinary usages of mankind, the other passages referred to above make it quite clear that no new ground is being broken: the passage is merely a restatement of the earlier law. In particular it does not eliminate the long established distinction between receipts and expenditure on capital account and on revenue account.

34. This view was taken by the Full Court of this court in F. C. of T. v Spedley Securities Limited 88 ATC 4126 at 4130 where the court said as follows:

"The second principal submission relies on the recent

decision of the High Court in F. C. of T. v Myer

Emporium Ltd 87 ATC 4363; [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 61 ALJR 270. The

decision in that case was given jointly by five

Judges, doubtless with some recognition that the

Court was reversing cumulative decisions of the

Supreme Court of Victoria, and this Court on a

question of whether a receipt was, on the application

of the Income Taxation Assessment Act 1936 ("the

Act") sec. 25(1), one of capital or income. The case

is strong authority for what it decides, but it may

only have taken a different view of the facts than

had the lower courts. The use made of the decision

in this case on behalf of the Commissioner is to say

that the amount in question was received in the

course of business operations, the operations, taken

broadly, being intended to produce a profit. The

phrase "in the course of" involves a temporal

connection. If the proposition were correct, it

would mean that any receipt by a business would

necessarily be of an income nature, and this would be

contrary to authority, to the Act itself and to the

basic concepts concerning the distinction between

capital and income. In Myer what was received

related solely to income by way of interest on a loan

made by the taxpayer, the amount received being for a

transfer of the right to receive the interest in the

future. The High Court did not base its decision on

Myer being, in a broader sense, a profit-making

company. The purpose of profit making must exist in

relation to the particular operation."

(See also Statham and Anor v F. C. of T. 89 ATC 4070 at 4074-5; F. C. of T. v Cainero 88 ATC 4427 at 4435-36; Cooling v F. C. of T. 89 ATC 4731 at 4737, 4740).

35. Myer has been the subject of further consideration by the High Court of Australia in GP International Pipecoaters Pty Limited v Commissioner of Taxation (Unreported, 20 June 1990) where the passage cited above is referred to as indicating "the importance of ascertaining the scope of a business and a recipient's purpose in engaging in it as a means of determining the character of a receipt".

36. The facts of the case are remote from the present one and no purpose is to be served by making reference to them in detail. Suffice to say that the case involved the question of whether certain amounts received by the appellant under a contract as "establishment costs" were received by it on capital or revenue account. The monies were to be and were expended on the construction of an item of plant essential for the performance by the appellant of its contract work of coating pipes. The following passages from the judgment of the court contains matters of general principle of relevance in the present case:

"The question in this appeal is the character of the

establishment costs as receipts in the hands of the

taxpayer. `Whether or not a particular receipt is

income depends upon its quality in the hands of the

recipient': Scott v Federal Commissioner of Taxation

[1966] HCA 48; (1966) 117 CLR 514, per Windeyer J at p 526. The

relevant question is not the character of the

expenditure by (the payer). A receipt may be income

in the hands of a payee whether or not it is

expenditure of a capital nature by the payer. Nor is

the relevant question the nature of the expenditure

made by the taxpayer in the construction of the

plant. A taxpayer may apply income in the

acquisition of a capital asset or, conversely, apply

a capital receipt to discharge a liability of a

non-capital nature. As Cozens-Hardy M.R. observed in

Hudson's Bay Co v Stevens (1909) 5 TC 424, at

p 436:

`...if the money is otherwise liable to income

tax it cannot escape taxation by reason of its

being applied to a capital purpose'

And thus a receipt may be income although the

recipient is bound to apply it for the purpose of

discharging a capital liability: Mersey Docks v Lucas

(1883) 8 App Cas 198, at pp 904, 909-910;

Commissioners of Inland Revenue v Corporation of

London (as Conservators of Epping Forest) (1953) 34

TC 293, at p 329; (1953) 1 WLR 652, at p 670;

(1953) 1 All ER 1075, at pp 1089-1090. The

Commissioner and the taxpayer both treated the cost

of constructing the plant as capital expenditure, but

the question is not whether that was an expenditure

of capital nor whether the plant was used for the

purpose of producing assessable income so that

depreciation of the plant was deductible under s.54

of the Act. The relevant question is whether the

receipt of the establishment costs was income in the

taxpayer's hands. It is necessary to keep that

question steadily in mind and not confuse the

character of the receipt with the nature of the asset

acquired by application of the moneys received.

...

Although the amount received as establishment costs

was expended by, and was intended by (the payer) to

be expended by, the taxpayer to meet the costs of

constructing the plant so far as that amount would

extend, and although the amount expended on the

construction of the plant was a capital expenditure,

it does not follow that the taxpayer's receipt of the

establishment costs was a receipt of capital. To

determine whether a receipt is of an income or of a

capital nature, various factors may be relevant.

Sometimes, the character of receipts will be revealed

most clearly by their periodicity, regularity or

recurrence; sometimes, by the character of a right or

thing disposed of in exchange for the receipt;

sometimes, by the scope of the transaction, venture

or business in or by reason of which money is

received and by the recipient's purpose in engaging

in the transaction, venture or business. The factors

relevant to the ascertainment of the character of a

receipt of money are not necessarily the same as the

factors relevant to the ascertainment of the

character of its payment."

37. The Court went on to hold that: "the activity of the taxpayer corresponds with what the contract required. The contract defines both what the taxpayer was bound to do and the consideration for doing it. It bound the taxpayer to construct the plant and to coat the pipe required by (the payer) and conferred on the taxpayer a right to receive the moneys payable thereunder including the establishment costs. ...The establishment costs were received by the taxpayer under the contract as part of the monetary consideration payable for the taxpayer's agreement to perform, or its performance of, the entire contract... It earned the money by doing the work it had contracted to do. The establishment costs were not received in exchange for an item of capital or a right of a capital nature".

38. In my opinion, this later decision clearly indicates that Myer is not authority for any general proposition that a profit earned in the course of business operations is automatically an income receipt. It clearly emphasises that, in characterising the profit, regard must be had in the first instance to the true scope and nature of the business of the recipient. It follows that where monies are received attendant upon the removal from the business of some item of equipment, it must be relevant to determine whether it was an integral part of the operation of the business that such an item could be disposed of for gain. If, for instance, a taxpayer were in a business of hiring out articles for use by its customers, which articles were part of its fixed capital and subject to depreciation, but in respect of which it had a policy of disposing of them by sale at a point of time in their history when a profit could be gained over and above the amount necessary to recoup past allowances for depreciation, it could hardly be doubted that the earning of such profits, being so clearly within the ambit of the business operations, would produce receipts on revenue account. Memorex was clearly such a case. Where, however, the sale of such equipment occurs in circumstances where, at the time of the acquisition of the equipment for use in the business, there was no intention that it would be sold at a profit to the business at some time in the future, then, in the absence of some intervening factor which might alter its nature as a capital asset of the business, a profit subsequently realised on sale would be one gained on the mere realisation of a capital asset and would, consequently, be to capital account. It is clear that the "sales off hire" in the present case have been so regarded, and, in my opinion, rightly so.

39. I turn to the respondent's submissions. Contrary to the applicant's apprehensions, they did not embrace a contention that the profits on the "chargeable losses" were part of the applicant's gross income, and within s 25(1) of the Act simply because they were earned in the course of the business operations of the applicant. They were put as having the necessary revenue quality because their earning was an integral part of the business operations. Those business operations were not to be defined in merely general terms as the hiring of equipment for use and return by the hirer, but were to be defined in a particular way by reference to the contracts of hire used in the business. It was put, indeed, that the applicant's business consisted in the entering into with customers of its contracts of hiring and the fulfilling of its obligations under those contracts. By the performance of its contracts, it earned the monies which its customers were obliged to pay it under those contracts. Those monies were received on revenue account as being simply business earnings. In this regard compensation payments for loss or non-return of hired equipment under the contractual conditions which have been set out above were to be regarded merely as payments made in the ordinary course of business, taking their character from that fact and not from the fact that they were made in relation to the loss of items which formed part of the fixed capital of the applicant.

40. The submission was further developed by reference to the fact that the contracts, in terms, do not provide for the sale of the non-returned items to the customer, nor do they require that the applicant apply the compensation monies received from the customer to the acquisition of capital items to replace those that the customer fails to return. It must be doubted whether this latter consideration is of any relevance, having regard to the passages cited from GP International Pipecoaters. It would appear that the use to which the money was to be put by the applicant would have no bearing on its character as a receipt.

41. The submission as to the absence of any contractual provision requiring the purchase of the non-returned goods at the contractual price or deeming them to have been sold is also, in my view, of doubtful utility to the respondent's case. Had such a provision existed it would, one would think, have provided a strong argument that whatever might have been the capital status of the goods at the time of hire, they were, upon their non-return, by the operation of the contract, converted into trade goods to be compulsorily purchased by the customer at a price conferring a profit upon the applicant. The "sales off hire" might be thought to have more readily produced profits on capital account by reason of the fact that they took place outside the hiring contracts, which, on the respondent's submission, form the very fabric of the applicant's business operations. In my opinion these arguments do not assist in the characterisation of the profits as income.

42. The respondent also relies upon the fact that there was a certain regularity about the receipt of compensation payments under the hiring agreements. There is no hard evidence as to the frequency with which these payments were received by the applicant; however, the sums of money involved in the relevant years of income suggest that they were at least not infrequent. Regularity of receipt can, of course, confer, or go some distance towards conferring an income quality upon those receipts in accordance with the ordinary concepts and usages of mankind.

43. The respondent also puts its argument on the basis of the analysis of Brennan J in the passage already cited from Federal Coke. He does so by describing the consideration given by the applicant, as recipient, as being simply the hiring-out of the items of equipment. In consideration of the hiring, it receives the promises, and performance of those promises where appropriate, of the customer under the terms of the hiring agreement. It is pointed out that the agreement is described as a "hiring" agreement and deals with the rights and obligations of the parties in relation to a hiring. "Hiring" is the applicant's business. It fulfils its side of the contract of hire and, relevantly, provides its consideration, when it provides its equipment on hire. Any payments which it receives under the agreement it receives for the hiring of its equipment. Such payments include compensatory payments for loss or non-return under the contracts. Being monies received in consideration of the hiring of the equipment, they necessarily bear an income character.

44. I have found these arguments attractive, but in the ultimate I have rejected them. I do not think it appropriate to view these contracts of hire in such a broad way. When examined closely, it is clear that they distinguish between receipts which are properly described as hiring charges for the equipment and payments received under the clauses providing for compensation. Quite clearly a different character is accorded to the compensation payment from that attributed to the hiring charges. Where there is a relevant non-return, the hiring charges are to continue until the compensatory payment is made. This clearly accords, in my view, with the conception that the hiring charges are attributable to the use by the customer under the contract of the applicant's capital asset whilst the compensatory payment relates not to its use but to the fact that the customer has deprived the applicant of it. Although payable under a provision in a contract described as a "hiring" contract, it does not relate to hiring as such. The receipt of money by the applicant under this provision is not a receipt of hiring monies; it is a receipt of money intended to compensate him for the loss of the object hired. The consideration provided by the applicant for this receipt is not the provision of the goods on hire. It is, in my opinion, a forbearance from suing for the return of the goods or their value. The goods to which this consideration relates are part of the profit-earning capital of the applicant. They do not lose that character simply because they have been lost or disposed of by the customer. Such regularity of receipt of compensation as is established does not, in my view, operate in this case to confer an income quality in the profits.

45. I am satisfied that the profits on "chargeable losses", even though made in the course of the applicant's business operations, are earned on capital and not revenue account and are therefore not brought to tax under s 25(1) of the Act.

46. I have come to this decision independently of any consideration as to whether I am bound, in any event, to come to it as a result of the decision of the majority in Cyclone. I am satisfied on the submissions of counsel and from a reading of the judgments in that case that, for practical purposes, no difference exists in the contractual documents under which Cyclone hired out equipment basically similar to that of the applicant to its customers who put it to the same use as the applicant's customers. The applicant and Cyclone are clearly in competition in the same market. In the case of Cyclone, as I have already indicated, hire goods and trade goods were held in the same pool. Cyclone did not operate on a divisional structure as did the applicant. Consequently it adopted a system of accounting which was designed to give a true reflex of its income earning position and which paid due heed to the fact that in any income year certain pieces of equipment would be sold as from trading stock, other pieces would be let on hire in the ordinary way and other pieces would be lost to the company through non-return or disposal by the hirer. In this last circumstance, the hiring contract called for compensation in the same way as did the applicant's contract, with the equivalent profit results. The Commissioner had accepted this method of accounting for the purpose of assessment of tax until, in respect of the years in dispute, he declined to do so. The adjustments that the Commissioner sought involved treating the amounts received by way of compensation for loss as being income. In doing so, he advanced arguments, which were obviously accepted by Wilcox J in his dissenting judgment as being correct. It is clear that these arguments corresponded fairly closely to those which have been put on behalf of the Commissioner in this case.

47. Although it is said in the majority judgment that the question in the appeal was whether the accounting procedures adopted by Cyclone were calculated to give a substantially correct reflex of its income, I am satisfied that the question whether the compensation profits were or were not income must also have arisen. The amounts received in this regard were described as having been received from "notional sales", a description which accords with the concept of sale forced by law upon a convertor of goods. It appears (see e.g. p187 et seq) that the majority (Bowen CJ and Beaumont J) regarded these goods, the subject of the notional sales, as "fixed capital"..."part of the taxpayer's profit-making apparatus as distinct from its stock in trade". In respect of such equipment, their Honours (at p188 et seq) appear to accept that such goods when sold directly or "as a result of loss or destruction of hired equipment" should be "treated as sales of plant, that is of capital items and as such non-taxable".

48. In another passage (p190) reference is made to the fact that "the sales in question are treated as the disposal of part of the taxpayer's profit-making apparatus and thus are fixed assets. Any profit thus accruing is on capital, not revenue account".

49. There is, however, a problem, in that their Honours are in these passages dealing with the way in which the taxpayer Cyclone had treated the relevant items in its accounts and, indeed, may be doing no more than repeating the taxpayer's own assertions as to the accuracy and propriety of so doing. However, it would seem, that there was before their Honours an issue as to whether the general approach, reflected in the passages I have cited was one which was sustainable in income tax law. Their Honours certainly did not reject the taxpayer's method of accounting as being vitiated by an obvious legal flaw. It may be, however, that the judgment falls short of being a final affirmation of the position that the taxpayer Cyclone's receipt of the equivalent of the applicant's compensation monies was a receipt on capital account. It may amount to no more than a finding that this was not obviously wrong, the final point of decision being that the Commissioner, in all the circumstances, was not entitled to challenge it.

50. In any event as I have come independently to the conclusion that, the taxpayer's objection should be upheld, it is not necessary for me to determine whether the majority judgment in Cyclone is binding upon me or not. I, therefore, decline to do so.

51. I order in respect of each appeal that it be upheld; that the decision of the respondent in relation to the applicant's objections be set aside; that there be substituted a decision allowing those objections and that the relevant assessments be amended accordingly. I further order that the respondent pay the applicant's costs of these proceedings.
