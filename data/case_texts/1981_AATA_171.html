Re Trevor George Mackrell and Commissioner of Superannuation [1981] AATA 171 (15 December 1981)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TREVOR GEORGE MACKRELL

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V81/208

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

The Honourable Sir William Prentice (Senior Member)

Dr H.W. Garlick (Member)

Mr I. Prowse (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - determination of retirement benefits - medical examination - whether failure to furnish full information - whether furnishing of false information - issue of benefit classification certificate

Superannuation Act 1922, s.5, s.48B

Superannuation Act 1978, s.184(2), (3), (5)

HEARING

CANBERRA

15:12:1981

ORDER

The Tribunal decides to revoke and set aside the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation:

(a) to issue pursuant to section 184(5) of the Superannuation Act 1978 any benefit classification certificate in respect of the applicant specifying "retinal or choroidal migraine" or "mild migraine variation";

(b) that the applicant's benefit entitlement in ceasing to be an eligible employee on 10 October 1979 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity fell to be determined under sub-section 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1978.

DECISION

The Application

1. The applicant joined the Commonwealth Public Service in 1966 aged 18, and thereafter became a contributor to the Superannuation Fund under the Superannuation Act 1922. Over the years he apparently developed a crippling condition of migraine (variously described by the doctors as "retinal" or "choroidal" migraine or "mild migraine variation"); and following medical examinations he was retired for invalidity on 10 October 1979 after only some 13 years service. Thereupon the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") issued a benefit classification certificate ("B.C.C.") which would have the effect of reducing considerably the amounts to which he would be entitled as superannuation benefits (section 66(2) the 1978 Act). The Commissioner grounded his actions in regard thereto on the facts that the applicant had not served 20 years in the Public Service, and had, when entering the Service, at a medical examination (a) failed to disclose information which he was required to disclose and (b) given false information. This was particularized in document T2 as follows:

"If at or in connection with the medical examination he underwent on 11 August 1966 he had disclosed the fact that he had suffered from 'retinal or choroidal migraine' he would not have been accepted by the Superannuation Board under s.5 of the Superannuation Act 1922 as a contributor to the Fund under Part III of that Act."

2. The B.C.C. Issued on 12 August 1980 by the Commissioner specified a condition of "retinal or choroidal migraine". After reconsideration, the Commissioner varied the B.C.C. so as to specify a condition of "mild migraine variation". The medical examination upon which action was taken under s.67 of the 1978 Act to retire, resulted in a certification of a "condition" of "retinal migraine - 100%".

3. The applicant appeals against the hedging of his superannuation benefits by the issue of a B.C.C. in any form.

The Grounds of the Commissioner's Actions

4. The action taken to issue a B.C.C. springs essentially from the answers given in 1966 when filling in the medical examination form, supported, the Commissioner contends, by medical histories taken by examining doctors around the time of retirement. The said form, the Tribunal concludes from the way in which it was filled in, was not attended to in the presence of the doctor. The questionnaire and answers are reproduced as appendix "A" hereto. This appears from the facts that though the first question is the only one requiring a reference to family, as distinct from personal history, the applicant carried his dual answers to question (1), down through each of the questions though obliterating the "family" reference which he had apparently made in answering question 2(b); and that what could be regarded as non-responsive answers were made to the "personal" section (the "family" section here being struck out) of question (11).

5. To question 2(d) - "Have you suffered from (if so give details and dates) - epilepsy, fits, headaches, migraine, dizziness or turns of any kind?"; the applicant returned the answer "No - No". To question 2(1) - "Have you ever suffered from . . . any other complaint or illness not shown above?"; he also answered "No - No". To question 11 - "When were you last under medical treatment (a) date (b) for what reason?"; the answers "No - No" were given to (a) and (b).

6. We will return to a further consideration of these answers; but should note at this point that to question 10 - "Do you wear glasses or contact lenses? If so please bring them with you"; the answers "Glasses - father, mother, sister" were given. Part 2 of the form indicates that the examining medical officer made a vision check and expressed the finding that there were no "other health conditions which it is advisable should be mentioned." We pause to mention the opinion of members of the Tribunal that a more accurate picture could be obtained if the examining medical officer himself supervised the answering of the form's questions. If such had been done in the applicant's case, the inaptness of his answers as to "family" history and his "non-responsive" answers to question 11 would have been picked up and the latter possibly made the subject of supplementary questions. We have used the term "non-responsive" in the light of medical evidence before us that to a doctor, "medical treatment" would include a visit to an eye specialist for check as to suitability of spectacles and for a possible change of prescription thereto; though the applicant has sworn that to him "complaint" in question 2(1) and "medical treatment" in question 11 connoted "some sort of sickness", just as "suffered" connoted "long term" (thus he did not regard his visit to the opthalmologist Sir Thomas Travers for vision check and glasses, as "treatment").

7. In the event counsel for the respondent Commissioner eschewed any reliance upon the answer "no" qua the phrase "turns of any kind" 2(d); and later abandoned any reliance at all upon the answer "no" to question 2(d).

8. The medical histories upon which the Commissioner relied, were those taken by Dr Greenberg, Dr King and Sir Thomas Travers, as reported on by Dr Hackett.

9. Dr King, to whom the applicant was referred by Dr Greenberg, appears to have seen him once only - on 11 September 1978. His report states: "at the time he left school which was in 1965, he began to develop attacks of visual loss in the left eye. Initially they occurred once or twice yearly but more recently, they have been occurring as frequently as once weekly." After finding a condition of "retinal migraine", he continued: "on one hand, it seems that he has now overlooked a history of migraine which started when he left school, however, migraine as such was not known to him to be the appropriate diagnosis" (emphasis ours), "and if he had been aware that he suffered from migraine, I would have felt that this was something which could be reasonably neglected in giving details of one's medical history. I don't know whether the particular question was asked and it seems unlikely that he could have been expected to have answered this in the affirmative at that time."

10. Dr Greenberg, reporting on 9 July 1979, states that he had seen Mr Mackrell on about 8 occasions over the last 2 years; "he has had episodes of retinal or choroidol migraine which causes him to lose sight in one or other eye, usually the left eye, for up to 10 minutes. These episodes began when he was doing examinations at school and have tended to recur at times of stress."

11. In our opinion neither Dr King's phrased history "he began to develop attacks", nor Dr Greenberg's "these episodes began" (both using the plural) are necessarily inconsistent with the version that the applicant has sworn to, viz., that he had one attack only (that while doing his Leaving Certificate at school in 1965) before undergoing the Public Service medical examination in August 1966, and that he recognizes that possibly to have been such an onset of "migraine" of some kind, only recently, with the benefit of hindsight and recently expressed medical opinion.

12. It should, however, be added that a medical officer first examining the applicant with a view to his retirement, summarized his medical history in July 1979 as revealing "transient loss of vision of the left eye for the last 10 or 15 years in infrequent attacks lasting from 1 to 15 minutes without other symptoms". The reference to "15 years" which might take the first attack back to 1964 was not pressed upon the applicant in cross examination, which having regard to the time lapse involved, the applicant's medical condition in 1979, and his age in 1964/65, appears with respect, a proper and reasonable attitude for respondent's counsel to have adopted.

13. And again, Sir Thomas Travers' notes of an examination in February 1966 read as to history "lately" (emphasis ours) "left blurs in a way rather like a migraine but no nausea or headache." The applicant was not cross examined as to this in any attempt to suggest that there had been more than one episode of "blurriness" before the Public Service entry examination - though some of Dr King's phrases in his history notes had been so put to him. Sir Thomas Travers in sworn evidence said he felt that his 1966 notation "lately" would have been consistent with a history then of one "blurring" only. In a statement made 13 October 1980 however, Sir Thomas had also said "I next saw him in February 1966. He was then complaining of an occasional blur in the left eye. I thought this was a mild migraine variation" - this report as to his 1966 opinion does not agree with his sworn evidence.

The Legislation

14. The Commissioner's power to issue a B.C.C. of the kind which he did, is essentially dependent upon s.184(5)(d)(i) of the Act as amended in 1978. Whatever the remainder of s.184(5)(d) may mean (or be intended to mean), it is at least clear (and that is all that is clear about it) that the Commissioner must be satisfied that at or in connection with the medical examination the applicant underwent for the purposes of the Superseded Act, the applicant failed to furnish any information required to be furnished by him or furnished false information. Complicated and detailed argument was directed to the Tribunal as to the construction of the provisions of s.184 and other sections of the 1978 Act and of the 1922 Act, including an interesting submission that under s.48B of the 1922 Act a right to full superannuation benefits became vested in the applicant once 3 years had elapsed without his being retired on the ground of invalidity - a right which had not been expressly divested by the 1976 or 1978 Acts.

15. As the Tribunal when differently constituted has pointed out, in several preceding decisions, S.184 raises well-nigh insuperable difficulties; and for ourselves we are by no means confident that a sensible construction of the 1978 Act can be arrived at even if the new s.15AA of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 be prayed in aid. However, in the view we take of the facts we consider it unnecessary to attempt the Herculean task of construing it and its predecessor.

Findings

16. We find as a fact, from the sworn evidence of the applicant and of Sir Thomas Travers that at the time of the Commonwealth medical examination in August 1966 the applicant had met with one episode only of blurriness of vision to the left eye (and that for a few seconds) in 1965 prior to doing his final examinations - that the second such episode occurred some 18-24 months later. Despite the elliptical phrases, which have been referred to above, in each of the histories as recorded by Dr King and Dr Greenberg and in the 1980 report of Sir Thomas Travers, respondent's counsel ultimately conceded that there was no evidence to the contrary.

17. We accept the evidence of Sir Thomas Travers that (a) he definitely did not diagnose a migraine condition in February 1966 (despite his written report in 1980), and that his opinion then was that clearly the applicant did not then suffer from migraine; (b) that he did not then think the episode of a few seconds blurring was a matter that should concern the applicant in any way - that he would have been unlikely to have mentioned his own notation of symptoms similar to "migraine scotoma" to the applicant, and hoped that he had given the applicant the impression that he had nothing to worry about. He himself "did not think it was a matter of any concern for the applicant at all one way or the other". He was "quite sure he (the applicant) did not have anything to worry about." He would have given the applicant "a clean bill of health", he said, in answer to the question - "If you had been consulted about Mr Mackrell in August, September or October 1966 and asked whether or not, knowing all you knew of him, he was a person who would be accepted to the Superannuation Fund and would have the ability to last the distance until aged 65, what would you have said, Sir?" Asked at that time, he stated, he would have said the applicant would quite definitely have lasted the distance until 1965 . . . "You could not say he had a migraine. I think it would be quite wrong" (to say so).

18. In the witness box, Sir Thomas' attention was drawn to the provisions of the notes to medical officers on the "examination of candidates for admittance to the Commonwealth service;" his view was that if asked in February 1966 in terms of migraine and eyesight he would "quite certainly" have said the applicant met the standard. And he would not really have regarded a transient thing like one blurriness of the eye as "a complaint". In particular in regard to the question "Have you ever suffered from . . . ?", Sir Thomas" view was that in February 1966 the applicant could not be said to have been "suffering" from blurriness of vision at all.

19. We further find as facts that the applicant was not referred to Sir Thomas Travers in February 1966; but as he was about to enter the Service, went of his own motion to have a check of his vision and prescription of spectacles, and merely incidentally mentioned the one episode of blurriness of vision; and that no one had suggested to him at the time of filling in the form in August 1966 that the episode of blurriness in 1965 had been "migraine" of any kind. It is interesting that Dr Davis, the applicant's regular doctor over 15 years, had never treated him prior to 1978 for "mild migraine variations, retinal or choroidal migraine or blurred vision."

20. The Tribunal notes that under paragraphs 132 and 133 of the guidelines to Commonwealth medical officers an applicant would normally be accepted for the Superannuation Fund if he had no attack of migraine during a period of 6 months or more of medical probation. A period of some 10 or 11 months attack-free (after the first episode) had elapsed when the applicant was examined and accepted for admittance to the Superannuation Fund.

21. We are of the opinion that the information actually given by the applicant in filling in the medical examination form, might not be thought to be "full information". But in view of the transient and miniscule nature of the single episode of blurriness of vision, which clearly was regarded as and treated as of no significance by Sir Thomas, the applicant could not reasonably have been expected to regard himself as having "suffered from any other complaint or illness" in regard thereto. We accept incidentally, the applicant's understanding of "suffering" as connoting something of some significant duration (as to which he had been, and intentionally, disabused by Sir Thomas). We understand (despite what an optician or optometrist might allege) that a doctor would regard the checking of vision and prescription of spectacles as "medical treatment", but the Tribunal finds no difficulty in accepting that the applicant (and indeed many, possibly most laymen) would regard such a visit to a doctor as not involving "medical treatment."

22. We do not regard his inappropriate answers to question 11, therefore, as false - more particularly when seen underneath the immediately preceding answer, indicating by implication that he had been found (by an eye specialist) to have had defective vision needing the correction of glasses.

23. The Tribunal is quite able to understand the view taken by the Commissioner in the light of the medical reports of Drs King, Greenberg, Hackett, Travers and others, but we, its members, have had the benefit of sworn and tested evidence from the applicant and Sir Thomas Travers. We conclude that there has been neither failure to furnish information nor furnishing of false information such as should ground the Commissioner's action under s.184(5) of the Act. And we are confident that if the episode of blurriness of vision had been recorded in the applicant's answers, the matter would have been referred to Sir Thomas Travers, then a senior ophthalmologist and specialist (and treating doctor) and would have produced a certificate of fitness for admission to the Superannuation Fund in accordance indeed with paragraphs 4, 132 and 133 of the Medical Guidelines.

24. The Tribunal is of the opinion therefore that the Commissioner's decision to reduce the Superannuation entitlements of the applicant by the issue of a B.C.C., cannot be supported in the light of the evidence brought before it, and should therefore be revoked and set aside.

25. Our decision will be that the decision of the respondent to issue a B.C.C. pursuant to the provisions of s.184(5) of the 1978 Act, should be set aside and revoked; and further that his decision that the applicant's Superannuation entitlement should be decided under s.66(2) of the Act should also be set aside.
