Sormaz and Commissioner for Superannuation [1997] AATA 760 (2 May 1997)

Last Updated: 9 January 2009

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION NO 11830

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No V96/852

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )



Re NIKOLA SORMAZ

Applicant



And THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal Mr J. Handley, Senior Member

Mr G. Woodard, Member

Mr J. Brassil, Member

Date 2 May 1997

Place Melbourne

Decision The decision under review be set aside and in substitution therefore IT IS DECIDED that the election made by the applicant be recognised and be treated as if it were made within the time allowed under s.137 of the Superannuation Act.

..............................................

Senior Member

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Application for late election - applicant resigned in 1982 and received contributions then made - rejoined Public Service in 1991 and made election in 1993 - whether late election should be recognised.

Superannuation Act 1976 - s.137, 157

Re Ward and Commissioner for Superannuation [1995] AATA 19; (1995) 36 ALD 287

Re Drake and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs [1979] AATA 179; (1980) (2 ALD 634 at p. 642-643)

Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239

Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 50 FCR 150

Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 3 FCR 344

REASONS FOR DECISION

2 May 1997 Mr J. Handley, Senior Member

Mr G. Woodard, Member

Mr J. Brassil, Member



  1. The applicant applies to review a decision of a delegate of the respondent of 24 May 1994, subsequently affirmed upon reconsideration on 20 September 1995 not to direct that an election, made by him on 23 December 1993 to preserve superannuation benefits, be treated as if the election had been made within the period prescribed by the Superannuation Act 1976 (“the Act”).
  2. Upon the hearing, Mr Sormaz who is presently an employee of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal, appeared unrepresented. Mr Howe appeared on behalf of the respondent. Documents provided by the respondent pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act were received into evidence as were a number of other documents which will be referred to in these reasons.

THE LEGISLATION

  1. Section 137(1)-(3) of the Superannuation Act 1976 says as follows-
“137.(1) A person who:

(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which paragraph (b) does not apply and, upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or

(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue of which the person is to be deemed, under subsection 58(3), to have retired involuntarily;

may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner, that this Division apply in relation to the person.

(2) If a person makes an election for the purposes of this section within 1 month before he ceases to be an eligible employee, the election has effect as if it had been made on the day after he ceased to be an eligible employee.

(3) Subject to subsection (4) an election under this section is of no effect unless the person who made the election gives notice in writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, after the expiration of the period that is the prescribed period in relation to him:

(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of the prescribed period and, if so, the name and address of his employer; and

(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the prescribed period a member of the superannuation scheme applicable in relation to that employment and, if so, specifying the scheme concerned.”
  1. Section 157(1) of the Act says as follows-
“157.(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly”.

THE FACTS

  1. A useful summary of the background of this application is to be found in the statement of facts and contentions filed by the respondent prior to the hearing. It says as follows-
“1. The Applicant was born on 3 November 1950.

2. On 27 July 1971 the Applicant became a member of the superannuation scheme established under the Superannuation Act 1922. The Applicant was an “eligible employee” for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1976 (the Act) at its commencement on 1 July 1976.

3. On 5 March 1982 the Applicant submitted his resignation from the Australian Federal Police, to have effect on 13 April 1982.

4. On 13 April 1982 the Applicant completed a form S2A benefit application and applied for a refund of contributions. On 5 May 1982 an amount of $7,866.65 was paid to the Applicant.

5. On 6 May 1991 the Applicant became a member of the Public Sector Superannuation Scheme established under the Superannuation Act 1990 after joining the Australian Archives.

6. On 23 December 1993 the Applicant wrote to the Respondent seeking to make a late election for preservation of the Applicant’s superannuation benefits in accordance with s.137(1) of the Act.

7. On 24 May 1994 a delegate of the Respondent Commissioner for Superannuation decided that in all the circumstances of the case it was not desirable that the election under s.137(1) made by the Applicant should be recognised.

8. Pursuant to s.154(2) of the Act, by letter dated 23 June 1994 the Applicant applied for reconsideration of the delegate’s decision.

9. On 20 September 1995 another delegate of the Respondent confirmed, on reconsideration, the decision of 24 May 1994 that the election under s.137(1) made by the Applicant should not be recognised.

10. On 14 October 1995 the Applicant applied to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review.”
  1. In making his application for late election for preservation of superannuation benefits, Mr Sormaz wrote to the respondent on 23 December 1993. That letter appears at page 65 of the T-documents, and omitting formal parts, said as follows-
“I separated from the Commonwealth Public Service on 13 April 1982, after having joined the Superannuation Fund under the Superannuation Act 1976 on 27 July 1971, and request that the late election made by this letter under the 1976 Act for preservation of superannuation rights be recognised under section 157(1) of the Act.

At the time of my separation I was employed with the Attorney General’s Department in Canberra. I recollect signing a form, which I understand was a Form S2A. I cannot recall crossing out any of the options contained in that form, but I did speak to an officer in Personnel at the time. I received a refund of contributions, the amount being $7,866.65.

I have a clear recollection of what was, and was not, conveyed to me in my discussion with the Personnel officer. What was conveyed was I had a one time choice as to what to do. I was given no information as to the option to elect for preservation for superannuation rights. In particular I was not given any information about employer contributions that would be applicable under that option. There was no suggestion that I should take advice in relation to making the choice such as that I speak to an accountant or to the superannuation office.

What I did with the refund, which amounted to $7,866.65, was to invest it in the home that I was then building and purchasing at Fadden. It helped me to achieve a situation in which I was able to build and pay for that home without any mortgage. I had no use for spending the money otherwise than by this investment.

After the 1982 separation I worked in my own right as a computer consultant, and set up a family trust and a superannuation scheme. The scheme was set up on 16 November 1982 and details can be provided if required. Attached is a completed “Preservation of Superannuation Rights - Notice” relating to my employment/superannuation status three months after my separation.

I rejoined public sector employment on 6 May 1991 and joined the PSS. There was no induction or other briefing provided that made me aware of the contents of the preservation of superannuation rights option or of the ability to make a late election.

My awareness of these matters has been brought about by recent articles in the “Canberra Times”, confirmed by reading an Australian Federal Police Association National Bulletin dated 21 October 1993 which was given to me about a week later by a close friend who is a serving AFP officer. I then sought accountancy and other advice on what my rights were.

My basic position is that the law gave me an election which through lack of information and misleading advice, I never exercised until this letter. Properly advised I would have chosen the preservation option.”
  1. The primary decision made by a delegate of the respondent is found at T-4 pages 16-20 inclusive. Upon receipt of that decision the applicant applied for reconsideration. Two letters were written by him on 23 & 24 June 1994 in support of the request for reconsideration. The reviewable decision is found at T-2 pages 2-12 inclusive, made on 20 September 1995.
  2. Mr Sormaz, when giving evidence, said upon his resignation from the Australian Federal Police and when his application for refund of superannuation contributions was then made, he was given “advice” which “was misleading” because there was “no mention of late elections or any other such thing” (transcript p. 10). He said there was also no mention made to him of deferred benefit or returning to the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme or obtaining accountancy advice. He admitted that he was advised that his application for accumulated benefit was “a one time option” but said that having asked “the officer” for advice he relied on personnel staff within the Federal Police because “they were the experts about the rules and regulations of the public service which I thought included superannuation”. He said that it has subsequently become apparent to him that the advice then given to him was “misleading”. To this extent Mr Sormaz referred to the application for refund of accumulated contributions (a copy of the form appears at page 55 of the T-documents) which requires the applicant to either “apply for a refund of accumulated contributions” or “apply for payment of a lump sum benefit” or “elect for preservation of superannuation rights”. Insofar as the form discloses that the last two options were crossed out it would appear from the form that Mr Sormaz applied for a refund of accumulated contributions.
  3. Subsequent to his resignation from the public service Mr Sormaz entered self employment and engaged a firm of accountants to establish a family trust and a superannuation company. He filed a copy of profit and loss statement for the “Nikola Sormaz Family Trust” for the 1986 financial year which showed superannuation contributions to that trust of $6,717. Mr Sormaz said that this was “a considerable sum” and it demonstrated his “commitment to superannuation”.
  4. Subsequent to resignation from the Federal Police, Mr Sormaz purchased and sold real estate held by him jointly with his wife and entered into a number of contracts and consultancies with private enterprise and semi government organisations in Canberra. Mr Sormaz produced copies of letters of appointments to these engagements which he said supported his argument of ignorance of superannuation rights at or about that time because the letters referred to salary only.
  5. The applicant said that he first became aware of his rights to apply for late election to preserve superannuation benefits in October 1993 when a relative gave him a copy of a Bulletin published by the Australian Federal Police Association, identified in these proceedings as S.12 and received into evidence. Subsequently Mr Sormaz sought advice from a former colleague who assisted him prepare his application for late election. At that time Mr Sormaz was a member of the Commonwealth Public Service. Upon rejoining the Public Service he said that he was not given any induction training nor given any other advice concerning superannuation or rights as to late election.
  6. In cross-examination, despite not having an accurate memory of whether his wife was or was not working at the time he resigned from the Federal Police in 1982, Mr Sormaz was adamant that he had a clear recollection of discussions he held with a personnel officer concerning his superannuation rights. He was referred to his letter of 23 December 1993 (T-18 p.64) where he said the concept of preservation of superannuation rights was then “alien” to him and denied specifically that he was given information upon the availability of preservation of his superannuation. He denied that with a passage of 11 1/2 years until he commenced these proceedings that his memory may have failed him and said-
“The reason I can say no (in answer to the question of whether he had forgotten the discussions of 1982) is, I got a distinction in accounting, I got distinctions in operations research, I understand nett present values. I understand the meaning of money and how to judge an investment against another investment and a record can be looked up to prove that. There was no issue of understanding what the options were. I was an informed person and I was an intelligent person and I understand those concepts and I did at the time and my results in studies were at distinction level.”
  1. When asked the date upon which his superannuation company was established Mr Sormaz said that he believed it was 16 November 1982 but was unable to produce a copy of the Superannuation Fund Trust Deed. He acknowledged that he had been served with a summons by the respondent to produce the document and said that despite having made attempts to locate it, he had been unsuccessful. He denied that it was set up before the end of the 1982 financial year and explained that no contributions were made to the fund until the 1985/86 financial year “because the incomes in that first year was the formative year, the cash flow situation just wasn’t there”. By way of further explanation Mr Sormaz said that although he was earning more money in private enterprise than he earned with the Federal Police he had a number of major costs associated with establishing his self employment. He did acknowledge that at least from November 1982 when the superannuation fund was established until some time in the 1986 financial year, payments were not made to the fund. He later said that the sum of $6,717 was paid into the fund as a single payment only some time after 1 July 1985 and before 30 June 1986. The fund he said was subsequently wound up as part of property proceedings instituted in the Family Court in either 1987 or 1988. He admitted that he was advised by his accountant that the family trust be established to provide “taxation benefits” but denied that the superannuation fund was set up also for that purpose.
  2. Mr Sormaz agreed that his family trust was retrospectively assessed as having a liability for taxation in the sum of approximately $11,000 with respect to rulings that were made as to the distribution of monies from that trust. Mr Sormaz said that he did not agree then or now with that ruling but nonetheless paid the sum.
  3. Mr Sormaz also agreed that upon the dissolution of his marriage and the payment of monies under a family court property settlement he became entitled to “somewhere between $11,000-$15,000” upon the winding up of the superannuation fund however he said that he did not receive any benefit of those monies because he “gave” them to his wife upon the settlement. He acknowledged that on 4 September 1996 he received a letter from the respondent requesting documentary evidence regarding the superannuation fund and requesting advice also as to whether he continued to be a member and/or the location of proceeds of the fund if it was no longer existing or if he was no longer a member. Mr Sormaz acknowledged that he did not respond to the letter because he said that he was suffering from “acute anxiety”, “acute hypertension” and “a very high cholesterol level”. He said he perceived “ComSuper” as not “trying to assist me” and “not interested in helping me”.
  4. With respect to the sum of $6,717 referred to earlier having been paid by the family trust into the superannuation company in the 1985/86 financial year, Mr Sormaz denied that that payment was a “book entry” only. He acknowledged that the superannuation rules applicable to his superannuation company required the trustee - Concept Computer Pty Ltd of which he was a director - to keep records. Mr Sormaz relied on a copy of the 1985/86 income tax return produced into evidence as proof of the payment of that sum. He was unable to produce any records for the 1986/87 financial year in proof of payment by the family trust to the superannuation company within that year. He acknowledged that he did not attend the office of his former solicitors to determine whether they had a copy of the superannuation fund deed. He said his former wife who worked for that firm had told him that those documents probably would not have been retained. He acknowledged also that his present accountants attended the office of his former accountant in February 1992 and obtained a copy of files entitled “the Nikola Sormaz Family Trust Deed, an unsigned trust deed and a copy of a memorandum and articles of association.” Mr Sormaz said that he has not subsequently enquired whether his accountants hold the superannuation trust deed or the family trust deed. He said he did not make those enquiries because “it didn’t occur to” him.
  5. With respect to the completion by him of an application for refund of accumulated superannuation contributions on 13 April 1982 Mr Sormaz said that whilst he was aware that he would have been permitted to retain his contributions in the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund or he was permitted to apply for a refund of contributions to be paid to him by way of lump sum he did not regard the choice that he then had to make as being important or significant. He did say that he believed then that the choice he was making to be a “one time thing” only. He did regard the monies then payable to him as being “considerable” but did not understand that he was able to obtain advice from the Superannuation Commissioners office. He said at that time he was not “aware the Commissioner existed”. Similarly he made no enquiries or consider rates of interest applicable should he have elected to have preserved his superannuation monies in the Commonwealth Scheme.
  6. Mr Sormaz said that he could not recall signing the application form (T-11 - p.55) in the presence of the persons whose signature appear at the bottom of the form (identified by Mr Howe as Graham Bowman and Mark Slater). Nor could he recall whether he crossed out the options available with respect to either payment or preservation of superannuation monies or whether someone else deleted those options but he was adamant that he was not advised then as to preservation of superannuation monies.
  7. Mr Sormaz was asked to comment upon a number of purchases and sales by him and his former wife of real estate in the 1980’s. He denied that he intended by applying for a refund of accumulated contributions to secure monies to purchase property without mortgage. The family home was financed by the proceeds of sale of another property and the superannuation refund of $7,866.65. Mr Sormaz said that for the sake of $7,866.65 he would have qualified for a personal loan and would not have needed to take out a mortgage in the event that there were insufficient funds to allow purchase of property at that time. Upon his return to the public service in 1991 Mr Sormaz said that it did not occur to him at all that he should make enquiries as to his superannuation rights having regard to having obtained a refund of previous superannuation contributions prior to his exit in 1982. He said he made a decision in 1982 to leave the Commonwealth Public Service to attempt employment in the private sector and had no knowledge then that he would ever return to Commonwealth employment. He acknowledged when he made application for employment with Australian Archives in 1991 that he advised (T-16 page 62) that a “lump sum payment” was “taken on termination” from his previous commonwealth employment. He denied a suggestion made to him by Mr Howe that his disclosure of having taken a “lump sum payment” was evidence of knowledge that the payment, then, was an alternative to leaving contributions in the fund at 1982. He said he did not know that a pension was available to him at 1982 nor did he know that he could have left monies in the fund.
  8. Later Mr Sormaz became an employee of the Commissioner for Superannuation. He said that having made his application for late election in December 1993 he was frequently participating in meetings where a policy of the Commissioner was discussed by way of it being developed with respect to guidelines applicable for applications for late election. He said the meetings were conducted in his presence and with the knowledge of other persons present that he was an applicant for late election. He said the discussions were conducted upon the basis of applications being considered by reference to the informal policy rather than “considering the matters on their merits”. Mr Sormaz denied that he took notes of the meetings and acknowledged also that he did not intend to call any persons present at those meetings to give evidence in support of his application. Mr Sormaz said that persons present at the meeting “were circumspect in the way they discuss things” and other persons present were aware that Mr Sormaz had made an election for preservation of benefits. He said the meetings frequently discussed Administrative Appeals Tribunal decisions such as “Boardman” and “Ward” and officers present frequently gave an “update”. He said discussions also centred from time to time upon the “cost to the government of permitting persons to make a late election.” He said it was discussed that if persons were allowed to make late elections, the cost to the Commissioner for Superannuation would be reduced but that decisions of that type “would not go down very well with the Government”. Mr Sormaz said at this stage of the evidence as he did in his opening that the respondent “in terms of deciding cases has been in fact biased towards officers of the government and not towards the interests of the members”. He did acknowledge however that he did not allege against the respondent any “impropriety”.

CONCLUSION & REASONS FOR DECISION

  1. This application involves a consideration of the provisions of s.137 and s.157 of the Superannuation Act. In effect it requires consideration of whether recognition should be given to the application for election to preserve superannuation benefits as if that application had been made within the time limit prescribed by s.137. That time limit requires an election to have been made within 21 days after ceasing to be “an eligible employee”. In this case Mr Sormaz submitted his resignation from employment in March 1982 effective on 13 April 1982. He subsequently returned to Commonwealth employment and made an application to elect to preserve superannuation benefits on 23 December 1993.
  2. Despite the passage of 11 years this application will necessarily involve a consideration of a number of other similar applications heard by this Tribunal and the Federal Court in recent years as to the principles to be applied under s.157 so as to determine upon review whether “it is desirable that the late election be recognised”.
  3. It would appear, having regard to the relatively large number of applications for late election and the comments made by Deputy President McMahon and Members Attwood and Julian in Re Ward and Commissioner for Superannuation [1995] AATA 19; (1995) 36 ALD 287 that the respondent has published guidelines to assist - we assume - persons who are applying for late election because those persons would become aware of the issues that the Commissioner would consider in exercising the discretion he is required to exercise under s.157 (refer Re Ward at p. 293 - refer also Re Adams and Commissioner for Superannuation 23 AAR 309 at 326). At the commencement of the hearing of this application Mr Sormaz brought to our attention these guidelines which were sent to him in October 1996 by the respondent. Mr Sormaz submitted that we should follow those guidelines and find in his favour. He submitted that his circumstances fitted within the guidelines and we should find accordingly. We confess that we were taken by surprise concerning the attitude expressed by counsel for the respondent when these guidelines were brought to our attention at the commencement of the hearing. It was said that the “document is not capable of attracting any weight of itself to the extent to which it might represent a correct statement of principle” (transcript p. 4) and when pressed further by us where it was suggested that the guidelines must “have some relevance to us to the extent that they reflect, if nothing else, the current thinking of the Commissioner” counsel responded “the current thinking of the Commissioner is irrelevant” (transcript p. 6).
  4. Guidelines published by an agency designed to assist persons by permitting them to have knowledge of the ambit and principles of a discretion to be exercised of course are not binding upon us and our duty remains to make the “correct or preferable decision”. “The policy of which guides the exercise of a discretionary administrative power may rightly seek to achieve an objective of public significance and a balance may have to be struck between the achieving of that objective and the interests of an individual” Re Drake and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs [1979] AATA 179; (1980) (2 ALD 634 at p. 642-643). As it was said in Re Ward when recommending that guidelines be published “the Tribunal would normally respect them in appropriate cases” (page 293). It is hard then to understand the relevance of the present guidelines published by the respondent when in this case counsel for the respondent said that the thinking of the Commissioner was an irrelevance. A copy of the guidelines and a covering letter to Mr Sormaz are annexed to this decision.
  5. We are conscious that a considerable period of time has elapsed between resignation and application for late election. As Von Doussa J said in Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239 at p. 249-
“After long lapse of time memories are likely to be deficient. In some cases the quality of the evidence could be so unsatisfactory that understandably the decision maker will not be satisfied either about particular facts which are important in the case or more generally that there is a sufficiently established factual base to justify a favourable exercise of the discretion”.
  1. Allowing for the imperfections in memory of both the applicant and persons that the respondent would have otherwise potentially wished to have called as witnesses we are satisfied that there is “a sufficiently established factual base to justify a favourable exercise of the discretion”.
  2. We are satisfied that at the time consideration was given to resignation from Commonwealth employment in 1982 Mr Sormaz did not obtain independent advice nor was he aware of his own knowledge of the ramifications of a decision he would make with respect to his superannuation entitlements. We are satisfied and find as a fact that he was advised by an officer of the Australian Federal Police who was then his employer that by reason of his expressed wish to leave the Commonwealth Public Service and seek employment in the private sector that he should “take the money”. We also accept as a fact that he was not given advice as to the three options that were then available to him namely, apply for a refund of contributions, apply for a payment of a lump sum benefit or elect to preserve superannuation rights. We are satisfied also that the first occasion upon which Mr Sormaz learnt that there was a concept of a right to preserve superannuation rights was when he became aware of a notice published by the Australian Federal Police Association dated 21 October 1993. Mr Sormaz then immediately sought advice from a former senior commonwealth public servant who assisted him in making his application for late election which was made upon the respondent on 23 December 1993. That is to say, upon learning of a concept of preservation of benefits, Mr Sormaz acted promptly to make a late election for preservation on those benefits. In making these findings we wish to state however that there is nothing to suggest that there was any impropriety or misleading conduct of any officer of the Australian Federal Police who had any responsibility with respect to giving of advice or processing the application made by Mr Sormaz in 1982. We are also satisfied Mr Sormaz is a witness of truth and despite the passage of time from 1982, the imperfections in his memory are not of a degree that cause us to be dissatisfied or prohibit exercising our discretion. We note also that Mr Sormaz was 31 years at resignation and had 11 years contributory service.
  3. We are therefore satisfied that the applicant did not make “an informed choice” when not electing to preserve his superannuation rights in 1982 (refer Re Adams at 313 and Re Raison and the Commissioner for Superannuation Decision No. 11414 delivered 20 November 1996) and, having regard to the comments of Davies J in Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 50 FCR 150, we note that a beneficial construction should be applied to s.157, the circumstances existing at the time the discretion is to be exercised should be regarded, the totality of service and totality of contribution should be considered and one of the objectives of s.157, namely “to alleviate the consequences of a change in circumstances” are all matters which have moved us to find in favour of the applicant. Certainly the principles expressed in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 3 FCR 344 are relevant in any consideration of an extension of time but as Wilcox J himself then said and as Davies J said in Chalk, those factors were not intended to limit a discretion available to a decision maker when it was necessary to consider extending time (refer also Comcare v A’Hearn [1993] FCA 498; 1993 18 AAR 366 at 369 where Hill J said that a relevant consideration was whether there was a reasonable explanation for delay, yet the absence of a reasonable explanation did not cause the application to be fatal). As we have said earlier, we believe that the applicant’s explanation for his delay is reasonable. Whilst acknowledging that the memories of some of the respondent’s witnesses are now deficient, the respondent still largely maintains a relevant file and it contains documents germane to this application. This prohibits elevation of prejudice to the respondent to a status greater than the acceptability of the explanation for delay on the part of the applicant and the beneficial considerations which should be extended to him.
  4. Insofar as the applicant has undergone a change in his circumstances we note that he intended upon leaving Commonwealth employment in 1982 to be engaged in the private sector in self employment. He apparently was successful in self employment for many years but returned to Commonwealth employment in 1991. As was decided in Re Adams the Tribunal should, as a matter of law, take into account a change in a person’s circumstances (page 316).
  5. A consideration sometimes also given in applications of this type is disadvantage to the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund by a later recognition to preserve superannuation rights. Whilst it is beyond doubt that Mr Sormaz had the benefit of monies paid to him in 1982, the sum then paid, even allowing for actuarial considerations, was relatively small. On the other hand, by payment to him in 1982, Mr Sormaz has been denied the benefit of receiving interest upon the monies during the period of time they were not retained in the fund. There is no evidence that the Commonwealth will incur any cost by him being permitted to make an election now to preserve the funds existing at 1982 (refer Boardman). As His Honour also then said “to grant the respondent (Mr Boardman) the benefit of years of service actually performed is consistent with the policy and objects of the Act”.
  6. In conclusion we note upon resignation from employment the applicant was not financially advantaged to the extent he would have been had he been retrenched (refer Re Raison) and, despite the inferences the respondent urged us to draw from the applicant’s conduct with respect to any benefit he derived from his family trust and his superannuation fund established subsequent to entering into self employment, we note that the monies then existing in those funds were largely dissipated upon Family Court proceedings where, it would appear on the evidence, the applicant himself (as opposed to his wife) received very little financial benefit.
  7. In all of the circumstances we are satisfied that it is desirable that the election made by the applicant in December 1993 should be recognised and be treated as if it were made within the period allowed under s.137.
  8. The decision under review must therefore be set aside and a decision in the foregoing terms be substituted.
