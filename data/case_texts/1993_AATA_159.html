Maurice Hinton Foley and Commissioner of Superannuation [1993] AATA 159 (25 May 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

MAURICE HINTON FOLEY v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. W92/246

AAT No. 8731

Number of pages - 8

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

T.E. Barnett(1) (Senior Member), J.G. Billings(1) (Member) and K.J. Taylor(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - resignation from Public Service - late election to preserve entitlements to superannuation benefits - special circumstances - failure of department to respond to requests for information about entitlements on resignation - signature requested at social gathering - failure to read document before signing.

Superannuation Act 1976 s.157

Superannuation Act 1922 s.119U

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 s.37

Beadle v Director-General of Social Security [1984] AATA 176; 60 ALR 225

Re Welsh and Commissioner for Superannuation 2 ALD 101

HEARING

PERTH, 9 March 1993

25:5:1993

The applicant appeared in person.

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr G. Fisher (Departmental Advocate)

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside and the following decision is substituted:

1. Whereas it is determined that there are special circumstances

within the meaning of s.119U(2) of the Superannuation Act 1922 the

period for the making of an election under s.119U(1) of the said Act is

extended to 25 January 1991.

2. The matter is remitted to the respondent to calculate and pay the

applicant's entitlements in the light of this decision taking into

account any amount which may be owed by the applicant to the respondent

by way of arrears of contributions and the repayment of amounts

previously received by him on resignation as refund of his

contributions to the fund.

3. The parties are granted leave to apply regarding the calculation of

the applicant's entitlements.

DECISION

T.E. BARNETT, J.G. BILLINGS AND K.J. TAYLOR This is an application to review a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation made on 13 September 1992 which reconsidered and affirmed an earlier decision of 19 August 1991. The decision was made under s.119U(2) of the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act") and the result was that the Commissioner refused to extend time for the applicant to elect, under s.119U(1) of the 1922 Act, to preserve his superannuation entitlements on resignation from the Commonwealth Public Service ("CPS"). The relevant parts of s.119U were as follows:

"s.119U(1) Subject to this section, where, after the commencement

of this section (25 May 1971)

(a) a contributor to the Fund who has not attained the age of sixty

years ceases to be such a contributor by reason of his resignation

or discharge; or

(b)...

he may, within twenty-one days after he ceases to be such a

contributor, elect that this Division shall apply in relation to

him.

(2) The Board may, if it is satisfied that there are special

circumstances that justify it in so doing, extend the period for

the making of an election under the last preceding subsection.

..."

2. The applicant had resigned from the CPS, on 21 June 1972 and ceased to be "an eligible employee" for the purposes of the 1922 Act. He then accepted a refund of his contributions to the Superannuation Fund. He did not avail himself of the option to preserve his rights in the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund which was available under the legislation in force at the time of his resignation. He thereby lost the benefit of the amounts which the Commonwealth had contributed to the fund on his behalf during the previous 24 years and he also lost his future pension entitlements.

3. At the time of his resignation in June 1972 the matter was governed by s.119U of the 1922 Act. By virtue of an amendment which came into force on 25 May 1971, s.119U provided a new option which allowed resignees to elect whether to claim a refund of their contributions or to elect to preserve their entitlements in the scheme. The election had to be made within 21 days of resignation. (Prior to the 1971 amendment the only option for a resignee in the applicant's circumstances was to claim a refund of his own contributions.) For reasons which will be fully discussed below the applicant waited until the 25 January 1991 to seek an extension of time to exercise his option to preserve entitlements. On that date he asked the respondent to exercise his discretion under s.119U(2) which provided that the Superannuation Board (now the Commissioner) may

"if it is satisfied that there are special circumstances that justify

it in doing so, extend the period for the making of an election ..."

4. In 1976 the 1922 Act was repealed and replaced by the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act"). In the new 1976 Act the phrase "special circumstances" was deleted and the rights of election were then governed by a new section 157 which provides a general provision enabling time to be extended for any election made under the 1976 Act. The new legislative scheme does not require existence of "special circumstances". The new section enables the Commissioner to determine that an election be treated as having been made within time if he is satisfied that

"in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable to do so."

5. As far as the applicant is concerned the practical effect of the legislative amendments in 1971 and 1976 is that as he resigned from the superannuation scheme after the 1971 amendment (which created the option to elect to preserve benefits) but prior to the commencement of the 1976 Act, if he wishes to make a late election after the commencement of the 1976 Act he is required to apply to the Commissioner for Superannuation (standing in place of the now defunct Superannuation Board). The Commissioner is obliged to apply the law as it existed at his resignation in June 1972. This requires the Commissioner (and this Tribunal) to apply s.119U of the amended 1922 Act and to consider the phrase "special circumstances". The Issues

6. The main issue in this application therefore is whether there were "special circumstances" to justify extending the 21 day period allowed for making an election to preserve superannuation benefits by over 18 years to the 25 January 1991.

7. The Tribunal heard oral evidence from the applicant and the following written materials were admitted into evidence:

The documents filed pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals

Tribunal Act 1975 (T documents);

Affidavit by John Clifford Metcalfe dated 8 February 1993;

Booklet of applicant's materials.

Agreed Facts

8. The following facts are not in dispute and the Tribunal makes formal findings accordingly. The applicant was born on 4 February 1930 and is 63 years of age. He joined the Commonwealth Public Service on 26 November 1947 when he joined the Post Master General's Department ("PMG"). At that date he became a permanent officer of the Public Service and a participant in the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund and he remained a participant until his resignation on 21 June 1972. The total period of his membership of the Public Service and the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund was therefore over 24 years.

9. Throughout his period of employment with the Commonwealth Public Service the applicant was employed in the Personnel Department starting as a clerk and rising to the position of Assistant Inspector Recruitment. On 22 June 1967 he commenced extended leave without pay to take up a Senior Personnel position with the United Nations Agency known as the International Civil Aviation Organisation ("ICAO") in Montreal, Canada. In that position he continued to make contributions to the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund until his resignation, though towards the end of the period he had fallen into arrears by approximately $1,000. At the time of his overseas secondment in 1967 he had a detailed "hands on" knowledge of the 1922 Act as it then existed but at the time of his resignation in June 1972 he was not aware of the 1971 Amendment, which had provided an option for resignees to elect to preserve their superannuation entitlements.

10. On 1 December 1971, six months before the end of his secondment period, he wrote to the PMG applying for a two year extension of his leave without pay to enable him to continue his employment with ICAO. He received no reply to that letter for a period of three months and then wrote again on 9 March 1972 pointing out that the PMG's failure to provide him with any information at all for a period of three months after he had requested an extension of his leave without pay would affect "serious personal decisions with substantial financial implications..." Eventually he received a short but encouraging reply dated 5 April 1972 in the following terms:

"Further to your letter of 9 March 1972 your application for leave

with pay under section 72A is at present under consideration and you

will be advised as soon as Public Service Board approval is received.

The question of superannuation is being investigated and you will be

advised shortly.

Any inconvenience we may have caused you for not replying earlier is

regretted."

The applicant interpreted this letter, which had been written 14 days after the PMG had received his reminder letter of 9 March, as indicating that his application for extension of leave had at least been recommended by the PMG to the Public Service Board. In fact, the PMG waited until 26 April 1972 and then recommended against extension of his leave.

11. On the basis of the 5 April letter from the PMG, however, the applicant believed it was likely that his leave would be extended. He therefore proceeded to make detailed travel arrangements to take his wife and four children with him on a round the world trip en route to Australia. Shortly before his departure he received a cable from the PMG on 17 May 1972 rejecting his application for extension of leave without pay. He then submitted his resignation in a letter dated 17 May 1972 to take effect from the 21 June 1972 and proceeded on with his travel plans. Although that letter was received by the PMG not later than 24 May 1972 and although directions were given by senior officers to advise the applicant of his entitlements prior to his resignation taking effect on 21 June 1972, this was not done. On the day after his resignation, and just prior to his departure from Montreal, the PMG posted a letter dated 22 June 1972 to him in Montreal asking him to sign a refund of superannuation contributions form, the letter was in the following terms:

"In reply to your correspondence of 17th May, 1972, you are advised

that a payment of $2,254-17, being payment in lieu of 3 7/30 months

furlough will be made into your account at the Commonwealth Savings

Bank, 225 Bourke Street, Melbourne, 3000.

You will be further advised of the date of the payment when this

information is available.

It would be appreciated if you would complete the attached Form SB-2A in

respect of the refund of your Superannuation contributions and return

it in the enclosed, post-free envelope."

It is quite clear that the applicant did not receive this letter before departing for Australia on 24 June 1972.

12. After a long and tiring trip with his wife and four children the applicant arrived in Perth on the 24 July 1972 and remained here for approximately ten days. He still had been unsuccessful in receiving the necessary paperwork from the PMG regarding his retirement and so he sought the assistance of a personal friend John Clifford Metcalfe who at that time was a Manager in the Personnel Department. As it is crucial to the outcome of this application it bears repeating that in July 1972 the applicant had no knowledge of the very recent amendment to the Act which provided an option to preserve entitlements and also that the nature of his employment had made him very familiar with the previous scheme which provided only for refund of contributions. The Tribunal accepts the applicant's contention that his many years of formal training and professional experience had given him a deep rooted and frequently reinforced conviction that the only option for a resignee from the CPS was to accept a refund of his contributions. He was also aware of the exact details of the scheme and that the refund was limited to the amount of his personal contributions and that not even the earnings on those contributions would be taken into account. He knew that after 24 years service the refund amounted to only about 25 per cent of the equity value of his contributions. After considering all the circumstances existing at the time the Tribunal accepts his claim that he would have elected to preserve his rights if he had been aware of that option.

13. Mr Metcalfe gave evidence by affidavit that he arranged for an SB2A form to be filled in for the applicant's signature and that on the 31 July 1972 he asked the applicant to sign the form at a social function at which the applicant was the guest of honour. There was no discussion or explanation and Mr Metcalfe witnessed the signature. The SB2A form was as follows:

OR

+ Signature of Witness...........Qualification......

OUTLINE OF CONDITIONS FOR PRESERVATION OF SUPERANNUATION
RIGHTS

An election to preserve superannuation rights may be made by a

contributor who has not attained the age of 60 years. It must be made

within the period commencing one month prior to ceasing as a

contributor and ending 21 days after ceasing. The election will result

in entitlement to either a transfer value, deferred benefit or refund

depending on eligibility based on past service and future employment.

On information to be provided after the expiration of three months

after ceasing as a contributor, the Board will determine whether the

preservation entitlement is to be a transfer value, deferred benefit or

refund. Broad descriptions of these entitlements and conditions are -

Transfer Value A lump sum based on employee contributions and

Commonwealth contribution payable to an eligible

superannuation scheme.

A transfer value is available if a contributor is a

member of an eligible superannuation scheme 3 months

after ceasing as a contributor.

Deferred Benefit A lump sum (Provident Account) or actuarially

calculated pension payable on or after attaining age

60 but before attaining age 65 or on invalidity;

deferred benefit makes provision for appropriate

reversionary widow's pension and children's benefits.

If a transfer value is not payable, a deferred benefit

is available on transfer to public employment or on

having completed a total of 20 years Commonwealth

ceasing as a contributor, except on transfer to public

employment with eligibility to pay a refund to a

superannuation scheme applicable to that employment.

To retain entitlement to deferred benefit the

contributor must continue in public employment until

reaching age 60 or until a total of 20 years

Commonwealth and public employment has been completed.

Refund A refund of all contributions paid, which, in the case

of Provident Account, includes interest.

If a person enters public employment and becomes a

member of a superannuation scheme which is not

eligible to receive a transfer value but will accept a

refund of contributions in exchange for benefits, a

refund only is payable. If at the expiration of 3

months, the person electing is not in public

Commonwealth and public employment his election for

preservation lapses and he becomes entitled to a

refund of contributions.

As an election to preserve superannuation rights is irrevocable you

should make sure that you fully understand the relevant provisions of

+ The signature to this application must be witnessed by a Commissioner

14. The Tribunal accepts that, on Mr Metcalfe's instructions, the form had been filled in by an employee of the PMG in such a way that although it provides for different options, the option for a refund of benefit square was ticked before the applicant was given the form. The Tribunal also accepts that the applicant signed the form without reading it and that he did this because firstly he was so certain that he knew that his only option was a refund of his contributions and secondly because it was handed to him by a senior PMG officer, without a covering letter or explanation, for immediate signature at a special social gathering in the applicant's honour while he was drinking alcohol in a relaxed and convivial atmosphere.

15. Shortly after signing the form the sum of $3,700 was paid into his account and the applicant proceeded with his family to visit relatives in Sydney before returning to Montreal. The applicant does not remember ever seeing the PMG letter dated 22 July after returning to Montreal. He says that even if he did receive it after some months it would have seemed unimportant as he believed that he had now done all that was possible under the legislation.

16. As a member of the ICAO the applicant was contributing to the United Nations Superannuation Fund ("UNSF") and he continued in that employment until he was forced to take early retirement in 1988 at the age of 58. He was entitled to, and received, a pension from the UNSF minus a penalty for early retirement. The applicant returned to Perth on 20 January 1989 and lived in retirement. The Tribunal accepts that at that stage he was still unaware that he had ever had entitlement to a deferred benefit in the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund.

17. In October 1989, in the course of conversation at a social function, the applicant became aware that, after an amendment to the 1922 Act, an option to preserve superannuation benefits had been created for contributors to the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund. At that stage he believed that the amendment had come into force in 1972 a few months after his retirement and so he wrote to Telecom on 12 February 1990 seeking a gratuitous payment of the deferred benefits. He sought special consideration because of his long years of service and because he had not been advised by the PMG at the time of his resignation that the Act was about to be amended. Several letters were exchanged but eventually he received a reply from Telecom which made it clear to him for the first time that the amendment to the 1922 Act which provided for an option to take deferred benefits actually came into force on 25 May 1971 shortly prior to his retirement. He then wrote a formal request dated 25 January 1991 asking the Commissioner for Superannuation to exercise his discretion under s.119U(2) of the Act to allow him an extension of time to make his election to receive deferred benefits. His request was considered by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation but refused on 19 August 1991 mainly on the basis that he had signed a form SB2A which had clearly set out an option to opt for a deferred benefit. The applicant then sought a review of that decision but the decision was affirmed on review on 13 September 1992. In the reasons for decision the delegate accepted the applicant's claims that he had been given no information by circular or word of mouth about the 1971 amendments to the Act and also apparently accepted the claims that the officers of the PMG were tardy in responding to his inquiries prior to resignation. The delegate was strongly influenced however by the fact that when the applicant was given the SB2A form, that give sufficient information to show him that there was an election option or at least it gave him sufficient information to alert him to that possibility so that it was reasonable to conclude that he should have made further inquiries.

18. Mr Fisher, appearing for the respondent, conceded that prior to resignation the applicant had not been given any of the circulars prepared by the Retirement Benefits Office ("RBO") which would have alerted him to his rights but he also relied upon the fact that the applicant had signed an SB2A form. Mr Fisher also submitted that even if the circumstances before, during and immediately after the signing of the SB2A form were such as to amount to "special circumstances" the applicant was still not justified in waiting eighteen and a half years before seeking to make his election though Mr Fisher agreed that as the applicant remained in Montreal throughout those years he was probably out of contact with the workings of the Commonwealth Superannuation Fund. Mr Fisher pointed out however that when the applicant returned to Montreal after his trip to Perth he should have received the letter dated 22 July 1972 enclosing an SB2A form. If he did receive that form then he would have had time to sit calmly in his office or his home to study it and Mr Fisher submitted that this certainly should have alerted him to his rights under the Act. To have failed to do this amounted to carelessness and the applicant would have nobody but himself to blame at that stage for not seeking early leave to make an out of time election.

19. The applicant pointed out however that the letter, which he may or may not have received in Montreal (because he had no recollection of it and there is doubt whether or not it was posted), was in fact misleading as it referred only to the refund of contributions option and, as he had already completed the SB2A form seeking the refund, there was no reason at all why he should have carefully read the form all over again and he did not do so.

20. Mr Fisher relied principally on the case of Beadle v Director-General of Social Security [1984] AATA 176; 60 ALR 225 in support of his submissions that there are no special circumstances to justify extension of time, quoting the following passage:

"An expression such as "special circumstances" is by its very

nature incapable of precise or exhaustive definition. The qualifying

adjective looks to circumstances that are unusual .... and that is not

to say the circumstances must be unique, but they must have particular

quality of unusualness that permits them to be described as special."

21. He submitted that in a case of lengthy delay weighty facts would be necessary to establish the required special circumstances. Mr Fisher concluded by emphasising that the Tribunal should be very wary of allowing an applicant to go behind the form that he had actually signed by claiming that he is not bound by it because he has not read it. He referred to the only other case in which s.119U has been considered Re Welsh and Commissioner for Superannuation 2 ALD 101. Mr Welsh completed a carefully planned retirement some two and a half years after the 1971 Amendment to the 1922 Act and then rejoined the Public Service in 1971 seeking to make a late election to preserve his superannuation benefits. He also had signed the same Form SB2A signed by the applicant in this case. The Tribunal was not prepared to accept his claim that he did not read the Form because he was "under duress" from the Department and "mad at the Department" and treated him as having read the Form with its clearly set out explanations of his entitlements.

22. There are many features distinguishing that case however, particularly the fact that Mr Foley was so isolated and that his strenuous and persistent requests for the required information were so badly mishandled by the PMG. This led to Mr Foley being forced into the situation during a brief return to Perth (after his resignation) of signing the form hurriedly when it was given to him by a senior officer in the Personnel Section of the PMG at a Hockey Club drinks reunion. This is a very different situation from that claimed by Mr Welsh and it bears the characteristics of being "unusual" and "special".

23. The Tribunal accepts the applicant as a witness of truth and there was no contest between himself and the respondent on the facts which he put before the Tribunal, all of which are accepted. On those facts the Tribunal has decided that there were special circumstances which should have justified the delegate of the Commissioner in extending the time for the application to make his election for deferred benefits until 12 February 1990. Those special circumstances arose from a combination of the following facts:

a) The applicant was on long term secondment overseas and was not

advised of relevant amendments to the 1922 Act.

b) He sought information about his entitlements six months prior to

resignation but it was not provided by that date nor within the 21 day

statutory period allowed for electing to preserve entitlements.

c) He was eventually handed Form SB2A in an irregular way, without any

explanation, at a social gathering where there was inadequate

opportunity to read and absorb the information provided.

d) The Form SB2A had been partly filled in by an employee effectively

removing the option to preserve entitlements.

e) By training and professional experience the applicant had a

reasonable frequently reinforced, but mistaken, conviction in July-1972

that his only option was to claim a refund of his contributions to the

Superannuation Fund.

f) In these circumstances he signed the SB2A Form at the party without

reading it as his attention was not focussed on the "unlikely"

possibility that there had been an amendment.

g) Within a few days after signing the Form SB2A the applicant departed

Australia for Canada to continue his work with the United Nations.

h) In Canada he did not receive any further information to alert him to

his entitlement to preserve his superannuation benefits for the

remainder of his overseas appointment which terminated in 1988. (If the

letter of 22 July 1992 was received it seemed to be superfluous).

i) As soon as the applicant became aware of the legislative amendment

which provided for an election to preserve his superannuation

entitlements he applied to the Superannuation Commission accordingly.

24. After studying the correspondence from 1971 to the 22 June 1972 it is clear that the applicant, writing from Montreal, was strenuously seeking access to the very information which would have alerted him to his rights to preserve superannuation benefits. From the oral and written evidence before the Tribunal it is clear that the applicant is a meticulous professional in personnel matters and had he been given the information he sought, in an orderly and timely way, there is a very high probability that he would have studied it carefully and become aware of his entitlements. If he had become aware, then the benefits of preserving his entitlements would have greatly outweighed for him the paltry benefits of accepting a refund of his contributions. Having heard the applicant on the subject, the Tribunal accepts beyond doubt that he would have elected to preserve his entitlements.

25. The failure to provide this information in an orderly and timely manner was entirely due to the inefficient and insensitive manner in which the PMG personnel section handled the communications aspect of his resignation, culminating in a Form SB2A being handed to him for signature at a social function with the election effectively exercised for him.

26. Once the applicant was effectively denied the chance to make an election within the 21 days statutory period there was little chance that he would become aware of the option throughout the remainder of his time in Canada. No criticism can therefore be made against him for the long extension of the delay. It seems unlikely that he ever received the tardily posted letter of 22 July 1972. There is an acknowledgment on the PMG file copy that the letter is "O dated" and that it would not reach the applicant in time. There are two possible way to view this letter. Possibly it was received in August-September 1972 but not carefully examined by the applicant, as he felt it had all been finalised previously in Australia. Alternatively it was never posted or at least not received. On the Tribunal's assessment of the applicant's meticulous professionalism and his complete honesty during his testimony it is more likely that he never received the letter. If he had received the letter it is very hard to believe he would not have read it. Had he read it in the atmosphere of his home or office, it is hard to believe he would not have immediately picked out the option provision which is set out very clearly indeed. Had he become aware of the option in August-September 1972, being the logical and forceful man which he clearly is, it is almost certain he would have immediately written to PMG seeking to exercise a late election. It is for these reasons that the Tribunal considers it is more likely that he never received the letter of 22 July 1972 in Montreal.

27. The Tribunal is aware that generally a person should be presumed to have read a document which he has signed. In this case, and in the light of all the circumstances however, the Tribunal finds that he did sign the SB2A Form at the party without reading it because of the unusual circumstances previously described.

28. The Tribunal accordingly determines that there were special circumstances within the meaning of s.119U of the 1922 Act. The Tribunal further finds that the actions of the applicant have been reasonable throughout and that there is no consideration of unfairness or injustice to the public or any other person.

Decision

29. The decision under review is set aside and the following decision is substituted:

1. Whereas it is determined that there are special circumstances within

the meaning of s.119U(2) of the Superannuation Act 1922 the period for

the making of an election under s.119U(1) of the said Act is extended

to 25 January 1991.

2. The matter is remitted to the respondent to calculate and pay the

applicant's entitlements in the light of this decision taking into

account any amount which may be owed by the applicant to the respondent

by way of arrears of contributions and the repayment of amounts

previously received by the applicant on resignation as refund of his

contributions to the fund.

3. The parties are granted leave to apply regarding the calculation of

the applicant's entitlements.
