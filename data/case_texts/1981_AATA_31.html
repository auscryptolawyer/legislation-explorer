Re Peter Allen Reeve and the Commissioner of Superannuation [1981] AATA 31 (28 April 1981)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: PETER ALLEN REEVE

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. 12014 of 1979

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mr R.K. Todd (Senior Member)

Dr M. Glick (Member)

Sir Ernest Coates (Member)

CATCHWORDS

superannuation _ mistaken treatment of applicant as contributor to Superannuation fund _ issue of benefit classification certificate _ whether the applicant was 'a contributor to the provident account' _ statutory history of provision for medical examination _ administrative procedures following examination _ presumption of regularity _ burden of proof Superannuation Act 1922 s.4(1), s.5, s.75, s.79(1)

Superannuation Act 1976 s.3, s.16(4), s.45(1), s.182, s.184(3)

HEARING

CANBERRA

28:4:1981

ORDER

The Tribunal decides to set aside the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation taken by his delegate to issue under s.184(3) of the Superannuation Act 1976 a benefit classification certificate in respect of the applicant specifying the condition 'anxiety state'.

DECISION

1. This application for review concerns some very difficult problems which have arisen in relation to the administration of the rights of the applicant under the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act") and the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act").

2. On 27 June 1973 the applicant was medically examined by one Dr Sedal, a Commonwealth Medical Officer. The purpose of the examination was to determine the fitness of the applicant to enter the Commonwealth Public Service. The applicant had completed the required statement of personal medical history and therein stated that from 15 March 1973 he had had a nervous condition and that he had last received medical treatment on 25 June of the same year from one Dr Richardson, a psychiatrist. Dr Sedal sought a report on the applicant's illness from Dr Richardson. Dr Richardson's report revealed that the applicant had first consulted him in November 1972 and that the applicant had 'quite a long history of similar circumstances'. This history was however very much related to his having been, through parental pressure, subjected to quite excessive academic and career goals. Dr Richardson concluded: 'It is my opinion that he is well worth a try because, provided his anxiety level does not rise too high, he could become a very useful member of the public service.'

3. Subsequently, in his report made on 25 July 1973, Dr Sedal stated:

Hasn't worked for 3 months _ pressure of work (accountancy) exams, and sickness of wife all built up _ referred by G.P. to psychiatrist _ when his boss heard _ he was sacked.'

His recommendation and reasons therefor were as follows:

'. Glucose tolerance test normal.

. Psychiatrist (Dr Richardson) report suggests that he would be worth a trial.

Recommend deferment for 11 months then consideration for Provident account.

Is fit to commence duties.

4. The applicant was then offered a position in the Public Service and in August 1973 he commenced duties as a Clerk Class 1 in the Australian Taxation Office.

5. In July 1974 he was advised that he was required to be medically examined again but when he attended for this purpose his papers had been lost. A new appointment was made and new forms had to be filled in. On 13 August 1974 the applicant was examined by Dr Delaine, who gave evidence at the hearing. Dr Delaine had no independent recollection of the event, but he noted on the applicant's record card (Exhibit 4) the following remarks, inter alios:

Since 1972. Nervous Disorder' Currently on Lithium and Tryptanol"

The words 'Since 1972' were written with a different pen or ink, the same as that which was used to write some other remarks relating to the applicant's medical history, but from Dr Delaine's evidence and from the visual appearance of the words in question we are satisfied hat the whole passage cited is clearly in Dr Delaine's distinctive handwriting. Likewise, in the bottom left hand corner of the card, written with the pen, or in the ink, that was used for the bulk of the words cited above, and in the same handwriting, appear the words 'Provident A/c'. In his evidence, Dr Delaine was quite satisfied that there could be no question of his having recommended the applicant save for admission to the Provident Account. The applicant however maintained otherwise.

6. The account given by the applicant of his encounter with Dr Delaine was as follows:

'...After preliminary examination by persons other than the doctor I was told to wait then called to see the doctor, the doctor asked me how I was in health, how I was and I said I was in good health and I was OK and he replied he would be recommending me for permanency and superannuation benefits and I asked him, 'Am I entitled to superannuation'.

... I asked him what my actual entitlement was to the superannuation fund and he wished me well in my career and said, yes, I was entitled to that fund and at that time I left the building and went back to work at Collins Street. The consultation with the doctor in question was only extremely brief, just a matter of an exchange of words between he and I. The doctor did not say, as indicated by the guidelines that are on record, he did not undertake to request any information from any doctor that I had attended for an opinion, nor did he have any tests whatsoever carried out in relation to forming his opinion.

Did he ask you any questions about your condition, your then condition, insofar as it might involve nervous conditions or anxiety state?... No, not that I can recall. It was very brief. The exchange of words was very brief, there was actually a hand shake and then out the door. He did actually shake my hand.'

7. On the same day Dr Delaine signed a Medical Report Form known as a Form PSB 9A. The concluding section of this form should be set out:

'This applicant has been examined by the Australian Government Medical Officer.

.(a)Determination under Sections 5(1) of the Superannuation Act and 34(b) of the Public Service Act. I determine that the health and physical fitness of the person named above justify his acceptance as a contributor to the .Superannuation Fund/Provident Account. This form should be attached to Superannuation Board Form SB5 when that form is returned to Sub-Treasury for processing and forwarding to the Australian Government Retirement Benefits Office.

.(b)The applicant is required to undergo medical re-examination in ..... months and/or produce certificates of dental/visual fitness, with a view to ultimate acceptance under Superannuation Fund/Provident Account conditions.

.(c)The applicant does not satisfy the health and physical fitness requirements for permanent appointment.

(Sgd) M.Delaine 13 Aug 1974

05/D36 ...................... ............ Public Service Inspector (Date)

. Strike out whichever does not apply. '

8. The original of this document was tendered in evidence (Exhibit 1). We cannot be sure from the document that the underlining of the words 'Provident Account' was effected by Dr Delaine, but on balance we consider that we should find that he did cross out the words 'Superannuation Fund'. We accept that the applicant's account of what transpired at the examination was given with a sincere belief that he was giving the Tribunal a truthful account of what transpired as he remembered it. It may be that his recollection was inaccurate, or it may be that there was a misunderstanding, a possibility that cannot be discounted. But the fact is that having regard to the medical history of the applicant, and in particular to the fact that the whole point of the medical examination in August 1974 was plainly to take up the recommendation that had been made by Dr Sedal in July 1973 that the applicant was 'worth a trial' and that he be deferred for 11 months and then considered for the Provident Account, we can see no way in which any consideration could at that stage have been given to membership of the Superannuation Fund. It will have been noted that Dr Delaine signed over the words 'Public Service Inspector', which he was not. There was evidence that early in August 1974 new procedures, designed to simplify those followed in relation to medical examinations were introduced. A letter from the Public Service Board dated 21 June 1979 and an accompanying attachment headed 'Appointment Procedures' indicate that pursuant to the new procedures the Commonwealth Medical Officer was to sign the Form PSB 9A in cases involving acceptance for the Superannuation Fund or the Provident Account, but an officer of the Public Service Inspector's Office was to subsequently countersign all decisions of the Commonwealth Medical Officers in the daily attendance record. Mr Huttner, who appeared for the Commissioner, said that the officer, having the appropriate delegation to do so, signified his approval by placing a rubber stamp on the book constituting this record. The record cannot be found. Mr Huttner sought to rely upon the presumption of regularity, of which more later.

9. What appears to have happened is that the Form PSB 9A in respect of the applicant was sent to the Taxation Office and misread. It is probable that the difficulty was that what was sent was a photocopy of the original or even perhaps a photocopy of a photocopy. In this document, Exhibit 2, the crossing out of 'Superannuation Fund' is very faint, and the underlining of 'Provident Account' is so faint that it would not be detected by anyone who had not seen the original. Whoever did underline those words in the original however, a faint trace thereof does appear on Exhibit 2. For what it is worth, the crossing out of paragraphs (b) and (c) can hardly be detected, and Dr Delaine's signature is almost invisible.

10. No doubt it may be said that nobody should have relied upon a document in the condition described. But somebody did, and apparently read it as determining that the applicant should be admitted to the Superannuation Fund.

11. The applicant said, and the documents confirm, that on 22 August 1974 he was advised 'by the Public Service Inspector via the Taxation Office' by letter that it was required that deductions of $8.77 per pay be made from his pay as of the pay day of 22 August 1974 as superannuation contributions and that he was obliged to make such payments based on a retiring age of 65. It is clear that this letter treated him as an incoming member of the Superannuation Fund. With the letter were two forms, one being an election to retire at age 60, and the other an election to take out reserve units. The applicant elected to retire at age 60 and to take out 12 reserve units.

12. The first actual deduction of superannuation contributions was made from the applicant's pay on 5 September 1974, calculated retrospectively to 22 August.

13. On 11 December 1974 the applicant completed an application for refund of contributions, relating to refunds in respect of reserve units, and a refund of an amount of $26.56 in respect of the 12 reserve units which he had taken out on 22 August 1974 was made on 14 April 1975. These and the foregoing actions including those referred to in paragraphs 11 and 12 above are consistent only with the applicant having been treated as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund. We do not think that the contrary was contended, but consideration of the effect of his having been so treated when, as we consider, there was no foundation therefor, may be deferred until later in these reasons.

14. In August or September 1977 the applicant became unwell. In fact it appears that he was at that time placed in a particularly stressful situation in his family environment. We are not concerned to discuss the problem, and we did not in fact go into it at the hearing. It has apparently now been resolved, but at the time it was a matter of great concern. On 30 September 1977 the applicant saw Dr Richardson, who indicated that he was not fit for employment and placed him on indefinite sick leave. An appointment was made for him to see a Commonwealth Medical Officer on 21 October 1977 but by 12 October he was in trouble in relation to his sick leave entitlements. The doctor, the applicant said, advised him that he was superannuating him out of the Public Service and that in 12 months he would be examined again. He also stated that should he feel fit enough to resume duties at any time within the 12 months he would be entitled to request a medical examination, and that if he was found fit for duty he would re-enter the Public Service without incurring any loss of seniority. He explained that in practical terms he had been retired as of that day and that he should not worry about not being able to support his wife and children as that was what Superannuation was for.

15. The applicant then took steps to obtain the necessary forms to progress this decision but on 9 November 1977 he was informed by an officer of the Taxation Office that what was described as an error of long standing had been discovered, and that he was not entitled to any superannuation benefits. His entitlement was to be limited to that provided by membership of the Provident Account. He was requested to sign documents acknowledging this but he refused. He was plainly very aggrieved by what had happened and he sought help from various quarters.

16. Happily, the applicant's health improved. Dr Richardson certified that he was fit to resume his employment. On 14 December 1977 he was examined by a Commonwealth Medical Officer and, after some insistence from the applicant, as the latter claimed, he was found to be fit for duty and returned to work.

17. On 3 February 1978 the Commissioner for Superannuation issued a benefit classification certificate in respect of the applicant. Between 1 July 1976, the date of commencement of the new Superannuation Scheme introduced by the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act") and 24 April 1978, s.184 of the 1976 Act provided that a benefit classification certificate should be deemed to have come into existence in the case of those who had been existing contributors to the Provident Account at 1 July 1976, such deemed certificate specifying the physical or mental condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the person as a contributor under Part III of the Superannuation Act 1922. There was no call to issue an actual certificate, but it was the Commissioner's practice to do so. The certificate issued to Mr Reeve specified 'anxiety state' as the relevant condition.

18. On 5 September 1978 the applicant wrote to the Commissioner requesting review of the decision to issue the certificate. He claimed that he was not a contributor to the Provident Account. Alternatively, he requested that the certificate be varied or revoked on the basis that there was 'no medical evidence to support a conclusion that for the stated conditions I will be not likely to cease to be an eligible employee before attaining my maximum retiring age'. He also asked for deferral of any action to recover a sum of $78.40, payment of which by him had been demanded upon the footing that had contributions been upon the basis of his being a member of the Provident Account the payment of that sum over and above his actual contributions would have been requisite.

19. By the time the applicant wrote this letter, in fact as from 24 April 1978, the law had been changed. The Superannuation Acts Amendment Act 1978 introduced a substituted s.184. Instead of a benefit classification certificate being deemed to have come into existence, provision was inter alia made, by the new s.184(3), for the issue of a benefit classification certificate in the circumstances and with the consequences set out in the sub-section:

'(3) Subject to sub-section (4), the Commissioner may, if he thinks fit, and shall, upon application in writing being made to him by an eligible employee who is a person to whom this section applies and who, immediately before the commencing day, was (otherwise than by reason of an election made under section 81 of the superseded Act) a contributor to the Provident Account, issue in respect of the eligible employee a benefit classification certificate in which the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that was or were relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act is or are specified and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(4).'

Section 16(4) of the 1976 Act is the basic provision in that Act whereunder the Commissioner may issue a benefit classification certificate. Where a certificate is in force accordingly the employee affected may, on grounds specified in the request, request the Commissioner to revoke or vary the certificate. As is apparent, the new s.184(3) permits the Commissioner to issue a certificate on application made in that behalf or 'if he thinks fit', that is, of his own motion.

20. The Commissioner's reaction to the applicant's letter of 5 September 1978 was to treat it as a request under the new s.184(3) for the issue of a benefit classification certificate. That letter can hardly be seen as having constituted such a request, but as has been pointed out the Commissioner may in any event act to issue a certificate without a request being made in that behalf, assuming that the other preconditions for so acting are satisfied. At all events the Commissioner issued such a certificate, which was dated 6 October 1978 and again specified 'anxiety state' as the relevant condition.

21. On 19 October 1978 the applicant asked the Commissioner to review this decision, and the request was treated as a request for reconsideration pursuant to s.154 of the 1976 Act.

22. At the hearing attention was at first concentrated upon the question of whether in the proved circumstances the applicant should be found to have been admitted to the old Superannuation Fund ("the old Fund") in 1974 pursuant to the 1922 Act, and upon the question whether in any event an estoppel operated to prevent the denial of the applicant's claim to have been admitted to the old Fund. The applicant also contended that there was a contract between himself and the Parliament of the Commonwealth, or perhaps between himself and the Commonwealth at large, under the terms of which when employment was offered to him he accepted the same on the basis of his classification as a Clerk Class 1 and his acceptance as a member of the old Fund in August 1974. He did not however seek to pursue at the hearing any question of the revocation of the benefit classification certificate on medical grounds, a matter which was not in fact raised in the application for review.

23. The arguments of Mr Huttner, who appeared for the respondent, initially followed a somewhat similar pattern. He argued that the applicant could not, having regard to the medical findings, have been accepted as a contributor to the old Superannuation fund, and that he could only have been accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account. The fact is, as it seems to us, that he became an eligible employee for the purposes of the 1976 Act on 1 July 1976 whatever his position may have been in relation to the old Fund or the Provident Account. The reasons for this are as follows:

(a)He became an 'eligible employee' for the purposes of the 1976 Act on 1 July 1976 because of:

(i) the definition of 'eligible employee' in s.3 of the 1976 Act which thereby includes, inter alios,

'(a) a person who immediately before the commencement of this Act was an employee for the purposes of the superseded Act and has continued to be, and is, such an employee;'

(ii) the definition of 'Employee' in the 'superseded Act', (the 1922 Act) which defined an employee as 'a person employed in a permanent capacity by the Commonwealth, who is by the terms of his employment required to give his whole time to the duties of his employment ...';

(iii)Section 182 of the 1976 Act which effected the applicant's admission to the new Fund by providing that 'a person who is an eligible employee referred to in paragraph (a) of the definition of 'an eligible employee' in sub-section 3(1) shall be deemed to have become an eligible employee on the commencing day'.

(iv) Section 45(1) of the 1976 Act which required the applicant as an eligible employee to pay fortnightly basic contributions.

(b)Assuming that the applicant became a permanent employee in August 1974, his membership of the new Fund by virtue of his being an 'eligible employee' was thus dependent not upon his having been admitted to the old Fund or to the Provident Account but upon his having been a permanent employee.

(c)The applicant was therefore deemed to have become an eligible employee on 1 July 1976 so that the 1976 Act thereby applied to him. Nothing can be done after that, save that in appropriate circumstances his position could be affected by the issue of a benefit classification certificate.

(d)Between 1 July 1976 and 24 April 1978, being the date of commencement of the new s.184 of the 1976 Act, the position was, under the old s.184, that a benefit classification certificate was deemed to have come into existence in the case of those who had been existing contributors to the Provident Account at 1 July 1976. In such a situation there was no call to issue an actual certificate, but it was the Commissioner's practice to do so, and this is what he did in this case, the certificate purporting to specify 'anxiety state' as the relevant condition.

24. As we know, the applicant on 5 September 1978 wrote to the Commissioner requesting a review of the decision to issue this certificate. He claimed that he was not a contributor to the Provident Account. Alternatively he requested that the certificate be varied or revoked on the basis of the medical evidence. As we have seen, the Commissioner responded to this by first pointing out that the effect of the amendments that were operative from 24 April 1978 was that the deemed certificate was itself to be deemed never to have come into existence. He then proceeded to issue a benefit classification certificate pursuant to the new s.184(3). We have already observed, but it bears repeating, that he was entitled to issue this certificate, of his own motion or upon application in writing being made to him, in the case of a person who was 'an eligible employee who is a person to whom this section applies and who, immediately before the commencing day, was ... a contributor to the Provident Account ...' The pre-conditions mentioned are without doubt satisfied with the exception of that which requires that he had been immediately before the commencing day 'a contributor to the Provident Account'. Whether he should be found to have been such is the real difficulty, and we see this as the fundamental point to be determined in the case. If this approach is correct we are not concerned with a question whether the applicant was or could have been admitted to the old Fund or with any question of estoppel. We are simply concerned with the question whether within the meaning of s.184(3) of the 1976 Act as amended he was 'a contributor to the Provident Account', and in a later written submission Mr Huttner for the respondent very properly conceded that this was so. To determine this question we must go back to the 1922 Act. We are concerned with the provisions of that Act as they stood in 1973 to 1976, but some study of the history of those provisions is requisite.

25. The word 'contributor' was defined in s.4(1) of the 1922 Act to mean 'an employee who is or has been contributing under this Act to the Fund'. 'The Fund' is itself defined to mean 'the Superannuation Fund established under this Act', and s.75 provided:

'There shall be a Provident Account which shall form part of the Superannuation Fund and into which shall be paid all contributions of employees and payments by the Commonwealth under this part.' (underlining supplied) In view of this provision, strictness would require us to refer to the old Superannuation Fund, in the sense in which we are in fact referring to it as contrasted with the Provident Account, as 'the Superannuation Fund under Part III', but for the sake of convenience we shall continue to refer to it as 'the old Fund'. We then go to s.79(1) of the Act which provides so far as relevant:

'(1) The following persons shall be contributors to the Provident Account _

(a) employees who by reason of the operation of section 5 ... of this Act are accepted as contributors to the Provident Account; and

(b) ...'

26. The critical provision for the purpose of considering the point is s.5 of the 1922 Act, which was introduced by s.7 of Act No.97 of 1965. That section reads:

'5(1) Except as otherwise provided by this Act, and notwithstanding anything contained in any other Act, a person who becomes an employee after the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1965 shall not contribute to the Fund under Part III of this Act or to the Provident Account unless _

(a) he has, before becoming an employee, or within such period after becoming an employee as the Board allows, undergone a medical examination by a legally qualified medical practitioner approved by the Board;

(b) a report of the result of the examination has been furnished by the medical practitioner to the Board; and

(c) the Board is satisfied, after considering the report of the medical practitioner, that the health and physical fitness of the person are such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund under Part III of this Act or to the Provident Account, as the case may be.

(2) Where _

(a) a person who becomes an employee after the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1965 has, whether before or after becoming an employee, undergone a medical examination by a legally qualified medical practitioner approved by the Public Service Board;

(b) a report of the result of the examination has been furnished by the medical practitioner to the Public Service Board; and

(c) the Public Service Board certifies, after considering the report of the medical practitioner, that the health and physical fitness of the person are such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund under Part III of this Act or to the Provident Account,

the Superannuation Board may, in its discretion, accept him as a contributor to the Fund under Part III of this Act or to the Provident Account.'

27. The full import of the distinction between sub-sections (1) and (2) does not readily appear from their wording. At first sight it would seem that sub-section (1) is dealing with admissions controlled by the Superannuation Board whereas sub-section (2) deals with admissions controlled by the Public Service Board. A closer reading however indicates that sub-section (1) did not include any provision having the effect of providing any mode of acceptance of a person as a contributor either to the old Fund or to the Provident Account. It simply prohibited a person from contributing to the old Fund or to the Provident Account unless certain criteria were satisfied. Sub-section (2) is however another matter. Where the criteria set out in paragraphs (a), (b) and (c) thereof are satisfied the sub-section goes on to provide that 'The Superannuation Board may, in its discretion, accept him as a contributor to the fund under Part III of this Act or to the Provident Account'. In order to attempt to assess the significance of the relationship between these two provisions the history of s.5 and associated provisions should be set out:

(i) Section 5 of the 1922 Act as originally enacted read:

' PART II _ THE SUPERANNUATION FUND

5(1) There shall be a Superannuation Fund, into which shall be paid the contributions of employees and payments by the Commonwealth under this Act; and from which shall be paid the benefits provided for in this Act.

(2) Income derived from the investment of the fund shall form part thereof.

(3) The income of the fund shall not be subject to taxation by the Commonwealth or a State.'

(ii) There was no provision in the 1922 Act itself requiring medical examination. Everyone who was an employee (with some exceptions provided in the Act) contributed.

(iii) Section 5 remained in its original form until 1959 when by s.6 of Act No.102 of 1959 the following amendment was effected:

'6. Section five of the Principal Act is amended by omitting from sub-section (1) the words '; and from which shall be paid the benefits provided for in this act'.'

(iv) Medical examinations were first required in 1937. Section 4B inserted by s.4 of Act No.28 of 1937 read:

'4B. Notwithstanding anything contained in any Act, no person who becomes an employee after the commencement of this section shall contribute to the Fund unless, prior to his appointment, a legally qualified medical practitioner satisfactory to the Board has certified that the employee is of sound bodily health and free from physical defects:

Provided that this section shall not apply to an employee who is at the commencement of this section an officer appointed under section twenty-one of the Australian Soldiers' Repatriation Act 1920, section fifteen of the War Service Homes Act 1918, section fourteen A of the Science and Industry Research Act 1920-1926, or section nine of the High Commissioner Act 1909.'

(v) This was amended by s.4 of Act No.53 of 1942 which read as follows:

'4. Section four B of the Principal Act is amended by adding at the end thereof the words, ', or to a person in respect of whom a notice is published in pursuance of sub-section (4) of section four of this Act and whose appointment to the statutory office was made before the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1942:

Provided further that this section shall not apply to a person who, at the commencement of the Superannuation Act 1942, was employed by an approved authority and in respect of whom an approved authority has notified the Board that the authority accepts full liability for the payment of pensions and benefits under this Act on his retirement, within three years after the date on which he commences to contribute to the fund, on the ground of invalidity or of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties, or on his death within that period'.'

(vi) A minor amendment was made in Act No.15 of 1945 by section 4 thereof:

'4. Section four B of the Principal Act is amended by omitting the word 'appointment' (first occurring) and inserting in its stead the words 'becoming an employee, or within such period thereafter as the Board allows'."

(vii) The entire s.4B was repealed and a new s.4B substituted by s.4 of Act No.11 of 1954:

'4(1) Section four B of the Principal Act is repealed and the following section inserted in its stead:-

'4B(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in any Act, a person who becomes an employee on or after the date of commencement of this section shall not contribute to the Fund or the Provident Account unless, before becoming an employee, or within such period after becoming an employee as the Board allows, he has been medically examined by a legally qualified medical practitioner approved by the Board, and a report of the results of the examination has been furnished by the medical practitioner to the Board or to a person or authority specified by the Board.

(2) A person referred to in the last preceding sub-section shall not contribute to the Fund under Part III of this Act unless the Board is satisfied, after considering the report of the medical practitioner, that the health and physical condition of that person are such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor.'

(2) Notwithstanding the last preceding sub-section, section four B of the Principal Act continues to apply in relation to a person who became an employee before the date of commencement of this section."

(viii)A minor amendment took place in 1959 by s.4 of Act No.102:

'4. Section four B of the Principal Act is amended by omitting from sub-section (1) the words 'Notwithstanding anything contained in any Act' and inserting in their stead the words 'Except as provided by this Act, and notwithstanding anything contained in any other Act.'

The first schedule of the same Act renumbered many sections of the Act and s.4B became s.5: See s.78.

(ix) Finally, by Act No.97 of 1965 a new s.5 was inserted in the form set out in paragraph 26 above.

28. It is true that this history does not shed a great deal of light on the relationship between sub-sections (1) and (2) of s.5. But it does indicate that from 1954 until the substitution of s.5 in 1965, a provision, namely s.4B as it then was, in a very similar form to s.5(1), was the sole provision in relation to the question of the establishment of the medical fitness of a person for the purpose of acceptance into the Fund or into the Provident Account. Like s.5(1), that provision at first sight appears to operate as a prohibition and not as a facultative provision providing a means of acceptance into the Fund or the Provident Account. Yet the latter must have been its effect. Section 4B(2) required that the Board be 'satisfied, after considering the report of the medical practitioner, that the health and physical condition of that person are such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor'. Thus some act expressing such satisfaction must have been required. In the absence of a provision the equivalent of s.5(2) which by the insertion of words expressly providing for positive acceptance (in that instance by the Superannuation Board) seems to afford a contrast with s.5(1) with its lack of such express provision, a reading of s.4B would not lead to the suspicion, so readily entertained when first reading s.5(1), that it was a mere prohibition. On the whole we consider that s.5(1) itself is not a mere prohibition, partly because of its history as outlined above, but also because of its relationship to s.5(2). Were it not more than a mere prohibition, it would in fact have no discernible effect. Section 5(1) emphasises the role of the Superannuation Board in allowing time after becoming an employee within which to undergo a medical examination, in approving medical practitioners, in the furnishing of medical reports, and in reaching the required state of satisfaction as to the health and physical fitness of the person in question. In s.5(2) the role in these matters is conferred upon the Public Service Board, the only difference being that it must certify such fitness and not merely be satisfied of it, the reason for this being that the Public Service Board could not decide the matter for itself but must certify the required matters to the Superannuation Board. Having so certified, the position was that 'the Superannuation Board may, in its discretion, accept him as a contributor to the Fund under Part III of this Act or to the Provident Account'. Thus the position seems to have been that, from the commencement of Act No.97 of 1965, it was intended that there be two alternative administrative procedures, one entirely under the control of the Superannuation Board, and one under the control, subject to the exercise of an ultimate discretion by the Superannuation Board, of the Public Service Board.

29. It follows that we must consider whether either of s.5(1) or of s.5(2) may be said to have applied in this case. First, it seems quite clear that s.5(2) could have had no operation, for the following reasons.

30. Where the criteria set out in paragraphs (a), (b) and (c) of s.5(2) are satisfied the sub-section goes on to provide that 'The Superannuation Board may, in its discretion, accept him as a contributor to the fund under Part III of this Act or to the Provident Account'. Let it be assumed that paragraphs (a), (b) and (c) were in this case satisfied. Even after making such assumptions, how could it be said, on the evidence placed before us, that the Superannuation Board accepted the applicant as a contributor to the Provident Account? There was no delegation by the Superannuation Board in relation to s.5(2). As far as we can tell the only evidence of any action by the Superannuation Board is that the applicant was allotted a number in the Superannuation fund. As we understood his argument, Mr Huttner did not contend that s.5(2) could have any application, and we do not consider that he could have so contended.

31. Mr Huttner, however, in the written submission previously referred to developed a theme which demands close attention to the provisions of s.5(1) in the light of the relevant facts. The term 'contributor to the Provident Account' in s.184(3) was said to be concerned with the status which a person held under the 1922 Act and not at all with the consequences which under that Act flowed from the holding of that status, so that any attempt to ascertain what status the applicant in fact held in terms of the events which occurred was misplaced. He pointed to s.79(1) of the 1922 Act, the relevant portions of which are set out in paragraph 25 above which provides that employees who by reason of the operation of s.5 of that Act are accepted as contributors to the Provident Account 'shall be contributors to the Provident Account'. Section 80(2) was then referred to as showing that the relevant status as a contributor to the Provident Account might exist independently of, and prior to, any contributions being in fact paid, or having become payable. Applying the facts to this analysis, it was submitted that the only conclusions for the Tribunal to draw were:

(i) That the applicant was recommended for the Provident Account;

(ii) That the recommendation became the Board's determination under s.5(1), through the actions of the Board's delegate at the Public Service Board, _ in or about the week of 13 August 1974, _ pursuant to the new procedure referred to in paragraph 8 of these Reasons.

(iii)That after the above determination was made, and after the Applicant had, as a result thereof, already acquired the status of 'contributor to the Provident Account', unfortunate administrative errors occurred, resulting in various actions being taken which were incorrect in the light of the actual legal status held by the Applicant.

32. We consider that this submission correctly emphasises the role of s.79(1). However even if it can be said that a 'status' of being 'a contributor to the Provident Account' should be recognized, nevertheless the critical question posed by s.184(3) remains whether, by application of the provisions of s.79(1)(a) to the facts as found, the applicant was 'a contributor to the Provident Account'. That critical question is ultimately answered by asking, was the applicant 'by reason of the operation of section 5' of the 1922 Act 'accepted' as a contributor to the Provident Account within the meaning of s.79(1)(a)? 'Acceptance' depends, s.5(2) of the 1922 Act being inapplicable for the reasons previously stated, upon the provisions of s.5(1) and upon an answer being given to the question whether the requisite criteria laid down by s.5(1) were satisfied.

33. It was emphasised at the hearing that the applicant's history as revealed at his medical examinations, and the decisions of the doctors as recorded after those examinations, was such that he could never have been accepted as a contributor to the old Fund, and could only have been accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account. Translated into the terms of s.5(1), there could never have been satisfaction that the applicant's health and physical fitness were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the old Fund. There could have been such satisfaction as to his acceptability only in relation to the Provident Account. So much may be conceded, but what is the position when attention is concentrated upon the s.5(1) criteria?

34. Those criteria raise a number of questions requiring answers, but the only one which we find it necessary to discuss is: Was the Superannuation Board satisfied that the health and physical fitness of the applicant were such as to justify his being accepted as a contributor to the Fund or to the Provident Account? The difficulties posed by the question are formidable. The only evidence before us of any action by the Superannuation Board itself was of its allocation to the applicant of a number in the old Fund. There was however a delegation (see Exhibit 5) under this sub-section from the Superannuation Board with the effect that the Board's powers and functions under s.5(1) might be exercised by the persons occupying, in Victoria, three specified offices, namely 'Inspector _ Class 9 (Position No.30); Inspector Recruitment, Assistant Inspector _ Class 7 (Position No.76) Office Categories Recruitment; and Assistant Inspector _ Class 7 (Position Nos. 22 and 62) Special Recruitment No.1 and No.2'. One or other of these three, or a person acting in such office, (and not just 'somebody (sent) down once a week' _ see transcript p.154), has to be supposed, for Mr Huttner's submission to be accepted, to have exercised the powers and functions of the Board by placing the rubber stamp previously referred to. Mr Huttner submitted that no evidence whatever was produced by the applicant, nor was any credible explanation suggested, to justify the view :

(i) that the newly laid down procedures had been ignored;

(ii) that for some inexplicable reason the delegate or delegates had decided to fly in the face of the Medical Officer's recommendation; or

(iii) that the delegate or delegates had played no part in the matter at all and that the Board had acted independently in totally rejecting, for no apparent reason, the recommendation of the Medical Officer.

He also said that in the light of evidence given by Mr A.J. Bugden of the Public Service Board, there was an onus upon the applicant to support the three views above stated.

35. We have considered this submission with great care, but have come to the conclusion that it cannot be supported. It seems to us to go back to the submission earlier referred to that a presumption of regularity should be applied. Presumably the contention is that the case is one apt for the application of the presumption omnia praesumuntur rite ac solemniter esse acta (see Cross on Evidence, Second Australian edition, paragraphs 2.12, 6.9 and 6.10). This is a rebuttable presumption of law, under which 'once the basic fact is established, the conclusion as to the existence of the presumed fact must be drawn in the absence of evidence to the contrary'. Some of the basic facts here proved are that a new procedure had been directed to commence, and that the Commonwealth Medical Officer had signed a form which, if the procedure had been properly followed, and all necessary further steps taken, would have resulted in the applicant becoming a contributor to the Provident Account. But there were other facts proved also. One is that a new procedure having just been directed to commence, the end result of it in this case went completely wrong, in that the applicant was treated as a contributor to the old Fund. Further, when Mr Bugden, having been referred to later examples of appointment sheets that had received the necessary rubber stamp, was asked (transcript p.155) whether he recalled 'having ever seen such stamp sheets in or around August 1974' answered 'Not at that time. I can recall seeing them in October 1974'. In view of the fact that Mr Bugden had previously stated that he could speak of the change in procedure from his own knowledge, this evidence, with the other, does not do much to help to create a presumption that all things had been duly done. We do not consider that any such presumption can be applied, and we likewise consider that no onus of the kind sought to be invoked can be identified. On the evidence before us, it would be equally possible to conclude, having regard to the end result, that the new procedures were not properly carried out. And if there were, contrary to our opinion, an onus of any kind to be discharged, why would it not lie upon the administration whose duty it was to maintain the relevant records? Lord Mansfield, in a passage of timeless value said in Blatch v. Archer [1774] EngR 2; (1774) 1 Cowp 63 at p.65:

'It is certainly a maxim that all evidence is to be weighed according to the proof which it was in the power of one side to produce, and in the power of the other to have contradicted.'

In a passage admittedly of no antiquity and unquestionably of much less authority, the present Presiding Member said in Re Eckersley and the Minister for the Capital Territory (1979) 2 ALD 303 at p.305:

I think that this is an instance in which, while no general responsibility of proof rests upon an applicant in an application to the Tribunal for review, (see Ladybird Childrens Wear Pty Ltd and Department of Business and Consumer Affairs (1978) 1 ALD 1 at 5) yet, when either party to such an application raises a specific fact for consideration, a situation can arise in which the responsibility of proving the existence of that fact must be accepted as falling upon the party who asserts its existence, in particular where that fact is, or has been, peculiarly within his own knowledge.'

See further Cross on Evidence, op cit, paragraphs 4.17 and 4.18. For these reasons we feel confident in saying at the very least that no onus of the kind suggested rested on the applicant.

35. The position is in our opinion that the requirement imposed by s.184(3) of the 1922 Act that the applicant 'was a contributor to the Provident Account' was not satisfied, and that there was thus no ground for the issue of the benefit classification certificate. The decision to issue the same must therefore be set aside.
