Re E and Commissioner of Police [1987] AATA 146 (22 April 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: E.

And: COMMISSIONER OF POLICE

No. A86/13

AAT No. 3392

Gun Licence

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.K. Todd (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Gun Licence - fit and proper person - exercise of discretion - prior firearms offences - attitude towards weapons

Gun Licence - fit and proper person - psychiatric illness

Gun Licence - inadequacies of Ordinance

Gun Licence Ordinance 1937, s.6

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, s.37

Re Hollands and Commissioner of Police (22 May 1987, Decision No. 470)

Re Sewter and Commissioner of Police (26 September 1983, Decision No. 1247)

HEARING

CANBERRA

22:4:1987

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

The applicant herein initially sought review of a decision of the Registrar of Gun Licences to refuse to grant to him gun licences under the Gun Licence Ordinance 1937 ("the Ordinance"). At the hearing the Commissioner of Police was by consent substituted as the respondent as it was the delegate of the Commissioner who, pursuant to s.6(2), objected to the grant of a licence to the applicant. The provisions of the Ordinance which relate to this procedure appear in paragraph 9 below.

2. The applicant brought into the Australian Capital Territory an M1 semi-automatic carbine a licence for which was refused due to its range. This weapon was apparently, after initial confiscation and then return for the purpose of enabling sale, sold through an authorised dealer and is not involved in these proceedings.

3. Other guns discussed at the hearing were three Baikal 12-gauge single-barrel shotguns, a Boito single-barrel shotgun and a Diana .22 air rifle. Of these, the first Baikal (serial number P38159) was licensed in New South Wales but was confiscated by police after an incident behind the Australian War Memorial in January 1986 which is referred to below. A NSW licence was also held for the second Baikal (serial number P38254) which was brought into the ACT and subsequently seized by Police in May 1986 after a search of the applicant's room at a hotel. The applicant appeared to think that he had applied for licences for a Boito 12-gauge single barrel shotgun (serial number 806936) on 29 January 1986 and for a Diana .22 air rifle on 3 February 1986. Both of these applications were for guns which were yet to be purchased. The documentation before the Tribunal however indicates that the application on 3 February 1986 was for a third Baikal (serial number P34876), also yet to be purchased. This third Baikal and the Boito are the subject of this application for review.

4. There was also evidence that the applicant had on 26 February 1986 applied for, and had been refused, a licence for a Diana .22 air rifle, but that refusal appears not to have been made the subject of this appeal.

5. Evidence was given that on 27 January 1986 the applicant was apprehended and subsequently convicted after shooting at magpies in Remembrance Park behind the Australian War Memorial. While being interviewed by the Police in relation to that offence the applicant admitted to having earlier that day shot at a snake which he had satisfied himself was a taipan and to having discharged the gun on the previous day when testing its range. He was consequently charged with, and convicted of four charges of "while not being the licencee under a gun licence using a gun between the hours of seven ante-meridian and seven post-meridian". These convictions were supported by relevant certificates of conviction.

6. Evidence was also given of the seizing of the second Baikal on 2 May 1986 from a hotel where the applicant was living at the time. This was carried out when the applicant was absent, but he attended at the Police Station as requested on the following day and was charged with having a gun in his possession while not being the licencee under a gun licence. The certificate of conviction tendered in relation to this event is flawed. Apart from referring to the wrong serial number of the weapon, it states that the applicant was convicted on a date several months before the date of the commission of the offence. The facts of an incident at a hotel, involving the second Baikal (serial number P38254) were however clearly given in evidence at the hearing of this application and those facts are relevant and indeed critical to this decision. While accepting and taking into account a conviction that is properly proved, in a review on the merits such as this, involving the exercise of a discretionary power, the true facts, conviction or no conviction, are properly before the Tribunal. I approach this part of the matter on this footing, and not on the basis of a conviction having taken place. In the circumstances, there is no question of the Tribunal proceeding on the basis of facts inconsistent with a conviction, assuming there was one: See Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs v. Gungor [1982] FCA 99; (1982) 42 ALR 209.

7. The respondent also called the applicant's psychiatrist, who gave evidence that the applicant had been admitted as an in-patient in January 1985 and January 1987. He also identified the applicant's medical records which were, with the applicant's consent, tendered by the respondent. Under cross-examination the applicant agreed that he had been admitted for "treatment of manic depressive illnes".

8. Also before the Tribunal was the statement lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") which stated:-

"The objection was lodged on the basis that the

Applicant was not a fit and proper person to be the

holder of a licence under the Ordinance in the

Australian Capital Territory. The Applicant was

at the time of the objection before the Courts for

firearm offences."

9. Section 6 of the Ordinance is of little assistance in determining what basis should be adopted for deciding whether a person should be granted a licence. The part of that section that is relevant states:-

"6.(1) Subject to this section, the Registrar

may, as he thinks fit, in pursuance of applications

made in accordance with Form A in the Schedule -

(a) grant a gun licence to any person who

satisfies him that he is not less than

sixteen years of age; and

(b) grant a pistol licence to any person who

satisfies him that he is not less than

eighteen years of age,

and renew any such licence.

(2) The Registrar shall submit to the

Commissioner of Police all applications for

licences under this section, and, if the

Commissioner or a member of the Police Force

thereto authorized in writing by him certifies that

he objects to the grant of a licence to any

applicant, the Registrar shall not grant a licence

to that applicant but shall forward to that

applicant a notice in writing informing him of the

objection."

10. As was stated in Re Hollands and Commissioner of Police (22 May 1981, Decision No. 470) handed down 22 May 1981, at paragraph 10 by Mr. A.N. Hall, Senior Member (as he then was):-

"As will be observed, however, s.6 of the Ordinance

is silent as to the considerations which should

guide the exercise of the discretionary powers

conferred on the Registrar and the Commissioner of

Police respectively. It does not follow,

therefore, that the powers are arbitrary or

completely unlimited: cf Water Conservation and

Irrigation Commission (NSW) v. Browning (1947) 74

CLR 492 at 505 per Dixon J. They are powers which

may appropriately be exercised 'for the purpose of

attaining the object and securing the purpose' of

the legislation, 'such object and purpose being

ascertained by an examination of the terms' of the

legislation: per Latham C.J. in Shrimpton v.

Commonwealth [1945] HCA 4; (1945) 69 CLR 613 at 620. Within the

limits of the considerations which the statute by

implication makes relevant, the discretionary

powers are nevertheless very wide: cf R. v. Murphy;

Ex parte Clift (1980) Qd R 1 at 8."

11. From all of the evidence, including the evidence given by the applicant, the discretion should not in my view be exercised in favour of the applicant.

12. During his evidence the applicant made statements, inter alia, as follows:-

"That is also the same Baikal 12-gauge single shot

shotgun. Quite inoffensive."

"I do not keep a car as some people do and that

psychological niche to me is filled by keeping a

gun."

"It (Canberra) is not a metropolis, although it has

laws of a metropolis. We do live in a very

dangerous part of Australia from the point of view

(of) savage native life. I have shot a snake

which has been identified as a taipan here in the

ACT and I do not - I say I did it but I am not

guilty about it. I do not feel guilty about doing

such a thing . . ."

13. In addition to these statements, the applicant in his final address, stated, inter alia:

"Firstly, I do intend to own another firearm simply

because Australia - there are too many verminous

pests about not to. And I will do so at some

location whether within the ACT or New South Wales

or Queensland, whatever the outcome."

"So I think it is quite a normal thing to own and

despite the very strong laws on endangered species

in the ACT, it is in fact far from a metropolis

with a few rare species fluttering around. There

are plenty of wild and dangerous pests such as

taipans, magpies and other verminous species of

that nature, foxes even, so I do not think we are

actually talking about a man-designed or

God-planned nature reserve. We are looking at

chaos in most of the areas of the ACT."

"So I do not regard it myself as unreasonable to

shoot particularly bad pests such as magpies or

taipan, although given that the laws are so strict

in the ACT and that the chances of apprehension are

so high, I certainly would not wish to undertake

that again but, nevertheless, I shall acquire a

licence whether here in the ACT or in New South

Wales or Victoria or Queensland . . ."

14. It is the Tribunal's view that, considering the evidence, the above statements and the convictions for firearms offences, the applicant is not a fit and proper person to hold a gun licence and that the decision of the Commissioner should be affirmed on that basis. In the course of both evidence and final address the applicant indicated a reckless attitude towards the dangerous nature of firearms. Additionally, he expressed the views that he did not feel guilty about killing protected species, that guns fulfilled a psychological need for him and that he would move outside the ACT to obtain firearms if necessary. In addition, the medical evidence also raises concerns as to the fitness of the applicant to hold a licence in relation to firearms.

15. Comment must be made on the inadequacies of the Ordinance in this area. The Tribunal endorses what Mr. Ewart Smith (Senior Member) said in Re Sewter and Commissioner of Police - (26 September 1983, Decision No. 1247), at paragraph 15:-

"Before concluding, I think I should observe that

the evidence in this case demonstrates the

desirability of the inclusion in the Ordinance of

specific criteria to be applied by the Registrar or

the Commissioner of Police in considering

applications for gun or pistol licences. This

matter was adverted to by Mr. A.N. Hall (Senior

Member, now Deputy President) in Re Hollands and

Commissioner of Police (1981) 3 ALN N78 (Note 46.)

He commented, inter alia, as follows:

'(iv) Finally, as the (Gun Licence) Ordinance

specifies criteria by reference to which other

forms of licences or approvals may be granted

(cf ss.7F(2) and 10(3)), and as the requirement

that an applicant for a gun or pistol licence

must be a 'fit and proper person' to hold such a

licence appears, quite properly in my view, to

be fundamental to the administration of the gun

and pistol licensing provisions, it may be more

satisfactory if that requirement, and any other

appropriate requirements (cf s.7F(2)), were

specified in the Ordinance rather than being

left to implication.'

I would respectfully adopt those comments. If the

control of firearms by individuals is to be left as

it is under the Ordinance, it would seem that only

a person who has a recorded offence or is

notoriously an associate of criminals is likely to

be challenged on an application for a gun licence

however unfitted he may be to possess firearms.

The question whether there should be a requirement

on an applicant for a licence to demonstrate some

positive evidence of fitness to possess the gun in

respect of which the licence is sought - if only in

the form of character references from responsible

persons in the community - is a matter to which the

policy Department might with profit direct its

attention."

16. For the reasons given above the Tribunal will affirm the decision under review.
