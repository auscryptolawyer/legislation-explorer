NT85/232 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 399; (1987) 18 ATR 4027; 87 ATC 1133 (6 November 1987)

Last Updated: 30 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

RECOUPMENT TAX - vendor and former shareholder - objection to assessment of company not lodged by nominee of majority objection to assessment of company disallowed - whether T (UCT) A legislation invalid as "uncontestable" tax.

RECOUPMENT TAX - practice and procedure - application to amend objection - proposed amendment not raising arguable case - amendment refused.

INCOME TAX - practice and procedure - application to amend objection - proposed amendment not raising arguable case amendment refused.

Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act 1982 - s5;

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss 188A, I88B, 190(b).

MacCormick v FCT [1984] HCA 20; (1985) 158 CLR 622;

Case U175 87 ATC 1005;

NT80/53 & Ors. - 5 November 1987;

REASONS FOR DECISION

6 November 1987 Mr Peter M Roach (Senior Member)

  1. The Applicant in these references is one of many persons who genuinely cannot understand how it is that they could be faced with a tax liability under the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act 1982 ("the T (UCT) A Act") when at all times they conducted themselves in accordance with the law as it stood and who, in doing so, had not subjected themselves to any liability in tax such as is now claimed against them.
  2. Although the Applicant is an accountant by profession he has no experience in tax litigation and little understanding of taxation law. Despite that, he chose to represent himself in these proceedings. I suspect that, at the conclusion of them, his understanding of the issues and of the law to be applied in their resolution will have changed but little.
  3. The Applicant was a member of a group who came to be interested in understanding the stock market. He perceived of the interest as an educational one which involved using a company as "an investment company whose objects were to teach its members in the operations of investments and ancillary functions and was not investing to make a profit on resale but merely the operations of investing and the education of the members in investing and the 'whyfores' of investing". Be that as it may, the educational process was very practical. It involved the investment of available resources in shares; it involved "profits"; and, according to the Commissioner, it involved income tax.
  4. The group comprised 22 members. They met periodically and discussed the share market and investment policies and decided what was to be bought and what was to be sold. The Applicant claimed that they formed two companies, each with the same 22 shareholders: one to trade and deal in shares with a view to profit, the other to hold shares by way of investment only. He produced no evidence to establish the existence of a second company and no evidence to indicate the activities of either company. I am not persuaded that his evidence was accurate. On the basis of the accounts presented in evidence before me by Counsel for the Commissioner, there was but one company: the target company in question.
  5. The Applicant held 3 of the 99 $100 shares in that company and it, by its own accounts, shortly before the critical date held an investment and trading portfolio of shares which had respectively cost $145,242 and $122,124. By mid-1980 trading operations had realized a substantial profit and, on the advice of accountants from within the group, it was decided that the entire shareholding in the target company should be sold. The Applicant understood that it was essential that the company be "cashed up" in order to effect the sale and that, that having been done, all the shares in the company were sold to a third party for a price of $169,290: $8,835 less than total shareholders' funds represented, by Capital $9,900 "Capital Reserve $111,991" and "Retained Profits $56,234", His share was $5,130.

The Accounts warranted as accurate by the Vendors included an endorsement:-

"Note re Income Tax

As these are interim accounts, no provision has been made for Income Tax.

Our estimate is - Net Profit 52,462

- Less Dividends

Rebateable (2,007)

- Capital Profits-

within 12 months 50,977

101,432 @ 46% - $46,658.72"

In order to effect the "cashing up" of the company all shares, including those in the investment portfolio, had to be sold and those sales also generated a substantial profit. The Applicant claims, and may well claim correctly, that no portion of the profit on the sale of "investments" generated assessable income for the target company. His claim was that the only reason proposed for the sale was to avoid the risk of having to contest an ill-founded assessment seeking to tax the company on the profit from the investments. I am not persuaded that that was so.

  1. The Applicant claims that, by the legal norms of the time, having ceased to be a shareholder in the target company, he ceased to be responsible for, or to have control in any degree over, the affairs of the target company. He claims that by those norms, the target company, under the control of its new owners, was responsible for paying any tax - whether by way of income tax or undistributed profits tax - which might be properly levied against it.
  2. These norms changed in December 1982 when the Parliament enacted the T(UCT)A Act. In due course in MacCormick v FCT [1984] HCA 20; (1985) 158 CLR 622 the High Court held that legislation to be constitutional.
  3. Following the passage of the T (UCT) A Act by letter of 30 September 1983 the Commissioner gave notice to the Applicant of assessments against the target company in sums of $41,135-04 by way of income tax for the year of income ended 30 June 1981 and in sums of $1,756 and $7,712-50 for Division 7 tax for the years of income ended 30 June 1980 and 1981 respectively. The same letter gave notice to the Applicant that

"any former owner nominated by a majority of the former owners who received an original/originals and/or a copy/copies of the company notice (s), is entitled to exercise the same rights of objection and appeal against the company's assessments as are available to the company itself."

The Applicant was also advised that "these notices are being served on you as a preliminary step towards the raising of a recoupment tax assessment".

  1. The Applicant objected. He did so on his own behalf only and without having been nominated to do so by any other shareholders. His explanation for that was that his fellow-shareholders, for their own reasons, had preferred to concede the claims against them rather than "stand up to" the Commissioner and resist unjustified assessments.
  2. The Applicant made his objection within the next 60 days. But it was only by letter of 15 May 1983, more than five months after the period for objecting had expired, that the Commissioner notified the Applicant "that your letter is unable to be treated as a valid objection against the company's assessments".
  3. Assuming that that statement was soundly founded in the law, that put an end to that matter because, by the standards of that time, an objection not lodged within the strictly limited period of 60 days allowed for the purpose was a nullity. There could be no extension of time. As a result of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986 that harsh procedural limitation has been removed in that extensions of time may now be granted Css.l88A, 188B of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Tax Act")]. But that power cannot be exercised to provide relief for this Applicant because the last date for the filing of an objection fell before 1 July 1986 [s.221 Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986].
  4. The next development was that, by assessments dated 20 December 1984, the Commissioner assessed the Applicant as liable to pay $1,494-96 with reference to the unpaid income tax of the company for the year of income ended 30 June 1981 and amounts of $65.28 and $286.72 in respect of unsatisfied Division 7 assessments against the company for the years of income ended 30 June 1980 and 1981. The total demanded of him was $1,846.96.
  5. He objected to the three assessments. I do not propose to set out the terms of the objections in full. It suffices to say that he contended:-
    • (a) that the target company had not derived a taxable income;
    • (b) that the T(UCT)A legislation was unconstitional;
    • (c) that all responsibility on his part for the actions of the target company had ceased upon the sale of his interest in the company; and
    • (d) that it might have been that, following that sale, the target company had participated in a scheme or had paid the tax with the result that he would have been relieved from liability.

14. When it was made clear to the Applicant that the High Court of Australia had upheld the validity of the material parts of the legislation in its decision in MacCormick (ante), the Applicant abandoned that objection. That decision also established the third contention to be unsound.

  1. When it appeared that the essential complaint of the Applicant was that he was being denied justice in that the Commissioner was seeking to recover from him an amount which could only be due by him if the company had failed to discharge its tax liabilities, and that he was being denied the opportunity to establish that the company had no such liabilities, he sought leave to amend his objection in two respects: first, to contend that he had been denied any right to put in issue the question of the validity of the assessments against the company; and secondly, that by providing that objection could only be made to the assessment of the company by the nominee of a majority of shareholders, the T(UCT)A Act had established an incontestable, and therefore, unconstitutional tax.
  2. The application to amend was opposed by Counsel for the Commissioner and, having considered his submissions, I formed the view that the amendments would not raise any arguable case (cf. Case U175 87 ATC 1005 pa 43) and, therefore, should not be allowed.
  3. As to the first amendment sought, the T (UCT) A legislation clearly made provision that objection to the assessments against the company could be made by the nominee of a majority of shareholders. If the Applicant had sought and obtained that support, a sufficient objection could have been lodged in time. Further, the Applicant was not misled in that regard. Not only did the Act make provision to that effect but, when the Commissioner gave notice to the Applicant that he was being looked upon as a person potentially liable for vendor recoupment tax, the Commissioner set forth an accurate summary of the relevant provisions. In short, the opportunity was there for the Applicant to object and he did not avail himself of it. He might do so now were it not for the fact that, for reasons already mentioned, no extension of time may now be granted.
  4. By the second amendment proposed, the Applicant sought to contend that he is being denied justice because, in the events which have happened, he has been denied the opportunity to challenge the assessments against the company. That denial stems in part from the fact that only a nominee of a majority of shareholders might mount such an objection; from the circumstance that he failed to heed the provisions of the Act and the notice from the Commissioner informing him that only such a nominee was so entitled; and by the circumstance that the harsh time restrictions of days past remain in full force and effect for persons for whom the last day for objecting fell before 1 July 1986. The requirement that the objector should be the nominee of a majority means that, in a three-man company, two vendor-shareholders could deny their fellow former-shareholder, with as much as 99.9% of the shares, the right to object to the assessment of the company.
  5. However, as Counsel for the Commissioner pointed out, this aspect of the argument had already been dealt with in the reasons for decision of Gibbs, CJ, Wilson, J, Deane, J and Dawson, J in MacCormick (ante, at page 641), where their Honours said:-

"However, the liability which the legislation imposes to pay recoupment tax is not incontestable in this sense. One of the criteria of liability for recoupment tax is a pre-existing, unpaid liability on the part of a target company to pay company tax. The fact that a person not liable to pay company tax but liable to pay a different tax in the form of recoupment tax has a limited right or no right at all to contest the liability of the relevant target company for company tax is not to the point. It is the existence of the overdue company tax which is one of the criteria of liability for recoupment tax and that existence is established once an assessment of company tax is made and any objection has been finalized for the period for objecting has expired and the tax remains unpaid at the relevant time. Liability to pay recoupment tax does not arise until these events have occurred and it arises only upon the assessment of those persons to whom the legislation applies. The assessment of those persons is open to the ordinary processes of review and appeal. This is because the 1982 Assessment Act incorporates the relevant parts of the Income Tax Assessment Act relating to the assessment and collection of tax, including review and appeal.

It would not be to the point if no right to contest the liability of a target company to pay company tax were given to those persons liable to pay recoupment tax upon the basis of the overdue company tax. The limited rights given to those persons to contest the liability of the company are, from the point of view of legality, gratuitous. If in a particular case the provisions have the effect that a person liable to pay the recoupment tax is unable to contest the liability of the target company, and the assessment of that liability was in fact incorrect, the result will be plainly unjust, but it will not mean that the recoupment tax is incontestable. Of course, it was not argued, nor could it be argued, that company tax is itself an incontestable tax. Liability to pay that tax arises upon the assessment of a company pursuant to the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act and in relation to that assessment the ordinary processes of review and appeal are open. Once those processes are complete or the time for taking them has expired, the existence of the company tax may be proved by recourse to s. 177(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act which provides that production of a notice of assessment shall be conclusive evidence of the due making of the assessment and that the amount and all the particulars of the assessment are correct."

20. For this Applicant, the critical words in the passage quoted are

"If in a particular case the provisions have the effect that a person liable to pay the recoupment tax is unable to contest the liability of the target company, and the assessment of that liability was in fact incorrect, the result will be plainly unjust, but it will not mean that the recoupment tax is incontestable."

The fact that their Honours considered such a situation to be "plainly unjust", despite which they upheld the validity of the legislation, does not indicate that the Court was indifferent to such an injustice. Rather, the observation reflects the circumstance that the High Court has a very important, but not unlimited, role to play in the affairs of society. It has the responsibility of declaring as invalid such Acts of Parliament as infringe the Constitution. It has no general mandate to strike down laws which it considers to be unjust. The responsibility for the enactment of laws lies with Parliament and, if it happens that a law is within the Constitutional authority of Parliament, it will be a valid law even though it works injustice. When such a law is enacted, the Parliament fails in the discharge of its responsibility to the community. Similarly, when a Parliament fails to use its power to correct unjust legislation, it fails in the discharge of its responsibility to the community. But it is for the Parliament, and not the High Court or any other institution in the community, to rectify such injustices.

21. That being so, it is not open to the Applicant in these proceedings to contend that the target company did not derive a taxable income or even, as would seem to be more appropriate, to contend that it derived a lesser taxable income than that assessed by the Commissioner.

  1. The objection raised a fourth point, namely that actions of the target company following the sale might have resulted in the Applicant being relieved from liability. The Applicant advanced no evidence in support of any such contention. As the Applicant bears the onus of proof [s190 (b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936], that ground of objection must fail.
  2. I conclude by addressing three other questions which might have been raised by the grounds of objection. The first is that the assessments should fail because conditions specified in s5(1) of the T (UCT) A Act have not been satisfied. Such contentions were successful in a recent case (NT80/58 & Ors 5 November 1987). A second argument might have been that the Applicant only held his shares as a "bare trustee" (s5) - an argument considered in Case U1 (87 ATC 101 - presently subject to appeal). The third contention might have been that the Tribunal should have exercised the discretion conferred by s5(4) of the T(UCT)A Act enabling the Commissioner, and upon review this Tribunal, to grant relief from liability for the tax - a course followed in another recent case (NT85/625 & Qrs. -5 November 1987). I have addressed those questions with a view to fairness to an unrepresented Applicant but, having done so, in my view the answer is quite clear. Nothing was advanced by the Applicant in evidence, or even so much as suggested in argument, indicating that there might be any basis in those arguments for allowing his objections.

24. As the Applicant bears the onus of proving the assessments to be excessive and has not discharged it, the decisions under review will be affirmed.
