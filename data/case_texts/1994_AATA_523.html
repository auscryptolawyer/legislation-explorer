WT92/77 and Commissioner of Taxation [1994] AATA 523; (1994) 28 ATR 1199; 94 ATC 280 (2 June 1994)

Last Updated: 15 May 2009

INCOME TAX - Allowable deductions - Expenditure of amount of meal allowance on food and drink by cinema projectionist (not entitled to meal break) for consumption in projection room during shift - Whether deductible - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, sub-ss 51(1) and 82KT(6).



Case U148 87 ATC 868

Case U212 87 ATC 1195

Atwood Oceanics Australia Pty Ltd v FCT 89 ATC 4808

Case Y8 91 ATC 166

FCT v Cooper 91 ATC 4396



AAT Decision No 9518



DECISION AND REASONS



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) WT92/77

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re: APPLICANT



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION RESPONDENT



Tribunal: Dr P Gerber (Deputy President)



Date: 2 June 1994



Place: Perth



Decision: The Tribunal affirms the objection decision under review.



(Sgd) Dr P Gerber

DEPUTY PRESIDENT

REASONS FOR DECISION



1. The background to this application can be briefly set out. It is contained in the paragraphs under the heading "A. Background" in the "Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions" of the applicant (hereafter referred to as "T") in respect of this application which was lodged with the Tribunal, and which the respondent accepts. In those paragraphs it is stated:



The Applicant was employed [full time] by [Employer] as a Cinema Projectionist at the [name] Cinema in [capital city] throughout the 1990 year of income. His ordinary hours of duty during the year of income were 7 hours a day for five days a week and he generally worked every second Sunday. On occasion he worked six days a week.



Included on the 1990 group certificate issued to the Applicant by the Employer was a meal allowance of $754 which was claimed by him as a deduction for work related expenses in his income tax return for that year of income. Attached to the income tax return was a schedule which stated that the amount of meal allowance shown on the group certificate was incorrect and that the correct amount should have been $1655; the additional $901 having been included as gross salary on the certificate.



The Respondent issued a 1990 assessment to the Applicant on 13 December 1990 on the basis of the taxable income returned by the Applicant.



On 18 December 1990 the Applicant objected to the assessment on the grounds that a further deduction of $901 was allowable for meal expenses.



On 25 February 1991 the Respondent notified the Applicant that his objection had been disallowed.



A notice of amended assessment for the 1990 year was issued by the Respondent to the Applicant on 20 March 1991 in which the deduction of $754 allowed in the original assessment was disallowed.



The Applicant lodged an objection against that assessment on 22 March 1991 and claimed that the amount of $754 was an allowable deduction.



By letters dated 17 April 1991 and 14 June 1991 the Applicant requested that the decision [of 25 February 1991] by the Respondent to disallow his objection [in relation to the $901] be referred to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review.



2. On 10 December 1992 the Respondent complied with that request.



. At the hearing of this application, T was represented by Mr. C Vella of Beattie & Associates, his tax agents and accountants, and the respondent was represented by Dr. C J Carr of Counsel, who was instructed by the Australian Government Solicitor.

4. As part of T's case, T gave oral evidence, and called a Mr. Kenneth Charles Ophel, who gave evidence by way of a telephone link-up. The respondent did not call any witnesses, but did tender a letter to T's representatives dated 21 January 1993 (together with annexures) from a company in the group of which T's employer was a part (exhibit 1). The "section 37 documents" became exhibit A.

5. From the paragraphs under the heading "B. Statement of Facts" in T's "Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions", and the respondent's "response" to that document, it is apparent that the parties have also agreed on the following further facts:



...



2. [T] was employed under the Theatrical Employees ([Employer] and [name] Cinemas) Agreement 1989 ("the Agreement").



3. Clause 3(a) and Schedule "A" of the Agreement binds the Employer to the Agreement.



4. Clause 5 of the Agreement provides that the Agreement replaces the Theatrical Employees (Cinema and Drive-In Industry) Award 1983 ("the Award") in respect of [T's] employer and their employees.



5. Clause 10(e)(i) of the Agreement states:



The span of hours for employees shall be as follows:



(1) cleaners 12 midnight - 12 midnight

(2) maintenance employees 7.00am - 12 midnight

(3) drive-in employees

(a) 9.00am - 12 midnight, or

(b) 10.00am - 1.00am in daylight saving

(4) all others - 9.00am - 12 midnight



All work performed outside those hours shall be overtime and be paid at the rate of double time except where otherwise provided for.



6. Clause 12(a) of the Agreement states "Overtime shall be time worked outside the span of hours as 10(e)(i) or in excess of 7 hours on any day or in excess of 5 days per week ...".



...

9. Clause 13(a) of the Agreement states "Weekly employees required to work on a Sunday as part of their normal 5 days work shall be paid for such work at the rate of double time and a quarter."



10. "Continuous Cinema" is defined in clause 41(a)(vii) as meaning "a Cinema where continuous sessions or performances are usually held daily".



11. [T] was not entitled to meal intervals under the Agreement.



12. Clause 29(e) of the Agreement states:



(i) For Projection Staff and Box Office Staff a Meal Allowance of $33.23 per week shall be paid. This amount will be indexed six monthly based upon the consumer price index take-away food component.



(ii) Projection Staff and Box Office Staff required to work for more than one and a half hours beyond their ordinary finishing time shall be paid an additional Meal Allowance calculated at 1/5 of the weekly Meal Allowance.



(iii) Where projection or box office staff



(1) work on a sixth day; or



(2) commences work at or before 1.30pm on a Sunday and works through the period 5.00pm to 7.00pm;



they shall be paid an additional meal allowance calculated at one-fifth of the appropriate weekly meal allowance.



13. The meal allowance of $33.23 per week provided under clause 29(e)(i) of the Agreement was payable in connection with normal hours worked.



14. The meal allowance of $754 shown on [T]'s 1990 group certificate represented the allowance paid to him during the period 1 July 1989 to 11 December 1989 from which no tax instalments were deducted.



15. A meal allowance of $901 was paid to [T] during the period 12 December 1989 to 30 June 1990 and included in the gross salary shown on the group certificate from which tax instalments were deducted.



On six occasions during the 1990 year of income [T] was paid an additional meal allowance under clause 29(e) of Agreement as follows:



07/08/89 $ 6.65

21/08/89 $ 6.87

04/09/89 $ 6.87

18/09/89 $ 6.87

15/01/90 $13.80

25/06/90 $ 7.10

Total $40.56



On two occasions - 10/07/89 and 24/07/89 T was paid $6.65. T claims that these were also occasions when he was paid an additional meal allowance under clause 29(e) of the Agreement, whereas the respondent claims that these two payments were made under "The 1986 Cinema Agreement". For present purposes, nothing turns on this disagreement.



6. The reasons given in the respondent's "section 37 documents" for the objection decision of 25 February 1991 are:



1. The amount of $901 received by [T] from the employer during the 1990 year of income was paid to compensate him for working during meal breaks and was not an allowance paid for the purpose of enabling him to purchase food and drink in connection with overtime or normal hours worked by him.



Any expenditure that he may have incurred in the purchase of food and drink during the 1990 year of income is of a private or domestic nature and was not incurred in gaining or producing assessable income within the meaning in sub-section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") and does not therefore constitute an allowable deduction within that sub-section.



2. Alternatively, $880 of the amount was paid as a meal allowance for the purpose of enabling [T] to purchase food and drink in connection with normal hours worked by him.



Any expenditure that he may have incurred in the purchase of food and drink in relation to that meal allowance during the year of income is of a private or domestic nature and was not incurred in gaining or producing assessable income within the meaning of sub-section 51(1) of the Act and does not therefore constitute an allowable deduction within that sub-section. (exh A, T2, p 7)



7. However, in paragraph 9 of the respondent's "response" to the Applicant's "Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions", it is stated:



The Respondent says that:



(a) of the total amount claimed by [T] as allowable deductions ($901), the Respondent no longer contests, and concedes, the claim for $21 in respect of expenditure incurred by the Applicant on 15 January 1990 and 25 June 1990;



(b) as to the balance of $880 in issue in this application, the Respondent accepts that [T] incurred the expenditure on meals and accordingly no question of substantiation of that amount arises; and



(c) that the balance of $880 is not an allowable deduction for the reasons stated in paragraphs 12(f) and (g) hereunder.



Paragraphs 12(f)and (g) state:



(f) the sum of $880 is not an allowable deduction against [T]'s assessable income for the year ended 30 June 1990 as the expenditure was not incurred in gaining or producing assessable income as required by section 51(1) of the Act: see the decision of the Full Federal Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation -v- Cooper (1991) 91 ATC 4396; and



(g) further the sum of $880 is excluded from deductibility by section 51(1) of the Act as an outgoing of a private nature: Federal Commissioner of Taxation -v- Cooper (supra)



8. More facts emerged as a result of T's evidence.

9. T was a cinema projectionist for some 40 years before taking "a voluntary redundancy" in 1991. It became clear during his evidence that when he:



first started in the industry we used to be able to take a meal break. There were sufficient projectionists in those days to enable us to roster ourselves for meals. Eventually they cut down the staff - oh, in those days they used to have to have two people in the bio box at all times because we were running nitrate film. Later on they introduced safety film. The staffing in the bio box was reduced gradually until in the end we found that we were running the shows on our own without any assistance ... So we found ourselves on our own without assistants. They were dispensed with, presumably cutting down wages, expenses, and we were compensated by being given a meal or, alternatively, given money to purchase a meal so that we would not have to take a break during our working period. ... (tr p 6)



. T was to add that he was without an assistant in the projection room from approximately 1984, from which time his duties as a projectionist had become a "full time position" - "you could sit down for five or ten minutes but you constantly had to be aware of what was going on on the screen. You couldn't relax ...". Whilst on duty (during the relevant period) T could not leave the projection room for any long period of time, only to do such things as go into the theatre to check the sound volume, or to go to the toilet.



11. During the relevant period, T alternated between day and evening shifts. The day shift was from 9.00am until "approximately 4, 4.20 in the afternoon". The evening shift was from "prior to 4 o'clock ... until the showdown, 11, 11.30, whatever".

12. During the relevant period T used the meal allowance he was given to purchase food and drink to be consumed during his shift, not after ("I could not imagine going through an entire shift without eating or drinking" - tr p 8). He "very, very rarely brought food in [to work] with" him, generally sending "one of the usherettes ... to pop out and buy something" for him. (tr p 9)

13. Although, during the relevant period, T had no "defined" meal break whilst on duty, and "could only eat while ... on the job" in the projection room, he generally ate his lunch or dinner once he had changed from one projector that was running out of film to a second projector with a full spool of film, rewound the first spool of film, and then prepared that projector for the next session ("then you can maybe relax a little and have a bite to eat" - tr p 9).

14. During cross-examination, Dr. Carr took T to extracts from "The Theatrical and Amusement Employees Awards 1947/1948" ("the 1947/1948 award"), "The Theatrical Employees (Cinema and Drive-In Industry) Award 1983" ("the 1983 award"), as well as the subject 1989 agreement. As a result, it became clear that, prior to the 1989 agreement, the respective provisions which entitled T to a meal allowance stated that the allowance was "for the purchase of a meal", whereas clause 29(e) of the 1989 agreement (the source of T's entitlement to the meal allowance during the relevant period) makes no reference to the allowance being for "the purchase of a meal". This notwithstanding, T's evidence was that it was his understanding that the allowance under clause 29(e) of the 1989 agreement was paid



to purchase a meal to consume, as I understand it, while we are working to enable us to give the employer a continuous show, not paid a bit extra for not going on a meal break at all. If we were going to do that, we would want double time or time and a half. I mean, we are looking at $7 to purchase a meal for the convenience of the employer. (tr p 14)



15. T's employer's understanding as to the reason for the payment of the subject allowance can be gleaned by setting out relevant extracts from a letter dated 2 July 1991 from the respondent to the employer (exh A, T21, p 115), the employer's reply of 12 July 1991 (exh A, T22, p 116), and the subsequent letter to T's representatives which became exhibit 1. The respondent wrote:



Further to the allowances paid to the above-named taxpayer [T] during the 1990 financial year, would you please supply the following:



1. The amount paid as meal allowance, where "meal allowance" is defined as meaning "an allowance paid or payable to an employee for the purpose of enabling the employee to purchase food and drink, but not including any part of a travel allowance":



...



3. The amount of allowance paid as compensation for the taxpayer having worked during a meal break: and



...



T's employer replied:



...



1. The amount paid as meal allowance, where "meal allowance" is defined as meaning "an allowance paid or payable to an employee for the purpose of enabling the employee to purchase food and drink, but not including any part of a travel allowance" was NIL.



...



3. The amount of allowance paid as compensation for the taxpayer having worked during a meal break: ALL APPLICABLE, and



...



The subsequent letter to T's representatives stated:



...



We [the group of which T's employer was a member] do not provide a meal allowance for the purpose of purchasing food and/or drink. It is an amount paid because the projectionist is unable to leave the projection box to have a meal break, as is a booking clerk/ticket seller unable to leave the cashier area for a meal break.



...



16. The other witness, Mr. Ophel, was federal industrial officer with the Australian Theatrical and Amusement Employees Association between 1984 and 1986, and federal secretary of that association between 1986 and 1991. He was involved in the negotiations which took place between that association and employers in the drafting of the 1989 agreement. Mr. Ophel was ultimately allowed to give evidence as to his understanding as to the purpose of the subject meal allowance (partially as a result of the fact that T had already given evidence as to his understanding of the intention behind the meal allowance, and the T documents contained references to T's employer's understanding of the matter):



It was for the purchase of meals and because projectionists in what were either known as continuous theatres or city cinemas had always worked a continuous shift, ... their pay rate in fact already had loaded within it an amount of money to allow for that fact. It simply didn't change. Certainly ... it has always been my understanding and, I believe, the understanding of all the parties at the times I was involved that it was for the purchase of meals. (tr p 22)



17. However, as far as T's, his employer's or Mr. Ophel's understanding is concerned, I have kept in mind that, ultimately,



"[t]he question of interpretation of [clause 29 of the 1989 agreement] is a matter of construction according to ordinary principles. Any understanding that parties to the award may have had as to the meaning of the clause would not assist although regard must be given to the fact that the award was not the product of a professional draftsman" (Atwood Oceanics Australia Pty Ltd v FCT 89 ATC 4808, at pp 4814-5).



18. Mr. Ophel's explanation as to the history of the granting of a meal allowance to projectionists is, on the other hand, useful in placing the allowance in its historic perspective. He stated:



It derives from an award which was entitled the Theatrical Amusement Employees Award 1947 which had been around, obviously, from that year and was later consolidated into a version entitled the Theatrical Amusement Employees Award 1947/1978. In that award there was a meal allowance expressed in money terms and, at the time that I became involved, it had not been increased for many years. I cannot remember the precise rate but it was very, very low. ... It was agreed that it ought to be indexed, as we regarded it as a reimbursement allowance, and it was agreed at that stage that we should base it on the rates of pay in the award, so, for some time, the agreement was in fact expressed in terms of an amount of time paid. ... Then later down the track, getting up to quote the period you are talking about, it was agreed that was inappropriate because in fact it gave some sort of indication that perhaps that allowance was something other than a reimbursement allowance, and that had never been the intention. So it was decided to seek another form of indexing and that it was to be the CPI was agreed ... I think one of the third indexes in the CPI being the closest ... it may have been the take-away food index ... So, roughly speaking, that was the history. There was a meal allowance from the very earliest awards ... for projectionists who worked this continuous shift and were not permitted to leave their bio, as they called the projection room, and they always received an allowance to purchase their meals. It was simply the way it was expressed that was altered when we got up those amendments [the 1989 agreement]... (tr p 21-22)

19. Of further relevance to emerge from Mr. Ophel's evidence was the fact that it was not just because of the need to ensure that the projection and sound equipment continued to operate correctly that projectionists (including T) could not leave the projection room whilst on duty; they also "had chief responsibility for ensuring the safety of the patrons":



[The projectionist] could see the cinema, he could see the patrons, if there was a fire, if there was any kind of problem. It was his duty to shut down equipment, to ensure that the safety lights were put on, and indeed part of his job was to always make sure that all the emergency lighting systems operated as well, and so on ... (tr p 28)



He also confirmed, what some may consider would be obvious, that the 1989 agreement did not compel projectionists to eat a meal.



20. In the present circumstances, I accept the respondent’s submission that, ultimately, “whether the $880 was paid [to T] for working through what would otherwise have been meal intervals or for the purchase of meals is irrelevant”. I would, however, for the sake of completeness, state that, having regard to the background, and, more importantly, the terms of the 1989 agreement, I am satisfied that, contrary to the respondent’s submission, the subject $880 meal allowance was, indeed, paid to T for the purpose of enabling him to purchase food and/or drink.



21. Much of the submissions made on T’s behalf were taken up with establishing that fact, and then reference to provisions of Subdivision F of Division 3 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”) to attempt to establish that T was not required to substantiate his expenditure of the amount of the meal allowance on food and/or drink. Having (so it was said) established those points, it was submitted that T must succeed. However, intending no discourtesy to T’s representatives, such an approach misses the point entirely. Firstly, there was no dispute that T incurred the expenditure on meals, so that no question of substantiation of the amount in question arose, and secondly, in light of sub-s 82KT(6) of the Act, which provides:



For the avoidance of doubt, it is declared that---



(a) nothing in this Subdivision shall be taken by implication to affect the interpretation or application of this Act as in force at any time before the commencement of this Subdivision; and



(b) nothing in this Subdivision (other than section 82KUD and paragraphs 82KW(2)(a) and (3)(a) and (b) and 82KX(1)(a)) entitles a taxpayer to a deduction to which the taxpayer would not have been entitled if this Subdivision had not been enacted.,



it is necessary for T to establish that the subject expenditure is an allowable deduction under sub-s 51(1) of the Act before consideration of the substantiation provisions becomes relevant.

. The respondent relied upon the Full Federal Court decision in FCT v Cooper 91 ATC 4396, as authority for the proposition that the expenditure was not an allowable deduction under sub-s 51(1) because it was not incurred in gaining or producing [T’s] assessable income, and/or it was of “a private or domestic nature” and thus excluded from deductibility by the concluding words of that sub-section.



. The relevant principles to apply in determining the deductibility of T’s expenditure on food and drink are usefully set out by Lockhart J in Cooper. His Honour noted at pp 4399-4400:

The phrase “incurred in gaining or producing assessable income” in the first limb of s.51(1) has been construed to mean incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable income: ...



For expenditure to be an allowable deduction as an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, it must be incidental and relevant to that end; Ronpibon at ATD 435; CLR 56. This test of deductibility has been explained in subsequent judgments of the High Court, so that to be deductible the expenditure must be incidental and relevant in the sense of having the essential character of expenditure incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable income. ... The essential character test is also applied to determine if the expenditure is of a capital, private or domestic nature as these cases illustrate.



Outgoings incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and outgoings of a capital or domestic nature are not mutually exclusive; Ronpibon Tin at ATD 435; CLR 56; Forsyth, John at ATC 4105-4106; CLR 427.



. Particularly in relation to food, his Honour also noted at p 4401:



These are the relevant principles to apply in determining the deductibility of the taxpayer’s expenditure in this case. The application of s.51(1) gives rise to difficulty in some cases because there is a large variety of factual situations to which it may apply. The deductibility of expenditure on food, clothing and housing poses difficult questions. In one sense expenditure on food is always relevant to the derivation of income because a person must eat to enable him to live and therefore to work. Obviously that alone is not a sufficient connection with the earning of assessable income to permit a deduction. On the other hand a person whose business is the publication of a food guide may buy and taste food in the course of his business, so there is a clear nexus between the expenditure and the derivation of income. The cases that lie in between the two extremities give rise to the difficulty and this case is one of them.



. Lockhart J concluded in that case at p 4403:



The taxpayer incurred the expenditure on additional food and drink for the purpose of increasing his weight and thus to play professional football and earn assessable income. But its character as the cost of additional food and drink is neither relevant nor incidental to the training for and playing of football matches, which is the activity by which he gained assessable income. The expenditure was not incurred in or in the course of that activity. The taxpayer was paid money to train for and play football, not to consume food and drink. His income producing activities did not include the consumption of food and drink.



In my opinion the taxpayer’s expenditure on additional food and drink was not incurred in gaining or producing his assessable income and is therefore not deductible from his assessable income during the relevant years of income.



Furthermore, for the reasons already given I would regard the expenditure as being of a private nature and therefore within the exclusionary limb of s.51(1).



. The other member of the majority in Cooper, Hill J, arrived at the same conclusions by very similar reasoning. It is, however, worth noting some of the judge’s comments in relation to whether the “private or domestic” exclusion in sub-s 51(1) applied to the circumstances of that case. Thus his Honour noted at p 4415:



Food and drink are ordinarily private matters, and the essential character of expenditure on food and drink will ordinarily be private rather than having the character of a working or business expense. However, the occasion of the outgoing may operate to give to expenditure on food and drink the essential character of a working expense in cases such as those illustrated of work-related entertainment or expenditure incurred while away from home. No such circumstance, however, intervenes here. ...



In my view, the expenditure in question fails to be deductible because its essential character remains private.



. Having examined T’s income producing activities as an employee projectionist, the details of which I have set out with some particularity, I have concluded that the same result as Cooper should be reached. The cost of the food and drink purchased by T for his consumption whilst on duty in the projection room is neither incidental nor relevant to those activities. The subject expenditure was not incurred in, or in the course of, those activities. The fact that the carrying out of his duties entitled him to a meal allowance to enable him to purchase a meal does not alter this conclusion. The subject expenditure was not incurred in gaining or producing T’s assessable income, and is therefore not deductible under sub-s 51(1).

. Furthermore, I respectfully adopt Hill J’s comments noted above, and conclude that the circumstances of this case do not “operate to give to [the subject] expenditure on food and drink the essential character of a working expense”. The subject expenditure remains of a private or domestic nature, and is, therefore, in any event, excluded from deduction under sub-s 51(1).

. Dr. Carr also referred me to a number of decisions of the former Boards of Review and of this Tribunal concerning the deductibility of expenditure on meals. It is unnecessary in the circumstances to set them out. In relation to Dr. Carr’s submission that “in view of the decision in Cooper”, two of those cases - Case U148 87 ATC 868, and Case U212 87 ATC 1195, “should not be regarded as even persuasive authority”, I would simply reiterate the comments I made in relation to those decisions in Case Y8 91 ATC 166, and agree that their correctness is questionable in light of Cooper.

. For the above reasons the objection decision under review is affirmed.
