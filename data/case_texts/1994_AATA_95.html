DRAFT JUDGMENT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

By SENIOR MEMBER ALLEN

No NT 93/195-8

and COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

SYDNEY, TUESDAY, 12 APRIL 1994



MR ALLEN: By notice lodged 10 September 1993, the applicant sought review of an objection decision dated 30 August 1993 disallowing objections to assessments made in respect of returns lodged on the applicant's behalf in respect of the tax years ended 30 June 1989, 1990 and 1991.



In 1989, the applicant who is a crane driver by occupation was introduced to one Paul Langlands of the Fortune of War Hotel which was a hotel frequented by employees in the construction industry. Langlands was introduced to the applicant as a person who was a tax agent and who was knowledgeable regarding what deductions could be obtained by construction industry workers. Langlands apparently had a reputation of someone who could obtain large refunds.



Subsequent events have proved that Langlands was in fact a rogue. He was not a registered tax agent although he held himself out to be one. Further, he embarked upon a course of fraudulent activity involving not only the applicant but a large number of other persons. Langland's fraudulent methods are well illustrated by the applicants case. The applicant gave evidence that Langlands handed to him a business card which stated that Langlands was a registered tax agent. Initially an appointment was made to see the applicant at Langlands's business address which was at Bexley. There the applicant found an office, plus office equipment such as a computer and a woman who appeared to him to be a secretary of office assistant.



Langlands obtained from the applicant both copies of his group certificate. After asking the applicant a few perfunctory questions such as his address, occupation and whether he was married with dependants, Langlands assured him he knew what deductions to claim. He also told the applicant that he, Langlands, could sign the tax return on his behalf. A few was negotiated on a contingency basis, in this case 30 per cent of the refund obtained. The applicant was then contacted by Langlands when the refund was obtained. In the first instance, the fee in cash was handed over at the Man of War Hotel. In the two subsequent years, arrangements were made for the applicant to deposit the fee into Langlands' bank account.



In fact Langlands prepared fraudulent returns claiming that the applicant had conducted a business which had made a loss. A different business was claimed each year so no question of ongoing accounts could arise. There were also other false claims for travelling expenses and spouse rebate. This fraudulent scheme particularly as it involved large numbers of people could not last although clearly it depended for its maintenance upon the system of self assessment plus the cupidity and gullibility of the taxpayers concerned. In passing, it should be mentioned that it was stated from the bar table by both parties that Langlands is now in Thailand and understandably resisting all attempts to have him return to Australia.



The applicant's tax returns were audited as were those of others who had returns prepared by Langlands. Exhibits B and 1 are the recording and transcript respectively of an interview between the applicant and officers of the Commissioner of Taxation's Bankstown office. I am satisfied that the record of the interview and transcript is a true and correct record of what passed between the applicant and the officers investigating his affairs. I have also had the benefit of having seen and heard the applicant give evidence and be cross examined. I accept that he believed Langlands to be a professional and that he accepted in good faith the answers Langlands gave to him regarding queries as to the necessity of Langlands retaining the notices of assessment.



Further, I accept that the applicant genuinely believed that Langlands had an expertise over and above other tax agents and took in good faith Langlands' statement that he could sign the returns on the applicants behalf if, as I do accept, the applicant believed Langlands to be genuine, then who should he not accept the advice Langlands gave him. On the other hand, the applicant is a mature man who has been paying tax in this country for some 30 years and I do not accept that he believes in Santa Clause.



Prior to engaging Langlands, he had had tax agents prepare returns on his behalf and his refund had been modest as one might expect with an employee and PAYE taxpayer. To suddenly be obtaining returns out of all proportion to previous returns, whereas his gross earnings had remained at the same level, must strain credulity. For example, the applicant's return in 1988 was some $1000. After engaging Langlands in 1989, it leapt to $11,117 and in 1990, $16,194 with, as I stated earlier, his gross wages remaining on the same level.



The Commissioner's argument was well put in the written submissions put forward on its behalf and I simply quote paragraph 7(d):



The applicant entrusted the preparation, signing and lodgment of his returns to Langlands. He thereby entrusted Langlands the task of informing the Commissioner of all the facts on which his income tax assessment would be based. The applicant can be in no better position than that conduct than if he entrusted the preparation of the returns to Langlands then signed them himself without reading them at all, ie with wilful blindness as to their contents. He cannot complain if, by abdicating his personal responsibility to an agent who acts fraudulently, he incurs a heavy penalty, a fortiori if he has created the incentive for fraud.



The facts of this matter are rather unique, although it seems from statements in the papers that at least some 500 other taxpayers are in the same situation as the present applicant. The guiding principle must be as stated by his Honour Mr Justice Evatt in Commissioner of Taxation and Trout Wine [1936] HCA 48; 56 CLR 211 at 217, namely:



The object of the section is to impose a heavy penalty so as to ensure the accuracy of returns upon which the whole income tax system of the Commonwealth is based. To this, however, must be added the circumstances of the individual and I consider the remarks of Senior Member Roach in case U36 reported at 87 ATC 266 at 269 apposite, namely, but it should not be overlooked that a 20 per cent per annum rate does work severely and even harshly against persons such as these applicants. None of them belongs to the world of high finance, none of them controls great wealth or holds high office or exercises a great power.



It was argued for the Commissioner that the applicant had been reckless. The term "reckless" in conjunction with penal provisions really imports a criminal law terminology as to which see the Full Court of the Federal Court in Crabb v the Crown [1984] FCA 321; 56 ALR 733. At page 756, his Honour Mr Justice Muirhead quoted from Professional Granville Williams' work, Criminal Law A General Point and stated:



Recklessness as to consequence occurs when the actor does not desire the consequence but foresees the possibility and consciously takes the risk. In advertent negligence, on the other hand, there is no such foresight. For many, if not most, legal purposes recklessness is classed with intention. It is like intention in that the consequence is foreseen but the difference is that whereas in intention the consequence is desired or is foreseen as a certainty, in recklessness it is foreseen as possible or probable but not desired. If the issue is whether the defendant was reckless as to a given consequence the question is whether he foresaw the possibility of that consequence but he will probably deny that he foresaw it and it is impossible to look directly into his mind to know whether he is speaking the truth. Usually, the question will have to be solved by examining the defendant's conduct and his opportunities of knowledge yet conduct is by no means so certain as a guide to the issue of recklessness as it is to that of intention. When a man bends himself to secure a result, he will often leave evidence of telltale pieces of behaviour which are inexplicable except on the assumption that he intended the result but recklessness be a mere passing realisation instantly dismissed which leaves no mark upon conduct. Also, a man is capable of self deception. He may decide that an unpleasant result is not likely because he does not want it to be likely.



Later, in examining recklessness as to consequence and as to circumstance, Professor Glanville Williams states at page 149:



The element common to the two kinds of recklessness is the conscious taking of a risk. The presence or absence of the fact, the consequence or circumstances is not part of the actor's purpose but he chooses to ignore the possibility of the fact in order to pursue his purpose.



The respondent also argued that the applicant had been wilfully blind. I consider that that term, again from the criminal law, is, although well understood and descriptive, unhelpful for the reasons stated by the High Court in the Crown v Crabb [1985] HCA 22; 58 ALR 417 at 421, namely:



When a person deliberately refrains from making inquiries because he prefers not to have the result, when he wilfully shuts his eyes for fear that he may learn the truth he may for some purposes be treated as having the knowledge which he deliberately abstained from acquiring. According to Professor Glanville Williams Criminal Law, the general part, 2nd edition, page 159, a court can properly find wilful blindness only where it can almost be said that the defendant actually knew, he suspected the fact, he realised its probability but he refrained from obtaining the final confirmation because he wanted in the event to be able to deny knowledge. This and this alone is wilful blindness. It requires, in effect, a finding that the defendant intended to cheat the administration of justice. Again, in his text book of Criminal Law at page 79 Professor Glanville Williams said in a passage cited by Lord Edmund Davis in the Crown v Caldwell 1982 Appeal Cases 341 at 358:



A person cannot in any intelligible meaning of the words close his mind to a risk unless he first realises that there is a risk and if he realises that there is a risk that is the end of the matter.



These statements support the view that it cannot be said than an accused was wilfully blind to the consequences of his acts unless he knew that those consequences were probable. If that is so -



They then speak of the specific instances. It strikes me, therefore, that what I must consider is the applicant state of mind when he handed over the entire management of his tax affairs to Langlands. That part of this took place in an hotel I do not find so suspicious, given the nature of the construction industry. I also draw the inference that Langlands deliberately chose his marks amongst people who, whilst with money and desirous of reducing their tax, were not commercially astute that they would immediately realise that the results obtained were simply too good to be true. On the other hand, the applicant was culpable in that he did not exercise due care and attention in the maintenance of his affairs.



It is clear that deterrence plays a part in these proceedings. the role of deterrence was well summed up by the Victorian Court of Criminal Appeal in the Crown v Kane, (1974) Victorian Reports 759 at 764, namely, after referring to the New South Wales case of the Crown v Cook, (1955) 72 New South Wales Weekly Notes 132 and the New Zealand case of the Crown v Radich, (1954) New Zealand Law Reports 96, the court said:



I think the matter has been well expressed in a recent decision of the Court of Appeal in New Zealand in the case of the Crown v Radich, a passage which this court has had occasion to quote in other cases which have come before it. After referring to the argument with regard to the elements of retribution and reformation, the court expressed their views as follows:



We should say at once that this last argument omits one of the main purposes of punishment which is to protect the public from the commission of such crimes by making it clear to the offender and to other persons with similar impulses that if they yield to them they will be met with severe punishment. In all civilised countries in all ages there has been the main purpose of punishment and still continues so. The fact that punishment does not entirely prevent all similar crimes should not obscure the cogent fact that the fear of severe punishment does and will prevent the commission of many that would have been committed if it was thought that the offender could escape without punishment or with only a light punishment. If a court is weakly merciful and does not impose a sentence commensurate with the seriousness of the crime it fails in its duty to see that the sentences are such as to operate as a powerful factor to prevent the commission of such offences. On the other hand, justice and humanity both require that the previous character and conduct and the probable future life and conduct of the individual offender and the effect of the sentence on these should also be given the most careful consideration although this factor is unnecessarily subsidiary to the main considerations that determine the appropriate amount of punishment. I think the court must accept the burden imposed upon them, not a burden pleasantly undertaken of seeing that sentences are imposed of such a character as will deter others from embarking on similar exploits.



I accept that the applicant perceived Langlands to be a tax agent. In handing to him the whole of his affairs, he was grossly negligent but I do not regard his actions as going further and amounting to recklessness, that is, that he had a foresight of consequences. He has paid a price in regard to the fees paid and to the disruption of his affairs. I also note he has been unemployed for some 16 months. I consider that the culpability factor should be reduced to 30 per cent to take into account his unemployment and not to crush him totally but in all other respects the decision under review is affirmed.
