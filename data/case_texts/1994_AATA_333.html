Josephine Vallentine and Parliamentary Retiring Allowances Trust [1994] AATA 333 (11 November 1994)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

JOSEPHINE VALLENTINE v. PARLIAMENTARY RETIRING ALLOWANCES

TRUST

No. W93/336 AAT No. 9836

Number of pages - 10

Retirement Benefits

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

T E BARNETT (Senior Member), J G BILLINGS (Member) AND N MARINOVICH (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Retirement Benefits - Parliamentary Retiring Allowances Trust - application for parliamentary retiring allowance - ill health due to migraines, whether voluntary retirement or resignation made bona fide on account of ill health - existence of other reasons for resignation in addition to ill health.

Parliamentary Contributory Superannuation Act 1948 ss.17(3), 18A

HEARING

PERTH, 8-9 August 1994

11:11:1994

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr R Guthrie

Solicitor for the Applicant: Mr R Guthrie

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr E Carlose

Solicitor for the Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside and in substitution therefore the Tribunal decides that:

1. The applicant's resignation was made bona fide on

account of ill health.

2. The matter be remitted to the respondent to calculate

the applicant's entitlements in accordance with the reasons

for decision.

DECISION

T E BARNETT, J G BILLINGS AND N MARINOVICH This is an application for review of a decision by the Parliamentary Retiring Allowances Trust ("the Trust") dated 22 November 1994 confirming its primary decision dated 2 April 1992 that the applicant had retired voluntarily and her resignation was not made bona fide on the grounds of ill-health.

2. At the hearing the applicant was represented by Mr Guthrie of counsel and the respondent was represented by Mr Carlose from the Australian Government Solicitor's office. The Tribunal had before it the documents lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the T Documents") and the following exhibits:

R1(A) Letter dated 11/10/91 from Respondent to Applicant and

enclosures

R1(B) Letter dated 3/4/92 from Respondent to Applicant

R1(C) Letter dated 19/5/92 from Respondent to Applicant

R1(D) Letter dated 26/5/92 from Applicant to Respondent

R2 Bundle of Extracts from Hansard

A1 Report Mr K M R Grainger dated 10/5/94

A2 Report Dr H Cohen dated 23/3/94

A3(A) Hansard Extract page 1348

A3(B) Hansard Extract page 1236

R3 National Library Cutting

A4 Statement of Agreed Facts

3. Oral evidence was given by Mr Peter Downes who at the relevant time was Acting Director of the Parliamentary and Award Superannuation Section in the Department of Finance; Josephine Vallentine, the applicant; Mr Keith Grainger, a specialist Neurologist; Senator Christabelle Chamarette; Dr Jo Mazengarb, a Community Medical Officer with ACT Health who at the relevant time worked for the Australian Government Health Service as the director of policy; and Dr H Cohen, a specialist Gynecologist.

4. The only issue for determination by the Tribunal is whether pursuant to s.17(3) of the Parliamentary Contributory Superannuation Act 1948 ("the Act") the applicant should be deemed to have retired voluntarily because she has failed to satisfy the Tribunal that her resignation was made bona fide on account of ill-health. The relevant parts of s.17(3) of the Act are now reproduced:

"(3) A member who resigns his place before the expiration of his

term of office shall be deemed to have retired voluntarily if he

fails to satisfy the Trust that his resignation was made bona

fide:

(a) on account of ill-health;....."

5. If the applicant satisfies the Trust that her resignation was made bona fide on account of ill-health then pursuant to s.18A of the Act her period of service shall be deemed to be 8 years thus entitling her under s.18 of the Act to a retiring allowance.

6. The following facts are not disputed by the parties:

a) All senators are members of the Parliamentary

Contributory Superannuation Scheme during their service in

Parliament. The details of the scheme are contained in the

Act which provides that the Trust exercises certain

administrative powers.

b) The applicant was elected to the Australian Senate following

a general election in 1984. She commenced service in the Senate

on 1 July 1985. When she entered the Senate she submitted a

medical certificate to the Trust, for the purposes of s.18A of

the Act, to the effect that she was unlikely to become, within a

period of eight years, incapable by reason of ill-health of

performing the duties of a member of Parliament and such medical

certificate has been considered by the Trust.

c) The applicant made contributions to the Parliamentary

Contributory Superannuation Scheme pursuant to the Act between

1 July 1985 and 31 January 1992.

d) On 16 September 1991 the applicant announced, by media

release, her resignation from the Senate, effective from 31

January 1992. In the media release the applicant stated

several reasons for her resignation:

" - the desire for more time with my family;

- the fact that my health is deteriorating;

- the belief that seven years of Parliamentary service is

enough, and that it is time for a new voice to be heard

representing green issues".

e) On 19 September 1991 a letter was sent to the applicant from

the Parliamentary and Award Superannuation Section of the

Department of Finance enclosing a copy of the Parliamentary

Contributory Superannuation Scheme Handbook which sets out the

conditions applying to entitlement to benefits under the scheme.

f) The applicant was then referred to Dr S Cutler, a doctor with

the Australian Government Health Service, to be examined. On 5

November 1991 Dr Cutler provided a report to the respondent on

the applicant. Dr Cutler expressed her opinion on the condition

of the applicant as follows:

"Senator Vallentine is currently not fit to perform the duties

of her position as Senator. It is most likely that removal from

this source of stress will result in an improvement in her

medical condition. However, it should be pointed out that she

was at no stage referred to a specialist neurologist who may

have given more definitive opinion in regard to the causes of

the migraine headaches, and who may have recommended a trial of

prophylactic anti-migraine medication.

In my opinion, Senator Vallentine will in the future be fit for

a variety of occupations, but that these should be of a

non-stressful nature."

g) On 27 November 1991 the respondent considered the matter of

the applicant's impending retirement and concluded that before

reaching a decision with respect to the applicant, it required

the opinion of a specialist neurologist. The applicant was

therefore referred to and assessed by Mr Keith Grainger, a

specialist neurologist who provided the respondent with a report

dated 22 January 1992.

h) In that report Mr Keith Grainger relevantly reported that:

"stress caused by parliamentary conflicts appear to act as a

precipitant for at least some of the headaches...The diagnosis

is that of common migraine, with the increasing frequency and

severity being related to the pressures of her work

situation....While a period of sick leave would probably be

beneficial for her migraine, my usual practice would be treat

the patient while they continued in their work situation to

obtain a more realistic view on their response to treatment".

i) On 2 April 1992 the Trust decided that the applicant's

resignation was voluntary and not made bona fide on the grounds

of ill-health and it therefore refused her application.

7. From April 1992 until December 1992 the applicant and her family went on a trip around Australia. The applicant explained in evidence that between resigning on 31 January 1992 and leaving to travel around Australia in April 1992 her migraines had intensified so she tried acupuncture in the hope that this would heal her, however, it did not work. The applicant was then prescribed Sandomigraine and Ergodryl to be taken daily. Ms Vallentine said in evidence that during the 9 months of travel she took the two medications daily, however the side effects were that she needed about 11 hours sleep a day, felt drowsy and put on a great deal of weight. During her travels Ms Vallentine was advised of the respondent's primary decision dated 2 April 1992.

8. On 27 October 1993 the applicant sought an extension of time and a reconsideration of the decision dated 2 April 1992 and provided additional medical opinions from Dr Phillip Soh and Dr H Cohen in support of her application. The respondent then requested the Director of the Australian Government Health Service, Dr J Heathershaw ( now Dr J Mazengarb) to review and assess all the relevant medical reports relating to the applicant.

9. On 22 November 1993 the Trust met and decided to extend the 30 day time limit for the acceptance of the request by the applicant to reconsider the 2 April 1992 decision from 2 May 1992 to 27 October 1993 and reconsidered its earlier decision. Then, on 23 November 1993, the Trust confirmed its earlier decision by advice in writing to the applicant.

10. On 20 December 1993 the applicant applied to this Tribunal for review of that decision.

The Applicant's Evidence

11. Prior to discussing the applicant's evidence it should be noted that the Tribunal found Ms Vallentine to be a credible witness who was very committed to social causes and to her role as a Senator. This is evidenced by the fact that prior to entering the Australian Parliament she was a member of many community groups and during her years at home with young children she was very busy in community activism. She worked as a Peace Educator with the Fremantle Education Centre and Ms Vallentine was chosen as a candidate on the issue of nuclear disarmament because of the considerable work she had done in that area.

12. In evidence the applicant explained that her ill health consisted of frequent severe migraines leading to nausea and vomiting. She said that a headache was the first symptom which was quickly followed by nausea. Then it took about 8 hours to work up to a vomiting attack and once this started she was bed ridden and could not take any medication because she would vomit everything up. She could not eat or drink while the vomiting persisted, which sometimes lasted up to 48 hours. She was not able to concentrate on anything other than getting rid of the migraine. The only time she would get out of bed was to vomit. Dr Cutler and Mr Grainger suggested that removal from the stress at work would probably have benefited Ms Vallentine but as Ms Vallentine said in evidence she was, and still is, a very conscientious person and, as she was the sole representative for the Greens, she could not just take "days off" or take an extended leave of absence because each "day off" would disenfranchise the Greens. She was not willing to do that because, as she said in evidence, "it would leave my constituents totally unrepresented for that time" because there was nobody to fill her vacancy. Her vote would be lost because she was unable to pair with any of the other parliamentarians as she was not a member of one of the major political parties. As the frequency and intensity of the migraines increased in 1990 to 1991 Ms Vallentine, on advice from her physician, kept a calendar to record the frequency and duration of her migraines and to see if there was a correlation with her menstruation cycle. (A copy of that calendar appears at T13 page 29 of the T Documents). From the recordings on the calendar Ms Vallentine estimated that she was absent from work on about 48 occasions during the 1991 year. In evidence Ms Vallentine said:

"When I was faced with this calendar, it was actually quite a

shock to me and I thought, I can't keep taking the tax payers'

money, at the rate of $75,000 a year to do a job, when I am

actually absent for 48 days, or not able to give full

effectiveness on 48 days out of a year. It was really quite a

shock to me and I felt that in conscience, I couldn't keep doing

the job. I couldn't keep taking the money to do the job, which

I was not actually able to do as fully as I would like."

In cross-examination she conceded that on some of the 48 days she had appeared at the Parliament in order to be recorded but had then retired from further participation.

13. The applicant said that she had been unwilling to take medication as a prophylactic because the drugs available made her dull and lethargic and this affected her lifestyle and made her ineffective for her work as a Senator which required her to be alert and active. She said she had tried the so-called wonder drug Sumatriptin when it became available just before her resignation but it made her feel worse and caused vomiting.

The Evidence of other witnesses

Mr Keith Grainger

14. Mr Grainger saw Ms Vallentine as a patient on referral. He provided two reports, one dated 22 January 1992 (which was referred to in paragraph 14 above) and the other dated 10 May 1994. He gave evidence that Ms Vallentine suffers from moderate to severe migraine and that she takes Maxalon which has two benefits - it is an anti-nausea drug for the nausea and vomiting and it aids absorption of pain tablets such as Mersyndol Forte which he agreed she was taking at the time.

15. In his report dated 22 January 1992 Mr Grainger said that Sumatriptin is a specific anti-migraine preparation which became available in Australia in about October 1991. Accordingly, it could not have been used by the applicant in the eighties. Mr Grainger said that it is effective in about 75 per cent of people in large trials. Sumatriptin is available in tablet form at $30 per tablet and by injection at a cost $70 an injection. Mr Grainger agreed that Sumatriptin is not prophylactic - it is purely a treatment of the acute attack and this evidence was corroborated by Dr Mazengarb. Mr Grainger said that the side-effects of Sumatriptin are lethargy, drowsiness and possible weight gain. He agreed that removing yourself from the stressor is a legitimate form of treatment but it is not really a practical option for most people so they have to learn to manage their stress.

16. In cross-examination a series of questions were put to Mr Grainger relating to the treatment of migraines. The relevant parts of the transcript are now reproduced:

"Now, would I be correct in saying that a GP is qualified to

treat a patient with migraines initially? --- Yes, certainly.

Yes, and what he would do is he would prescribe whatever

treatment or medication he thinks is right. Now, would I also

be correct in saying that if the treatment and the medication

does not help he would in the ordinary course of events refer

the patient to a neurologist? --- That's correct, yes.

Well, obviously because a neurologist would be more qualified

and more capable of treating the patient? --- Yes.

Now, Sumatriptin, would I be correct in saying that it would

be - it would have to be taken over a period of time to decide

how effective it is, usually, not just once? --- No. Well,

certainly more than once. Usually if it's going to work it

works, and - but there are patients that have taken - it has

worked on the second time and maybe third time, but if you -

you know, if it hasn't worked at that stage - now, with the

proviso that some people may need two tablets to work, and

then if some people have nausea and vomiting or at the

beginning of their migraine, so they're just going to vomit

up the tablet, or if they wake up with a severe migraine as

some patients, they're already well in the attack, then there's

an indication for the subcutaneous injection in that sort of

situation, yes. So there is a sort of range, really."

17. Mr Grainger concluded (p121 of the transcript) that it is unusual that Ms Vallentine was not referred to a specialist by one of the several treating doctors she attended. He also said in evidence (see page 122 of the transcript) that if someone has migraine to the extent of getting headaches and vomiting with each of their attacks such a person would not be able to sustain concentration in normal work environments, they would just have to stop work.

Dr Jo Mazengarb

18. Dr Mazengarb in her evidence said that she was never asked to examine Ms Vallentine and made her assessment simply from a file review some 20 to 22 months after Ms Vallentine's resignation. The reports before Dr Mazengarb were those of Mr Grainger, Dr Cutler, Dr Soh, Dr Sadka and Dr Miliotis.

19. Dr Mazengarb's report of 13 April 1993 was not tendered so the Tribunal has only the report of 22 November 1993 (T22) before it.

20. Dr Mazengarb agreed that the occurrence of Ms Vallentine's migraines was of a relatively high frequency. It appears from her evidence that she experienced some difficulty in deciding what qualifying criteria to use to assess Ms Vallentine. She continually referred to "invalidity" retirement and agreed in evidence that when she used the word "invalidity" she understood it to refer to somebody that is unable to work at all or very substantially unfit for work. The Tribunal finds that the criteria in this case is that the applicant show ill health and that the resignation is bona fide on account of ill health.

21. Dr Mazengarb insisted that Ms Vallentine should have seen at least one specialist neurologist who may have prescribed Sumatriptin which is a treatment of the acute attack and not prophylactic or that Ms Vallentine try a period of leave of absence.

22. The Tribunal notes that Dr Mazengarb was given no information about Ms Vallentine's parliamentary status or her relationship with any political party. She was not aware that if Ms Vallentine took leave her vote would be lost and that the Greens at that stage were not able to participate in the process of pairing in Parliament.

23. Dr Mazengarb agreed that migraine medication is something that is constantly changing and what you were told by a specialist five years ago is not necessarily the case for today.

Dr Harry Cohen

24. Ms Vallentine was also examined by Dr Cohen (who was called to give evidence) to see whether there was some specific hormonal basis for her migraines and whether a hormone replacement therapy would be of any value. This therapy is given to women who are post menopausal because the hormone level decreases and sometimes requires a supplement. A lot of Dr Cohen's patients are women with a medical history of migraine.

25. Dr Cohen said in evidence and in his report dated 23 March 1994 that Ms Vallentine does not have the capacity to work at this stage. He based his opinion on the severity of her symptoms together with the fact that she would have to frequently take days off work.

26. Dr Cohen formed the opinion when he saw Ms Vallentine, which was after her retirement, that at that time her migraines were continuing and it would be difficult for her to be re-employed. Dr Cohen formed the opinion that Ms Vallentine's migraines had not changed in intensity at all following her resignation.

Mr Peter Downes

27. At the relevant time Mr Downes was acting director of the Parliamentary and Award Superannuation section in the Department of Finance which is the section responsible for the day to day administration of parliamentary superannuation and which handled the applicant's benefit entitlements. It appears to the Tribunal that the thrust of Mr Downes evidence was to show that Ms Vallentine was well informed of her entitlements upon resignation and that she never gave any indication that she was upset with the initial decision by the trust. Presumably this is intended to reflect on her credit. The Tribunal accepts the genuineness of Ms Vallentine's explanation for the delay however and finds it a credible explanation which throws no doubt on the question of whether she was suffering ill health and resigned for that reason.

28. During the initial stages of the hearing some emphasis was given by the respondent to a file note signed by Mr Downes and Ms Ann Burhop, Assistant Director, Parliamentary and Awards Superannuation Section on 11 December 1991. The contents of that note are now reproduced:

"NOTE FOR FILE

On 11 December 1991 Anne Burhop and I went over to Senator

Vallentine's office at Parliament House to meet her successor

(Christabelle Chamarette) following her retirement from the

Senate on 31 January 1992.

Ms Chamarette told us that the Greens in Western Australia had

decided to rotate their Senate position and that it was now her

turn. As a consequence of this policy she indicated that she

was unlikely to qualify for a Parliamentary pension."

The file note was signed by both Mr Downes and Ms Burhop. The note was never shown to Senator Chamarette. Presumably this evidence was intended to provide an alternative reason for the applicant's resignation other than the question of ill health.

29. The Tribunal arranged to have Senator Chamarette called and when questioned about the Greens policy to rotate their Senate position she categorically denied the existence of such a policy and was adamant that no pressure whatsoever was placed on Ms Vallentine to resign by the Greens. She also said that the file note was never drawn to her attention and she was never asked to affirm that the words contained therein were her words.

Findings of Fact

30. The Tribunal finds as facts all of the undisputed matters set out in paragraph 6 above and also the following findings of fact:

a) Since about 1967 the applicant has suffered from migraine

headaches. The headaches had become more frequent and more

severe since 1986 and by 1991 were occurring once, twice or

more per month lasting 2 to 3 days on most occasions.

b) There is a strong history of migraine headaches in the

applicant's family and in 1985 the applicant saw a neurologist

at the request of her family. She was examined and it was

found she had no discernible neurological problems.

c) At all relevant times the applicant was suffering from

moderate to severe migraines which interfered with her work.

She genuinely and reasonably believed that sick leave was not

an option . The Tribunal also finds that Ms Vallentine had a

genuine reluctance to rely on drugs as a preventative measure

and that she genuinely and reasonably believed that the side

effects of drowsiness and lethargy would interfere with her

work as a Senator to an unacceptable degree.

d) The Tribunal finds that the applicant's migraines were the

main reason for her resignation and that her continuing to

work while suffering from migraines was one cause of her

family problems.

The Law

31. The Act does not define "ill health" and neither counsel could point to any relevant case law on the interpretation of these particular words. The Tribunal accepts the two dictionary definitions referred to by Mr Guthrie in his opening submissions:

"an unsound or disordered condition of health" - the Macquarie

Dictionary Second Edition.

"An unsound, disordered condition of health; that state of health

which is characterized by the presence of some disease or by the

imperfect functioning of the physical processes" - the Oxford

English Dictionary.

On these definitions there is no doubt that Ms Vallentine suffered ill health before, at the time of and since her resignation.

32. During the hearing the respondent emphasized the fact that options of other treatment were available to Ms Vallentine instead of resignation. The Tribunal disregards the sick leave option as it accepts Ms Vallentine's explanation that she genuinely believed this was not possible in her position as sole representative for the Greens, as there was nobody to fill her vacancy and accordingly a voice for the Greens could not be heard during her periods of absence.

33. The Tribunal accepts the submission made by the applicant's solicitor and is satisfied that under the legislation the ill health does not have to be permanent. It is not necessary for the Tribunal to find that the applicant is totally or partially incapacitated for work. The Tribunal merely has to satisfy itself whether her resignation was bona fide on account of ill health. Certainly, if she is incapacitated this goes to her bona fides in relation to her resignation but the Tribunal does not have to make a specific finding as to her incapacity. Permanency is a consideration but it is not a fixed criteria.

34. The crucial question in this case is whether she resigned voluntarily or, in the terms of s.3 of the Act, whether the resignation was made "bona fide on account of ill health."

35. The respondent's challenge to the applicant's bona fides can be summarized as follows:

a) there were reasons other than ill health which motivated her

to resign (ie the Greens rotation policy and personal family

reasons.)

b) She did not seek the opinion of a specialist neurologist.

c) She could have relieved the migraines by taking sick leave

or prophylactic drugs.

d) She did not give the drug Sumatriptin a sufficient trial.

e) She was not incapacitated by her migraines.

Considering each of those reasons in turn:

The Greens Rotation Policy

36. The rotation policy has not been established and the Tribunal finds it was not one of the reasons for the resignation.

Family Reasons

37. It is not required that ill health be the only reason for the resignation. The applicant agrees that her family was under stress before her resignation but this was largely a result of her ill health at that time. The Tribunal finds that the most significant reason for her resignation was the ill health

Specialist Neurological Opinion

38. None of the applicant's consulting doctors thought it necessary to refer her to a neurologist and in any event she had consulted a neurologist in 1985 and was told that there were no neurological problems.

Sick Leave and Prophylactic Drugs

39. The Tribunal accepts the applicant's explanation that sick leave would leave the Greens unrepresented, especially as "pairing" was not possible, and that prophylactic drugs would leave her drowsy and ineffective as a Senator.

Sumatriptin

40. This drug was not generally available until just before the applicant's resignation. The Tribunal accepts her evidence that she tried the drug and felt that it made her worse. The genuineness of this evidence is also supported to by the fact that three years further on she has still not tried Sumatriptin again to relieve her continuing migraines.

Incapacity

41. Given the very full-time nature of the work of a national politician the migraines must have seriously affected her capacity as a Senator. It is not necessary however for her to show that she was "incapacitated" by the migraines, merely that she bona fide resigned "on account of" them. The Tribunal accepts the genuineness of her explanation that she could not, in good conscience, continue to represent the Greens while she was being rendered ineffective by migraines so frequently and for such long periods.

42. Finally, the Tribunal must consider whether the real and overriding reason for the applicant's early resignation may have been a desire to exaggerate the effect of the migraines in order to fraudulently gain an early entitlement to pension. The Tribunal finds that this was not the case and nothing in the applicant's character, previous history or her actions in this instance give any support to this possibility.

Decision

43. The decision under review is set aside and in substitution therefore the Tribunal decides that:

1. The applicant's resignation was made bona fide on account of

ill health.

2. The matter be remitted to the respondent to calculate the

applicant's entitlements in accordance with these reasons for

decision.
