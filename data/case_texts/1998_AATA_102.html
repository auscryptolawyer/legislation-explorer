DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No QT97/54

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Date 23 February 1998

Place Brisbane

Decision The Tribunal decides that:

(a) the objection decision under review is set aside;

(b) the objection is allowed; and

(c) this matter has terminated in a manner favourable to the applicant.



(Sgd) K L Beddoe

Decision No 12639 (Senior Member)



CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - assessable income - foreign currency transactions - default assessment - excessive assessment - burden of proof on taxpayer - source of moneys - land sale - money buried in applicant's yard - motive for burial - concealment from ex-wife - not assessable income



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 : ss 6(1), 25(1), 48, 166, 167(b), 177, 190(b)

Taxation Administration Act 1953 : s 14ZZK

Income Tax Assessment Act 1922 : s 39



Trautwein v FCT [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63

Saffron v FCT, 94 ATC 4049

REASONS FOR DECISION

23 February 1998 Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)



  1. In or about July 1992 the applicant, acting through his tax agent, lodged an income tax return for the year of income ended 30 June 1991 (“1991 year”) in the Australian Taxation Office. That return disclosed income derived as an employee of a business but it would be more correct to describe that income as part of a share of net income of a partnership and subsequently a sole proprietorship. Nothing turns on this and the respondent Commissioner apparently made and notified an assessment of tax in accordance with the return. There is not, however, a copy of such a notice of assessment before the Tribunal.
  2. The applicant was selected for a subsequent audit by the Taxation Office because of reports from the Cash Transactions Reports Agency. Copies of these reports (T7) disclosed transactions for the purchase of US dollars costing $AUD20,000.00 in total. The Taxation Office enquiries had their genesis from a report by the Australian Federal Police about the currency transactions (T6).
  3. By a notice of amended assessment dated 6 July 1993 (T42) the assessed taxable income for the year ended 30 June 1991 was amended by including a further $20,000 as assessable income. The adjustment was described as follows:
“Net Business Income

- Adjusted in the absence of satisfactory detail or failure to supply requested information.

+ $20,000.”


It is clear from the material before me, and I so find, that the $20,000 added to assessable income was the $AUD20,000.00 expended in the exchange transactions.

  1. By a notice of objection dated 1 September 1993 the applicant objected against the amended assessment. In the notice of objection the $20,000 was described as “private funds deposited into a business account as loan funds” (T43). The applicant further described the funds as “cash funds obtained from currency conversion transactions resultant from the sale of a property” at Port Douglas and ”other cash accumulations over the years”. The respondent disallowed the objection.
  2. By an application dated 10 February 1997 the applicant applied to this Tribunal for review of the decision.
  3. Section 25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the 1936 Act”) relevantly provides that the assessable income of a resident of Australia shall include the gross income derived directly or indirectly from all sources whether in or out of Australia which is not exempt income. Section 6(1) defines “assessable income” to mean all the amounts which under the provisions of the 1936 Act are included in assessable income. That section also defines “exempt income” as income which is exempt from income tax and includes income which is not assessable income.
  4. “Gross income” and “income” are not defined in the 1936 Act. However, in Scott v C of T (NSW) (1935) 3 ATD 142, a stated case as to whether compensation for termination constituted income decided by the Full Court of the Supreme Court of New South Wales, Jordan CJ stated what has become the classic statement on the meaning of “income” at pages 144-5:
“The word “Income” is not a term of art, and what forms of receipts are comprehended within it, and what principles are to be applied to ascertain how much of those receipts ought to be treated as income, must be determined in accordance with the ordinary concepts and usages of mankind, except in so far as the statute states or indicates an intention that receipts which are not income in ordinary parlance are to be treated as income, ... “


  1. Section 48 of the 1936 Act provides that in calculating the taxable income of a taxpayer, the total assessable income derived by the taxpayer during the year of income shall be taken as a basis, and from it shall be deducted all allowable deductions.
  2. Section 166 of the 1936 Act provides for the making of an assessment of the amount of the taxable income and the tax payable thereon.
  3. Section 167 of the 1936 Act applies to authorise the making of a “default assessment” and provides, in particular, that where the Commissioner is not satisfied with a return of income furnished by a person, the Commissioner may make an assessment of the amount upon which in his judgment income tax ought to be levied and that amount shall be the taxable income of that person for the purpose of s 166.
  4. Section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (“TA Act”) provides, in part, that on review of a reviewable objection decision the applicant has the burden of proving that the Commissioner’s assessment is excessive.
  5. At the hearing the applicant was represented by Mr Garonne and an officer of the Australian Taxation Office represented the respondent. The documents filed in the Tribunal pursuant to s37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 were before the Tribunal as the T documents. Further documents were tendered for the applicant and marked as exhibits. Oral evidence was given by the applicant and another witness called to corroborate the applicant's evidence.
  6. In 1985 the applicant and his then wife purchased a retail/wholesale business which the applicant was still conducting in the 1991 year. The material before me indicates that the business was conducted on a cash basis (not unusual for the particular type of business) and business proceeds were deposited in a bank account.
  7. In the early 1980’s (I infer, prior to 19 September 1985) the applicant and his then wife purchased land at a purchase price of $33,000.00 on $3,000.00 deposit with the balance on settlement financed by a loan from a finance company.
  8. This land was sold to a company in 1987 with settlement in March 1987 for a consideration of $840,000 resulting in a net payment to the applicant and his wife of approximately $400,000. The applicant says that there had been a number of loans obtained from the finance company on security of the land and these were discharged upon effect of the sale.
  9. Documents T14 and T16 show the net payout to the finance company of $405,699.00 leaving the balance sale proceeds of $433,763.00 after adjustments but before commissions and costs were deducted. At settlement two cheques for the balance in the amounts of $32,557.25 and $317,205.39 payable to the applicant and his then wife were exchanged.
  10. The cheques exchanged on settlement were paid to the joint bank account of the applicant and his wife on 12 March 1987 and 13 March 1987 (T17) and the proceeds of the sale effectively withdrawn from the account on 13 March 1987 when an amount of $400,000.00 was withdrawn by cheque presented. The $400,000.00 was deposited on the same day in a money market account conducted at the same bank by the applicant and his wife (T18).
  11. On 17 March 1987 $185,000.00 was withdrawn from this account by transfer to the joint bank account and on 20 March 1987 an amount of $107,500.00 was also withdrawn and transferred to the joint bank account. Those funds were then withdrawn from the joint account by cheque presented (T18).
  12. These funds (plus interest from the money market account) were subsequently deposited with the finance company on 3 April 1987 and draw down to nil balance on 16 June 1987 (T19).
  13. In February 1990 the applicant's then wife deposited $AUD70,000.00 in a foreign currency investment account with the Commonwealth Bank (T23). On 28 March 1990 the applicant held $US53,488.17 in a deposit account with the Commonwealth Bank (T24). These proceeds of that account were apparently transferred to the applicant's and his wife’s joint account at the Commonwealth Bank on 29 May 1990 (T26).
  14. The applicant and his wife then divorced in March 1991. In April and May 1991, the applicant made three transactions converting in total $AUD20,000.00 into US dollars at Thomas Cook Ltd.
  15. Those transactions were reported to the Cash Transaction Reports Agency (T7). In the event, the respondent's officers apparently became concerned about the applicant's cash flow and proceeded to make enquiries about the foreign currency transactions.
  16. The applicant told the respondent and also this Tribunal that the funds used for currency exchange transactions came initially from the proceeds of sale of the land described in paragraphs 15 and 16 above. He became concerned as to the possibility of devaluation of the Australian dollar in the light of the then Treasurer’s “Banana Republic” speech. He converted Australian dollars into United States of America dollars and buried these US dollars in jars in two locations in the back yard of his home. He did not tell his wife about the buried money but he did confide in a friend. That friend is a professional person who gave evidence to the Tribunal and corroborated the applicant's evidence about money hidden in the yard of the applicant's home. There was a discrepancy in the evidence as to the detail of the actual sites of the jars of banknotes. However, I am satisfied that the discrepancy as to detail was caused by the effluxion of time (over seven years) between the time of giving evidence and the time that the witness was told about the buried treasure.
  17. The witness was unable to corroborate the applicant's evidence that he had buried $50,000.00 to $70,000.00 nor could he corroborate the applicant's evidence about the money being in Australian or United States currency. It is likely that the amount buried was $70,000.00 (or part thereof) withdrawn from a savings investment account on 25 July 1988 (T44). I am satisfied that the witness gave unrehearsed evidence which corroborated the applicant's evidence about there being money buried in the yard of the applicant's home. I have no reason to doubt the applicant's evidence about the nature and value of the buried treasure. I therefore find that in or about 1987 the applicant buried currency to the value of at least $AUD50,000.00 in the yard of his home.
  18. I also accept, and find, that the applicant used the buried currency over an unascertained period of time to supplement funds used in his business. While I am unable to make a finding as to when and how the buried currency was used I am satisfied that amounts of currency were removed from the ground from time to time and used in the business.
  19. I am not satisfied that I should accept the applicant's evidence as to the origin of the buried currency being traced back to the land sale and the net proceeds approximating $400,000.00. In coming to that conclusion I have taken into account the applicant's evidence about his currency transactions, his concern about using the business safe and failure to take into consideration the possibility of using a safe custody box.
  20. In coming to a conclusion about this matter I have also taken into account the history of the applicant's dealings with the Taxation Office as disclosed by the T documents. Unfortunately the applicant was not fully examined in relation to much of this material and, in particular, the inconsistencies in his explanations. The applicant has not been consistent in his explanation of the three currency transactions, but only because of motive. It eventually transpired, as he said in evidence before the Tribunal, that he was motivated by a desire to hide money from his wife in the circumstances of a disintegrating marriage. That was an explanation that could not be given while his wife was privy to his affairs as she was in the earlier days of the audit by the Taxation Office. I am satisfied the breakdown of the marriage was a factor which caused the applicant to bury the money, unbeknown to his wife - as was established by the evidence before the Tribunal. A report by the Australian Federal Police dated 2 March 1992 is generally consistent in relation to the currency transactions with the applicant’s evidence to this Tribunal (T6).
  21. In Trautwein v FCT [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63 the Commissioner had made default assessments based upon an unexplained accretion of assets, treating the accretion as income derived over seven years of income. Section 36 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922 (“the 1922 Act”) was, for present purposes, in similar terms to paragraph 167(b) of the 1936 Act. At pages 87-8 of Trautwein Latham CJ referred to the operation of s 39 of the 1922 Act (now s 177 of the 1936 Act) in the context of default assessments made under s 36 as follows:
“Similar considerations apply to sec. 36. It seems obvious that in this case the commissioner was not satisfied with the returns made and the information provided from time to time by the taxpayer, and that therefore a condition empowering him to act under sec. 36 was fulfilled. If he had been satisfied he would not have issued notices of amended assessments. It was not necessary for the commissioner to proclaim that he was not satisfied with the returns made by the taxpayer in order to justify the exercise of the powers conferred by sec. 36.

Thus, in my opinion, both sec. 39 and sec. 36 apply to this case. If so, the taxpayer is liable to pay tax upon the amount stated in the assessment as that upon which income tax ought to be levied, unless he displaces the prima facie presumption created by sec. 39.”


The “prima facie presumption” referred to by Latham CJ was contained in s 39 of the 1922 Act but not repeated in s 177 of the 1936 Act. Section 190(b) of the 1936 Act stated that upon every reference or appeal the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive was on the taxpayer. The applicable provision to like effect is s 14ZZK(b)(i) of the TA Act.

  1. In Saffron v FCT, 94 ATC 4049, the Federal Court heard an appeal essentially about the operation of paragraph 190(b). The judgment of Davies J contains an erudite summary of the authorities at pages 4050-1 as follows:
“In any review of an objection decision, it is necessary for the Court or the Administrative Appeals Tribunal to keep in mind that s. 190(b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), now s. 14ZZK(H)(i) and s. 14ZZO(b)(i) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth), provides that the burden of proving that an assessment is excessive shall lie upon the taxpayer. In respect of this, Walsh J. said in Krew v. FC of T 71 ATC 4213 at 4216:-

‘I think it is clear that the onus of proof in relation to the main issues of fact in the case is on the appellant. Furthermore, it is not sufficient, in my opinion, for the appellant to make it seem probable that the overall discrepancy ... disclosed by the betterment statement is not wholly correct. Each year must be considered separately and it must be shown in respect of a particular year that the challenged assessment was wrong. If it is not proved that the whole of the amount of income which is in dispute has been wrongly included as taxable income, then material must be provided upon which the amount of income which should have been excluded can be ascertained. What I have just stated is subject to the qualification that in some cases it may be enough for a taxpayer to show that the assessment has proceeded upon a wrong basis or upon wrong principles: cf. Henderson v. F.C. of T, (1970) 70 ATC 4016; 44 A.L.J.R. 115.’

In FC of T v Dalco 90 ATC 4088 at 4093; [1990] HCA 3; (1990) 168 C.L.R. 614 at 624-625, Brennan J., with whom Mason CJ., Deane, Dawson, Gaudron and McHugh JJ. expressed agreement said:-

‘The manner in which a taxpayer can discharge that burden varies with the circumstances. If the Commissioner and a taxpayer agree to confine an appeal to a specific point of law or fact on which the amount of the assessment depends, it will suffice for the taxpayer to show that he is entitled to succeed on that point. Absent such a confining of the issues for determination, the Commissioner is entitled to rely upon any deficiency in proof of the excessiveness of the amount assessed to uphold the assessment, though the taxpayer is limited to the grounds of his objection. In Gauci v. F.C. of T, 75 ATC 4257; [1975] HCA 54; (1975) 135 C.L.R. 81, Mason J. said (at ATC p. 4261; C.L.R. p. 89):

“The Act does not place any onus on the Commissioner to show that the assessments were correctly made. Nor is there any statutory requirement that the assessments should be sustained or supported by evidence. The implication of such a requirement would be inconsistent with sec. 190(b) for it is a consequence of that provision that unless the appellant shows by evidence that the assessment is incorrect, it will prevail.”

That view, expressed in a dissenting judgment, now prevails: Macmine Pty. Ltd. v F.C. of T. 79 ATC 4133 at pp. 4139, 4146, 4159; (1979) 53 A.L.J.R. 362 at pp. 366, 371, 381; McCormack’s Case [79 ATC 4111; [1979] HCA 18; (1978-1979) 143 CLR 284], at ATC pp. 4121, 4132; C.L.R. pp. 303, 306, 323.’

However, in proceedings by way of review of an objection decision, the assessment itself is not evidence of the facts upon which the assessment was based. The review authority, the Court or the Tribunal, must come to its own decision on the facts and the issues. Walsh J. said in Krew’s case at 4216:-

‘I have a duty to reach my own conclusions on the questions of fact which have to be decided and to give effect to those conclusions. ... I must make my own decisions as to the facts.’

Although the taxpayer may be untruthful and a tax evader, that factor, of itself, is no reason not to undertake a calculation of income tax as contemplated by the Act, if such a calculation can be made. As Walsh J. said in Krew’s case at 4219, ‘A witness upon whose word one cannot rely may yet give evidence which is true in part’. In that case, although Walsh J. held that he could not accept the appellant himself as a reliable witness, he nevertheless found that several of the facts alleged by the taxpayer were established as a matter of probability and accordingly that four of the amended assessments before the Court should be set aside. Moreover, in the calculation of the taxable incomes of years past, exact proof may not be available. As Burchett J. said in Ma v. FC of T 92 ATC 4373 at 4379:-

‘... the making of estimates upon inexact evidence, which is so much a feature of both judicial and administrative decision-making, cannot be uniquely excluded from appeals against betterment assessments.’

If, despite the general untruthfulness of a witness and the lack of records, the Court or the Tribunal nevertheless feels satisfied, as a matter of probability, as to the existence of a relevant fact or factor, effect must be given to that conclusion.”


  1. In this case the respondent has included as assessable income three amounts that were the subject of the currency transactions. There is no doubt on the material before me that those transactions took place. An exchange transaction by itself does not cause the amount of the transaction to become assessable income. In the circumstances of this case it is the source of those funds which will characterise the amount for taxation purposes. If, for example, the amount is income derived from personal exertion in Australia during the year of income then it will, most likely, be assessable income. It’s character will not change because the $AUD dollar amounts so derived are then converted into $US dollar amounts.
  2. I am satisfied, and find, that the funds used for the three currency exchange transactions in April/May 1991 were, on the balance of probabilities, extracted from the “hidden treasure” in the applicant's yard rather than from another source. I am also satisfied, on the balance of probabilities, that the funds had been hidden in the yard for some considerable time, at least earlier than 1 July 1990. Its seems possible, but I am not satisfied it is so, that the $AUD20,000.00 was part of the proceeds of the land sale.
  3. I am satisfied that the $AUD20,000.00 converted to US dollars is not an amount of assessable income derived by the applicant in the year of income ended 30 June 1991. Because the respondent made a specific adjustment to the assessable income based on the currency transaction, it follows that I am satisfied that the amended assessment is excessive and the objection should be allowed.
  4. The objection decision under review will be set aside and the objection allowed. I will certify that these proceedings have terminated in a manner favourable to the applicant.

I certify that this and the ten (10) preceding pages are a true copy of the decision and reasons for decision herein of Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)
