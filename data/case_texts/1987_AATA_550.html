NT86/5150 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 550; (1987) 18 ATR 3364; 87 ATC 398 (9 March 1987)

Last Updated: 14 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax – assessable income – profit on sale of land purchase of parcel of land subject to subdivision - parcel purchased with intention of keeping part and selling remainder whether purpose of profit making by sale.

Income Tax - -assessable income - consideration of penalty imposed.

Words and Phrases - "profit"

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss.26(a), 226

Case T4 0 (1967) 18 TBRD (New Series), 183

Chapman v. FC of T [1968] HCA 4; (1968) 117 CLR 167, 15 ATD 21

North Coast Grazing Pty. Ltd. v. FC of T 1986 86 ATC 4846

FC of T v. McClelland (1969) 69 ATC 4001

FC of T v. Rabinov 1983 14 ATR 415, 83 ATC 4437

Smith v. FC of T (1972) 46 ALJR 518, 3 ATR 204, 72 ATC 411

REASONS FOR DECISION

1. The applicant in this case is a manager and qualified valuer employed by the First National Bank of Chicago. In 1978, after having been in the bank's Dubbo branch, he was transferred to Sydney. The proceeds from the sale of his Dubbo home were modest relative to the price of housing in Sydney, and after considerable efforts to find a home of equivalent standard within his means, he finally found a property for sale ("Blackacre") in a northern suburb of Sydney owned by an elderly lady who had found the upkeep of the large grounds too much for her. This lady had succeeded in obtaining approval from the local Council to subdivide Blackacre into four allotments - lots A, B, C and D. Lot D would contain the lady's home on a small suburban block whilst the remaining three blocks would be on separate titles. Blackacre was for sale on terms that the intending purchaser would undertake, at his own expense, the proposed subdivision. The applicant, who found Lot A ideal for the purpose of building his future home, did a feasibility study of the project to see where he stood if he were to undertake the subdivision with the purpose of retaining Lot A after conveying lot D to the vendor and selling lots B and C.

  1. The sale price of Blackacre was $43,000. After making due enquiries of land values in the area, the applicant concluded that lots B and C would fetch $26,500 each. He calculated that the total cost involved in the subdivision would be approximately $26,383. These calculations were recorded contemporaneously and made an exhibit. They include what was referred to as a "risk factor" calculated at 15% to take into account such imponderables as interest rates, unforeseen contractual problems, possible drainage hazards, etc. In short, the exercise bore all the hallmarks of a well-thought-through, business-like undertaking, albeit on a small scale (and lacking, as its main or dominant purpose, the obtaining of a cash profit from the sale of lots B and C) .
  2. Having concluded that the project was viable, in the sense that the applicant could resell part of the subdivided land in a manner which would enable him to retain lot A at below its market value, the applicant and his wife decided to proceed with the purchase of Blackacre and signed a contract of sale on 1 March 1979, binding themselves to subdivide on the terms previously referred to.
  3. To complete the historic narrative, the subdivision duly proceeded and resulted in the sale of lots B and C for $52,300 and $51,000 respectively. The (agreed) costs of subdivision were $50,225 (not including the cost of the land). The applicant built his home on lot A and still resides there.
  4. The basic facts set out above were duly disclosed in the applicant's 1981 return, the year in which the two lots were sold, the applicant adding "that income from the project (his italics) should be non-taxable and that this should also be the case when and if Lot A is sold at some future time".
  5. Over two years later, and after the initial assessment, the respondent adopted a different view, issued an amended assessment in which he calculated that the applicant had made a taxable profit of $41,150 and imposed additional tax by way of penalty. The profit was arrived at by subtracting from the sale price of lots B and C ($103,000) two thirds of the cost of subdivision ($62,150, which includes the cost of the land) and treating this difference as assessable income, attributing one half to the applicant and the other half to his wife. This calculation assumes that lots A, B and C were of equal value - an assumption not borne out by the evidence, which indicated that lot A, the lot retained by the applicant was more valuable than the other two. The issue before the Tribunal is whether the sum of $20,575 (or some other sum) is taxable income.
  6. To complete the factual narrative, the respondent investigated the surrounding circumstances of this acquisition and sale and, after interviewing the applicant, wrested from him certain admissions which, if they were to constitute an estoppel, would conclude this issue in favour of the respondent. However, the applicant resiled from these admissions, contained in a letter (Exh 1) addressed to the investigator, dated 1 September 1983, and explained the circumstances in which the letter came to be written. On a voir dire, I would be tempted to exclude the letter if I accepted the applicant's evidence on this point. In the end, I have been able to reach my decision without having to rely on the contents of this impugned piece of evidence.

8. Dealing with the evidence, the applicant deposed that he lacked a profit-making purpose when he decided to acquire Blackacre and embarked upon its subdivision. I reject this evidence. Even before setting out on this venture, the applicant wrote a letter (Exhibit G), dated 15 February, 1979, addressed to the staff manager of his bank, in which he stated that:

"The proceeds from the sale of the two blocks will not, having regard to the present market conditions, be sufficient to cover all costs and the purchase price of the land. No cash benefit will be received. The only benefit will be that possibly I will obtain a residential block at below its market value."

His own preliminary calculations disclose that he estimated that - at the end of the day - his own allotment would cost him $16,000 against his estimated market value of $29,000. In other words, the proceeds from the sale of lots B and C were intended to reduce the cost, to the applicant, of the lot intended to be retained.

9. The law relating to the first limb of sec. 26(a) has been conveniently set out by Mr JD Davies (Member, as he then was) in Case T40 18 TBRD 183, a case which was destined to become Chapman v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1968] HCA 4; (1968) 117 CLR 167, the facts of which bear a striking similarity to the facts in this case. Mr Davies' interpretation of sec. 26(a) was upheld on appeal to the High Court, Menzies J concluding that where a parcel of land is purchased with the intention of retaining part of it and selling the remainder at profit, two independent purposes may be found in the purchase, and the profit on the sale of the remainder may be included in the assessable income of the purchaser. That view was, in turn, adopted as correct by the Full High Court in Smith v FC of T (1972) 46 ALJR 518. Applied to this case, I have concluded that where land is bought with the intention of subdividing it and selling off a part in order to reduce the cost of the part to be retained, there is both a "profit" and an "acquisition" for the purpose of profit-making by sale within the meaning of of sec 26(a) of the Tax Act. The concept, that each or either purpose must be the main or dominant purpose for the acquisition of the whole before a gain from such subdivision, is misconceived.

10. The term "profit" has a well-defined legal meaning which, by and large, coincides with the fundamental conception of profit in general parlance, where that term is applied to mean financial gain, that is to say, money received over and above money expended. However, section 26, taken as a whole, brings to tax "profits" which are not regarded as such, either in mercantile or general parlance. Thus Section 26(e), for example, treats as assessable income the value to the taxpayer all benefits given or granted in relation to any employment. Against the whole background of the section, it would, to my mind, constitute a misconstruction of the purpose of the section if one were to apply so restrictive an interpretation of "profit" and "profit-making by sale" so as to limit it to the difference between money received and money expended. What the section is designed to achieve is, in some circumstances, to bring to tax the amount by which value acquired exceeds value expended. In other words, if at the end of the financial year, the value of a taxpayer's assets have increased as a result of an undertaking deliberately embarked upon after shrewd commercial calculation designed to achieve that end, then the enhanced value in his assets constitute "the assessable income" embraced in Section 26(a). It may well be that such a construction adds, in parenthesis, the words "or gain" so that the section reads:

"The assessable income of a taxpayer shall include profit or gain from the sale by the taxpayer of any property acquired by him for the purpose of profit-making by sale...."

Furthermore, it is likely that in the days when "strict construction" was applied to fiscal legislation, such addition would have been regarded as part of original sin. However, as I read more modern canons of construction, it seems to me that the "purposive" test has replaced strict construction. Applying this more modern approach, I have concluded that to buy land with the intention of subdividing it so that the lots sold will enable the subdivider to acquire the lot to be retained at fire sale prices will bring to tax the difference between the commercial value of the retained allotment, fixed at the time of acquisition, and the (notional) bargain rate for which it is acquired as a result of the subdivision undertaking - it is therefore a "profit" arising from acquisition for the purpose of profit-making by sale within the meaning of the first limb of section 26(a).

  1. I have therefore concluded that this applicant made a "profit" within the meaning of the first limb of sec 26(a). Having reached that position, thereafter, the problem is merely one of arithmetic and I echo the words of Menzies J when he pointed out in Chapman that "where an entirety is purchased and part only is sold there is always the problem of determining what profit has been made. Sometimes this may prove insuperable." (at p 171). Fortunately, in this case, the dispute is not on quantum but merely whether the gain constitutes assessable income, both parties adopting a somewhat simplistic approach in its calculation, ignoring the problems, highlighted in FC of T v McClelland 69 ATC 4001, which arise whenever the retained property has a different value from the property which is sold.
  2. In the circumstances, no concluded opinion is required whether the subdivision in the instant case is a profit-making undertaking or scheme as that term has been explained in FC of T v. Whitfords Beach Pty Ltd 82 ATC 4031.
  3. The final matter to be considered is the question of penalty. My discretion to reduce or remit additional tax levied pursuant to section 226(2) arises only at the point where such additional tax exceeds 20% of the tax avoided. In the instant case, the penalty imposed is below that figure so that I have no power to deal with the question of penalty. It may be worth repeating, however, that a taxpayer becomes liable to additional tax whenever he fails to include an amount of income, and this is true notwithstanding that the facts have been disclosed in the return, albeit given a non-taxable character; cf. Newton v FC of T (1958) 98 CLR 2, 11-12; FC of T v Rabinov 83 ATC 4437; North Coast Grazing Pty Ltd v FC of T 86 ATC 4846, 4850. In any event, the applicant in this case overstated his subdivisional expenditure in this return by nearly $10,000, an omission which the respondent may legitimately take into account when arriving at quantum.

14. The decision of the Tribunal is to affirm the decision under review.

This and the preceding eight pages are a true and correct copy of the Decision and Reasons of Dr P Gerber, Senior Member, in the matter of NT86/5150.
