QT93/51 and Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1995] AATA 647; (1995) 31 ATR 1393; 96 ATC 230 (27 October 1995)

Last Updated: 13 February 2009



DECISION OF PRESIDENTIAL MEMBER JPO BARRY

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX DIVISION

TRIBUNAL REF: QT93/51

RE:

AND

THE DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

PLACE : TOWNSVILLE

HEARING DATE : 7TH MARCH 1995

JUDGMENT DATE: 27TH OCTOBER 1995

AAT No 10737



DECISION



  1. The Tribunal allows the Applicant's appeal so far as it relates to the objection to the disallowance of interest claimed pursuant to Section 51(1).

  2. The matter to be adjourned to a date to be fixed on the giving by either party of seven days' notice, one to the other for the re-arguing, if necessary of the late payment penalty imposed.

HIS HONOUR: This matter was heard before me on 7th March this year. At the time of hearing, the law was in a state of considerable uncertainty as exemplified by the conflicting decisions of Deputy President McMahon in Case 1/95 reported in 1995 AAT decisions and No. 5/95, a contrary decision of Deputy President Gerber reported in the same volume.



I reserved my decision, intending to write the definitive determination on the proper interpretation of section 51(1) so far as it applies to situations such as the present. Having read all of the cases cited in the Respondent's list of authorities, I was certainly more persuaded by Deputy President Gerber's approach to the problem than that advocated by Deputy President McMahon.



As events transpired, my research was to little avail.



On the 7th July 1995, the Full Court of the Federal Court (Davies, Hill and Sackville JJ), handed down a judgment in the matter of Placer Pacific Management Pty Ltd and Commissioner of Taxation. In the course of its judgment, the Full Court observed:



In our view AGC should be taken as establishing the proposition that provided the occasion of a business outgoing is to be found in the business operations directed towards the gaining or production of assessable income generally, the fact that that outgoing was incurred in a year later than the year in which the income was incurred and the fact that in the meantime, business in the ordinary sense may have ceased, will not determine the issue of deductibility. There is no relevant distinction to be drawn between losses and outgoings. Provided the occasion for the loss or outgoing is to be found in the business operations directed to gaining or producing assessable income, that loss or outgoing will be deductible unless it is capital or of a capital nature.



On the facts of the present case, the occasion of the loss or outgoing ultimately incurred in the year of income was the business arrangement entered into between Placer and NWCC for the supply of the conveyor belt which was alleged to be defective. The fact that the division had subsequently been sold and its active manufacturing business terminated does not deny deductibility to the outgoing. A finding to the contrary would lead to great inequity. Many businesses generate liabilities which may arise in the considerable future. Such liabilities are sometimes referred to as "long tail liabilities". To preclude deductibility when those liabilities come to fruition on the basis that the active trading business which gave rise to them had ceased, would be unjust.



I proceed on the basis that, notwithstanding earlier High Court determinations, this is an authoritative determination by an appellate court on this matter.



I turn to a consideration of the facts in the present case.



The parties filed a statement of agreed facts (Document A of the T documents). Rather than attempting a summary of such facts, it is, in my view, appropriate to set out in full the terms of the agreed facts:



AGREED FACTS

  1. On 23 July 1985, the applicant and his spouse entered into a franchise agreement (the 'Agreement') with Interlock International Pty Ltd (refer attachment A1). This agreement was drawn up by Spina and Lawrence solicitors on behalf of The applicant and his spouse.

  2. The Agreement allowed The applicant and his spouse the sole right to market and sell the 'Interlock Home Building System' (the 'System' - a timber kit home system) within the designated area identified as the 'Townsville' district (A1, clause 2(a)).

  3. One of the conditions of the Agreement was the requirement that The applicant and his spouse erect and maintain a display home utilizing the System (A1, clause 10).

  4. On 21 August 1985, The applicant and his spouse ordered the kit that would become the display home (A2).

  5. Land at 627 Ross River Road, Townsville was purchased by The applicant and his spouse as the site of the display home.

  6. As part of the finance for the business, a $70,000 loan from Nat West Finance was obtained (A8).

  7. The display home was completed in February 1986, at which point The applicant and his spouse commenced to market the kit homes in the Townsville district, using the display home as a business office (A3).

  8. On 4 April 1986, receivers and managers were appointed to Interlock International Pty Ltd. The applicant and his spouse became aware of this fact through a letter sent to Spina and Lawrence solicitors dated 17 April 1986 (A4).

  9. On 7 May 1986, a circular was issued by the receivers and managers to agents/franchisee holders (A5). Amongst other things, the circular stated that the receivers and managers considered they held the proprietary rights over the patent on the System (the 'Patent').

  10. On January 22 1987, the prospective buyer of the Patent contacted The applicant and his spouse offering a new franchise agreement to replace the previous Agreement (A6).

  11. On 3 February 1987, The applicant and his spouse received legal advice that the Agreement could be ignored by the receivers and managers or by any third party purchaser of the Patent (A7).

  12. In March 1987, The applicant and his spouse lodged their 1986 income tax return (A8).

  13. In or about March 1987, the applicant obtained work as a real estate salesman.

  14. During the period April 1986 to June 1987, The applicant and his spouse contracted and completed eleven homes under the Agreement. After March 1987 however, sales were not actively pursued and all houses previously contracted were finished by June 1987.

  15. The Patent was sold by the receivers and managers to Petarli Pty Ltd (the 'Purchaser') in April 1987 (A9).

  16. In June 1987, the display home was sold.

  17. The proceeds from the sale were used to pay out the nat West loan of $70,000. These proceeds were not sufficient to cover the full amount of the loan and $24,000 remained owing to Nat West.

  18. On 23rd July 1987, the Purchaser sent a letter to the applicant offering a new franchise agreement, though on less favourable terms than the original Agreement (A10).

  19. In August 1987, the applicant took out a $15,000 personal loan which was used as detailed in attachment (A11.)

  20. On 31 August 1987, the applicant wrote to the Purchaser requesting a resolution of the situation surrounding its refusal to recognize the Agreement (A12).

  21. On 26th October 1987, the applicant was granted legal aid (A13).

  22. On 17th November 1987, The applicant and his spouse received a legal opinion on whether they had any rights with regard to the Patent (A14).

  23. On 25 November 1987 the solicitors for The applicant and his spouse wrote to them offering to negotiate a new franchise agreement (A15).

  24. On 1 march 1988, The applicant and his spouse lodged their 1987 Partnership come Tax Return (A16). This referred to Interlock International Pty Ltd's receivership and stated that this put the Agreement at risk and caused 'business to falter in the 1987/88 year'.

  25. On 17 November 1988, the 1988 partnership return was lodged (A17). This return made reference to the business having 'ceased' and stated that it would be the final return for the partnership.

  26. On 21 July 1989 an objection was lodged on behalf of the applicant against his 1987 assessment (A18). One of the claims made, was that borrowing costs that were to be spread over five years, should be allowed in full due to the fact that the business had closed down in the year ended 30 June 1988. This aspect of the objection was subsequently allowed.

  27. In April 1990, the applicant wrote to the respondent to clarify the issue of the interest that continued to be paid on outstanding loans and the loss in value of The applicant and his spouse's franchise (A19).

  28. On 4 June 1990, the respondent advised the applicant that no deductions were allowable in relation to the matters raised in his April 1990 letter (A20).

  29. On 26th August 1990, The applicant and his spouse sought a legal opinion on whether an action for negligence existed against the solicitors who acted for them in the preparation of the original Agreement.

  30. On 2 May 1991, The applicant and his spouse received legal advice that supported a negligence action being undertaken (A22).

  31. A negligence action was commenced with the filing of Plaint in the District Court, Townsville on 2 July 1991 (A23).

  32. On 27 April 1992, the applicant lodged his 1991 Income Tax Return (the return was lodged in electronic form - A24).

  33. The applicant's 1991 return included:

(a) a $2448 deduction claim for interest paid on the outstanding partnership loans - both the original Nat West loan and the later personal loan; and,

(b) a request for a ruling on the deductibility of the interest.



  1. The respondent ruled that the interest was not deductible and on 28 September 1992 issued the applicant's 1991 assessment, disallowing the claim for an interest deduction (A25).

  2. On 16 October 1992, the applicant lodged an objection to the disallowance of the interest claim made in his 1991 return (A26).

  3. The respondent disallowed the applicant's objection 23 February 1993 (A27).

  4. On 8 march 1993, the defendants in the negligence action taken out by The applicant and his spouse filed an amended Entry of Appearance and Defence (A28). Attached to this was a copy of the April 1987 sale agreement of the Patent.

  5. The sale agreement expressly preserved the rights obtained by The applicant and his spouse under the original Agreement - a fact The applicant and his spouse had not been made aware of until the filing of the Defence. The negligence action was subsequently discontinued.

  6. On 27 April 1993, the applicant lodged a reference with the Administrative Appeals Tribunal in relation to the respondent's disallowance of his 1991 objection (A29).

  7. In response to an inquiry made by the applicant, Hyne & Son Pty Ltd - the previous supplier/manufacturer of the kit homes - wrote to the applicant on 6 April 1994, advising him that it could no longer supply the kits (A30). The reason stated for this was the sale of its Bundaberg manufacturing facility "some years earlier".

  8. On 11 April 1994, the applicant wrote to the respondent (A31). This letter reiterated the applicant's view of the situation and requested the respondent reconsider the matter. The letter also conceded that as a result of the Hyne & Son Pty Ltd sale of plant, the former business could not recommence.

  9. On 17 February 1995, an oficer of the respondent called Hyne & Son Pty Ltd to determine the exact date of sale of its Bundaberg plant. he was advised by a representative of the company that the records were not currently available, but that the sale could have been as far back as 1987/88 (A32).

FACTS NOT AGREED



  1. One of the underlying issues in this reference, certainly as far as the respondent is concerned, is when, if at all, the business conducted by The applicant and his spouse ceased. This issue is a question of fact, one upon which the applicant and the respondent cannot agree.

THE ISSUE AND QUANTUM IN DISPUTE



The following is also agreed.



  1. The interest incurred on the relevant loans during the year ended 30 June 1991 was:

Nat West Finance: $430.88

ANZ Personal Loan $2424.37



  1. Of the above ANZ interest figure, $690.70 can be classified as private expenditure.

  2. The quantum at issue therefore, is the applicant's half share of the remaining interest, that is:

Nat West Finance $215.44

ANZ Personal Loan: $866.83

Total $1082.27



  1. The issue in dispute is whether or not the applicant should be allowed this $1082 interest expense as a deduction under section 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act for the year ended 30 June 1991.

I am satisfied the applicants were influenced to a great extent in their business dealings by their misunderstanding of their rights. When the sale of the proprietary rights over the patent on the Interlock system took place, it appears the rights of the applicants were preserved (A9). It was not until the applicants took legal action for negligence against their former solicitors who had drawn the original agreement that this fact came to light, so far as the applicants were concerned. By the time the applicants ascertained their true position, namely, that their rights under the original franchise agreement were still protected, the fabricator/supplier Hyne & Son Pty Ltd no longer had the facilities available to supply the Interlock kits (refer A30). By letter of 30th March 1992, the applicant's accountants had written to the Commissioner in the following terms:



The partnership built a display home and commenced business with a profit being achieved in the 1986/87 financial year. It was then that the parent company supplying the building system went into liquidation which affectively (sic) caused the franchises to become worthless and the business ceased in the 1987/88 financial year.



Notwithstanding the terms of this letter, there is no evidence the partnership operated by the applicant and his wife was formally dissolved or wound up in any way. It did, however, cease to trade in the normal use of that term, but only because of the legal difficulties inherent in the dispute over the rights to the patent. Subsequently, the dispute became irrelevant as Hyne & Son Pty Ltd no longer fabricated Interlock units pursuant to the patent.



By letter of 24th February 1993, the Commissioner wrote to the applicant Taxpayer in the following terms:



Your (sic) are advised that the interest payments on the restructured debt are not considered to be income tax deductible as there is no nexus or link between the expense and income producing activity.



In a Statement of Facts and Contentions which the Taxpayer had prepared for a Directions hearing, the Taxpayer contended, paragraphs 22 to 24:



The Applicant contends that the partnership interest expenses continued to be deductible between 1987 and 1994 as the business conducted by The applicant and his spouse had in fact not ceased. The business operations were merely suspended, not for the purpose of abandoning the business, but to enable its continuance until such time that the partnership regained the right

to use the Interlock name and patents.



  1. The applicant contends that the fact that the partnership did not earn assessable income between 1987 and 1994 does not indicate that the business ceased, the partnership still held assets (Interlock name and patents) that were subject to legal dispute. The mere continuance of legal action should be considered to be business activity in this case.

  2. The partnership contends it had at all times intended to begin building and selling Interlock homes as soon as it regained the right to use the Interlock name and patent. At all times the partnership believed that it was only a matter of time before it was earning assessable income by building Interlock kit homes.

The respondent lodged written submissions under three headings:

1. Cessation of business.

  1. Deductibility of expenditure after the cessation of business.
  2. Deductibility of interest in the present case.

The submissions on behalf of the Commissioner were made in detail under each of these headings. In view of the approach that I take on the facts of this case, and in view of the decision of the Full Court of the Federal Court in the Placer Pacific case (supra), I find it unnecessary to go into any further detail of the respondent's submissions.



The only oral evidence in the case was from the Taxpayer himself. He was cross-examined as to his assertion of the facts.



The conduct of Taxpayer at all times has been exemplary. I have no doubt at all he is an honest citizen, and I have no hesitation in accepting his testimony. I accept Taxpayer's assurance that at all material times, he was of the belief he was not allowed to build kit homes because of the legal wrangle which had developed. When that issue was resolved in his favour after a good deal of incorrect advice had been received, Taxpayer learnt that Hyne & Son could no longer provide the kits. He was unable to locate any further supplier.



In my view, Taxpayer had not ceased operating his business until he was totally frustrated by the news that Hyne & Son could not supply his orders. It makes no sense that he should make enquiry of Hyne & Son were the position to be otherwise.



I find, then, that at all material times, Taxpayer was carrying on his business and the interest payments are related to such income under the terms of Section 51(1).



If necessary, in the present case, I would be prepared to interpret the facts to include a situation where Taxpayer was still carrying on his business so long as there were outstanding accounts to be paid.



Having regard to the sorry history of debtors in this country in recent years, Taxpayer stands out as a person who wished to pay all outstanding business obligations. In order to preserve his good name, he did not wish to entertain bankruptcy. If the only way to do this was to make repayments over a period of time, in my view, Taxpayer is still carrying on his business, and the expenditure incurred is deductible because it necessarily relates to the income previously acquired.



In any event, having regard to the passage quoted at the commencement of this judgment from the Placer Pacific case, no such findings are really necessary in order to give relief.



It is abundantly clear on the facts of the present case, the occasion of the business outgoing is to be found in the business operations directed towards the gaining or production of assessable income. It matters not, in those circumstances, that the outgoing was in a year later than the year in which the income was incurred, nor does it matter that the business has ceased to exist.



I propose to allow the applicant's appeal so far as it relates to the deductibility of the outstanding interest.



Late Payment Penalties:

The original Notice of Assessment for the year ended 30th June 1990 reveals an additional tax for late return of $2974.38. The assessment notes quote additional tax for late return calculated at 20% p.a. . In the Commissioner's 169A ruling dealing with Income Tax year '89/'90, the Commissioner finds:



Accordingly the Commissioner is of the opinion that the interest paid during the '89/'90 year of $2388.58 is not allowable as a deduction under Section 51(1) as the expense was not incurred in carrying on a business.



The only document included with the Statement of Agreed Facts and Supplementary documentation, Document A25, is the Notice of Assessment for the year ended 30th June 1991. This document, which issued on 28th September 1992, on page 2 notes:

Adjustment to Deductions

Interest

- Not Allowable $2448.



I am satisfied from Document A26, the letter of Taxpayer of 16th October 1992, that Taxpayer was objecting to the interest payments assessed for the 1990 and 1991 years. In that typewritten letter, Taxpayer also makes it clear that he is objecting to the additional tax for late returns.



During the course of submissions the advocate for the Commissioner submitted that under the Taxation Administration Act Section 14ZS, this Tribunal had no power to review the late lodgment penalty, that is, in circumstances where additional tax is 20% or less. On behalf of the Commissioner it was submitted that combination of Section 14ZQ, 14ZS and 14ZZ of the Taxation Administration Act (1953) precluded the Tribunal from reviewing the late lodgment penalty. It was submitted in the alternative that even if the Tribunal was entitled to review, then, on the facts of this case, it did not warrant a remission.



I must confess that I have considerable difficulty understanding the terms of Section 14ZS. I have read it a number of times and when read a particular way, seems to have the very opposite meaning as that contended for by the advocate for the respondent.



It may be that the applicant's accountant accepts the reality of the situation and does not press for a determination on the late penalty issue.



If this is not the case, then I propose to allow the matter to be relisted for further argument and production of further material if necessary. It seems to me that the focus, understandably enough, was on the contentious legal issue concerning the proper interpretation of Section 51(1). Little attention, if at all, was paid to the late payment penalty, but, in financial terms, this amount exceeds interest claimed to be deductible.



In view of my delay in delivering this determination, I am reluctant to bring it back on for further argument, but I will give either party liberty to apply on seven days to have the matter relisted as a priority hearing for the taking of further submissions on the late penalty issue.



It may be that the Commissioner, in view of the observations and findings that I have made, may elect to waive the late payment penalty imposed, but that, of course, is not a matter that concerns this Tribunal.



At this point in time, the determination I give is to allow the applicant's appeal so far as it relates to the objection to the disallowance of interest claimed pursuant to Section 51(1). The only further order of the Tribunal will be to adjourn this matter to a date to be fixed on the giving by either party of seven days' notice one to the other for the re-arguing, if necessary, of the late payment penalty imposed.
