ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

Nos. NT92/305-307

AAT No. 8936

Number of pages - 12

Income Tax

[1993] AATA 267; (1993) 26 ATR 1217

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

B.J. McMAHON (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - assessable income - amended assessments - decisions rejecting applicant's objections - review of - whether distributions from company deemed to be dividends - lack of credit - lack of corroborative evidence - evasion - aggravating factors - penalties

Income Tax Assessments Act ss 6(1)(a), 44

Black v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 86 ATC 4113

Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Black 90 ATC 4699

Summons (Kenneth A) Pty Ltd and Ors v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 86 ATC 4979

HEARING

SYDNEY, 9-11 August 1993

23:8:1993

The Applicant appeared in person.

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr D McGovern

Solicitors for the Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decisions under review and the matters are remitted to the respondent with the direction that the assessments be amended in accordance with agreed mutual concessions and in accordance with the Tribunal's reasons.

DECISION

B.J. McMAHON The applicant was one of the 2 directors and one of the 2 shareholders in an excavation company which ceased trading during January 1989 and which is now in liquidation. An audit of this company commenced in October 1990. As a result of preliminary findings made by the respondent's auditors in relation to the company's financial affairs, a decision was made to include the applicant's own affairs in the ambit of the company audit. As a result of the audit, adjustments were made to his 1988, 1989 and 1990 income tax returns and amended assessments reflecting these adjustments were issued on 10 January 1992. This application is brought to review decisions of the respondent rejecting the applicant's objections to the amended assessments.

2. Since disallowing the objections in full, additional documents and information were made available by the applicant, leading to further concessions on the part of the respondent. Since the commencement of these proceedings, even more concessions have been made on both sides. There remain, however, a number of specific items which are in dispute. They fall into 3 broad categories. There were a number of payments made to the applicant, which the respondent treated as "deemed dividends". Secondly there were a number of bank deposits from unexplained sources. The third area of disagreement related to miscellaneous amounts of salary, living away from home allowance and interest.

3. Additional tax was also included in the amended assessments. The culpability and per annum rates have not been adjusted, although the dollar amounts, of course, have changed. Before dealing with the specific items, I will make some general observations, which will apply to the decision in relation to each of the items in question.

4. The applicant has a background in accounting, although he does not have formal qualifications. He has been a company secretary, he has conducted a financial services business, he has assisted (without fee) in the preparation of returns for friends and relatives and has prepared his own returns. He is therefore quite familiar with accounting concepts and with the way in which sums should be treated for taxation purposes. He conducted his own application before me and displayed a good grip of the accounting aspects of all the items in question. Nevertheless, his conduct during the course of the audit indicated that he was not prepared to put this ability to honest purposes.

5. The initial interview took place on 7 November 1990 when the applicant was asked a number of questions relating to his own taxation affairs. These answers were recorded in a questionnaire which was tendered in evidence. Some of them were quite false, to the applicant's knowledge.

6. He was asked to list the real estate properties owned by himself, his wife or children. He said that he owned properties at Mosman and in Queensland. Subsequent to this interview, property searches made by the respondent revealed that he had failed to disclose 2 properties at Old Bar and another property at Bondi. When confronted with this, he then agreed that he did in fact own a joint interest in these properties with Mr I. No real explanation was offered, even in evidence, as to why these facts had been suppressed.

7. During the initial interview, he was also asked to identify all the bank accounts which he held. He answered that he had one Westpac account, a Westpac Superfund account, which had not been operated on for some time, and a Westpac Loan account. Subsequent investigations revealed that he in fact had 3 accounts with the Commercial Banking Company of Australia and a further account with Westpac, in all of which there had been substantial transactions. Again no satisfactory explanation of this omission was given.

8. During the initial interview he stated in answer to a specific question that he did not gamble. On 15 April 1991, when he provided some further information to the auditor, he wrote advising that the Bondi property was "bought mainly from gambling wins". He said in his letter : "I did not see any point in disclosing my gambling habits as I am not a professional gambler. I will only bet on tips I may receive from time to time. I have some records (luckily) of major winnings which you are now receiving with this letter. My wife is not aware of it (an issue kept private)". In support of this, he furnished some hand written papers detailing the names of horses and races at particular race meetings and the alleged amounts of his bets. He also provided copies of bookmakers' tickets. All of these were later shown to be false. When cross-examined before me, he said of his false statements that "I thought it was the easy way out". He had not then received professional advice in relation to the audit when this letter was written.

9. It was later discovered that the funds used to purchase the Bondi property were sourced to a payment from the undisclosed CBA account and moneys from one of his associated companies.

10. At the time of the initial interview, the applicant did not disclose a Westpac bank account which he held jointly with Mr I. A total of $344,817 was deposited into this joint account in the 1989 year. The money was the proceeds of a contract with a local council to fill in a waste dump. The contract was with the company and trucks and equipment belonging to the company were used to fulfil the contract. All expenses relating to the contract were claimed in the company's returns. Nevertheless, the moneys were paid directly into an account in the name of the 2 shareholders and was appropriated by them for their own purposes. This is no longer a matter in dispute as the applicant now acknowledges that his share of these moneys was income. The failure to disclose the account and the transaction, however, is relevant to his credit.

11. It took some time to establish the real facts. They were not disclosed at the initial interview. Books of account were reconstructed from bank statements. The applicant falsely represented to the auditor that one of the entries in the bank statements used for reconstructing the company's accounts related to this transaction. This was proved by vouching to be wrong.

12. He said in evidence that he was not responsible for recording the day to day financial affairs of the company, but was merely the company's secretary. A Mr H was an accountant employed by the company. However no attempt was made to locate him during the course of the audit. At the time of the initial interview, there were no financial records available for examination. A list of required records was provided by the respondent to the applicant. By 23 November 1990, he had provided some, but they were not complete. In a letter dated 11 December 1990, he claimed that certain books and records had been lost. As will be seen from the evidence to be reviewed later, the probability is that there were no such records at any time. In particular it is unlikely that there was any minute book recording resolutions of directors or shareholders.

13. Some of the payments that were made to the applicant were said by him to be moneys paid by the company in reduction of his loan account to the company. During the course of the audit from October 1990 to January 1992 there was no suggestion that any such loan account existed and there is certainly no documentary evidence of its existence. The applicant tendered oral evidence by his accountant that in circumstances which led to the incorporation of the company, it is likely in ordinary commercial circumstances that the promoters would have charged goodwill for the business they brought to the company and would have created a loan account to evidence this. This is purely hypothetical in the present circumstances as there is no evidence to that effect. There is no evidence that a loan account ever existed in fact except for the oral evidence of the applicant. I do not consider this sufficient in the absence of any corroboration.

14. The allegations concerning a loan account arose after the assessments had been issued. A number of other similar allegations were made, such as the supposed letter relating to a living away from home allowance which came to light during the period between the date of the assessment and the date of the application to this Tribunal. I will deal with the living away from home allowance evidence later in these reasons. It is sufficient for present purposes to say that I have concluded that the documentary evidence that the applicant produced to support his claim for this allowance was fabricated and that the statements of fact contained in the so-called evidence are untrue.

15. In the balance sheet of the company for the year ended 30 June 1989, an amount of $328,692 was identified as earth moving equipment. During the initial interview the applicant stated that the company had transferred all of its equipment to a related company at book value and had disclosed this fact in its return. The written down value was $150,208 and the consideration for the transfer was $160,000 resulting in a gain on disposal of $9,792. The applicant even identified a deposit in the company's Westpac account of $155,110-60 and stated that this amount was from the sale of the equipment. In support of this assertion, the applicant reconstructed a ledger showing a credit in the sum of $155, 110-60 for earth moving equipment. All these allegations have subsequently been shown to be false. In fact the equipment was sold, in circumstances with which I will deal in more detail later, to a company associated with Mr I. The net proceeds of sale finished up in the hands of the applicant and Mr I, contrary to the information that had been given to the auditor.

16. In relation to some of the unexplained deposits, letters from friends and relatives were produced, well after the audit, which purport to explain the deposits as being of a non-income nature. I will deal with them individually in these reasons. It is sufficient for present purposes to say that I have concluded the letters were again concoctions of the applicant.

17. All of these general observations have led me to the conclusion that the applicant should not be given any credit for truthfulness. Unless his statements are corroborated by objective evidence, I consider that they would be quite unsafe to rely upon. I have concluded that the applicant has falsified records, has made false statements on some occasions and on other occasions has recklessly stated anything that he thought would be to his advantage at the time, without regard to the truth of any such statement.

18. The other general matter upon which I wished to comment at this stage relates to the treatment of moneys paid to the applicant while he was a shareholder in the company. For reasons to which I will refer, I do not consider them to be payments in reduction of any loan account. They are, in my view, clearly taxable in the applicant's hands. It is not necessary for the respondent to resort to the provisions of s 108. The moneys were paid by way of cheque directly to the applicant. There was never any suggestion that he was under any obligation to repay the moneys. They were not credited to his account with the company. In each case, there was a distribution within the meaning of paragraph (a) of the definition of "dividend" in s 6(1) of the Act, in that there was a "dealing out or bestowal", to use the words of Sweeney J in Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Black 90 ATC 4699 at 4705. Being dividends, they are taxable under s 44 of the Act. In the circumstances which I will describe, it is clear that the dividends were paid out of profits derived by the company.

19. The circumstances are similar to those considered by Ormiston J in Summons Pty Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 86 ATC 4979 at 5006 and 5007 -

"Counsel for the Commissioner submitted that sec. 108 proceeded from

the assumption that payments by way of advances or loans and payments

made by a company on behalf, or for the individual benefit of, the

shareholders are not dividends, nor are they distributions of income.

However, it enables the Commissioner to form the opinion that those

amounts represent distributions of income and the section thereby deems

them to be dividends. If the company has distributed the dividends out

and out, whether they be directly paid or whether they be credited, and

whether or not they are not formally described as dividends, there is no

need for the Commissioner to resort to sec. 108. So in practice the

Commissioner would need to form an opinion only where the company did

not purport to credit or distribute its moneys beneficially to

shareholders, but where the payments or credits were "disguised" as some

other transaction, especially advances or loans.

Counsel on behalf of the individual taxpayers was not inclined to

dispute this analysis and I have concluded that it is correct, although

it leaves a number of difficult practical decisions unanswered.

Perhaps the distinction is to be drawn between those advances and loans

which effectively put the money in the hands or bank accounts of the

shareholders, to be drawn on as and when they wish without suffering the

disadvantage (subject to sec. 108) of having to account to the

Commissioner for those moneys as part of their assessable income, and

those advances and loans which are mere book entries and which are not

intended to reflect the parties' real acts. The former is a good

example of circumstances where the Commissioner might fairly form an

opinion because the benefit of the distributions are received before any

formal dividends are declared. Subsection (2) of sec. 108 appears to be

directed to the effect of a subsequent but bona fide dividend catching

up with the moneys actually distributed."

20. Another example of a similar treatment of moneys paid in like circumstances can be found in Black v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 86 ATC 4113 at 4115 and 4116.

21. At the conclusion of the proceedings, a document was prepared and was agreed to by both parties, identifying the remaining specific matters in dispute. I will now deal with each of them in turn.

Year ended 30 June 1988

Item $30,137.

22. This amount was the applicant's share of an advance made by the company to the applicant and Mr I to assist in the purchase of the Old Bar properties. The moneys are said by the applicant to have been reimbursement to him for the purchase of office equipment, rent paid on premises, car allowance, stationery and other expenses They were included in a letter bearing date 8 July 1987 said to have been attached to another letter dated 27 May 1988 and said to have been signed by Mr I. He not only denied having seen the letter before, or having signed it, but also denied that in his view the applicant was entitled to reimbursement for the expenses claimed. None of the equipment had been transferred to the excavation company. It had been acquired on behalf of another earlier company in which they have both been associated. There is no agreement or minute evidencing the company's agreement to accept this obligation. In the absence of such evidence, I am not prepared to accept the applicant's unsupported word. The moneys constituted a dividend.

Unexplained deposit $13,000.

23. The explanation given by the applicant was that this sum was made up of $9,000 alleged to have been repayment of a loan by Mr G and $4,000 alleged to have been a repayment of a loan by Mr B. As these loans recur in other years of income, I will deal with the background to them at this stage. There were tendered 3 hand written documents purporting to be signed by Mr G. In one he certifies that he had lent the applicant $9,000 on 26 May 1988. In the second document he certifies that he "gave" the applicant $8,000 in 3 sums namely $3,000 on 2 December 1988, $4,000 on 13 December 1988 and $1,000 on 21 December 1988. In the third document he states that he had lent the applicant $8,000 on 24 April 1990. I was told that no attempt had been made to arrange for Mr G to give evidence until the end of the first day of the proceedings before me. When the applicant made some telephone calls that evening, he found that Mr G was in Adelaide and was unable to give evidence the following day. In view of the other evidence concerning letters signed by friends and relatives, I consider it highly likely that these 3 letters were composed by the applicant. Evidence was given that Mr G kept very poor records and has been a bankrupt since 1991. There was no evidence that his Statement of Affairs showed money owing by the applicant. Evidence was also given that the applicant carried out some accounting services for Mr G from time to time. In the absence of any corroboration, I am not prepared to accept these 3 documents as satisfying the applicant's onus of proof. The sums referred to in the 3 letters will therefore remain, so far as I am concerned, unexplained deposits.

24. Mr B gave evidence concerning 2 sums which he is said to have paid the applicant in repayment of loans. There were 2 letters in evidence which he acknowledges that he signed but which he agrees were prepared by the applicant. Both are dated 21 January 1992. The first states "This is to certify that the sum of $4,500 was lent to (the applicant) on 7-4-89". The second states "This is to certify that I have paid (the applicant) the sum of $4,058 on 24 May 1988 (details of cheque given). (The applicant) has been a personal friend of mine since 1981 and there were many loans between us over the years. There were never any business transactions between ourselves. This payment of $4,058 is definitely one of those loans." In addition, the applicant tendered a copy of a bank deposit slip showing a deposit of a cheque drawn by Mr B for $4,000 together with 2 smaller cheques from Telecom and Medicare, totalling $57-88. The total of these 3 deposits (approximately $4,058) is said to be the sum referred to in the longer letter from Mr B.

25. He gave evidence that the applicant telephoned him in recent times saying that he had problems with his taxation and asking him to signed papers about these loans. Mr B said that he did not recall the exact date that he made loans but he knew that he had paid money to the applicant in the past. He made no attempt to verify the contents of the letters, which were drafted by the applicant, and placed before Mr B for signature. He said he relied entirely on his memory of events 4 or 5 years ago. He has not since tried to verify the contents of the letters. He thought the payments he made were of the order of $3,000 or $4,000 "more likely $3,000 than $4,000". He said that the applicant had been his friend from earlier days and had helped him when he was starting his trucking business. He said that if he had problems with his truck, the applicant would advance him money for repairs. The applicant also did some accounting work for Mr B. Mr B kept careful check of contract records and particularly noted the day of each month he was due to be paid, when he did contract work for the applicant's company. His cashflow was so tight that he could rarely wait until the end of month for payment. He did not consider it strange that his transactions with the applicant were not so carefully recorded. He agreed that he was grateful for the applicant's help in his early days, but denied that he would sign anything that was put in front of him. Nevertheless he said "I am lazy and hopeless at paper work". He also agreed that in 1990 he earned very little, netting perhaps about $25,000 before living expenses. He agreed that he did not lend the applicant money in 1990, 1991 or 1992 and that the last loan was "ages ago" when he was with the company.

26. This evidence does not convince me that the 2 letters represent an accurate account of the transactions between Mr B and the applicant. As I stated earlier, I will not accept the applicant's word without independent corroboration. The likelihood of these sums of money being of the character described by the applicant is not supported, in my view, by Mr B's evidence.

Item $14,863.

27. This was a second sum of money paid by the company on the applicant's account to assist in the financing of the Old Bar properties. For the reason given above I consider that the amount is taxable income. It was said to have been debited against the loan account which I do not believe existed. It must be regarded as a dividend.

Year ended 30 June 1989

Item $40,000

28. This was an amount paid from surplus funds by the company to the applicant and said by him to be paid in reduction of his shareholder's loan account. As I have already indicated, I do not accept that there was such an account and the money, in my view, must be treated as a dividend.

Item $60,000

29. This sum represents an amount which was paid to the applicant on 21 February 1989. Some considerable time after the audit, the applicant produced a document said to be a copy of a letter signed by Mr I on behalf of the company and addressed to the applicant. It purports to authorise a payment of $20,000 to reimburse the applicant for expenses incurred in living away from home during jobs at Newcastle and Campbelltown. Accompanying that letter is another document, said to be a copy of a letter signed by Mr I on behalf of the company, and addressed to the applicant enclosing a cheque for $60,000. The accompanying letter says that the balance of $40,000 had been debited to the applicant's loan account and purported to give a then current balance of his loan account. It was delivered to the applicant while he was in hospital recovering from injuries he received in a motor vehicle accident. He said he had no recollection who brought the letter to him or how it was arrived at. He denied that he had drafted it. The fact is, however, that the cheque which accompanied that letter was countersigned by him (folio 120 T-documents). Mr I gave evidence that he did not prepare the letter which purports to bear his name. Having regard to his position in the company, I find it unlikely that he would have been involved with it. He appeared to have a rather casual attitude to payments made by the company. He said in evidence "my understanding was that if we have the money we can sort it out later...we will take out what money we wanted and left the treatment of it in limbo". This document, coming to light well after the audit, and of no authenticity whatever, in my view, can not characterise the 2 sums paid to the applicant.

30. For reasons which I have stated, the $40,000 can not be treated as being in reduction of the applicant's loan account. In my view it is a dividend and it is taxable in the applicant's hands. I have also concluded that the claim for reimbursement for living away from home expenses amounting to $20,000 is based on fabrications.

31. The claim relates partly to work done on a contract with a council in the Newcastle area from July 1986 to February 1987. The applicant claimed a sum of $60 per night for motel expenses on 3 nights per week and a meal allowance of $40 per day 3 times per week throughout the whole of this period. Mr I was on site the whole time. He gave evidence that, so far as he was aware, the applicant did not stay overnight on any occasion. He simply visited the site on 2 or 3 occasions. This evidence was borne out by further evidence given by Mr H who was the site foreman and whose job it was to supervise all persons on the site. His evidence was that the applicant came to the site only 4 or 5 times. To the best of his recollection, the applicant would drive up from Sydney for the day to inspect the progress of the project and then return to Sydney. Evidence was also given by Mr R, an operator who worked at the site. He lived at Newcastle during the course of the contract in a small flat with a number of other workers including Mr I. His evidence was that the applicant was on site only on 2 or 3 occasions, but Mr I was there everyday from 7 am to 3-30 pm. Documentary evidence produced in response to a summons supported the fact that diary entries recording the names of visitors did not include the name of the applicant.

32. The remainder of the claim for living away from home allowance related a project being carried out at Campbelltown for which the applicant claimed the cost of motel expenses of $40 per night for 4 nights per week for the whole period between September 1984 and May 1985 and a meal allowance of $20 per day. Similar evidence indicated that the applicant treated the Campbelltown job as a city job and travelled to the site when necessary during the course of a day.

33. I accept this evidence in preference to the applicant's evidence. It was tested in cross-examination and was not shaken. In my view, the sum of $20,000 must fall within the same category as the remaining amount in the cheque for $60,000 and must be treated as a dividend.

Item $50,000

34. In 1989 the applicant and Mr I had, to use his phrase, come to the parting of the ways. Both wanted to take their money out of the company. While the applicant was in hospital, Mr I had arranged for a document to be drawn up in February in which the applicant was to sell his one half interest in the equipment owned by the company to a company associated with Mr I for $170,000. Although drawn up by a solicitor, the document contains the odd recital that : "The vendor is a 50 per cent shareholder in the undertaking known as (company) and by virtue thereof has the ownership, right, title and interest to one undivided half share of the equipment referred to in schedule A hereto". From this recital, it proceeded to provide for the sale by the applicant vendor of his right title and interest in this equipment to Mr I's company. The consideration was to be paid as to $50,000 on the signing of the agreement and as to $120,000 on or before the expiration of 6 weeks from the date of the agreement. It was provided that if completion did not take place within that time, then the purchaser was to pay to the vendor interest at the rate of 15 per cent per annum. This figure was crossed out by the applicant and altered to 25 per cent per annum. As events transpired, the moneys were not paid on time, and Supreme Court proceedings were instituted by the applicant. He formally claimed payment of the balance of purchase money together with interest. The proceedings were ultimately settled.

35. Settlement was evidenced by another deed dated 24 October 1989 made between the applicant and Mr I and 2 companies. It purported to vary the February deed by extending the time for payment, by increasing the amount to be paid by $6,000 and by reconstructing the legalities of the arrangement. Clause 3 provided that the company was now to be the vendor and was to sell the equipment to the applicant and Mr I for $340,000. Clause 5 of the deed provided that the applicant and Mr I were to onsell their interest to Mr I's company and that if Mr I's company met its obligations, then the vendor company would release each of the applicant and Mr I from their respective obligations to pay to the vendor company the consideration of $170,000 each. The effect of this was to vest ownership of the equipment in Mr I's company but to arrange for payment to the 2 shareholders directly. The applicant received the deposit of $50,000 in February, which he appropriated to his own purposes. In October he received a further $126,000 which he also treated as his own property.

36. The equipment at all times belonged to the company and at all times the company was entitled to receive the purchase price. The diversion of the purchase price directly to the shareholders was the provision of a benefit to shareholders from funds which would otherwise have been surplus funds in the company's hands. The provision in the second deed releasing the intermediate purchasers from their obligation to pay for the equipment is, in my view, a sham. The true analysis of the transaction is that once again, moneys have been paid to the applicant by the company which should be treated as dividends. Accordingly this item of $50,000 will be taxable in the applicant's hands.

Item $23,200

37. The first component of this sum is said to be loans of $3,000 and $4,000 respectively paid to the applicant by the same Mr G referred to in paragraph 23 of these reasons. For reasons which I have already given, I do not accept that the applicant has discharged the onus which he bears of proving that these moneys were in fact loans. They remained unexplained.

38. The second component is the sum of $10,000 paid to the applicant on 13 April 1989. He said that it was paid to him as a loan by his father-in-law, Mr M. This was evidenced by a letter produced by the applicant, bearing date 14 December 1991. Almost certainly it was drafted by him. It certifies that Mr M paid the sum of $10,000 to the applicant on or about 13 April 1989 and that a further sum of $10,000 was lent to him on or about July 1990. The second sum is said not to have yet been repaid. As to the first sum, the applicant said that when he was buying the Bondi property he needed some additional funds to complete the purchase. His father-in-law owned 2 units in the building and offered to help him out. The applicant said that it was therefore arranged that his father-in-law would move into the premises the applicant was buying and cut out repayment of the loan by rent-free accommodation at the rate of $200 per week. Mr M was not called to give evidence. The applicant said that because of matrimonial difficulties, it was not expedient to approach his father-in-law. Without further corroborative detail, and without having the opportunity of having the ex post facto written record tested in cross-examination, I am not prepared to accept the version of events set out in that document.

39. The third component is a sum of $5,200 paid to the applicant on 3 June 1989 by his brother-in-law. He said in evidence that early in 1988 his brother-in-law came to Sydney looking for work and had no place to stay. The applicant invited him to stay with him. He was unable to pay anything at first but when he obtained work he started to make payments. Eventually it was agreed that he would pay the applicant a lump sum of $5,200 representing reimbursement of expenses of $100 per week for the whole year. The applicant's brother-in-law was said to be out of Australia and was not available to give evidence. For reasons that I have indicated, I am not prepared to accept the applicant's version of events unless it is corroborated by independent evidence. I therefore regard the sum of $5,200 as still an unexplained receipt.

Item $4,500

40. This is the second amount said to have been paid by Mr B by way of repayment of a loan. For reasons which I have already given, I do not accept that the applicant has discharged the onus in relation to this item.

Year ended 30 June 1990

Item $17,500

41. The applicant could offer no explanation at all for this amount which was credited to his account. Accordingly, he has not discharged the onus in relation to that item.

Item $8,000

42. This is another loan said to have been made by Mr G. For reasons I have already given, I do not accept that characterisation of the money.

Item $120,000

43. This was the balance paid in October 1989 after the Supreme Court proceedings had been settled and represents the balance of the value of the company's equipment. For reasons I have already given, I consider that this amount is taxable in the applicant's hands.

Item $6,000

44. This amount represents the difference between the purchase price shown in the February contract for the equipment and the agreed sum in the October deed. The applicant said that the terms were changed to add $6,000 to the amount of his claim so that his legal costs could be paid. Mr I, the adversary in the Supreme Court proceedings, thought at first that the amount represented interest on the purchase price but also later thought that there might have been some component for legal expenses. He could not really recall what was finally agreed. Having regard to the fact that the claim in the Supreme Court was for a principal sum and for interest, and having regard to the applicant's specific evidence that he was annoyed that Mr I's company had had the use of the equipment between February and October without payment, I consider it more likely than not that the purchase price was increased by $6,000 to reimburse the applicant for the loss of both the equipment and his money over that period of 8 months. Accordingly I hold that the amount concerned was interest and is taxable in the applicant's hands.

Item $19,200

45. The applicant was paid salary of $1,600 per month until the company ceased trading in January 1989. This amount continued after that date to be paid regularly to his bank account. The sum of $19,200 represents 12 payments of $1600 to the applicant's bank account at regular monthly intervals. The existence of the account was not revealed until later in the audit. The applicant said that it should not be regarded as salary because the company was not trading and he was not carrying out any services on behalf of the company. He said it was merely overlooked and attention should have been paid to altering the banker's order. Nonetheless he received the money regularly and did not return it. There is no suggestion that he considered himself under any obligation to return it. In any event, the evidence of Mr I, which I am prepared to accept in preference to that of the applicant, was that there was a residual amount of activity being carried on by the company, particularly in relation to a hard rock claim in a contract in North Sydney. The applicant did not offer any alternative suggestion for characterising the money, except to say that in his opinion the moneys could not be regarded as salary. However viewed, they are periodical payments in the nature of income and must be regarded as taxable in the applicant's hands.

46. This completes a review of the specific items which were in dispute by the time the matter was heard in the Tribunal. There remains the question of penalties.

47. Additional tax was imposed at the flat culpability rate of 80 per cent. In forming an opinion as to the appropriate level of additional tax, the respondent applied his guidelines set out in IT 2517. Paragraph 41 provides for a culpability rate of 45 per cent where there has been deliberate evasion without aggravating factors. In my view it can not be seriously contested that there was in fact deliberate evasion in this case. There were also aggravating factors. The applicant failed to answer questions honestly during the audit and deliberately set out to mislead the auditor. Examples of this were given at the commencement of these reasons. They include the fact that ownership of properties and of bank accounts were not disclosed, accounting records were falsified, misleading versions of events were given and after the audit, documentary evidence was fabricated. For these aggravating factors a further culpability rate of 25 per cent has been added.

48. In addition, a further 10 per cent has been added because the applicant failed to cooperate during the audit. It lasted from October 1990 until January 1992. During that time, the applicant failed to attend meetings on numerous occasions. He was requested on numerous occasions to produce documents of a specific nature which did not come to light until after the audit was completed. Records of telephone calls of aborted meetings, of requests for extensions of time, and of failures to respond, were tendered as Exhibit 7. All of these, in my view, justify the loading of 10 per cent to the culpability factor, making a total culpability rate of 80 per cent.

49. The applicant's deliberate evasion was compounded by his further steps to obscure the situation and to mislead the investigators. His failure to cooperate was aggravated because the many opportunities he had to make a full disclosure were not availed of. In addition, throughout these proceedings, he persisted in versions of events, such as the fact that he incurred the cost of motel accommodation and expenses for meals during the contracts at Newcastle and Campbelltown, which were palpably false. His knowledge of accounting and background in finance make these lapses even more blameworthy. In my view the additional tax both as to its culpability component and as to the per annum component are entirely justified.

50. Because of negotiations since the objection decision, an affirmation of those decisions would now no longer reflect the agreement of the parties and my findings. The decisions are therefore set aside and the matters are remitted to the respondent with the direction that the assessments be amended in accordance with agreed mutual concessions and in accordance with these reasons.
