Bouvet and Commissioner of Taxation (Practice and procedure) [2026] ARTA 1075 (18 June 2026)

Last Updated: 18 June 2026

Bouvet and Commissioner of Taxation (Practice and procedure) [2026] ARTA 1075 (18 June 2026)

Applicant: Robert Bouvet

Respondent: Commissioner of Taxation

Tribunal Number: 2025/7227

Tribunal: General Member C Willis

Place: Melbourne

Date: 18 June 2026

Decision: The Tribunal finds that there is no reviewable decision for the purposes of the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth) (‘the ART Act’) arising from the Respondent’s decision to disallow a PAYG withholding credit of $19.6 million to the Applicant in the year of income ended 30 June 2024, such that the Tribunal dismisses the application relating to such decision to disallow under section 97 of the ART Act, and further dismisses the application for review of the Objection Decision dated 19 November 2025 under section 101 of the ART Act.

General Member C Willis

Catchwords

TAXATION - PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE – applicant contends that he derived significant royalty income and is entitled to withholding tax credits – same correspondence from respondent contained objection decision relating to an assessment and decision to disallow withholding tax credit - whether refusal to allow withholding tax credit gives rise to a reviewable decision - whether grounds for dismissal of application –– whether Tribunal is satisfied that application has no reasonable prospects of success – whether application is frivolous, vexatious, misconceived or lacking in substance - whether there is a basis for applicant to argue he derived royalty income – no reviewable decision relating to refusal to allow withholding tax credit – tribunal satisfied that application for review of objection decision has no reasonable prospects of success – tribunal satisfied that application for review of objection decision is frivolous, vexatious, misconceived or lacking in substance - application dismissed

Statement of Reasons

BACKGROUND

Introduction

  1. This matter involves a claim by Mr Bouvet (‘the Applicant’) that he is entitled to be paid an amount arising from $19.6 million of tax which he says has been withheld by the Commissioner of Taxation (‘the Respondent’) in relation to royalties he says that he derived. Unusually, the Applicant is disputing an objection decision relating to an amended assessment issued by the Respondent which had the effect of reducing his assessable income by a substantial amount and removing his liability to pay tax. The Applicant is also seeking a refund of tax arising from withholding credits, and disputes the Respondent’s advice that he does not have a right to object on this issue.
  2. The Respondent submits that the Applicant does not have a right to object under Part IVC of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (‘the TAA 1953’) to the Respondent’s decision to disallow a PAYG withholding credit. The Respondent also submits that the Tribunal should dismiss the Applicant’s application, at least insofar as it relates to his objection against the removal of assessable amounts from his taxable income, under subsection 101(1) of the ART Act on the basis that the application is frivolous, vexatious, misconceived or lacking in substance and has no reasonable prospects of success.

Factual contentions and key events

  1. The Applicant says that he is an owner of the copyright in the movie ‘Crocodile Dundee’ as he was one of the individuals who created the original story of that movie. He says that a person publicly credited with creating the screenplay, Mr Ken Shadie, was in fact him and that ‘Ken Shadie’ was a pseudonym adopted by him.[1] On that basis he claims to be entitled to substantial royalties arising from the film. He said that an agreement was entered into in the late 1980s in relation to his entitlement to such royalties, but he has never been paid any royalty amounts. He believes that at some point one or more trust entities[2] had been holding the amounts owing to him.
  2. In the early 2000s the Applicant commenced various proceedings in the Federal Court which were underpinned by his claims to ownership of copyright in relation to Crocodile Dundee and therefore entitlement to royalties.[3] He was not successful in any of these cases.
  3. The Respondent is an agency which participated in Project Wickenby, a task force that was established some years ago to address tax avoidance through the use of offshore tax havens. The Applicant had become aware that persons associated with the production of Crocodile Dundee, or who had provided advice to individuals associated with that movie, had been subject to investigation as part of Project Wickenby and that some of these persons had been required to pay amounts to the Respondent. The Applicant formed the view that the trust entity which was supposed to have held royalty amounts owing to him had been committing tax fraud involving the use of overseas bank accounts. The Applicant believes that the Respondent had recovered amounts of tax from those persons or that entity, and that the Respondent held those amounts on trust for him in relation to the royalties he was owed.[4]
  4. When the Applicant lodged his income tax return for the year ended 30 June 2024, he included an amount of $20.4 million as assessable income which he said represented the amount of royalties owing to or held on trust for him. This was in addition to an amount of $26,728 of government age pension income and a small amount of bank interest.
  5. The Respondent initially issued a notice of assessment to the Applicant in October 2024 with an amount of tax payable of approximately $9.5 million. On 21 October 2024 the Applicant completed an objection form in which he said that he objected to the assessment on grounds which included the Respondent’s online forms being controlled by artificial intelligence (AI) and other complaints relating to algorithms and computer programming which he said affected his income tax return. He also queried the assessment on the basis that the delegate of the Respondent whose name appeared on the assessment was not listed as an acting Deputy Commissioner on the Respondent’s organisational chart.[5]
  6. The objection form was incomplete and missing pages when submitted to the Respondent. In November 2024 the Respondent contacted the Applicant to advise that this form was not valid as an objection in that state, however if the Applicant clarified his grounds of objection and provided the missing sections of the form the Respondent would be able to accept it.[6] Although the Applicant sent further correspondence, the Respondent advised him in January 2025 that he still had not stated fully and in detail his grounds of objection, and therefore he had not lodged a valid objection.[7]
  7. Nevertheless, the Respondent reviewed the Applicant’s assessment for the 2024 year and on 31 January 2025 issued him with an amended assessment which removed the amount of $20.4 million of assessable income. The Applicant therefore no longer owed any amount of tax.[8]
  8. The Applicant was not happy with this outcome. He said that the amended assessment reflected an artificial reduction of his income and an illegal reduction in tax payable. He lodged an objection form dated 5 September 2025.[9] This objection was accompanied by paperwork in which the Applicant suggested that tax of $19.6 million had been withheld on royalties to which he was entitled, and he should be paid or credited with that amount. The Applicant also suggested that he had been taxed twice, the first being in relation to tax he believes was incorporated into amounts recovered by the Respondent as part of Project Wickenby, and the second time through the amounts not being deposited into his bank account.
  9. Between September 2025 and November 2025 the Applicant sent a large volume of material to the Respondent. This included copies of his bank statements, correspondence he sent to the Inspector General of Taxation and Ombudsman, an application form he sent to the trust entity to open an account with them, a betting slip issued by the TAB, extracts from magazines and newspapers, pages from telephone books, photographs of himself with people said to be associated with the film or who were said to have inspired scenes in the film, correspondence with such people and some video clips including footage of the former Commissioner of Taxation talking about Project Wickenby. The Applicant also provided copies of documents which appear to have been lodged in the mid-1980s with the then corporate regulator by a company and individuals involved with the production of Crocodile Dundee, and statutory declarations from various individuals dated from the 1990s attesting to certain events which the Applicant said related to the inception of the movie’s storyline.[10]
  10. The Applicant has made certain allegations and claims about his belief that judges, politicians, executives of certain organisations and officers of the Respondent have been involved in a strategy to deny him the royalties he was due.[11] He says that he has been subjected to racial discrimination and death threats.
  11. The Respondent issued an objection decision dated 19 November 2025 (‘the Objection Decision’) in which it determined that the amount of $20.4 million should not be included in the Applicant’s assessable income on the basis that he had not provided conclusive evidence that he received the purported royalties. The Respondent also said that although the Applicant did not have a right to object to the Respondent’s decision to disallow a credit for PAYG withholding on the purported royalties, the Respondent had undertaken a separate informal review of that decision. The Respondent determined that the Applicant had not provided supporting information which confirmed that he had amounts withheld from royalty income for the 2024 year. Therefore, there was no evidence of a PAYG withholding event and the amount of $19.6 million would not be included in his assessment for the 2024 year.[12]
  12. On 11 December 2025 the Applicant lodged an application with the Tribunal for review of the Objection Decision. He said that the Objection Decision was wrong because information supplied had not been taken into account and there was evidence that key material facts which had been substantiated by a voluminous amount of exhibits had been discarded by the case officer of the Respondent. The Tribunal understood the Applicant to be seeking review of the content of the Objection Decision letter, which included an explanation of the Respondent’s ‘informal review’ decision relating to the PAYG withholding.
  13. The application was listed with a Tribunal Registrar for a case management directions hearing. After a directions hearing on 29 April 2026, the application was referred for hearing by a Member as to whether the Tribunal had jurisdiction to hear this matter and whether the Tribunal should dismiss the application.
  14. The matter was listed for an interlocutory hearing on 4 June 2026 to determine whether the Tribunal had jurisdiction to conduct a review of all of the matters in dispute between the parties, and whether the application should be dismissed. The Tribunal asked the Respondent to provide brief (no more than 2 page) submissions as to why it believed the application should be dismissed. The Tribunal asked the Applicant to then provide a brief (2 page) response to that request for dismissal.
  15. The Respondent referred the Tribunal to various decisions of the Federal Court in which the Applicant had previously made claims that he was entitled to royalties from Crocodile Dundee on the basis of having a copyright interest. The Respondent was not successful in these cases, with his applications being dismissed. The Respondent said that the Applicant is not Ken Shadie, referring the Tribunal to an affidavit filed by Mr Shadie with the Federal Court in one of these cases which exhibited a copy of Mr Shadie’s birth certificate and in which Mr Shadie rejected the claim that he and the Applicant were the same person. The Respondent also provided the Tribunal with information about Mr Shadie being awarded an Order of Australia Medal in 2015 and a link to Mr Shadie’s obituary in the Sydney Morning Herald following his death in 2020 which indicated that the Applicant was not Mr Shadie.
  16. The Respondent also said that there is no evidence that the Applicant derived or was entitled to receive royalties from the film Crocodile Dundee, or any royalty income in the amount of $20.4 million in the 2024 year. The Respondent said that it had relied on information from Westpac and the Commonwealth Service Delivery Agency to determine that the Applicant’s assessable income for that year comprised bank interest and the age pension. The Respondent also advised the Tribunal that the Applicant had lodged an income tax return for the 2025 year in which he also claimed to have derived $20.4 million of royalty income.
  17. The Applicant’s response was entitled ‘The OBVIOUS reasons why the Applicant’s Application of Case 2025/7227 SHOULD NOT be dismissed’ and comprised a two page overview with 8 attachments. The Tribunal had some difficulty in following the Applicant’s response, which was presented in a variety of colours and fonts and in incomplete sentences. However, it appeared to be structured to follow the format of the ‘tax questionnaire’ which the Tribunal asks parties to tax disputes to complete at the commencement of an application. The Tribunal understood the Applicant to contend that he did derive $20.4 million of royalty income in the 2024 year, that he is entitled to withholding tax credits in the amount of $19.6 million and that his application to the Tribunal should not be dismissed.
  18. The Applicant recited or set out in part a number of provisions from income tax legislation.[13] Other than confirming the non-controversial proposition that royalty amounts are generally income, the Tribunal could not discern any contentions as to whether the Applicant had evidence of having derived or received the $20.4 million of royalties, in the 2024 year or in any other year. He said that tax withheld from royalty payments was recovered and repatriated to Australia as lump sums during the course of Project Wickenby. He raised matters of complaint as to how his attempts to object had been dealt with by particular officers or executives of the Respondent.
  19. The Tribunal cannot provide advice to a party to a review. However, on the basis that the Tribunal had difficulty in following the written contentions or submissions of the Applicant, the Tribunal believed it was appropriate for the Tribunal to outline in more detail the issues and questions it believed were relevant for the Applicant to address, and to invite the Applicant to speak to these matters at the interlocutory hearing, if he wished to do so.
  20. At the interlocutory hearing the Tribunal invited the Applicant to clarify the basis upon which he believed he had derived the royalty income and become entitled to the withholding tax credits. The Applicant referred the Tribunal to section 219 of the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth) which is entitled ‘Reproduction of work upon payment of royalties’ which he contends created his entitlement to royalties as an owner of copyright in a work associated with the film Crocodile Dundee. He said that publicly available information indicates that revenue of around $800 million was earned from that movie. He suggested a royalty rate of 10% as appropriate, such that royalties of $80 million arose from the movie. His position is that he is a co-owner of the copyright with Mr Paul Hogan, such that his half share of royalties would be $40 million.[14] He said that the rate of tax at the relevant time was 49%, such that tax of $19.6 million would have been payable on such royalty income, with the balance of $20.4 million being payable to him. Tax payable by him on that $20.4 million would be around $9.6 million. After application of the credit for $19.6 million tax withheld, the Applicant says he would therefore be entitled to be paid a refund of tax of approximately $10 million.
  21. The Applicant is not apparently claiming that he has actually received any such amount. When the Tribunal queried how he believed he had derived the amount in the 2024 year (or any income year) he said that when the Respondent recovered amounts for tax related liabilities from entities he believed were holding royalty amounts on trust for him, the Respondent thereafter took over the role and responsibility of the previous trustee(s) to pay him those amounts. That is, he believe that the Respondent held amounts on trust for the Applicant in relation to unpaid royalties. The Tribunal explained the concept of amounts of tax paid to the Commissioner of Taxation forming part of the consolidated revenue fund and asked the Applicant if he had any submissions he wished to put to the Tribunal as to why he might be entitled to a portion of amounts of tax recovered by the Respondent within Project Wickenby. The Applicant told the Tribunal that he did not believe there was any law which could change material facts and the reality was that he was entitled to the amount.
  22. The Applicant did not directly address the issue of whether there was a reviewable objection decision for the Tribunal in relation to the PAYG withholding credit of $19.6 million or whether there was evidence of tax having been withheld from a royalty amount. He referred to the calculation of that amount based on a tax rate of 49% and suggested that tax must have been or should have been withheld.

DOES THE TRIBUNAL HAVE JURISDICTION IN RELATION TO THE PAYGW ISSUE?

  1. A person whose interests are affected by a ‘reviewable decision’ may apply to the Tribunal for a review of the decision: section 17 of the ART Act. Not all decisions of a government agency or department can be reviewed by the Tribunal. A decision is a ‘reviewable decision’ if an Act or a legislative instrument provides for an application to be made to the Tribunal for review of that decision: subsection 12(1).
  2. In the context of taxation matters and decisions of the Respondent, subsection 14ZZA(1) of the TAA 1953 says that the ART Act applies in relation to the review of ‘reviewable objection decisions’ and ‘extension of time refusal decisions’, as well as ‘ART extension applications.’
  3. The circumstances of the present case do not involve any extension refusal or application. Therefore, the Tribunal can only conduct a review to the extent that the Applicant has received a ‘reviewable objection decision’ which is defined in section 14ZQ of the TAA 1953 to mean ‘an objection decision that is not an ineligible income tax remission decision.’ Section 14ZY defines an ‘objection decision’ by reference to decisions that the Respondent can or must make after receiving a taxation objection.[15] A taxation objection can only be made if a provision of an Act or a legislative instrument provides that a person who is dissatisfied with an assessment, determination, notice or decision, or with a failure to make a private ruling, may object: section 14ZL.
  4. The Respondent does not dispute that the Objection Decision it issued to the Applicant on 19 November 2025 is an ‘objection decision’ and a ‘reviewable objection decision’ for the purposes of review by the Tribunal. The Tribunal agrees that this Objection Decision is a ‘reviewable objection decision’ for the purposes of the ART Act.
  5. However, not all of the matters set out in the correspondence from the Respondent to the Applicant on 19 November 2025 are part of the Objection Decision. The Respondent’s letter of 19 November 2025 explains under the heading ‘Informal review of PAYG withholding credits’ that the conclusion it had reached in relation to the withholding credit of $19.6 million (as claimed by the Applicant on royalty income) could not be the subject of an objection by the Applicant. As the Respondent could not conduct an objection process, it undertook a further review in accordance with its internal policy, which affirmed its previous conclusion. The Respondent advised the Applicant that he could not seek Tribunal review of its conclusion on PAYG withholding.
  6. Before the Tribunal the Respondent confirmed its position that the Applicant did not have the right to object to the decision to disallow the PAYG withholding credit for the purposes of Part IVC of the TAA 1953. The Respondent said this is because such a credit is not part of an income tax assessment, even where details of the credit may appear on a notice of assessment, and is only considered after the assessment of tax has been made.[16]
  7. As noted above, the Applicant did not expressly address this issue in his written submissions and other materials filed with the Tribunal, nor did he provide oral submissions during the interlocutory hearing. He reiterated his view as to an amount of $19.6 million of tax being correct based on a rate of 49% applied to a gross royalty amount of $40 million.
  8. The Tribunal has doubts as to whether there is evidence of a withholding of tax such that a withholding credit arises. However, the Tribunal agrees that a decision by the Respondent to deny a credit for a PAYG withholding credit cannot be the subject of an objection by the Applicant and therefore there is no reviewable decision in relation to this matter.
  9. The Tribunal considered this issue in CJYB and Commissioner of Taxation where it was observed that:[17]

64. These provisions indicate that PAYG and other withholding payments, as defined for this purpose in s 10-5(1) of Schedule 1, are credited against the tax assessed as payable rather than forming part of the ascertainment of the tax payable.

65. It is perhaps for these reasons that the applicant was unable to cite a single instance in which it had been held that the Tribunal has jurisdiction to review a decision to disallow claimed PAYG credits or it had purported to exercise such jurisdiction.

  1. The Tribunal appreciates that the fact that the correspondence from the Respondent to the Applicant dated 19 November 2025 included both an objection decision and the outcome of a separate informal review process relating to the PAYG withholding credit might have caused some confusion. However, the Tribunal believes that the position communicated by the Respondent to the Applicant was correct. That is, although the Objection Decision relating to the non-inclusion of royalty income of $20.4 million was a decision that could be reviewed by the Tribunal, its ‘decision’ relating to the PAYG withholding credit was not.
  2. As mentioned, section 14ZL of the TAA 1953 says that a taxation objection can only be made if a provision of an Act or a legislative instrument says that a person dissatisfied with the relevant assessment or decision may object. In the absence of a right to object, there is no objection decision and there can be no reviewable objection decision for the purposes of the ART Act. The Tribunal must dismiss an application made for the review of a decision if the Tribunal is satisfied that the decision is not reviewable by the Tribunal: section 97 of the ART Act. In circumstances where the Applicant has applied for review of the Respondent’s decision to disallow PAYG withholding credits, the Tribunal must dismiss that application. Accordingly, the Tribunal has no ability to review the Applicant’s claim for entitlement to a tax credit of $19.6 million.

SHOULD THE TRIBUNAL DISMISS THE APPLICATION FOR REVIEW OF THE OBJECTION DECISION?

  1. The Respondent acknowledges that the question of whether the amount of $20.4 million should be included in the assessable income of the Applicant for the 2024 year was the subject of an objection decision which is reviewable by the Tribunal, being the Objection Decision dated 19 November 2025. However, the Respondent submits that the Tribunal should also dismiss the application relating to the Objection Decision.
  2. Subsection 101(1) of the ART Act provides that:

(1) The Tribunal may, at any time, dismiss an application made to the Tribunal if the Tribunal is satisfied that the application:

(a) is frivolous, vexatious, misconceived or lacking in substance; or

(b) has no reasonable prospects of success; or

(c) is otherwise an abuse of the process of the Tribunal.

  1. The Federal Court has said that in relation to the power of the Tribunal to dismiss an application without proceeding to a final review:
    (a) This power must be exercised ‘sparingly’ and as a ‘last resort.’ The Tribunal should consider whether dismissal is the correct approach, or whether another course of action might be more appropriate.[18]

    (b) Procedural fairness must be afforded to the applicant, which extends to giving the applicant the opportunity to make submissions as to why the matter should not be dismissed.[19]

  2. The Tribunal has previously said that:
    (a) If proceedings have ‘no reasonable prospect at all of success’ they should be dismissed on the basis that it would be futile for them to continue.[20]

    (b) For there to be no ‘real’ prospect of success of a given application, there must be ‘a high degree of certainty about the ultimate outcome of the proceeding’ if it were to proceed to hearing in the usual way. This would involve an assessment of the merits of an application to determine whether any reasonable contention or line of argument can be sustained.[21]

    (c) In considering whether an application should be dismissed under section 101, the exercise of the Tribunal’s discretionary power should take account of the Tribunal’s objective as set out in section 9 of the ART Act and the Tribunal’s obligation to give each party to a proceeding a reasonable opportunity to present their case under section 55. Proceeding with an application once the Tribunal is aware that it lacks substance is counterproductive to the Tribunal’s objective. Where no practical or legitimate purpose can be achieved from determining the substantive application, it suggests that the application is vexatious or lacking in substance.[22]

    (d) For the purposes of the Tribunal, the word ‘frivolous’ (as used in the phrase ‘frivolous, vexatious...’) means ‘the application is futile or pointless, most obviously because the Tribunal is not able to assist the applicant in a meaningful way’ including where the Tribunal ‘is unable to make any order that will have a practical effect’ with the applicant’s position not being able to be improved if the matter proceeded to a hearing.[23]

  3. The High Court has also indicated that once it appears that there is a real question (whether of fact or law) to be determined and the rights of the parties depend upon it, then a proceeding should not be dismissed as frivolous and vexatious or an abuse of process.[24]
  4. The Respondent outlined its contentions as to why the application should be dismissed as follows:
    (a) There can be no favourable outcome to the Applicant. If the Tribunal were to find that he had derived $20.4 million of royalty income he would have a tax liability of $9 million which he would be unable to pay. The Respondent would be unable to enforce a tax liability on income that has not been derived.

    (b) Although the Respondent is not putting forward a view on the Applicant’s entitlement to royalties relating to Crocodile Dundee, the Respondent notes that the Applicant has pursued his claim to an entitlement to such royalties in the Federal Court, without success. In one case the Federal Court dismissed his application as vexatious or frivolous in the absence of any reasonable cause of action. The Respondent does not believe the Tribunal (via this application) is the correct forum for the Applicant to press his claim that he is entitled to such royalties. Nor does the Respondent believe that it is the correct party for the Applicant to pursue for recovery of royalties he believes are owing to him. Even if the Tribunal were to find that the Applicant derived $20.4 million of royalty income, it would not address his concern that he is owed unpaid royalties.

    (c) Despite contending that he derived the $20.4 million of royalty income in the 2024 year, the Applicant has lodged his income tax return for the 2025 year on the basis that he derived the $20.4 million of royalty income, and was entitled to the $19.6 million credit, in the 2025 year.

    (d) To proceed with the application in such circumstances would be inconsistent with the Tribunal’s objective as it would involve additional formality and expense.

  5. In response the Applicant said:
    (a) The Respondent had misconceived the facts of this case, including the true identity of Mr Shadie.[25]

    (b) The Federal Court judges who heard the various matters in 2001 had not been aware of certain matters and their decisions had been wrong. He believed that the judges had behaved in a way that was inconsistent with the Federal Court rules in some instances.

    (c) He had concerns about the way his matters had been handled by the Respondent. He had provided a significant amount of material with the Respondent which supported his claims and contentions. He had a concern that officers of the Respondent may have destroyed or interfered with some of this material. He said that one of the Respondent’s officers had certified his objection as valid, but this had been overturned by another officer.

    (d) The Respondent had allowed him a credit for a small amount of tax withheld from his bank interest but when it came to a larger amount, refused to do so.

    (e) It would be consistent with the Tribunal’s objective in section 9 of the ART Act for the application not to be dismissed, because that would be fair and just and promote transparency.[26]

  6. The Applicant also told the Tribunal that he believed the birth certificate for Mr Shadie exhibited in the affidavit filed with the Federal Court was not genuine. He had undertaken research in electoral rolls and telephone books which raised doubts about the authenticity of that evidence. He raised a query about the Deputy Commissioner whose name appeared on his notice of assessment because he could not find that person’s name on the Respondent’s online organisational chart when he checked in 2024.
  7. The Tribunal formed the impression that the Applicant genuinely believes that he is Ken Shadie, that he had a significant role in the concept for Crocodile Dundee and that he is owed royalties. These issues have clearly been the source of some distress to him over a period of decades and the Tribunal does not believe that the Applicant has deliberately sought to mislead the Tribunal.[27]
  8. However, if this application were to proceed the substantive issue relates to the derivation of a particular amount of royalty income by the Applicant in a particular year of income, rather than a broader examination of his copyright ownership and royalty entitlements. In this respect:
    (a) The Applicant expressly acknowledges that he has not been paid or received, directly or indirectly, any royalty amounts which is why he believes such amounts are still owing to him (and why he believes the Respondent should pay him amounts in respect of his claims).

    (b) The Applicant has sought to include the same item of income in his income tax return for the 2025 income year as he did in the 2024 year. Taxable income is worked out for an income year,[28] therefore if the Applicant has subsequently said he derived the amount in the 2025 year, this is inconsistent with his claim that he derived the amount in the 2024 year, which is the subject of the Objection Decision.

    (c) Even if the Tribunal were, hypothetically, to accept that the Applicant was entitled to such royalty amounts, it could not find that the Applicant had derived such royalty income via the Respondent’s collection of tax liabilities from third parties in prior years. When amounts of tax are paid to the Respondent or tax liabilities are recovered by the Respondent, by law such amounts form part of the consolidated revenue fund. These amounts are not held on trust by the Respondent for any person. Therefore to the extent that the Respondent recovered any tax liabilities from persons associated with the movie Crocodile Dundee, no part of this amount could be held for or on behalf of the Applicant as a matter of law. Further, from a timing perspective no reason was given by the Applicant as to why amounts received by the Respondent pursuant to Project Wickenby many years ago could be attributable to the Applicant’s taxable income for the 2024 year.

  9. Although the purpose of this interlocutory proceeding is not to conduct a full merits hearing, the Tribunal is satisfied on the basis of these matters that this application has no reasonable prospects of success, from both a factual and legal perspective.
  10. Having regard to the Tribunal’s objective under section 9 of the ART Act, further progression of this application would be inconsistent with the Tribunal’s obligation to ensure resolution in a timely manner with as little expense as appropriate and would not promote public trust and confidence in the Tribunal accordingly. The Tribunal must have regard to the resources to be allocated not only by the Applicant, but by the Respondent and by the Tribunal.
  11. The Tribunal has already found that the Applicant did not have a right to object against the Respondent’s refusal to give him a withholding tax credit, and therefore the Tribunal has no jurisdiction to review that refusal. However, the Tribunal observes that a natural consequence of finding that the Applicant did not derive the $20.4 million of royalty income in the 2024 year is that no withholding tax credits could arise in relation to the Applicant in relation to such income. The Tribunal does not believe that the Applicant will be denied fairness and justice in circumstances where a hypothetical ‘favourable’ outcome for the Applicant would only result in a significant debt to him.
  12. The Tribunal believes it is appropriate for the application to be dismissed under paragraph 101(1)(b).
  13. The Tribunal is also satisfied that the application is frivolous, vexatious, misconceived or lacking in substance such that it would appropriately be dismissed under paragraph 101(1)(a). Under the circumstance where the only ‘successful’ outcome for the Applicant would result in him becoming subject to a substantial tax debt which he would be unable to pay, the Tribunal believes the application is ‘futile or pointless’ because it is unable to assist the Applicant in a meaningful way.
  14. The Applicant told the Tribunal that he has been fighting for his royalty entitlements since the mid-1980s. The Tribunal cannot provide any determination of his royalty entitlements, whether contractual or under the Copyright Act 1968 in the context of a review of the Objection Decision.

CONCLUSION

  1. The Tribunal has therefore concluded that:
    (a) The Respondent’s decision, as set out in its letter of 19 November 2025, to disallow PAYG withholding credits to the Applicant is not a reviewable decision for the purposes of the ART Act and therefore the Tribunal must dismiss an application for review of that decision under section 97 of the ART Act.

    (b) The Tribunal is satisfied that the Applicant’s application for review of the Objection decision:

    (i) Has no reasonable prospects of success, and

    (ii) Is frivolous, vexatious, misconceived or lacking in substance,

    such that the application should be dismissed under section 101 of the ART Act.
