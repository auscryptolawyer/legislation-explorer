CATCHWORDS

TAXATION : Allowable Deduction : prepaid management expenses - sale of partnership interest



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 : s51(1)



FC of T v Energy Resources of Australia Ltd 96 ATC 4536

FCT v Brand 95 ATC 4633

Fletcher and Ors v FCT 91 ATC 4950

REASONS FOR DECISION

3 October 1997 Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Mr J D Horrigan (Member)

Dr E K Christie (Member)



  1. The applicants are the partners in a firm carrying on business as solicitors. They seek review of objection decisions made by the respondent in respect of the year of income ended 30 June 1993. The respective objections are against an adjustment made by the respondent in calculating the net income of the partnership and involved the disallowance of a claimed deduction for management fees amounting to $270,000. There are also consequential objection decisions concerning assessment of additional tax before the Tribunal for review.
  2. The question at issue is whether the partnership incurred outgoings allowable as a deduction within the terms of s51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”).
  3. At the hearing the applicants case was conducted by Mr Clutterbuck of counsel and the respondent was represented by Ms Holmes of counsel. The documents lodged in the Tribunal by the respondent were put before the Tribunal as the T documents and further documents were tendered and marked as exhibits. A chartered accountant (“A”) was called to give evidence on behalf of the applicants.
  4. The applicants have been in a partnership (“the firm”) and carrying on business as solicitors in a provincial city since at least 1991. In May 1993 the applicants met with A on a number of occasions to explore the proposed disposal of the half interest in the firm held by one of the applicants.
  5. At that time the solicitors’ practice was carried on by the firm in conjunction with an associated company (“A Pty Ltd”) which acted as a service provider to the partnership and also owned real property in its own right. A Pty Ltd was controlled by the applicants and they did not wish to change that situation.
  6. It was therefore proposed that the applicants would continue to control A Pty Ltd but the company would cease to be the service provider for the solicitors’ practice and be replaced by another partnership constituted by the trustees of two family trusts.
  7. A gave the applicant's certain advice about setting in place the new arrangement. That advice caused the applicants to decide that there should be a prepayment of the management fee payable by the firm to the new partnership in accordance with the new service agreement. The fee to be paid in advance was calculated to be one half of the annual fees. Apparently the payment was limited to half of the annual fees because of the expected sale of the 50% interest in the firm by one of the applicants on 31 December 1993.
  8. Exhibit A is a copy of a minuted resolution by the applicants dated 27 May 1993 that includes the following:
“In view of the impending sale of the practice on 1 January 1994 it was agreed that management fees of $270,000 be prepaid for the year ended 30 June 1993.”


  1. Exhibit B is a copy of dissection sheets containing the accountant’s calculation of the management fee to be paid in advance. It is essentially a calculation of estimated wages and salaries together with other expected outgoings marked up to determine the management fee payable.
  2. Document T4 is a copy of a draft service agreement which is unexecuted. It indicates a proposed agreement between A Pty Ltd and the applicants. The essence of the document is that A Pty Ltd was to provide computer, administrative services, management services and other facilities to the firm. Those services are particularised in Clause 4 of the document. We note that the document did not include the provision of legal services to the firm.
  3. Although document T4 was not executed we understand and accept that the document formed the basis for the arrangement whereby A Pty Ltd provided services to the firm.
  4. No issue arises before us in relation to services provided by A Pty Ltd to the firm. The material before us indicates that for the year of income ended 30 June 1993 the firm paid a management fee of $356,686.00 to A Pty Ltd which amount was treated as assessable income derived by A Pty Ltd during that year of income (T5).
  5. The material also indicates that the firm paid the further amount of $270,000 as a management fee to the management partnership constituted by the trustees. That amount is said to have been paid in accordance with the service agreement dated 24 May 1993, between the trustees of the family trust constituting the management partnership, and the applicants constituting the firm (Exhibit C). Under this agreement the management partnership undertook to supply services particularised in Clause 4 of the agreement. Consideration for these services provided was to be paid by the firm in accordance with Clause 2 of the agreement with provision for the firm to prepay an estimate of the fee as set out in the First Schedule.
  6. It became apparent during the course of cross-examination of A by Ms Holmes that the estimated fee, which is the subject of these proceedings, had been calculated on a basis not consistent with Exhibit C. Although that management agreement is an agreement for the provision of computer, administrative and management services and other facilities the estimated fee appears to have been calculated on a basis that includes remuneration of solicitors working in the practice. We formed the view that A prevaricated on this issue in answer to Ms Holmes’ questions. The Tribunal was advised that the papers relevant to the calculation of the estimate were not available at the hearing, having been archived at A’s office.
  7. A result of further questions by the Tribunal we are satisfied, and find, that the estimate of the management fee payable includes at least $80,000 for remuneration of the employed solicitor and the partners of the firm. It follows, we think, that we cannot be satisfied that the estimated management fee was calculated in accordance with Exhibit C. Because we cannot be satisfied that the payment of $270,000 was paid in accordance with Exhibit C it follows, in our view, that it would be incorrect to characterise the payment as a payment in advance made in accordance with the service agreement (Exhibit C) in consideration for services to be provided to the firm in accordance with the agreement.
  8. Contrary to the submissions on behalf of the applicants we are not satisfied that the firm was under any contractual obligation to make the payment both as to the amount of the payment and as to the time of the payment, nor are we satisfied that the payment had the essential character of an outgoing for the provision of services.
  9. Taking into account the resolution in Exhibit A we are satisfied that the payment of $270,000.00 was effected under the guise of a management fee so as to adjust the structure of the accounts of the firm prior to the sale of the half interest in the firm. The effect achieved was that the interests of the applicants in the firm became a negative figure of $142,299 at 30 June 1993 making the firm insolvent on its balance sheet (T6). We understand that the firm continued to carry on business as solicitors notwithstanding that it was insolvent.
  10. We accept the applicants’ submission that the outgoing of $270,000 was incurred by the firm, and we so find. We further find that it was incurred on or before 30 June 1993 albeit that it was paid by a journal entry presumably raised after 30 June 1993.
  11. In FC of T v Energy Resources of Australia Ltd 96 ATC 4536 the High Court decided that a taxpayer incurs a loss or outgoing in respect of promissory notes upon incurring the liability to pay the face value of the notes and not upon payment of the notes. At page 4539 the Court said:
“If there is a presently existing liability to pay a pecuniary sum of a revenue nature, the taxpayer incurs an outgoing for the purposes of s51(1).”


  1. The applicants submit that there was an existing liability to pay the calculated fee in the year ended 30 June 1993. We think that submission is correct for reasons which follow.
  2. In FCT v Brand 95 ATC 4633 the Full Federal Court considered an appeal by the Commissioner from a decision of Nicholson J (95 ATC 4262) involving a claim for deduction under s51(1) of licence fees paid in advance. The Full Court relied in particular on the judgment of the High Court in Fletcher and Ors v FCT 91 ATC 4950. Lee and Lindgren JJ specifically relied upon the following passage at page 4957 of the High Court’s judgment:-
“ The question whether an outgoing was, for the purposes of s51(1), wholly or partly ‘incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income’ is a question of characterisation. The relationship between the outgoing and the assessable income must be such as to impart to the outgoing the character of an outgoing of the relevant kind. It has been pointed out on many occasions in the cases that an outgoing will not properly be characterised as having been incurred in gaining or producing assessable income unless it was ‘incidental and relevant to that end’. It has also been said that the test of deductibility under the first limb of s51(1) is that:

‘it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income or, if none be produced, would be expected to produce assessable income’.”


In the course of that paragraph the Court cited a number of earlier decisions but it is not necessary to set out those citations for the purposes of these reasons.

  1. The Federal Court went on to dismiss the appeal and in doing so Lee and Lindgren JJ referred to the judgment of Nicholson J at first instance at page 4647 as follows:
“His Honour accepted the Taxpayer’s evidence that he prepaid the licence fees to obtain a ‘discount’ on those fees while he had the money on hand to enable him to do so. Whether there was any commercial value in that step was not in issue and his Honour was satisfied that in paying the sum the Taxpayer had in mind a ‘commercial objective’ as distinct from moulding the transaction to maximize the tax advantage obtainable thereunder.

The Commissioner did not submit that the lump sum paid by the Taxpayer for licence fees included a voluntary prepayment by the Taxpayer of fees that were not payable under the licence Agreement until subsequent year of income and, therefore, that the outgoing the Taxpayer incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income in the year of income under 30 June 1987 was incurred only to the extent of the first annual licence fee, being the amount of the outgoing that was properly referable to that year of income. Before us, the case was argued on the basis that the whole of the sum of $15,000 either did or did not fall within the first limb of sub-s 51(1) of the Act. In particular the Commissioner did not submit that the outgoing should be treated as having been incurred only as and when receipts were derived or expected to be derived; cf Coles Myer Finance Ltd v FC of T 93 ATC 4214 at 4222; [1993] HCA 29; (1993) 176 CLR 640 at 665-666 per Mason CJ, Brennan, Dawson, Toohey and Gaudron JJ); and see RW Parsons, ‘Income Taxation: an Institution in Decay’ [1991] SydLawRw 28; (1991) 13 Syd L Rev 435 at 461.

The Commissioner also abandoned a ground of appeal which relied upon the argument that the outgoing, in whole or part, was of a capital nature (cf Case 42/95, 95 ATC 367). Accordingly we have not dealt with these issues.”


  1. In the present case the respondent submitted that the outgoing demonstrated by a book entry for prepaid management fees does not constitute an “outgoing incurred”, in part because there was not a presently existing liability to make payment and relies on the decision in Ogilvy and Mather Pty Ltd v FCT 90 ATC 4836.
  2. Put at its highest Exhibit C is an agreement for the provision of services as set out in Clause 4 of the agreement and Clause 2 allows for prepayment in relation to those services.
  3. That is, however, not the claim put forward by the applicants. The amount claimed has not been calculated in accordance with Clause 2 of the agreement.
  4. We are satisfied, and so find, that the amount of $270,000 was paid by the firm so as to reorganise the assets of the applicants prior to sale of the 50% interest in the firm. As such there was a rearrangement of capital to protect the interests of the applicants (Exhibit A). That was the essential character of the transaction and it is only on that basis can the decision to make the firm insolvent be explained. The outgoing of $270,000 was of capital or of a capital nature and therefore excluded from deduction under s51.
  5. Little was said to us about the additional tax as assessed by the respondent after making an adjustment on one objection decision. The effect has been to remit so much of the additional tax imposed by s226H of the Act as to reduce the additional tax to 24.5% of the tax avoided. That section applies where there has been a tax shortfall caused by recklessness. If the section applies the taxpayer is liable to pay additional tax equal to 50% of the amount of the shortfall.
  6. In Case 74/96 96 ATC 662 at 676 the Tribunal, as then constituted, discussed the meaning of recklessness in the context of s22H as follows:
“Recklessness is a concept known to the law, and perhaps better known in the context of criminal law, but my understanding is it should be accepted as meaning ‘having no regard to the consequences of an action’. The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary defines the meaning as ‘careless of the consequences of one’s actions, heedless (of something) lacking in prudence or caution’. Similar meanings are given by the Macquarie Dictionary. In Derry v Peek [1889] UKHL 1; (1889) 14 App Cas 337 at 374, Lord Herschell gave a meaning of reckless as ‘careless whether it be true or false’. In tax ruling TR 94/4, the ruling has gone to some length to explain the respondent’s understanding of the term ‘recklessness’ and, in effect, describes the term as meaning gross carelessness. I think the meaning goes beyond gross carelessness and I think there must be some disregard for the consequences before it can be said that an action is, in fact, reckless.”


We respectfully adopt that discussion as our own.

  1. In this case the applicants acted on the advice of their accountant in circumstances where it might be thought that they as solicitors would be concerned to ensure that the advance payment was made in accordance with the agreement. That it was not so made attributes to their claim an element which goes beyond carelessness and demonstrates a disregard for the consequences.
  2. The Commissioner has been generous in the partial remission of the additional tax imposed by the Act. We will not disturb the Commissioner’s generosity but merely acknowledge it.
  3. For these reasons the objection decisions under review will be affirmed.
