Bartlett Estates Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1979] FCA 27; (1979) 38 FLR 295 (12 June 1979)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

BARTLETT ESTATES PTY. LTD. v. FEDERAL COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION (1979) 38 FLR

295

Income Tax

COURT

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

GENERAL DIVISION

Bowen C.J(1), Brennan(2) and Toohey(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Whether land can be acquired partly for profitmaking by sale and partly as investment - Powers of court on appeal - Burden of proof on taxpayer with reference to assessment - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth.), ss. 25, 26 (a), 190 (b) - Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 (Cth.), s. 28(1)(a). The taxpayers, whose business was the purchase and subdivision of land, acquired twenty-five acres of land in 1959. On ten acres of the land were three warehouses, the other fifteen acres was vacant land. The taxpayer contended that it purchased the section of ten acres as a source of rental income and for possible subdivision and sale in the future, and the section of fifteen acres with the intention of subdivision and sale. The taxpayer from the inception let the warehouses, and returned a profit in each of the years 1960-1974. The taxpayer was forced by its creditors to commence to subdivide and sell the ten acres in 1966. The Supreme Court held that, whilst there were some features pointing to the dominant purpose of the acquisition of the ten acres being the letting of the warehouses, there were others pointing to the purpose being for subdivision and resale, and that it was acquired for resale at a profit.

Held: (1) An appeal to the Federal Court of Australia is a full appeal both as to law and fact.

Edwards v. Noble [1971] HCA 54; (1971), 125 CLR 296, and Imperial Chemical Industries of Australia and New Zealand Ltd. v. Murphy (1973), 47 ALJR 122, followed.

(2) The finding that the dominant purpose of acquisition was resale was an inference reached by a consideration of the actual facts proved and the probabilities to which they gave rise, and because it was a matter of inference, the Federal Court was free to look at the matter for itself, not upsetting any finding of fact, unless satisfied that it was wrong.

Paterson v. Paterson [1953] HCA 74; (1953), 89 CLR 212; Benmax v. Austin Motor Co. Ltd., (1955) AC 370; Jones v. Capaldi [1956] HCA 37; (1956), 98 CLR 615, and Flannery v. Cohuna Sewerage Authority (1976), 51 ALJR 135, referred to.

(3) There is no reason in principle why, with reference to s. 26(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, land may not be acquired for one purpose as to part and for another purpose as to the remainder.

Chapman v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1968] HCA 4; (1968), 117 CLR 167; Smith v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1972), 46 ALJR 518, referred to.

(4) The dominant purpose of the acquisition of the ten acres was not that of profit-making by sale within the meaning of s. 26(a) of the Act, nor was the profit derived from the sale of the ten acres income within the ordinary concepts and so assessable under s. 25(1) of the Act.

(5) The effect of s. 190(b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 is that unless the appellant shows by evidence that the assessment is incorrect, it will prevail.

McCormack v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1979] HCA 18; (1979), 53 ALJR 436, followed.

HEARING

Sydney, 1979, June 12. 12:6:1979

APPEAL.

Appeal from the judgment and orders of the Supreme Court of Queensland (Hoare J.).

G. L. Davies Q.C., for the appellant.

T. F. Sheperdson, for the respondent.

Cur. adv. vult.

Solicitors for the appellant: Walsh, Fitzgerald & Hilton.

Solicitor for the respondent: B.J. O'Donovan (Acting Commonwealth Crown

Solicitor).

N. GOOD

DECISION

June 12.

The following judgments were delivered.

BOWEN C.J. I agree with the orders proposed by Toohey J. and with his
reasons for judgment. (at p296)

BRENNAN J. I have had the advantage of reading the reasons for judgment prepared by Toohey J. I concur in those reasons and I would allow the appeal accordingly. (at p296)

TOOHEY J. These six appeals were heard as one. In each the taxpayer appealed against a decision of the Supreme Court of Queensland confirming an assessment made by the commissioner in which he brought to account as assessable income profits derived by the taxpayer from the sale of land as subdivided industrial lots. The years under consideration were 1967, 1969, 1972, 1973, 1974 and 1975. In each appeal the questions were the same, there being no dispute about the amount of profit involved. (at p296)

2. In essence, the conclusion of the Supreme Court was that "on the balance of probabilities the dominant purpose was to resell the land at some time in the future, although in the meantime, pending development for that purpose, a very useful income would be derived from the land". That approach placed the taxpayer's activities within the first limb of s. 26(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. Before this Court the commissioner contended also that the profits derived were income according to ordinary concepts, hence assessable within s. 25(1) of the Act. No argument was addressed to us that the second limb of s. 26(a) had any application. (at p296)

3. These appeals raise an issue whether this Court should disturb a finding of the Supreme Court as to the dominant purpose for which land was acquired. To look at that matter, it is necessary first to say something about the taxpayer's activities and the circumstances under which the land was acquired and sold. (at p296)

4. The taxpayer was incorporated in January 1959 at the same time as John Bartlett Constructions Pty. Ltd. and Bartlett Investments Pty. Ltd. None of the memoranda and articles of these companies was produced but the business of the taxpayer was described by its chairman of directors, John Colin Bartlett, as the purchase and subdivision of land. The business of John Bartlett Constructions Pty. Ltd. was to build houses on land acquired by the taxpayer. Bartlett Investments Pty. Ltd. was described as "the holding company". There was already in existence another company, J. Sidney Bartlett Pty. Ltd., which had been incorporated by Mr. J. C. Bartlett's father. It became the selling company for the subdivided land, either built on or vacant. Mr. Bartlett was the chairman of directors of all the companies mentioned. The case before the Supreme Court and the argument before this Court proceeded on the assumption that the mind of Mr. Bartlett was the mind of the taxpayer. (at p297)

5. Early in 1959 Mr. Bartlett became aware that the Commonwealth of Australia had for sale by tender land in Aitkenvale, Townsville. The area, location and nature of the land are important in considering the purpose for which it was acquired. It comprised 25 acres 1 rood 12.9 perches. It was already subdivided into a number of lots, but it is not possible to trace through and reconcile the land descriptions appearing in the correspondence between the taxpayer and the Commonwealth, the transfer to the taxpayer and the certificates of title that ultimately emerged in its favour. It is important to keep distinct the actual subdivided state of the land and what it was seen by Mr. Bartlett to be, the latter being more important when the taxpayer's purpose of acquisition has to be resolved. For this reason the use during the hearing of the appeals of Ex. 1, a plan of the land, was apt to mislead. It was a useful guide to the state of the land but it was not a contemporary document. Certainly there was no evidence that at the time of inspection Mr. Bartlett appreciated that the land was in three blocks, one of fifteen acres to the north and two, of four and five acres respectively, to the south. (at p297)

6. Broadly speaking, the land had a frontage to Charles Street on its northern boundary and to Anne Street along the northern half of its western boundary. That was the area of fifteen acres and it was vacant land. In the southern section were three warehouses, each some 300 feet by 100 feet. Two were on the four-acre block and one was on the five-acre block. Other land separated these blocks from Anne Street to the west and from Wotten Street to the south. (at p297)

7. Only two of the warehouses were noticed by Mr. Bartlett on his first inspection. That may seem strange, but the explanation was that the land was densely overgrown with China apple trees. He first saw the third warehouse after tender and before acceptance. (at p297)

8. Mr. Bartlett said that he focused his attention on the southern section of the land where the warehouses were, thinking that it was "an opportunity to secure a basic foundation of secure income for Bartlett Estates, especially in its inaugural year, with the land around it, it gave rise to a very secure rental income; it gave room for future expansion and rental income and possibly at some date a shopping centre or something like that went through my mind". He added that at the time the associated company, John Bartlett Constructions Pty. Ltd., the builder, was increasing production and was looking for storage space. (at p298)

9. On the western boundary of the land Fleming Street had a junction with Anne Street. In 1959 Fleming Street did not extend into the twenty-five acres but notionally may have been seen as capable, in an extended form, of cutting the land in two just north of the warehouses. (at p298)

10. In his evidence Mr. Bartlett described how "if you take a line through from Fleming Street and you looked through the land, you would see on the right-hand side you have the warehouse land - the two warehouses - and on the left-hand side you have surplus. Vacant land of fifteen acres, approximately fifty-five or fifty-six lots. That at some future date would be saleable, and the profits from that you could put back into your rental income security here (indicating)". (at p298)

11. In my view that is as far as the evidence will take the taxpayer, that it saw the land not as divided into three blocks but as an area of twenty-five acres, some ten acres of which was valuable for income and storage, with another section of about fifteen acres which it intended at all times to sell. (at p298)

12. Although a transfer of the land was not registered until 8th December, 1961, it would be wrong to regard that as the date of acquisition. The transfer recited a contract of sale made 14th October, 1959, but that document could not be found. On 31st August, 1959, the Commonwealth of Australia wrote to the taxpayer's agent to say that executive council authority had been obtained for the sale. In my view that should be taken as the date of acquisition for the purposes of the Income Tax Assessment Act. (at p298)

13. The taxpayer bought an area of land (referred to as "the Wormald land", from the name of the vendor) fronting Anne Street, adjoining the northern section of the twenty-five acres and on the western edge of the fifteen acres. A transfer of that land to the taxpayer was registered on 1st October, 1959. The notification of change of ownership lodged with the Department of Valuers-General (Ex. 32) gave the date of sale as 14th August, 1959, about a fortnight before the date on which the Commonwealth accepted the taxpayer's offer to buy the land under consideration. The taxpayer's object in acquiring this land was to round off the northern section, making easier the process of subdivision and sale in lots of that section. This must be seen against the background of the taxpayer's evidence that from the outset the fifteen acres were destined for sale. When that land was later subdivided into industrial lots and sold, the profits derived were at all times treated by the taxpayer as part of its assessable income and tax was paid. (at p299)

14. There is no reason in principle why, with reference to s. 26(a) of the Act, land may not be acquired for one purpose as to part and for another purpose as to the remainder: Chapman v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1968] HCA 4; (1968) 117 CLR 167 ; Smith v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1972) 46 ALJR 518 . Nor did the commissioner suggest that there was. The question remains - with what dominant purpose did the taxpayer acquire the area of ten acres or thereabouts upon which the three warehouses stood? No inference adverse to the taxpayer may be drawn from the sale of the ten acres ultimately effected. To begin with, the land was not sold at a time "proximate to its acquisition" (Gauci v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation per Barwick C.J. [1975] HCA 54; (1975) 135 CLR 81, at p 87 ). The proposal to subdivide the ten acres seems not to have begun until 1966: see the letter from the taxpayer to the commissioner of 13th July, 1972, (Ex. 38). And that proposal was very much forced upon the taxpayer by its creditors, following some two years unsuccessful trading by it and its associated companies. The learned judge commented: "It is clear that the serious financial position of the appellant company led to the meeting of creditors and the decision to sell off all available assets of any or all of the companies in the group." That finding was not challenged before this Court. (at p299)

15. The use made by the taxpayer of the land was consistent with the purpose for which it was said to have been acquired, as a source of income and to provide storage. From inception the warehouses were let, returning a profit in each of the years 1960- 1974. (at p299)

16. Mr. Bartlett described the condition of the two warehouses when he first saw them. They had suffered some damage but could be repaired at little cost. He told of calculations he made, based on letting both warehouses, so that the rent would service a purchase price of 7000 pounds spread over five years. That was in fact the substance of the taxpayer's first tender. The offer accepted was 7750 pounds, payable by a deposit of 2750 pounds and the balance over five years. That came about because Mr. Bartlett learned that another tender higher than his initial one had been received. (at p299)

17. He added that a cyclone in Townsville three years earlier had led to "a shortage of supply of suitable, reliable and reasonable warehouse accommodation within a reasonable proximity to the city". This made the land an even more attractive proposition, a view confirmed by Mr. Hollamby, a valuer who gave evidence. (at p299)

18. John Bartlett Constructions Pty. Ltd. rented part of one warehouse and was still doing so at the time of the hearing although the original building had been demolished and reconstructed. Mr. Bartlett found other tenants, particulars of whom were in the material before the court. He explained that by the end of 1962 the taxpayer had received in rent "150 per cent of (the) capital outlay . . . and possibly two and a half to three times my cash outlay". (at p300)

19. Curiously, although Mr. Bartlett spoke of securing tenants for the warehouses, he claimed ignorance of any existing tenancies and said that he made no inquiries about them. Such an inquiry would ordinarily be a priority in the mind of anyone interested in buying property to let. The explanation may lie in a letter dated 3rd September, 1959, (Ex. 31) written by the Commonwealth to J. Sidney Bartlett Pty. Ltd., as agent for the taxpayer, in which details of five existing tenancies were set out. There was no advantage to the taxpayer in denying knowledge of these tenancies; it may be that after nearly twenty years Mr. Bartlett's recollection was faulty. No criticism of his credibility on this score was made by the learned judge. (at p300)

20. His Honour accepted that there were some features pointing to the dominant purpose of acquisition as the letting of the warehouses, but he thought that there were others pointing to it being no different from that of other land acquired by the taxpayer, admittedly for subdivision and resale. Put shortly, those features were, first, that at the time of purchase the land was referred to as a twenty-five acre block, not one of fifteen and others amounting to ten. Second, the total area of land included with the warehouses was greater than the financial resources of the taxpayer and its associated companies would permit to develop as warehouses. Third, the taxpayer bought other land for resale, including Wormald's and some three-quarters of a mile away. Fourth, for some time the ten acres, along with the fifteen acres, were treated in the books of the company as trading stock. Fifth, while the land had potential for industrial development, there was no reason to suppose it had none for residential development. As I read his Honour's judgment, it was the cumulative effect of these factors that led him to conclude that the dominant purpose of acquisition was resale at a profit. (at p300)

21. The taxpayer contended that all these matters were capable of explanation and that seen in their true light none was inconsistent with the avowed purpose of acquisition for rental and storage. (at p300)

22. There is much to be said for this contention. Although during negotiations the land was referred to as twenty-five acres, that does not preclude, either in law or in fact, the taxpayer from having one purpose with regard to part and another purpose as to the rest. His Honour was satisfied "that the fact that there were warehouses on the land, which would be likely to earn a substantial income, was an important reason why Mr. Bartlett favoured the purchase". (at p300)

23. It may be that the area of ten acres was more than could be developed within the taxpayer's resources. At the same time the existing warehouses were very large, described by one witness as "monstrous", and, as his Honour found, "might well be expected to meet the letting requirements in the reasonably near future". (at p301)

24. As to the other activities of the taxpayer, it made no secret of the fact that it engaged in the buying of land for subdivision and resale, with the important qualification that except for the twenty-five acres those activities concerned only residential land. (at p301)

25. The treatment of the ten acres in the taxpayer's books of account was explained in this way. At first the entire twenty-five acres appeared in the profit and loss account as part of the taxpayer's trading stock. This was done by Mrs. Brabazon, an employee of the company. In 1963 Mr. Trembath, a qualified accountant, joined "the Bartlett group". For the financial records of that year he removed the twenty-five acres from the trading stock account but in 1965 restored the fifteen acres to it, leaving the 10 acres under the classification "properties for rental etc.". Mr. Bartlett seems to have played little part in these decisions. The learned judge regarded it "as a very important matter" that for some time the ten acres was treated on the same basis as other land acquired by the taxpayer, admittedly for the purpose of resale. In my view it was important but not in any sense decisive. Furthermore, the separate treatment in 1965 of fifteen acres and ten acres was entirely consistent with Mr. Bartlett's evidence of dual purpose of acquisition. (at p301)

26. As to the last matter upon which his Honour relied, there was no evidence that the land had any potential for residential development notwithstanding the existence of such activity not far away. (at p301)

27. The taxpayer's counsel made much of the evidence of Mr. McDermott that at the time of acquisition the land had potential as lettable warehouse space but none for subdivision. In Mr. McDermott's words: "I would be looking at it as a warehouse because quite frankly at that time I didn't think Townsville would expand to the extent it did. Unbelievable." (at p301)

28. However the force of that comment is considerably weakened by the taxpayer's own evidence that he had, from the outset, the intention of subdividing and selling the northern fifteen acres. Admittedly that operation did not begin until after a meeting of creditors in 1964 and it took place at the instigation of a board of directors that included representatives of the creditors. But that does not detract from the state of mind of the taxpayer in 1959; I do not regard that part of Mr. McDermott's testimony as of help to the taxpayer. (at p301)

29. There is of course no doubt that this Court may "affirm, reverse or vary the judgment appealed from" (Federal Court of Australia Act 1976, s. 28(1)(a)). As Barwick C.J. said in Edwards v. Noble: "An appellate court considering such a finding is not confined to considering whether there was any material to support the finding or whether the finding was against the weight of the material properly available to the judge. The appeal is a full appeal that is to say, both as to fact and law. The relevant question here, as I have said, is whether the appellate court, as a matter of judicial restraint, ought to reverse the findings of fact" [1971] HCA 54; (1971) 125 CLR 296, at p 306 . (at p302)

30. The difficulty is, as Gibbs J. pointed out in Imperial Chemical Industries of Australia and New Zealand Ltd. v. Murphy: " . . . the attitude of the court of appeal to findings of fact made by the trial judge must be governed by rules which are based on two principles which appear to some extent to be in conflict with each other. These principles, shortly stated, are that the appellate court has a duty to make up its own mind as to the facts, and that the court must give due weight to the position of advantage enjoyed by the judge of trial" (1973) 47 ALJR 122, at p 129 . (at p302)

31. I have come to the conclusion that this Court should set aside the decision of the learned judge as to the purpose for which the ten acres were acquired. My reasons are as follows. His Honour was satisfied that the taxpayer "very much had in mind the great advantages of the subject land because of the availability of rent from the property". That finding was not challenged by the commissioner and hardly could be, given the weight of evidence to support it. His Honour then went on: "However, I am also satisfied that the appellant company intended to resell the land in the future." (at p302)

32. The considerations that led to that conclusion have already been mentioned in these reasons. They were challenged by the taxpayer and I have discussed the force of that challenge. But as well, they must be measured against the weight of the testimony of Mr. Bartlett whom his Honour accepted as a witness of truth. I do not overlook the qualifications regarding both Mr. Bartlett and Mr. McDermott, that after a long passage of time memory is likely to be inaccurate and also that an honest witness may purport to recall details that accord with his wishes. But neither qualification seems to have caused his Honour to doubt Mr. Bartlett's general veracity. (at p302)

33. What the taxpayer challenges is the finding that the dominant purpose of acquisition was resale. There was no admission by the taxpayer to support that finding nor can it be supported by evidence of an observable fact. It had to be an inference: "A true inference of fact is an inference of an actual matter or event. It is reached by a consideration of actual facts proved and the probabilities to which they give rise" (Da Costa v. Cockburn Salvage & Trading Pty. Ltd. per Windeyer J. [1970] HCA 43; (1970) 124 CLR 192, at p 213 ). (at p302)

34. It was not an evaluation in the sense in which that term was used in Da Costa's case and in Edwards v. Noble [1971] HCA 54; (1971) 125 CLR 296 . Because it was a matter of inference, this Court is free to look at the matter for itself, not upsetting any true finding of fact unless satisfied that it was wrong. The line of authorities justifying this approach includes such decisions as Paterson v. Paterson [1953] HCA 74; (1953) 89 CLR 212 ; Benmax v. Austin Motor Co. Ltd. (1955) AC 370 ; Jones v. Capaldi [1956] HCA 37; (1956) 98 CLR 615 and Flannery v. Cohuna Sewerage Authority (1976) 51 ALJR 135 . (at p303)

35. After the hearing of this appeal the High Court delivered judgment in Warren v. Coombes [1979] HCA 9; (1979) 53 ALJR 293 . The joint judgment of Gibbs A.C.J., Jacobs and Murphy JJ. contains an analysis of the powers and duties of an appellate tribunal to which there is an appeal by way of rehearing. The judgment includes this paragraph: "Shortly expressed, the established principles are, we think, that in general an appellate court is in as good a position as the trial judge to decide on the proper inference to be drawn from facts which are undisputed or which, having been disputed, are established by the findings of the trial judge. In deciding what is the proper inference to be drawn, the appellate court will give respect and weight to the conclusion of the trial judge, but, once having reached its own conclusion will not shrink from giving effect to it" (1979) 53 ALJR, at pp 300-301 . (at p303)

36. More recently the High Court delivered judgment in McCormack v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1979] HCA 18; (1979) 53 ALJR 436 . It is a fair conclusion from the judgments of the members of the court in that case that the purpose spoken of by s. 26(a) is largely a matter of inference to be drawn from the evidence. (at p303)

37. Both decisions support the views expressed earlier in these reasons, namely that this Court, in considering the purpose for which the relevant land was acquired, is free to look at the matter for itself. Where credibility is involved, it has the advantage of the assessment made by the learned trial judge that Mr. Bartlett was a witness of truth. (at p303)

38. It may be that the taxpayer had in mind that the ten acres would appreciate in value over a period of years; it would be surprising if it did not. But that is not enough to bring the taxpayer within the first limb of s. 26(a): "The question in the application of s. 26(a) is not whether the taxpayer, when purchasing, hoped that at some time in the future he could sell the land at an enhanced value. The question is whether he was then intending to sell it at a profit, doing so as a matter of 'business'. The purchase of land as a long term investment, or as a hedge against the depreciating value of money does not, in my opinion, come under s. 26(a)" (Gauci's case per Barwick C.J. (1975) 135 CLR, at p 87 ). I do not say that this was a case of acquisition as a hedge against depreciation. But, in my view, it was not a case in which the taxpayer acquired land intending to sell it at a profit, as a matter of business. This land was acquired in 1959 and no profits were derived from the sale of any part of it until 1967, and only then because of the action of creditors in forcing a sale. Over that intervening period of nearly ten years the warehouses had been let, income derived and the land used in a manner entirely consistent with the taxpayer's avowed purpose of acquisition for income and for storage. (at p304)

39. Before and after Gauci's case there was some difference of opinion expressed by members of the High Court on the effect of s. 190 of the Income Tax Assessment Act on appeals under the Act. In the recent decision of McCormack v. Commissioner of Taxation [1979] HCA 18; (1979) 53 ALJR 436 the majority, Gibbs, Stephen and Murphy JJ., gave express assent to the statement of Mason J. in his dissenting judgment in Gauci's case that it is a consequence of s. 190(b) that "unless the appellant shows by evidence that the assessment is incorrect, it will prevail" (1975) 135 CLR, at p 89 . (at p304)

40. I am of the opinion, for the reasons given, that the taxpayer showed by evidence that its dominant purpose was not that of profit-making by sale and so showed that the assessment was incorrect. (at p304)

41. The commissioner argued that if the profit derived from the sale of the ten acres is not assessable within the first limb of s. 26 (a), it should be regarded as income according to ordinary concepts and so assessable under s. 25(1). Various authorities were cited, both as to principle and by way of illustration. (at p304)

42. In McClelland v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1970] HCA 39; (1970) 120 CLR 487 the Privy Council saw s. 25(1) as introducing no new element leading to a different conclusion from that reached in regard to s. 26(a): "The whole of the facts have still to be considered; the same criteria have to be applied; the question to be asked and answered is still whether the facts reveal a mere realization of capital, albeit in an enterprising way, or whether they justify a finding that the appellant went beyond this and engaged in a trade of dealing in land albeit on one occasion only" (1970) 120 CLR, at p 496 . (at p304)

43. In the present case there is of course no dispute that the taxpayer engaged in the trade of dealing in land. With the exception of the ten acres in question, it seems that the taxpayer brought to account as assessable income the profit derived from those dealings. Hence the taxpayer may find itself at a disadvantage compared with someone involved in a "one-off" transaction. And it is urged against the taxpayer that, for a time at least, it treated the ten acres as part of its trading stock, making no distinction between it and other land which it held. (at p304)

44. These considerations are important but on the end it seems to me that the matters taking the case out of the first limb of s.26 (a) take it also out of s.25 (1). The business of the taxpayer was essentially that of buying, subdividing and selling residential land. It is true that when it acquired the twenty-five acres it did so with the intention of subdividing and selling the northern portion of fifteen acres. If the ten arces had been vacant, at the time of aquisition and had it remained vacant, the taxpayer would have been hard put to persuade a court that any distinction should be drawn between it and the fifteen acres or perhaps, for that matter, between it and other land purchased. But the land was not vacant. It carried three warehouses which were let and continued to be let up to a time shortly before the land was sold. When it was sold, it was at the instigation of the taxpayer's creditors. (at p305)

45. Counsel for the taxpayer argued that the profit derived from the sale of the ten acres should be seen as analogous to a profit made on the sale or winding up of a business because it was brought about by the action of the creditors in trying to recover the money due to them. I do not think it is necessary to adopt that analogy. The purpose for which the ten acres were acquired and the use to which the land was put, coupled with the circumstances surrounding the sale, point to a capital rather than an income gain. See Eisner v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 45 ALJR 110 (at p305)

46. The appeals should be allowed. The assessments should be remitted to the commissioner to be amended in accordance with these reasons. The commissioner should pay the costs of this appeal and of the appeal to the Supreme Court of Queensland. (at p305)

ORDER

Orders accordingly.
