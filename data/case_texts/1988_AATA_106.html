Re JH Coffey and Commissioner of Superannuation [1988] AATA 106 (5 April 1988)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: J.H. COFFEY

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. S86/204

AAT No. 4255

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.A. Layton (Deputy President)

B.C. Lock (Member)

Dr D.B. Williams (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - "spouse" of eligible employee - whether applicant living on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis with deceased - applicant not living with deceased at time of his death - whether apart on a temporary basis - whether separation resulted from illness or infirmity - whether applicant wholly or substantially dependent on deceased at time of death - effect of receipt of pension in considering dependency.

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.3, 3(1)(a), 3(1)(b), 3(2)

Family Law Act 1975, ss.72, 75(2)(f)

Re Australian National Railways Commission and Edna Edith Gerlach and Others, S.86/29, delivered 13 March 1987 (Decision 3294)

Re Schlatter and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1985) 8 ALD 133

Commissioner for Superannuation v Scott G.62 of 1986, delivered 11 March 1987

Kauri Timber Co (Tas.) Pty Limited v Reeman [1973] HCA 8; (1973) 128 CLR 177

Aafjes v Kearney [1976] HCA 5; (1976) 8 ALR 455

New Monkton Collieries Limited v Keeling (1911) AC 648

Kajewski (1978) FLC 90-472

Bailey (1981) FLC 91-041

Mathews (1980) FLC 90-887

HEARING

ADELAIDE

5:4:1988

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

This is an application for review of a decision made by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation on 13 November 1985 and confirmed on 14 July 1986, that the applicant does not fall within the definition of "spouse" contained in s.3 of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") and is therefore not entitled to a spouse's pension in accordance with the provisions of the Act.

2. At the hearing of this application for review, the applicant was represented by counsel, Mr Gallasch, and the respondent was represented by counsel, Mr Eadie. The information before the Tribunal consisted of the documents lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (the "T documents") and further documentation tendered at the hearing. In addition, the applicant gave oral evidence and the respondent called two medical witnesses, Dr Jagermann, a psychiatrist, and Dr Ling, a medical practitioner.

3. The issue before the Tribunal is as to whether or not the applicant, at the time of death of her late husband, Mr Reid (hereinafter referred to as "the deceased") was a "spouse" within the meaning of paras.(a) and (b) of that definition of sub-s.3(1) of the Act, having particular regard to the deeming provisions contained in sub-s.3(2) of the Act.

4. The applicant's primary submission was that she was legally married to the deceased and was living with him on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis at the time of his death. The applicant claims this, notwithstanding that she was living separately from the deceased at the time of his death, because this separation constituted a "temporary absence" within the meaning of sub-s.3(2) of the Act and that therefore she should be deemed to have been living with him on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis at the time of his death.

5. In the alternative, it was submitted that the applicant should be deemed to have been living with the deceased on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis as the separation resulted from the illness or infirmity of the deceased.

6. In the further alternative, it was submitted that even if the applicant were not deemed to be living with the deceased on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis at the time of his death, the applicant was nonetheless "wholly or substantially" dependent upon him as at the time of his death and was therefore, pursuant to para.(b) of the definition of "spouse" contained in sub-s.3(1) of the Act, a spouse in relation to the deceased.

7. In determining these issues, the Tribunal makes the following findings of fact. The deceased was born on 13 April 1922 and married his first wife in or about 1943. In about 1974, the deceased's first wife left him and thereafter the deceased resided alone.

8. The applicant was born on 25 August 1924 and married her first husband, Samuel Coffey, in 1942. The applicant and Mr Coffey had four children. Mr Coffey retired on the basis of invalidity when he was 58 years old. He died suddenly in December 1980. At the time of his death, the applicant and Mr Coffey were living in a South Australian Housing Trust house and were supporting themselves on an invalid pension and an associated spouse's pension.

9. The applicant met the deceased on her first outing following the death of Mr Coffey. This took place in about March 1981. They developed a friendship over the ensuing months and in about May or June 1981, the deceased began living with the applicant in her Housing Trust house in a de facto arrangement.

10. The applicant at that time was in receipt of a widow's pension as a result of her husband's previous contribution to the National Insurance Scheme in the United Kingdom in which he participated prior to coming to Australia. In addition, she received a widow's pension in Australia.

11. At the time when the applicant and the deceased first had sexual relations, it was apparent that the deceased suffered a problem of partial impotence. The applicant and the deceased both thought that part of the reason for this problem may have been the fact that they were not legally married, and they married on 19 September 1981.

12. At the time of their marriage, the applicant owned a full house of furniture consisting of main bedroom furniture (double bed, dressing table, continental quilt and all appropriate bed linen), another single bed with appropriate bedroom fittings; a lounge suite, coffee tables and smoker's stands; kitchen table, crockery, cutlery, pots and pans and the usual kitchen equipment. She also had approximately $4,000 to $5,000 in a bank account, being moneys remaining from Mr Coffey's estate.

13. After her marriage to the deceased, the applicant was no longer eligible to receive either the widow's pension from the United Kingdom, or the Australian widow's pension. The deceased and the applicant therefore lived on the wages which the deceased earned as a fitter and turner with the Department of Defence.

14. The deceased continued to suffer from an impotence problem (hereinafter referred to as "sexual difficulties"). He consulted his local doctor, Dr Hamden, on 22 October 1981 suffering from depression and sexual difficulties. Dr Hamden referred him to a physician, Dr Graham, who found no organic basis which would explain the deceased's sexual difficulties. In due course, Dr Hamden referred the deceased to a psychiatrist, Dr Jagermann. Dr Jagermann saw both the applicant and the deceased on two occasions, those being 26 October 1981 and 23 November 1981, with regard to marital difficulties related to sexual matters. We will refer to Dr Jagermann's opinion later in these Reasons for Decision.

15. The applicant began seeing Dr Hamden from about October 1981 and continued such consultations up until March 1982. During that time, she obtained prescriptions for tranquil lisers to treat her symptoms of anxiety and depression. The applicant considered that her depression was due in part to the death of her first husband and also due to the problems which she was experiencing in her marriage to the deceased.

16. On 16 April 1982, the deceased retired from his employment at the age of 60 years. This was a voluntary retirement as he could have continued in his employment until the age of 65 years. As part of his superannuation entitlement he received a lump sum payment. At this time, contrary to the applicant's wishes, the deceased wanted to purchase a house in their joint names. The purchase price for the house was $23,000. Half to two-thirds of the deposit was paid by the deceased from his superannuation payout, and the balance of the deposit was paid by the applicant from her bank account.

17. Also in April 1982, the applicant purchased a motor vehicle for the deceased at a sum of $1,999.99. This vehicle was registered in the deceased's name. The applicant was unable to drive. The applicant also purchased a garage, awnings and fittings for the new house in addition to a refrigerator with her own money.

18. Taking into account all of the furniture that she owned prior to the marriage, together with the additional purchases, the applicant estimates that approximately $12,000 of furniture and fittings in the new house were provided or paid for by her. The deceased's contribution to the furniture was a chair which he brought from his previous house, a television set which he bought, a recliner chair and a microwave oven which he bought for the applicant. At some later stage, a table was purchased, although it is unclear from the evidence as to who paid for it.

19. Following the deceased's retirement, the income received by the applicant and the deceased consisted of a service pension paid to the deceased by the Department of Veterans' Affairs and a corollary spouse's pension to which the applicant became entitled. In addition, the deceased was in receipt of a regular superannuation pension.

20. Unfortunately, this was not enough to repay the bridging finance obtained for the purchase of the house by the applicant and the deceased in April 1982 and they were forced to sell the house in July 1982. After the forced sale of the house they were left with a deficit of some $2,000. The garage, awnings and fittings in the house went with the sale of the house. Thereafter, the applicant and the deceased moved with all their furniture to a Housing Trust flat.

21. The relationship between the applicant and the deceased so far as can be ascertained from the applicant's evidence and the information contained in the "T documents", was one of companionship and shared interests. The applicant and the deceased both enjoyed dancing and playing scrabble. The deceased however had an abiding interest in sport, particularly television sport on weekends, which was not shared by the applicant and which was a source of irritation between them.

22. The applicant presented herself at this hearing as an articulate and relatively extroverted personality. This contrasts with the impression gleaned through the evidence, of the deceased. The deceased appeared to be more introverted, to have experienced difficulties with interpersonal relations particularly in relation to women, and had difficulty demon strating affection. He appears to have had an unfortunate background of illegitimacy followed by being placed into a home at an early age. His first marriage, although it lasted some 31 years, appears to have lacked displays of affection and he and his first wife apparently had separate beds. There is also some suggestion that he was adversely affected because his first wife may have had a relationship outside their marriage.

23. The applicant was aware of these problems of the deceased but nonetheless loved him and he apparently loved her. However, over a period of time, the sexual difficulties experienced by the deceased, in combination with the difficulty he experienced with interpersonal relations, and the apparent differences that the applicant and the deceased had as to their interests, became more paramount. By June 1984, they began to have frequent arguments over trivia and the deceased displayed angry outbursts approximately once every two weeks, such outbursts being precipitated by minor matters such as losing or even winning at scrabble. At this time, they sought marriage counselling from a Mrs Schultz (T8/29).

24. On 28 June 1984, the applicant saw Dr Ling for her symptoms of anxiety, depression and complaints of low back ache. Dr Ling prescribed tranquillisers for the applicant and was also told that the applicant's husband wished him to see him. The deceased saw Dr Ling for the first time on 12 July 1984 com plaining of sexual difficulties. Dr Ling considered that the deceased may have been suffering from a hormone deficiency and gave him the first of a series of three hormone injections. The applicant returned on 2 August 1984 as did her husband. The applicant was still suffering from anxiety and depression and the deceased was given the second of the hormone injections indicating that he found some benefits from the first injection.

25. Also in June 1984, the deceased told the applicant that he wanted to try living alone for a time, as he had enjoyed his bachelor days. Nothing happened as a result of this conversation.

26. Dr Ling again saw the applicant and the deceased on 30 August 1984 when a third injection was given to the deceased and also at that time, the applicant was given a prescription for hormone cream due to her own problems related to sexual inter course. Dr Ling associated the applicant's anxiety and depression, at least in part, with the sexual problems experienced by both herself and the deceased.

27. In August 1984, the applicant and the deceased for the first time opened a joint bank account with Westpac bank. They had previously had separate bank accounts into which their respective pensions were paid. Thereafter their pensions were paid into the joint account, that being the only account which either of them held.

28. During September and October 1984, the applicant saw Dr Ling on several occasions. The reasons for these con sultations related mainly to bronchitis and depression.

29. On 18 December 1984, the applicant was lying in her recliner chair when suddenly and without provocation, the deceased attacked her by grabbing hold of her feet, which were arthritic, and banging them on the footrest of her chair in a way which was extremely painful to her. The applicant asked why he had done this, the deceased did not reply but returned to the kitchen and continued to make a cup of tea as though nothing had happened. Later that same evening, the applicant and the deceased played scrabble together and then went to bed in the usual way. The following morning after both of them had risen, the deceased told the applicant that he wished her to leave the house. He did not give reasons why he wished her to leave, but the applicant understood his reasons as being those which he had expressed as set out in para. 25 hereof. The applicant did not resist, although she said she was in a "stunned" condition. The deceased packed two bags for the applicant and called a taxi for her and she left.

30. The applicant had no money and went straight to a women's shelter. She had previously stayed at this shelter for some three to four days at some unspecified period after their marriage but had then returned to the house, sat down and asked the deceased for a cup of tea and thereafter they became reconciled and resumed their marital relationship.

31. When the applicant went to the shelter on this second occasion, she was very upset, hurt and later angry. She did not want to return to the deceased at her initiative as she had done previously, instead she wanted the deceased to ask her to come back.

32. On 20 December 1984, the day after the applicant left, the deceased went to the bank and, in the applicant's words, "froze" their bank account such that the applicant was unable to take any moneys from the account, and as a consequence her pension cheque was stopped. On that same day, the deceased went to the Department of Veterans' Affairs and signed a document which stated as follows:

"....my wife has left the above address on 19.12.84.

She can be contacted through her daughter Mrs J.

Prunty 17 Curawong Cres. Craigmore but resides in a

women's shelter for the time being.

We separated due to incompatibility and it is

impossible that there will be a reconciliation. We

separated by mutual agreement and I do not intend to

pay maintenance."

(T21)

33. Also on 20 December 1984, at the suggestion of the personnel from the women's shelter, the applicant went to the Department of Social Security and applied for a pension. In her application form, she provided the Department with the following statement:

"The main reason for our separation is that we are not

compatible, we mutually agreed to separate. I have

been receiving a Wifes Pension from the Dept. of

Veterans Affairs which my husband said he would

arrange to have cancelled. I realize that if we

reconcile I must advise the dept. immediately."

(T19)

34. The applicant was questioned as to this statement in her evidence before the Tribunal, and said that her reasons for stating that the main cause for separation was that they were not compatible and that they had mutually agreed to separate, was because she was angry and that this was not the reason for their separation. The reason for their separation was that the deceased had asked her to leave following an episode of violence the evening before, and she took the view that, if that was what he wanted to happen, then she would not resist.

35. Following her application, the applicant began receiving an age pension from the Department of Social Security for which she was then eligible having reached the age of 60 years.

36. The applicant remained at the women's shelter until about March 1985. During her time at the shelter she rang her husband each week to find out how he was. She was hoping that on one of these occasions he would ask her if she would return. He did not expressly ask her to return, although the applicant interpreted his comments such as expressions of his loneliness, his inability to play a "decent bowl" in carpet bowls since her absence and the fact that he was eating very little, to be indicative that the deceased wanted her to return without saying so. She gave evidence that as a matter of pride she did not wish to initiate a return as she had done in the past, although she was of the view that if she had gone to the house and asked for a cup of tea they would have resumed their matrimonial relations as had happened in the past.

37. The applicant also gave evidence that very early in their separation, she had asked the deceased for maintenance but he had refused saying words to the effect, "you are getting something from Social Security and you're not going to rip me off". (transcript, p.19). At that time, the applicant was still living in the shelter. It took some time before her age pension was paid and she was required to pay an amount to the shelter for her keep. Her daughter supplied her with cigarettes and toilet requirements in addition to giving her some $40 to $50 in cash. Without this money from her daughter, the provision made by the shelter and the age pension, the applicant would have had no source of income. The last time she had been employed was in 1976 as a clerical assistant grade 1 with various Commonwealth government departments and she had virtually no opportunity to obtain future employment.

38. In March 1985, the applicant managed to obtain a Housing Trust flat on a permanent basis. She said she could no longer remain in the shelter which was regarded as short-term accommodation only, particularly because of the high turnover of residents, some of whom were in great need being young distressed women with young children.

39. When the applicant obtained the Housing Trust flat, she telephoned the deceased to tell him of its acquisition and asked if he could send over the balance of her clothing, some kitchen equipment, linen and other items which were unspecified. The applicant described the deceased as being "most generous" in his response and said that if she sent her son to the house, he would give her what she needed. The applicant's son went to the deceased's house to collect the items. The deceased had already packed certain items into boxes. The deceased sent the balance of the applicant's clothing, a refrigerator, the better of two tables, her recliner chair, the microwave oven, one of the single beds, three of the six chairs, some kitchen equipment, bed linen and crockery. All of these items had been items which the applicant had owned prior to their marriage, paid for herself or were gifts to her from the deceased. The deceased retained all of the other furniture.

40. The applicant did not request any further assistance from the deceased as, in her view, one or other of them would have to do without and she considered that she would find it easier to organise herself than he would and further, she considered that it was pointless taking furniture out if they were going to be reconciled, as it would only have to be returned. A further reason given later in the applicant's evidence was that if the deceased did not wish to volunteer to give furniture to her, she did not wish to ask for it.

41. The applicant supplemented these furniture items with a carpet square given to her by her daughter and some furniture provided by charity which upset her greatly as she already had items which she owned which were being used by the deceased.

42. When she first moved into the Housing Trust flat, she spent some $59 on cleaning equipment and had very little money left over in order to buy her food and provisions.

43. The applicant gave evidence that after she had moved into her flat, the deceased rang her on about four occasions. He had not previously been able to telephone her because there was no easily available telephone at the women's shelter, and therefore she was the one who rang him. Furthermore, the deceased was not a person who was "good" on the telephone. On the occasions when he rang her, he asked her how she was getting on in much the same way as when she rang him on a weekly basis.

44. The last occasion on which they spoke was on 26 April 1985, being the evening prior to the deceased's death. On that occasion, the applicant telephoned the deceased to ask him how he was. On this occasion, he said he was "still badly". The applicant then said that she would rather he went out for "two more weeks" and "kick (his) heels up and to see if that (was) what (he) really want(ed)" (p.18 of transcript) and to "Be sure, because (she did) not want to be put out like a dog" (p.53 of transcript). He then replied that he had not done that and thereafter they had an argument as to whether or not the deceased had treated the applicant in that way. It was not a major argument, but more an exchange of different opinions which, bearing in mind that that was the last conversation which the applicant had with the deceased, was a matter of distress to her.

45. The next day, 27 April 1985, the deceased went to a dance and later died from what was diagnosed to be a heart attack. The applicant was very distressed at his death.

46. The applicant gave evidence at the hearing that apart from a short period when she was angry, throughout their period of separation she held the view that she and the deceased would become reconciled. She thought that she had "all of the time in the world" for it to happen. She also gave evidence that if, at the end of that period of two weeks of "kicking his heels up", the deceased had not asked her to return, she herself would probably have gone back anyway in the way that she had done before by asking him for a cup of tea and that thereafter they would have resumed their marital relationship. If his death had not intervened, she saw these events as being probable occurrences.

47. The applicant indicated that she still loved the deceased and that it was a matter of pride that she had not previously tried to become reconciled with him. She had not sought any advice concerning divorce and in fact expressed the view that divorce was contrary to her religious beliefs, even though it did not technically apply as she was a Roman Catholic who had married "outside the Church". She expressed the view that she would not have allowed the marriage to terminate. She had not taken any steps to obtain maintenance from the deceased other than the one request she made very early in their separa tion, and stated that she had been hurt by his reply. She had not sought to obtain a matrimonial property settlement from the deceased for reasons previously expressed in para. 40 hereof. She had not had any other relationship in the meantime. To the best of her knowledge, the deceased had also not sought any legal advice nor spoken of divorce to her nor had any other relation ships. On the contrary, to the applicant's mind, he had indicated to her that he wanted her to return, but that he had just not said so.

48. As at the date of the deceased's death, the applicant's only income was the age pension and without it, she would not have had any other means of support. Following the death of the deceased, the applicant became entitled to a United Kingdom pension as a result of the deceased's contribution to the National Insurance Scheme in the United Kingdom. She is unable to say whether this was a widow's pension or merely that she began receiving a pension that he would otherwise have been entitled to. This pension is payable at the rate of 11.10 pounds per week (equivalent to approximately $24 to $25 per week, depending on the rate of exchange). That continues to be the applicant's source of income to the present time.

49. Turning now to the medical evidence. The evidence of Dr Jagermann was that he did not consider the deceased to have been suffering from a psychiatric condition at the time when he saw him in October 1981. When certain matters were put to him as to the deceased's background and conduct towards the applicant, the doctor expressed the view that such conduct may be referable to one of three conditions, an occult personality disturbance, advancing senescence or intense frustration due to the deteriora tion of the marital relationship (transcript, p.41). Of these, the doctor considered that the deceased was more probably suffering from an occult personality disturbance. In expressing this view, Dr Jagermann stated that sometimes there was a fine distinction to be drawn between personality traits and the psychiatric condition of a personality disorder. In this instance, the doctor was firmly of the view that notwithstanding the deceased's apparently erratic behaviour, his anger and his inability to have warm interpersonal relations, these were merely personality traits and were not indicative of the psychiatric condition of a personality disorder. The Tribunal accepts his evidence.

50. The evidence of Dr Ling was merely to confirm the sexual difficulties complained of by the deceased and the conditions for which he treated the deceased and the applicant. There was no suggestion in his evidence that the deceased was suffering from any psychiatric or organic condition and that apart from pulmonary fibrosis, he appeared to be quite well.

51. We will now consider the legal issues and some further findings of fact in relation to those issues. The relevant sections of the Act are as follows. Paragraphs (a) and (b) of the definition of "spouse" contained in sub-s.3(1) is expressed in the following terms:

"3. (1) In this Act, unless the contrary intention

appears -

....

"spouse", in relation to a person who has died and

was, at the time of his death, an eligible employee or

a retirement pensioner, means -

(a) a person who was legally married to the deceased

person at the time of the person's death and who,

at that time, was living with the person on a

permanent and bona fide domestic basis;

(b) a person who was legally married to the deceased

person at the time of the person's death but who

was not living with the person on a permanent and

bona fide domestic basis at that time, and who,

in the opinion of the Commissioner, was wholly or

substantially dependent upon the deceased person

at that time;

...."

52. Sub-section 3(1) of the Act is qualified by sub-s.3(2) which deems a person to be living with another on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis if the following requirements are satisfied, namely:

"3. (2) Where the Commissioner is of the opinion that

a person would, but for a temporary absence or an

absence resulting from illness or infirmity, have been

living with another person at any time on a permanent

and bona fide domestic basis, the first-mentioned

person shall, for the purposes of the definition of

"spouse" in sub-section (1) of this section, and of

section 9, be deemed to have been living with the

other person on a permanent and bona fide domestic

basis at that time."

53. The primary submission of counsel for the applicant was that the applicant fulfilled the requirements contained in para.(a) of the definition having regard to the deeming provisions contained in sub-s.3(2) because her absence from the house was temporary.

54. The fact that as at the date of his death the applicant and the deceased were living separately would, prima facie, indicate that the parties were no longer living in a permanent and bona fide domestic relationship although, as instanced in the decision in Re Australian National Railways Commission and Edna Edith Gerlach and Others S.86/29, delivered 13 March 1987 (Decision 3294), there may be circumstances in which living in separate abodes may not necessarily mean that the parties could no longer be regarded as living together on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis. However, under the Act, the deeming provisions come into force if the Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant would, but for a temporary absence, have been living with the deceased on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis.

55. The Tribunal accepts the applicant's evidence that at the time when she separated from the deceased on 19 December 1984, she did so unwillingly and that, apart from a short period of time during which she was angry, she did not regard the separation as permanent and was merely waiting for the time when they would be reconciled. Whilst the Tribunal has some doubts, we give the benefit of our doubts to the applicant and accept her evidence that if the deceased had not asked her to return after two weeks from their last telephone conversation on 26 April 1985, she would have taken the initiative herself and returned to the house. However, the Tribunal is not satisfied that the deceased's attitude was the same as the applicant's. The deceased had been the prime mover in the relationship with the applicant. He told the applicant to leave; he "froze" their bank account so that the applicant had no funds; and further, he stopped the applicant's Veterans' Affairs spouse pension. He also refused to pay the applicant maintenance and did not request the applicant to return, which the applicant verified in correspondence in approximately June 1985, being T5/21 in which the applicant wrote, "I 'phoned Frank every week - but although he said he was lonely without me - he did not want me to return."

56. The deceased's comments which the applicant regarded as indicating that he wished her to return, were capable of an alternative explanation. The applicant described the deceased as a stubborn man and it would have been highly unlikely, in our view, that he would have asked the applicant to return. It is questionable, also, that even if the applicant had gone to the house and asked for a cup of tea that the deceased would have taken the view that she should remain. His communications with her expressing loneliness, inability to bowl well and not eating properly are more consistent with an indication of loneliness rather than a desire to be reconciled with the applicant. No moves were made by either party to reconcile, nor were moves made to obtain a divorce. A stalemate appears to have been reached and we are not satisfied that if the deceased had not died, the parties would probably have reconciled. For these reasons, we are not satisfied that the separation of the applicant and the deceased for the period of some four months prior to his death, was a "temporary absence" within the meaning of sub-s.3(2) of the Act.

57. We therefore come to consider the alternative sub missions. The first is whether or not the absence or separation of the applicant and the deceased resulted "from illness or infirmity". There is no suggestion that the applicant was suffering from any illness or infirmity, so the only question is whether or not the deceased was so suffering, and if he was, was this the reason for their separation?

58. The deceased does not appear to have suffered from any "illness or infirmity". The evidence indicates clearly that the deceased's personality traits had the effect of making his marriages more difficult but he was not suffering from a psychiatric disorder and in this we accept the probabilities expressed by Dr Jagermann. His sexual difficulties, even if they could be described as an "illness or infirmity", which we doubt having regard to the usual usage of those words, were not the basis for the separation of the applicant and the deceased. The applicant indicated in her evidence that the sexual difficulties of the deceased were not a matter which concerned her - she was more concerned about the deceased's attitude to the problem. In any event, there is nothing to indicate that the sexual difficulties experienced by the deceased were the cause of him asking the applicant to leave, nor of the applicant leaving. We therefore do not consider that their separation resulted from "illness or infirmity" within the meaning of sub-s.3(2) of the Act.

59. This leaves the final question as to whether or not the applicant fulfills the criteria set out in para.(b) of the definition in that being legally married to the deceased, but not living with him on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis at the time of his death, she was nonetheless at the time of his death, "wholly or substantially" dependent upon him.

60. The first question is as to the meaning of "dependency". The meaning of "dependency" was canvassed at length by the Tribunal in Re Schlatter and Defence Force Retire ment and Death Benefits Authority (1985) 8 ALD 133. The Tribunal in Re Schlatter (supra) expressed the view that:

(1) dependency is a question of fact;

(2) dependency had a more technical usage than ordinary

meaning;

(3) dependency was a financial concept as distinct from an

emotional concept;

(4) dependency relates to the provision of the necessities

of life but not necessities for the standard of living

of a person in a given position or class, but rather the

standard set by the family in its pattern of

expenditure;

(5) dependency was essentially concerned with lower order

needs of food, shelter and minimal clothing;

(6) dependency is to be determined according to the position

that exists as at the date of death;

(7) in deciding whether dependency exists at the date of

death, past events and future probabilities are factors

to be considered;

(8) the existence of dependency includes consideration of

the following factors -

(i) the payment of money or the provision of

support by a deceased to a claimant;

(ii) the existence of a maintenance order for

maintenance of the claimant by a

deceased;

(iii) the deceased has a legal obligation to

support the claimant which has not been

abandoned and there is a probability of

its enforcement.

61. It is with regard to the latter that the Tribunal in Re Schlatter (supra) said that having regard to the provisions of the Family Law Act 1975, the decision-maker was to consider whether or not the deceased had a prima facie legal obligation to support the applicant as at the date of his death, having regard to the criteria set forth in ss.72 and 75 of the Family Law Act (supra) coupled with the probability of its being effectively enforced. Those observations of the Tribunal concerned Fisher and Spender JJ. in the decision in Commissioner for Superan nuation v Scott, G62 of 1986 delivered 11 March 1987, who volunteered obiter comments at pp.13 and following. Their Honours expressed the view that dependency existed "so long as he or she has a need of support, notwithstanding the fact that the need is for one reason or another not being satisfied by that other". This statement on dependency was derived from the cases of Kauri Timber Co (Tas.) Pty Limited v Reeman (1973) 128 CLR 177 and Aafjes v Kearney [1976] HCA 5; (1976) 8 ALR 455. It is clear from the statements made by both Fisher and Spender JJ. in Scott's case (supra) that their Honours were endeavouring to find a solution to the undoubted difficulties of a decision-maker trying to ascertain dependency using the accepted legal criteria of a legal obligation to support when the common law right of a wife to have support from her husband has been subsumed by the Federal legislation in the Family Law Act (supra). Their Honours were concerned to try and overcome the problem of a decision-maker being required to "stand in the shoes" of the Family Court in order to decide in a given case whether a deceased had a prima facie legal obligation to support an applicant.

62. There are a number of problems with the test formu lated by their Honours which appear, in summary, to be as follows:

(1) It is uncertain whether their Honours, in putting

forward a "needs" based test, intended to exclude what

has been a previous ground for ascertaining dependency,

namely whether or not there was a legal obligation to

support coupled with a probability of it being executed.

If their Honours did not intend to exclude the legal

obligation to support as being a relevant factor, and

that factor still applied, then the "needs" test merely

provided yet another additional test rather than a

substituted test. If, on the other hand, their Honours

intended to exclude the previously accepted legal

obligation to support, then that would remove what has

been regarded for many years as being a basis upon which

dependency could be found. It would not seem

appropriate for this Tribunal to exclude what has

previously been accepted as a basis for proving

dependency.

(2) The Commissioner in this application has submitted that

there would be grave difficulties in applying the test

of "needs" as expressed by their Honours and that at

least the Family Law Act (supra) gave guidance as to the

factors to be considered when deciding whether or not

any spouse had a legal obligation to support another.

There is no such framework under a "needs" test. More

importantly, a "needs" test does not consider whether a

concomitant ability or obligation exists for a spouse to

provide for that need, nor whether the applicant is

likely to enforce the supply of that need.

(3) There appears to be some doubt as to whether the

proposition annunciated by their Honours is in fact

supported by the authorities which their Honours quote.

63. Another matter which arises from the obiter dicta of Fisher and Spender JJ in Scott's case (supra) is whether or not moneys received by a claimant from either charity or a government pension should be disregarded in ascertaining the extent of the need in order to decide whether dependency exists. Their Honours state at p.16 of their Reasons:

"....in assessing need we would have thought it would

appear that it would be proper to disregard charity

and pension payments which have been made because the

need was not otherwise satisfied. (See per Mason J. in

Aafjes v Kearney supra at p.464 line 40)"

64. A similar view has been expressed by Lord Robson in New Monkton Collieries Limited v Keeling (1911) AC 648 at pp.662-663:

"The wife does not necessarily cease to be dependent

on the husband simply because the latter refuses to

recognise or perform his obligation and succeeds in

throwing the burden of her maintenance for the time

being on the wife's parents or friend, or on the

State. They may fulfill the husband's duty for him,

but the wife's legal dependence is still on him and

not on them, and his death deprives her of the proper

stay and support on which alone she is entitled to

rely."

65. The respondent in this case submitted that the applicant was not dependent on the deceased as at the date of his death. As at the date of death, the applicant was in receipt of an age pension upon her application and was therefore not reliant upon the deceased for support for her basic necessities of life. In the alternative, the respondent submitted that even if there was a legal obligation on the deceased to maintain the applicant as at the date of his death, having regard to the application of the Family Law Act (supra), the applicant had abandoned that right in failing to make a claim for maintenance against the deceased, and failing to assert her right to maintenance other than by making a mere single request.

66. This Tribunal considers that ignoring the provisions contained in para. 75(2)(f) of the Family Law Act (supra) (to which we shall return) there is ample precedent for disregarding any pension payable to an applicant or, if charity is given to an applicant a charity payable to an applicant, if such pension or charity is one which is given to them because they would otherwise be unable to support themselves. However, it is arguable that an age pension is different in character from, for example, a Supporting Parent's Benefit, a widow's pension or a pension providing immediate financial relief for those in need. It is a pension to which every member of the community is entitled upon reaching the relevant age so long as the assets and income tests are fulfilled. The applicant, in applying for such a pension, although she undoubtedly made such application because she had no other source of income, nonetheless chose and continued to choose to support herself by the application for and receipt of an age pension to which she had an independent right, rather than by seeking any support from her husband who had indicated to her that he would refuse to pay such an amount. It is therefore different from the situation described in New Monkton Collieries (supra) where the State provides financial relief in necessitous circumstances in the absence of a spouse providing the necessary maintenance.

67. If this be a correct analysis, then as at the date of his death, the applicant did not have any "need" of support from the deceased for her basic necessities of life as she was adequately providing these for herself through the receipt of her pension to which she was entitled by reason of her age and her application for such a pension. This pension, incidentally, would have produced a greater amount than that to which she would have been entitled pursuant to the Veterans' Entitlements Act 1986 as the spouse of a service pensioner. It should be noted that her entitlement to that Veterans' Affairs Department pension did not cease upon her separation from her husband, as the definition of "spouse" is different under the Veterans' Entitle ments Act (supra) from that contained in the Act presently under consideration.

68. If, however, this argument is incorrect, and following the dicta in Scott's case (supra) and statements contained in Aafjes (supra) and New Monkton Collieries (supra), the applicant's age pension should be disregarded for the purpose of ascertaining whether she was dependent on the deceased, then the following situation applies.

69. If the "needs" based test espoused by Fisher and Spender JJ. in Scott's case (supra) is applied to the applicant, and her age pension is disregarded, then as at the date of death of the deceased, the applicant was in need of support from the deceased for her lower order needs, as she had no means of supporting herself. She had no employment nor likelihood of obtaining employment and she was assisted by the limited charity of relatives and others for basic furniture and floor coverings. The applicant was totally in need of support and with no resort to anyone else for that other than her husband. Using this criteria she was then totally dependent on her husband as at the date of his death.

70. On the other hand, if we were to apply the criteria in Re Schlatter (supra) as previously set out in para. 60 hereof, the applicant would not fit within these considerations. No payment of money or provision was being made by the deceased to the applicant at the date of his death. There was no order for maintenance existing at his death.

71. As to whether the applicant had a prima facie legal entitlement to maintenance from the deceased having regard to the criteria contained in ss.72 and 75 of the Family Law Act (supra), it is necessary to consider and prima facie apply the relevant matters contained in sub-s.75(2) as at the date of death of the deceased. Sub-section 75(2) of the Family Law Act (supra) provides as follows:

"75.(2) The matters to be so taken into account are -

(a) the age and state of health of each of the

parties;

(b) the income, property and financial resources of

each of the parties and the physical and mental

capacity of each of them for appropriate gainful

employment;

(c) whether either party has the care or control of a

child of the marriage who has not attained the

age of 18 years;

(d) commitments of each of the parties that are

necessary to enable the party to support:

(i) himself or herself; and

(ii) a child or another person that the

party has a duty to maintain;

(e) the responsibilities of either party to support

any other person;

(f) subject to subsection (3), the eligibility of

either party for a pension, allowance or benefit

under -

(i) any law of the Commonwealth, of a

State or Territory or of another

country; or

(ii) any superannuation fund or scheme,

whether the fund or scheme was

established, or operates, within or

outside Australia,

and the rate of any such pension, allowance or

benefit being paid to either party;

(g) where the parties have separated or the marriage

has been dissolved, a standard of living that in

all the circumstances is reasonable;

(h) the extent to which the payment of maintenance to

the party whose maintenance is under considera

tion would increase the earning capacity of that

party by enabling that party to undertake a

course of education or training or to establish

himself or herself in a business or otherwise to

obtain an adequate income;

(j) the extent to which the party whose maintenance

is under consideration has contributed to the

income, earning capacity, property and financial

resources of the other party;

(k) the duration of the marriage and the extent to

which it has affected the earning capacity of the

party whose maintenance is under consideration;

(l) the need to protect a party who wishes to

continue that party's role as a parent;

(m) if either party is cohabiting with another

person - the financial circumstances relating to

the cohabitation;

(n) the terms of any order made or proposed to be

made under section 79 in relation to the property

of the parties; and

(o) any fact or circumstance which, in the opinion of

the court, the justice of the case requires to

be taken into account."

72. Considering those criteria, the most relevant aspects are that the deceased lived on a service pension and a small superannuation pension, the parties had few assets and they were both in their sixties with no likelihood of future gainful employment. The main relevant criterion is that contained in para.75(2)(f) which is specifically concerned, inter alia, with eligibility for pension. The Family Court seems to have expressed differences in the application of this sub-section when taken in conjunction with s.72 (see discussion generally in Australian Family Property Law, Hardingham and Neave, 1984 at paras. 20136-20153).

73. Various Family Court judges have, however, expressed the view that:

"....if the other party is comfortably able to

contribute a sum sufficient to ensure the adequate

support of the applicant, any question of the

applicant's entitlement to such a pension should be

ignored"

(see Kajewski (1978) FLC 90-472; also Bailey (1981) FLC 91-041 and Mathews (1980) FLC 90-887)

74. On the facts in this review, the deceased had very limited ability to pay maintenance to the applicant as he was himself the recipient of a Commonwealth pension, therefore, it would be relevant to take into account that the applicant was entitled as of right to an age pension, and the quantum of that pension.

75. At best, the applicant may only have had a prima facie entitlement to a small proportion of the deceased's superan nuation pension. However, we have grave doubts as to whether the applicant would ever have applied for or enforced a maintenance order. Whilst we do not consider the applicant to have abandoned any such prima facie legal entitlement, her acceptance of the deceased's refusal to pay maintenance, taken in conjunction with her passive acceptance of his terms of separation, make such enforcement unlikely. Applying those considerations, the applicant was not dependent on the deceased as at the date of his death.

76. This Tribunal is not bound by the obiter of Fisher and Spender JJ. and in all of the circumstances, prefers the approach adopted by the Tribunal in Re Schlatter (supra) when considering the question of dependency. For these reasons, we do not consider that the applicant was dependent on the deceased either wholly or even substantially as at the date of his death.

77. However, even adopting the "needs" based test suggested by Fisher and Spender JJ., the result would be the same if the age pension is, in the circumstances of this case, to be taken into account in ascertaining dependency, for the reasons contained in para. 66 hereof.

78. For these reasons, therefore, we consider that the decision of the delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation should be upheld, and so find.
