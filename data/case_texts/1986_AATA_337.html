M505/1984 and Commissioner of Taxation [1986] AATA 337; (1986) 18 ATR 3035; 86 ATC 1101 (3 November 1986)

Last Updated: 6 August 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - private company - investment allowance on passenger coach*

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 – s 82AB(1)(c)(i).

Godecke & Anor. v. Kirwan [1973] HCA 38; (1972-73) 129 CLR 629.

Woodslde Offshore Petroleum v. Attwood Oceanics and Offshore Oil N.L. (unreported - Full Court of S.C. of W.A. delivered 2nd May, 1985).

Masters & Anor. v. Cameron [1954] HCA 72; (1953-54) 91 CLR 353.

Branca v. Cobarro (1947) 1 KB 854.

Niestnann v. Collingridge [1921] HCA 19; (1920-21) 29 CLR. 177.

Winn v. Bull (1877-78) 7 ChD 29.

Keppel v. Wheeler & Anor. (1927) 1 KB 577.

Kearney v. F.C. of T. 84 ATC 4295.

DECISION AND REASONS

Re: M505/1984

And: Commissioner of Taxation

AAT Decision No 2966

Tribunal: Mr. K.P. Brady (Senior Member)

Mr. J.E. Stewart (Senior Member)

Mr. D.J. Trowse (Member)

3 November 1986

Place: Melbourne.

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL ]

]

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION ]

REASONS FOR DECISION

MESSRS. K.P. BRADY & J.E. STEWART (SENIOR MEMBERS) & MR. D.J. TROWSE (MEMBER)

1. The issue for determination in this reference is whether a passenger coach was acquired by the taxpayer/applicant, a private company and hereinafter called "the applicant", under a contract entered into on or after 1st January, 1976, so as to attract the investment allowance.

2. Upon the Commissioner's disallowance of the claim, and of the objection against the disallowance, that latter decision was, at the request of the applicant, referred to a Board of Review for review. The Board concluded its hearing of the matter prior to 1st July, 1986, and reserved its decision. Under the provisions of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986, the functions of Taxation Boards of Review were transferred to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal with effect from 1st July, 1986. As a consequence, the decision in the instant case, together with reasons in writing for the decision, are given pursuant to the provisions of s.43 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975.

3. At the hearing, where both parties were represented by counsel, evidence for the applicant was given by its principal shareholder who was also a director and the main operative in the company. We shall refer to that person as A. In giving his evidence, he told us that the company, B, operated a taxi service in an isolated town, V, also a coach feeder service between the town's airport and the neighbouring township of X. It seems that the airline company, which we shall call M, and which operated in that part of the State, was keen to extend its coach feeder service to other neighbouring towns, and so, in approximately mid-December of 1975, it held discussions with A with a view to his company providing a service to transport air travellers from the V airport to the township of V and to the neighbouring towns of Y and Z.

4. On 22nd December, 1975, M wrote to A's company in the following terms:

"We confirm our verbal advice that you have been awarded the contract for operation of the above service to commence from the first service on Sunday, 25th January 1976.

The award of this contract is subject to your obtaining the necessary licence from the Transport Commission and to support your application we enclose herewith copy of our letter to the Commission in this regard.

Summarising our verbal discussions it is agreed that:-

(a) You will purchase from us a 17 seat airconditoned Ansair coach at $26,000 to be confirmed by a deposit of $1,000 and the balance on delivery.

(b) You will, at your expense, have the vehicle fitted with a suitable public address system.

(c) For a period of three years commencing from the date set out above, you will operate the coach between [V] Airport and [Y] for the purpose of carrying passengers and cargo over routes and to a timetable specified by us.

(d) In return for the operation of the coach, [M company] will pay $64.00 for each return coach journey and all revenues derived from the traffic carried will be the property of [M].

(e) The rate of $64.00 shall be negotiable having regard to any movements in wages or in the cost of fuel, materials and spare parts or any changes in route as may affect the service which may occur after the date of this letter.

(f) Within a month of the date of this letter the parties will enter into a formal contract which will more fully set out the rights and obligations of the parties.

If you are in agreement that this represents a brief summary of the arrangements between us, we will be pleased if you will sign and return the attached copy of this letter signifying your acceptance in principle of the contractual arrangements. We look forward to receiving this copy and also your deposit cheque of $1,000 and trust that we can look forward to a long and satisfactory association."

5. The applicant, B company, duly signified its acceptance as requested, and M's letter was returned by mail on or about 24th December, 1975, together with a cheque for $1,000 under cover of a letter from A which was as follows:

"24th December 1975

Dear Sir,

I acknowledge receipt of your correspondence dated 22nd December, 1975, which has been duly signed as requested.

Please find enclosed my cheque for ($1,000) one thousand dollars being deposit on Ansair Coach and the balance of twenty five thousand dollars ($25,000) to be paid on delivery.

I thank you for your courtesies and considerations and look forward to a long and mutually beneficial business association.

Yours faithfully,

[Signature of A]

[B] Pty. Ltd."

6. A went on to tell us that, at the time of accepting M's offer, his company did not have sufficient funds to acquire the bus and so before advising acceptance he enquired generally as to the likelihood of securing a loan, and received overall a favourable response. It seems that that enquiry resulted in A entering into a hire purchase agreement dated 21st January, 1976, with T Acceptance Co. Ltd., wherein the cash price was stated to be $26,750, comprising the coach itself $26,000, plus cost of cassette tapes and microphone equipment amounting to $750. The agreement which was tendered in evidence showed the hirer to be A (as opposed to being B) and covered a hire period of three years. Also tendered in evidence was a copy of a debit note dated 19th January, 1976, raised by M in regard to the coach purchase, whereby the hire purchase company was debited with the amount of $26,750 and B credited with a like amount, which had the effect of transferring B's indebtedness to the former company. A told us that he delivered M's invoice to the hire purchase company on 21st January, 1976, and obtained its cheque which he then took back to M. On the same day he took delivery of the coach, which had been fitted out with the required public address system on or about 14th January, 1976, some few days after registration of the vehicle was made in B's name. With those things done, M, under date of 14th January, 1976, invoiced B for the cost of the bus (less deposit) and the cost of the p.a. system.

7. It is appropriate to mention at this juncture that, at the hearing, A conceded that the moneys expended on the above system were expressly excluded from the investment allowance by the operation of s.82AF(2)(d) forming part of Subdivision B of the Assessment Act. Accordingly, the applicant's claim was reduced to cover the coach only, viz. 40% of $26,000 = $10,400.

8. The narrative as to the financial aspects may be completed by mentioning that the $1,000 deposit was refunded to B by M per medium of a cheque dated 20th January, 1976.

9. A told us that his company commenced the service between V Airport and the townships of V, X and Y on 25th January, 1976, and, whilst he received verbal advice as to approval of the licence some time before that date, he did not receive the formal documents until after he commenced the new service. He concluded his factual account by advising that the formal contract between his company and M was not executed until 17th March, 1976, some two months later than the time stipulated in M's letter of 22nd December, 1975. A, in giving his evidence, conceded that the formal contract covered the same basic matters as were contained in the above letter (see pp.24 and 25 of the transcript).

10. It was agreed by the representatives of both parties that the issue before us for determination was the very narrow one of whether the contract for the purchase of the coach was entered into on or after 1st January, 1976, and so complied with s.82AB(1)(c)(i), agreement having been reached that the bus was new and was a unit of eligible property as defined in s.82AQ(1); also, that it was acquired by B for use by it wholly and exclusively in Australia and for the purpose of producing assessable income (refer ss.82AA(a) and 82AQ(3)(4)(i). The above question in essence amounts to determining whether M's letter dated 22nd December, 1975, and A's acceptance of its terms was a final and binding agreement. If it were, the expenditure otherwise "eligible" could not be said to have been incurred within the stipulated dates. That basically was the argument of counsel for the Commissioner. On the other hand, counsel for the applicant argued that the offer and acceptance arrangement of 24th December, 1975, was concerned not only with purchase of the bus but with using it in extending the feeder service from the airport, which latter function was dependent upon receiving the approval of the appropriate transport authority. That approval, as we have noted earlier, was not obtained until January of 1976. Accordingly (so the argument ran), the property in the goods did not pass to B company on 24th December, 1975, as asserted by the Commissioner, and indeed did not pass to it until execution of the hire purchase agreement on 21st January, 1976. It is to a consideration of those matters that we now turn.

11. Where, as in the situation before us, there is a dispute as to whether the terms of an exchange of letters represent a binding contract, or merely a basis for the negotiation of a contract to be completed at some time in the future, it is a question of construction of the documentation as to what the parties have in fact decided (see Godecke & Anor. v. Kirwan [1973] HCA 38; (1972-73) 129 C.L.R. 629 at p.638, and more recently Woodside Offshore Petroleum v. Attwood Oceanics and Offshore Oil N.L., an unreported decision of the Full Court of the Supreme Court of Western Australia delivered 2nd May, 1985). On the basis of correspondence dated 22nd and 24th December, 1975, the parties in the instant case have agreed that there should be a sale and a purchase of a specific type of coach and the price and terms of payment are clearly settled, also the charter service to be performed by the coach and the payment to be made for such service by the charterer. Furthermore, the documentation meets the requirements of the Sale of Goods Act and is signed by the person to be charged, i.e. by A on behalf of his company, B. Thus, all the essentials of a contract are present. But whether there is a contract depends upon the meaning and effect of the words contained in paragraph (f) of the summary of the arrangement, viz:

"... it is agreed that:

Within a month of the date of this letter, the parties will enter into a formal contract which will set out more clearly the rights and obligations of the parties."

12. So, pending the execution of the formal contract which took place on 17th March, 1976, could it be said that there was a binding agreement in force as from 24th December, 1975, or was the situation one where no party was bound until the formal contract came into force some 12 weeks later?

13. The leading Australian case on the issue is Masters & Anor. v. Cameron [1954] HCA 72; (1953-54) 91 C.L.R. 353. In that case, at pp.360-361, the Full High Court categorised the various situations as follows:

"Where parties who have been in negotiation reach agreement upon terms of a contractual nature and also agree that the matter of their negotiation shall be dealt with by a formal contract, the case may belong to any of three classes. It may be one in which the parties have reached finality in arranging all the terms of their bargain and intend to be immediately bound to the performance of those terms, but at the same time propose to have the terms restated in a form which will be fuller or more precise but not different in effect. Or, secondly, it may be a case in which the parties have completely agreed upon all the terms of their bargain and intend no departure from or addition to that which their agreed terms express or imply, but nevertheless have made performance of one or more of the terms conditional upon the execution of a formal document. Or, thirdly, the case may be one in which the intention of the parties is not to make a concluded bargain at all, unless and until they execute a formal contract."

The Court went on to say:

"In each of the first two cases there is a binding contract: in the first case a contract binding the parties at once to perform the agreed terms whether the contemplated formal document comes into existence or not, and to join (if they have so agreed) in settling and executing the formal document; and in the second case a contract binding the parties to join in bringing the formal contract into existence and then to carry it into execution....

Cases of the third class are fundamentally different. They are cases in which the terms of agreement are not intended to have, and therefore do not have, any binding effect of their own.... The parties may have so provided either because they have dealt only with major matters and contemplate that others will or may be regulated by provisions to be introduced into the formal document, as in Summergreene v. Parker [1950] HCA 13; (1950) 80 C.L.R. 304, or simply because they wish to reserve to themselves a right to withdraw at any time until the formal document is signed."

14. An example of a case falling within the first class is Branca v. Cobarro (1947) 1 K.B. 854. There, a written agreement for the sale of a mushroom farm was stated to be a "provisional agreement until a fully legalised agreement" was drawn up and signed. It was held by the Court of Appeal reversing a decision of Denning J. (as he then was) that the provisional agreement was fully effective until such time (if ever) that the further agreement was drawn up and signed. Consequently, neither party could withdraw.

15. The case of Niesmann v. Collingridge [1921] HCA 19; (1920-21) 29 C.L.R. 177 is an example of the second class. There, the appellant agreed in writing to give the respondent "the firm offer" of his farm, and the written agreement stated that the price was to be paid as to $1,000 on the signing of the contract, as to $500 three months afterwards, and as to the balance within three years of the signing of the contract. The respondent accepted the offer. The High Court held that the use of the term "firm offer" indicated that the parties must have intended that a binding contract would result from the acceptance of that offer and that the reference to the signing of a contract merely meant that a formal document embodying the terms of the agreement was to be signed before the first instalment of the purchase price became payable.

16. Masters v. Cameron (supra) is an example of the third class of case where the courts have held that a particular document did not constitute a binding contract. There, by an agreement in writing, the respondent agreed to sell and the appellants agreed to purchase a farming property subject to the preparation of a formal contract of sale acceptable to the solicitors of the respondents on the terms and conditions set out in the agreement and to the giving of possession by a stipulated date. The Court held that expressions such as "subject to contract", "subject to the preparation of a formal contract" and others of similar import prima facie create an overriding condition so that what has been agreed upon must be regarded as the intended basis for a future contract and not as constituting a contract of itself. In its judgment, the Court quoted with approval the following dictum of Sir George Jessel, M.R. in Winn v. Bull (1877-78) 7 Ch.D. 29 at p.32:

"It comes, therefore, to this, that where you have a proposal or agreement made in writing expressed to be subject to a formal contract being prepared, it means what it says; it is subject to and is dependent upon a formal contract being prepared."

17. In our view, the situation before us belongs to the first of the three classes categorised above by the High Court. In other words, we believe that, by the exchange of letters, A and M had reached finality and intended to be immediately bound, but agreed to have the terms restated in a more comprehensive form by way of a subsequently drafted formal contract.

18. It is worth noting that paragraph (f) of M's letter of 22nd December, 1975, makes no mention of expressions such as "subject to the preparation of a formal contract" or "subject to contract", which terms suggest that what has been agreed upon must be regarded as the intended basis for a future contract, and not as constituting a contract per se. The legal position seems clear that when a person accepts an offer subject to contract, it means that the matter remains in negotiation until a formal contract is settled and the formal contracts are exchanged (see Keppel v. Wheeler & Anor. (1927) 1 K.B. 577 at p.584). However, we believe that that is not the sort of situation before us in the instant case.

19. It is, we believe, of some significance that the December correspondence is cast in the traditional language of offer and acceptance, and it seems difficult to imagine that the parties did not conceive themselves as entering into some form of legally binding obligation. Also, as the coach was to commence the new service as from the succeeding 25th January, it was manifestly the intention and indeed the mutual desire of both A and M to make a contract as soon as possible (see Kearney v. F.C. of T. 84 ATC 4295, where a catamaran was held to have been contracted for by a particular date even though at that time a condition relating to the obtaining of finance had not been fulfilled).

20. The formal contract of 17th March, 1976, was tendered in evidence and, apart from providing detail on relatively mundane matters, it reiterated the basic point contained in M's earlier letter of offer. In our view, the summary of the terms of M's offer as contained in that letter are stated in very explicit language, and we believe that when A advised his company's acceptance he considered that it was bound by the arrangement as from that date. Moreover, subsequent events, we consider, support that belief, and it is noteworthy that all such events took place before the date when the formal contract was executed, namely 17th March, 1976:

  • On 12th January, 1976, the coach was registered in B's name (although it was not clear as to which party arranged the registration).
  • On 14th January, A arranged for the p.a. system to be installed in the coach and, on the same day, M invoiced B for the cost of the bus (less deposit), plus the cost of the p.a. system.
  • On 21st January, A took delivery of the coach having first inspected it, both before notifying his acceptance and after completing such action.
  • On 25th January, the agreed date, B company put the coach into service, and presumably commenced to earn the amounts of income described in paragraph (d) of M's letter of offer.

21. Also, one cannot help but feel that both M and A were singularly inert as regards putting pressure on the solicitors to finalise the formal contract, unless they believed that that document was nothing more than a desirable appendage to the arrangement which had been made binding on the parties by the exchange of the letters. The above acts of the parties, particularly those of A, are only consistent with the belief of being bound by the arrangement of 24th December. They are hardly the actions of business people who believe that they are still at the stage of negotiating a contract's terms.

22. Lastly on this same point, we quote from Cheshire and Fifoot's "Law of Contract", Third Australian Edition by J.G. Starke and P.F.P. Higgins at p.34, which words, we consider, are very apposite to the parties in the instant case:

"It would appear ... that, whenever there is evidence that the parties have acted upon the faith of a written document, the courts will prefer to assume that the document embodies a definite intention to be bound and will strive to implement its terms. Such at least will be the instinct of a judge in a commercial transaction, where the the parties are engaged in a particular trade and may be taken to have accepted its special and familiar usages as the background of their bargain."

23. In making his submissions, the representative of the applicant seemed equivocal as to whether the exchange of letters amounted to a binding contract, but he asserted that, even if there were, the property in the goods did not pass to B company on 24th December, 1975, and, in fact, it did not pass from M to anyone until the coach was acquired by the hire purchase company on 21st January, 1976. He contended that, if there were in fact an agreement comprising the letters of 22nd and 24th December, then it was mutually rescinded when A entered into the hire purchase agreement. From that stance, the representative argued that B did not acquire the coach from M but acquired it, for purposes of the subdivision, through the operation of s.82AQ(3), which, to the extent considered relevant, states:

"In this subdivision –

(a) a reference to the acquisition of property by a person is a reference to –

(i) the person becoming the owner of the property or taking the property on hire under a hire-purchase agreement".

His conclusion was (and here we paraphrase s.82(1)(c)) that, as the eligible expenditure was incurred in respect of a unit of property acquired by his client under a contract entered into on or after 1st January, 1976, and before 1st July 1983 (later amended to 1st July, 1985), the company was properly entitled to the investment allowance.

24. Predictably, counsel for the Commissioner did not accept the above contentions. He submitted, properly in our view, that the hire purchase arrangement which A entered into with T on 21st January, could not affect A's agreement with M. That latter agreement was not expressed to be subject to finance, and the fact that A, in arranging his company's funds, entered into a financing arrangement with another party cannot be seen as impinging upon its obligations to carry out the terms of the agreement made with M. All that he was then doing was obtaining the necessary finance so that his company could honour an agreement which was already in train. As regards the further point made by the taxpayer's counsel that the property in the coach could not pass to B until the licence from the Transport Commission had been obtained, that licence only related to the operation of the coach on a particular route and did not, and indeed could not, affect the obligations of the applicant as regards the agreement made with M. Counsel for the Commissioner went on to contend that the property in the coach passed to B upon that company advising its acceptance to M's letter of offer on 24th December, 1975, and referred us to Rule 1 of Clause 18 of the Sale of Goods Act. We consider that that submission is clearly correct.

25. For the reasons outlined above, we consider that the passenger coach was acquired by the applicant under a contract entered into prior to 1st January, 1976, and therefore the deduction claimed is contrary to the requirements of s.82AB(1)(c)(i). Accordingly, we affirm the Commissioner's decision on the objection under review.
