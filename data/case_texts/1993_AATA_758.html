DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



TAXATION - Applicant Company reassessed in the sum of $61,700 being undeclared income. Managing Director of Company alleged he made a loan to the Company from his gambling wins. No sufficient evidence to support claim.



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - S190(b)



Gauci v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 54; 135 CLR 81 at p89

George v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 21; 86 CLR 183

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL ) No. NT93/40

)

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re:

Applicant



And: DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



Respondent



REASONS FOR DECISION



24 December 1993 Senior Member M.D. Allen





1. The Applicant taxpayer is a Company B which buys and sells motor vehicles. Following investigation of its affairs by the Respondent an amended assessment was issued to the Applicant on 25 September 1987 respecting the income gained by it for the tax year ended 30 June 1985.



2. By Notice of Objection dated 20 November 1987 the taxpayer objected to that assessment and claimed the income of the Applicant Company had been over assessed and should be reduced by the sum of $61,700.



3. Objection was also made respecting a culpability component of the reassessment which was imposed at a rate of 40%.



4. The said objection being dismissed the Applicant, by notice received 19 November 1990, sought review by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. The said application for review came on for hearing before me at Sydney on 15 December 1993.



5. At that review the Applicant was represented by its Managing Director T. T and his wife are the sole shareholders in the Applicant Company and part of the difficulties which have arisen and given rise to this matter is that T has regarded the Applicant as an emanation of himself and his funds and the Applicants have been intermingled. For example, T stated in evidence that he does not have any bank accounts but puts his money into the Company accounts.



6. The Company objected to the reassessment on the basis that the sum of $61,700 was not income gained or received by the Company but was a loan to it by T which loan had been repaid. For his part T claimed that the sum of $61,700 had been gained by him from successful betting on horse races.



7. T gave evidence in these proceedings and specifically made the point that he did not so much "gamble" on horses as make investments on horses regarding which he had obtained information as to their current ability and likely performance.



8. Unfortunately this case was poorly presented on both sides. T, as agent for the taxpayer, stated that he has dispensed with the services of the accountants who had prepared the Company's tax returns and represented it in the matter to the stage when it was set down for trial. It is noted that there was no notification to the Tribunal that the accountants for the Company had ceased to act.



9. So far as the Respondent is concerned I am satisfied that it was content to rest upon the provisions of S190(b) of the Income Assessment Act 1936 which provides that the burden of proving that assessments were excessive lies upon the taxpayer.



10. As an example of where the Respondent could have mounted a proper response to the Applicant's case, I refer to copies of TAB betting tickets which form document T12 in the documents prepared for these proceedings pursuant to S37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. The Respondent disputed the claim by T that these tickets represented wins in the tax year 1984/5 claiming that the tickets were from the year 1986. The tickets themselves only record a day and a month but no year. They do, however, record the horse's name. To give an example, I would have thought that information could have been obtained from the records of the TAB or the Australian Jockey Club as to what year the horse felicitously names "What a Nuisance" won or was placed in a race run on the Guyfalks Day, that is, 5 November.



11. Furthermore, it was claimed that some of the dates on the tickets were in the years 1984 or 1985 Sundays. T stated that, if this was the case, it represented meetings held in the Australian Capital Territory. Again, this could have been the subject of real evidence in order to rebut T's claims.



12. In cross-examination the Respondent's representative put to T allegations of fraud amounting to criminal activity, yet no scintilla of evidence was adduced to support these allegations.



13. T's evidence was that he had at all times during the relevant tax year, been a successful punter. He did not bet on newspaper or other "form" guides but because he himself owned horses and assisted with the training of others, he had cultivated trainers and jockeys and other racecourse hangers on who would provide him with information. This information then formed a basis upon which he placed his bets.



14. In support of this evidence T produced a teledex containing the names and telephone numbers of prominent trainers and jockeys. He also produced more winning betting tickets from the TAB and three receipts from race clubs verifying that he had at least on three occasions received a cheque as being the owner of a winning horse.



15. The purport of this evidence was to demonstrate to the Tribunal that T was a owner of successful horses and was also a successful punter with contacts in the racing industry.



16. Unfortunately for T the betting tickets produced bear no year, consequently their evidentiary value vis-a-vis the tax year in question is slight. Similarly the notifications which accompanied the winning owner's cheques referred to the calendar year 1986. The fact that T had in his Teledex the names and telephone numbers of jockeys and trainers does not evidence the relationship between T and those persons.



17. Furthermore, I find it difficult to accept T's evidence that he did not bet apart from the tips. He stated in evidence that he always placed his bets at the Forest Lodge TAB because, amongst other reasons, it was lucky for him. He also stated "you bet when you feel lucky". I note also that it was conceded by the Respondent that T had had a considerable win on Tatts Lotto which, of course, is a game of chance.



18. T also placed bets with a starting price bookmaker. He has stated to the Respondent the winnings he had from that bookmaker. Apparently, however, that bookmaker paid the Applicant in cash, consequently no verification of those wins was possible.



19. No record was kept by T of his betting activities. He stated he kept the winning TAB tickets as he liked to put them into a drawer as souvenirs and to look at them. But, as stated above, they bear no year date.



20. The tax investigator who audited the affairs of the Applicant Company gave evidence. I accept his evidence that the books of the Company were not complete, although I also accept T's evidence that this was due to the illness of the person who usually wrote them up. However, I have no reason to doubt the investigator's evidence that there was a discrepancy in the Company's books of account. It is this discrepancy which is sought to be explained by T by reference to his betting.



21. The investigator stated that the Applicant's Tax Agent had conceded to him that the betting tickets produced by T during the audit of the Company's accounts referred to the year 1986. I reject this evidence as there is no proof that the Tax Agent was authorised to make that admission and they were specifically denied by T in these proceedings.



22. Having regard to the evidence adduced I am prepared to make the following findings, namely:

(i) During the tax year in question T was a person who owned horses and made money by gambling on horses using information which was given to him by other persons in the racing industry.



(ii) I am not prepared to find that T only had winning bets during this period or at any period.



(iii) I disbelieve the Applicant when he states that he only bet on the tips received. To me this is incompatible with his habit at only betting at the Forest Lodge TAB as it was, amongst other things, lucky for him.



(iv) No records were kept by the Applicant of his betting activities.



23. It was submitted for the Commissioner that T's activities amounted to a business. On the evidence adduced I am not prepared to find that the betting and associated horse racing activities amounted to a business as opposed to a profitable hobby, albeit a hobby to which the Applicant devoted not inconsiderable time.



24. As stated above, S190(b) of the Income Assessment Act 1936 provides that the burden of proving that the assessment was excessive lies upon the taxpayer. As the objection decision was notified to the Applicant in 1990 (the actual document notifying the objection decision is undated), S190(b) applies in these proceedings - see S116 of the Taxation Laws Amendment Act (No 3) 1991.



25. In George v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1952] HCA 21; 86 CLR 183 at p201 the Court said:

"the law has always been taken to be that in an appeal from an assessment the burden lies upon the taxpayer of establishing affirmatively that the amount of taxable income for which he has been assessed exceeds the actual taxable income which he has derived during the year of income."



And in Gauci and The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1975] HCA 54; 135 CLR 81 Mason J (as he then was) at p89 said:

"The Act does not place any onus on the Commissioner to show that the assessments were correctly made. Nor is there any statutory requirement that the assessments should be sustained or supported by evidence. The implication of such a requirement would be inconsistent with s.190(b) for it is a consequence of that provision that unless the appellant shows by evidence that the assessment is incorrect, it will prevail."



The passage quoted above from Mason J was from a dissenting judgment but the view of S190(b) espoused by Mason J is now the accepted view - see Macmine Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation 53 ALJR 362 and McCormack v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 143 CLR ...



26. The betting tickets and other items produced by T simply lack specificity as to the tax year in question. Whereas I am prepared to find that T did win money during the relevant tax year I do not accept he had no losses but, more importantly, I am not satisfied on the balance of probabilities that the sum of $61,700 came from T's betting activities.



27. There was no material placed before me which would justify interfering with the decision to impose a penalty at the rate of 40%.



28. The decision under review is therefore affirmed.
