ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX – assessable income - medical practitioner -one-man practice - whether "cash receipts" basis of assessment appropriate.

INCOME TAX - assessable income - medical practitioner - one-man practice - assessment of book debts by way of gift whether assigned debts constitute 'income - whether income that of assignor or assignee - whether income derived on "cash receipts" basis.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss. 19, 36, 260.

Shepherd v. F.C.T. [1965] HCA 70; (1965) 113 C.L.R. 385;

Norman v. F.C.T. [1963] HCA 21; (1963) 109 C.L.R. 9;

Eisner v. Macomber [1919] USSC 119; (1919) 252 U.S. 189;

F.C.T. v. D.P. Smith [1981] HCA 10; (1981) 147 C.L.R. 578.

Smith v. Stretton (1904) 5 T.C. 36;

Commissioner of Taxes (S.A.) v. The Executor Trustee & Agency

Company of South Australia Limited [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 C.L.R. 108;

Fainsworth v. F.C.T. [1949] HCA 27; (1949) 78 C.L.R. 504.

REASONS FOR DECISION

9 September 1988 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. The Applicant is a medical practitioner who was born in 1944. Since the early 1970's he has practised as a specialist radiologist in a regional city. (I shall refer to him as "Ron"). At all material times prior to 1982 he practised alone on his own account. As a radiologist all his patients were referred to him by other medical practitioners. He carried out the required radiological examinations and provided his professional assessment of the results. He maintained his medical and fee records with simple efficiency. In a single strike a receptionist typed the personal particulars and fee particulars on a set of sheets incorporating such information on each sheet as was appropriate to its purpose. When the patient left the surgery he received one copy providing details of the amount for which he was indebted. A second document in the set recorded the medical assessment made by Ron and was delivered to the referring practitioner. The final copy was retained by Ron as his record of the service rendered; of his examination and professional assessment; and the amount of the patient's indebtedness.

  1. Ron was fortunate in his practice and in that he practised at a time when insurance schemes assured him of payment of a large part of his fees, and that nearly all in need of his services were able to afford them. In fact some 70%-75% of his patients paid within six weeks of service being provided although one group, representing possibly 25% of his fee income, a group of patients involved in Third Party and Workers' Compensation claims paid much more slowly - commonly only at the conclusion of litigation - and occasionally at intervals of up to ten years from the rendering of service. However, Ron estimated that bad debts probably never exceeded 3% of all fees rendered.
    1. The conduct of the practice involved Ron in substantial outlay on capital equipment. It also required the services of professional and clerical support personnel. At all material times he engaged the services of three full-time and one part-time radiographers and three full-time and one part-time secretaries. One of the full-time radiographers was Ron's wife, Nancy. Save for periods of maternity leave, Nancy worked full-time in the practice over many years. I accept that she was paid a salary while working; that, measured by the standard of what was paid to others, the salary paid to her was reasonable; and that, while she was in receipt of that salary, the moneys she earned were used to meet the ordinary domestic expenses of the household. However, I also find that, whenever additional moneys were needed for the purposes of the family, and whether she was working or not, Ron provided for all that was necessary.
  2. The operations of the practice were so organized that, throughout the year of income ended 30 June 1981, a "service company" controlled by Ron, but of which he and Nancy were Directors, operated to provide services needed by Ron in his practice. I find that the business carried on by that company was carried on by it as trustee for a family trust of which Nancy and the children - but not Ron - were beneficiaries.
  3. Ron prospered. By 30 June 1980 he owned several rented properties and by 30 June 1981 there were eight such properties, one of which only was owned jointly with Nancy. In that year the expenses incurred by Ron ($52,457) substantially exceeded income derived ($21,489) and the loss ($30,968) was claimed and allowed as a tax deduction.
  4. By the early 1980's, Ron and Nancy were observing the breakdown of many marriages among their acquaintances. That was a matter of some concern to them both, but to Nancy in particular. She felt inadequate in relation to her contribution to the prosperity of the family and that she lacked control of substantial resources in her own right. In a society which seems to accept that what cannot be measured in money terms has no value, I accept that that was how she felt. But I do not accept that that was a consideration which played any significant part in what followed.

7. As 30 June 1981 approached, Ron's situation with his practice was that - as usual - he had quite large sums due to him in unpaid fees - approaching 10% of annual fees; that his outgoings (including payments to the service company ) were of the order of 80% of fees received per annum; and that his income tax returns were presented to the Commissioner on a "cash receipts" basis. The appropriateness of so presenting his income tax returns was not in dispute until the year ended 30 June 1981.

8. By early in the calendar year 1981 Ron had had in train many undertakings, the effect of which would be likely to result - assuming them to be effective - in Ron not having to make any contribution by way of income tax to the public revenues which were in large measure the source of his prosperity. Sometime before mid-April 1981 Ron was advised by his accountant that there was a course of action open to him which would reduce his tax liability; would enable him to increase the funds of Nancy, while diminishing his own resources to the same extent; and increase the resources available to the family by avoiding any liability in Nancy to income tax on the funds proposed to become hers. Having satisfied himself that the proposal was feasible, he advised Nancy of the proposal. She did not decline his offer. She had no reason to do so.

9. As a result Ron spent much of the weekend of 12/13 April 1981 going through all his patient records to identify all unpaid accounts which had been rendered. He composed a list of debtors and of their debts which he carefully and clearly detailed in 25 pages of handwritten foolscap. The debtors were grouped as individuals (in alphabetical order) and by Government authorities. The total of debts so listed amounted to $35,371. Ron caused each of the cards so identified to be marked boldly with a distinguishing coloured stripe. On Saturday 12 April 1981 Ron also executed a Power of Attorney in favour of two solicitors empowering them jointly or severally "to transfer, assign, convey or otherwise alienate whether by way of gift or for full consideration personal property of the appointor". No limitation was expressed as to the identity of any transferee. The Deed authorised the attorneys to do whatever might be necessary or incidental to the effective exercise of that power. Having done so I find that Ron forwarded the executed Power of Attorney and the schedule of debts to his accountants.

10. On 15 April, 1981 one of the two solicitors executed a "Deed of Gift of Book Debts" in Canberra. The parties to the Deed were Ron (as Donor) and Nancy (as Donee). By Clause 4.1 it was provided:

"In consideration of his affection and regard for the Donee, the Donor hereby assigns to the Donee all the Donor's right, title and interest in and to ninety-nine percent (99%) of each of the debts comprising the Book Debts to hold the same unto the Donee absolutely".

The Book Debts were defined by the Deed as those more particularly described in the Schedule which was annexed to the Deed, that being the schedule prepared by Ron the previous weekend.

It was expressly provided by Clause 4.3:

"This Deed shall operate and be effective as from the date upon which it was executed by the Donor".

  1. There was no clear evidence as to when either Ron or Nancy became aware that the Deed of Gift had been executed. However, at some point prior to 29 April 1981 Ron arranged for a new bank account to be opened with his bankers. The account was opened in the name of Ron, but distinguished by the description "Book Debt Account No. 1" (the "Book Debt Account"). Ron was certainly a signatory to the account. Nancy may have been, as it was common practice that she should be empowered to operate bank accounts opened in his name. However, there is no suggestion that she ever operated the Book Debt Account, or that it was ever expected that she would do so. It is also quite clear upon the evidence that no debtor was ever advised of the assignment.
  2. Ron instructed the surgery staff that all amounts received in respect of the assigned accounts - readily identifiable to them by reason of the coloured stripe - were to be banked to the Book Debt Account.
  3. The first entry to that account was made on 29 April 1981. Then and thereafter credits and debits were made as follows:

In the next 25 months the credit balance of $46.31 was whittled away by bank fees and "FGOV Tax" until it fell into overdraft. In February the account was closed following the deposit of $41.69.

14. It is to be noted that by 30 June 1981 the credit balance in the account stood at $13,640.40 but only after a deduction of $2.50 for a cheque book. I find that up to and including 30 June 1981 the amount received by Ron in payment of the assigned debts was $13,642.90; that thereafter a further $10,460 was both received and credited to the Book Debt Account; and that later as much as another $12,000 was received by Ron but not credited to the Book Debt Account or paid to Nancy. In addition the evidence satisfies me:

(a) that the amount of $300 charged as an establishment fee on 28 July 1981 was transferred to another account of Ron on 1 December, 1981;

(b) that the amount of $19,932.66 drawn on 1 December 1981 was paid to Nancy in pro tanto satisfaction of her entitlement under the Deed of Gift; that the amount was deposited by her to her loan account with the family company (trustee of the family trust) interest free; and that the funds were then used by that company in funding the acquisition of a particular property;

(c) that the amount of $2,871.00 drawn on 25 November 1982 was received by Nancy in pro tanto satisfaction of her entitlement under the Deed of Gift and was used to her own purposes;

(d) that Nancy drew on her loan account with the family company a sum sufficient to pay $37,683.22 to the Commissioner on 17 December, 1984 on account of, or in satisfaction of, her indebtedness to him;

(e) that the amounts of $201.34 and $29.00 were drawn by Ron and deposited by him to his practice account and thereafter returned by him as income;

(f) that only the amounts last mentioned were subjected to assessment to income tax;

(g) that further amounts - possibly by this date of the order of $12,000 - neither having been treated by Ron as held in trust as to 99% or at all for Nancy nor claimed beneficially by Nancy, have been received by Ron to the credit of his practice account and returned as income of the practice;

(h) that Ron only acknowledged $230 of the $13,642.90 as assessable income and then only after 30 June 1981;

(i) that Ron has appropriated to himself all of the payments made in excess of $22,303 and has accounted for the moneys so received as assessable income; and

(j) that Nancy has never acknowledged any amount as assessable income.

15. On some date unknown Ron completed his income tax return as prepared by his accountants for the year of income ended 30 June 1981. In doing so he reported a loss of $254,146, notwithstanding that he had returned a net profit from his practice of $70,229 (gross fees $415,954 less expenses $345,725 -a loss only partly explained by the net loss of $30,968 on real estate investments. In presenting that return he reported (inter alia):

"Payments to Associated Persons;

The Taxpayer paid his spouse a salary as a fulltime radiographer/secretary employed in the practice.

On 15th April 1981 the Taxpayer assigned for no consideration 99% interest in book debts valued at $27806 to (his wife). The Taxpayer has received legal advice that such debts do not constitute assessable income. Of the debts assigned $13641 had been paid up at 30th June 1981."

I find that the amount paid by debtors in respect of the assigned debts actually amounted to $13,642.90 at 30 June 1981.

  1. On 4 May 1982 the Commissioner assessed the Applicant and adjusted the loss returned to fix upon a taxable income of $106,246. One adjustment was to treat as an item increasing taxable income: "book debts included in assessable income $27,806". (I find that the figure of $27,806 expresses neither the interest assigned to, nor the benefit received by, Nancy. It originates with the passage quoted from the return of income presented by Ron. No basis for the "valuation" was disclosed.) On 30 June, 1982 Ron objected to the assessment so made. By the commencement of the hearing all grounds of objection other than that relating to the assignment of book debts had been abandoned.
  2. The case for the Applicant put at its simplest is very simple indeed. It involves two propositions: first that a "cash receipts" basis is the most appropriate basis for the assessment of the Applicant; and secondly, that the Applicant did not "receive" the $27,306 or any part thereof in such circumstances as to make him liable to assessment in relation to any part of the $27,806 upon the cash receipts basis. Therefore, it is said, the assessment of taxable income is excessive.

18. Counsel for the Commissioner replied to those contentions by arguing:

  1. that for Ron in the year of income ended 30 June 1981 an "accruals" basis of assessment was the more appropriate basis for the assessment of his liability to income tax; and, alternatively,
  2. that there was a "constructive derivation" or receipt of the amount of debts at the point of assignment;
  3. that s.19 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") deems the assigned interest to have been derived at point of assignment;
  4. that the assignment was a sham such that the moneys received and deposited to the Number 1 account were in fact derived beneficially by Ron as a reward for his personal services; and
  5. that s.260 of the Act applies to render void against the Commissioner the entire transaction.

19. Underlying the issue between the Applicant and the Respondent is a dispute as to whether the moneys received by the Applicant constituted assessable income at all. Counsel for the Applicant contends that the moneys were neither assessable to the Applicant nor to his spouse.

20. In relation to a business of providing personal services such as the medical practice carried on by Ron, questions which arise for determination are:

- What constituted assessable income generated by that practice?

- Who derived the assessable income generated by that practice?

- When was assessable income derived from that practice? and

- In what sums was assessable income derived from .that practice?

21. Similar questions arise in relation to other types of income: for example

- Interest on loan moneys;

- Dividends on shares;

- Royalties in respect of intellectual property; and

- Rents in relation to real estate.

22. In each case the income is seen as something produced from and separated from that in which the income is sourced. That source may be recognized as a species of property recognized by law (the loan moneys, the shares, the copyright, the freehold) or it may be seen as personal exertion but expressed for legal purposes as a legal relationship such as a contract for reward; a derivation by assignment; or a gift. But whether the source of the income be regarded as property or personal services or a combination of both it is recognized as having the character of income by reason of that source, or its relationship to that source, as assessed at a point of time. It is then taken into account in measuring the income derived by relevant persons over particular periods of time.

23. If the right to income is sourced in property it is recognized that the right to the income or future income may be transferred by way of sale or gift along with the right of property giving rise to it. In those circumstances the right to the income passes with the property which is the source of the right to the income. Similarly, it is recognized that the person having the right to future income may assign the right to that future income to another without divesting himself of an entitlement to the property giving rise to the income. In that way the right to income can not only be assigned, but it can be assigned so that, upon the prospect of the right to income becoming a vested right to income, that income is considered to be derived by the assignee - despite him not having an interest in the property generating the right [Shepherd v. F.C.T. [1965] HCA 70; (1965) 113 C.L.R. 385 and, in particular, Kitto J. at p.396]. In such cases the income is said to be derived by him by reason of property rights held as assignee, regardless of whether he is entitled to the benefit of the assignment by reason of valuable consideration given by him or by way of gift. Furthermore, the "income" so generated in the hands of the assignee by the assignment retains its character as "income" in the hands of the assignee, notwithstanding that it may come to the assignee as a single payment; an unsolicited entitlement gratuitously conferred; and without the prospect of repetition. But on the other hand, if the entitlement to the income (be it by way of interest, dividends, royalties, or rents) arises before any such assignment is made or takes effect, the income so generated is considered as having been "derived" by the assignor, notwithstanding any subsequent actions on his part in vesting in any assignee an entitlement to receive the moneys [Norman v. F.C.T. [1963] HCA 21; (1963) 109 C.L.R. 9].

  1. But when a person provides personal services (whether the services of himself, or of others or of both) for reward, the source of the income is considered as being the services rendered by that individual. That is so even though, as here, substantial capital may be involved. It is those services which generate the right to receive moneys by way of income. The right to receive the income has its foundation in the personal service. The income is considered as the product of that service.
  2. In consequence, where, as here, the right to payment was complete in all respects upon the rendering of the service and presentation of the account, the right to that income is an entitlement which then subsisted in the person providing the service. It was his right to income. In the circumstances of this case the person entitled to receive that income was Ron.
  3. Whether such an entitlement will ever give rise to assessable income in the hands of the person entitled will depend upon whether, in consequence of the right, anything is ever "derived". Further, when it will have to be brought to account will depend upon "when" it will be considered to have been derived; and what amount will be considered as having been derived as assessable income will depend upon the value of what it is that is considered to be derived.
  4. Applying those considerations in relation to the affairs of the Applicant, but for immediate purposes setting aside from consideration the question of any assignment, debts which were for ever to remain unpaid by the debtor would never come to be assessable income of Ron. On the other hand as a general rule as, and when, and to the extent to which, those debts were paid to Ron, Ron would derive the moneys so paid as income of the year in which the payment was effected. However, it might have been that Ron realized the right to receive future payment of those debts by assigning that right to income for reward: a factoring of the debts. Had he done so the amount received from the factor would have constituted his assessable income. Further, the consideration would have been assessable income of Ron for the year in which he received it. If later the debtors had paid a larger sum than the assignor had received upon the assignment, the surplus so paid would not constitute assessable of the assignor. Either way, in the circumstances pertaining to Ron, the tests for measuring the amounts of assessable income and identifying the year in which it was derived would be determined by reference to the amount and date of the moneys received by Ron.
  5. But Ron argues for a very narrow concept of receipt. He contends that, having gratuitously assigned to his wife his existing right to receive payment in future for no consideration and not having beneficially received any portion of the disputed moneys thereafter that he has "derived" nothing. In my view that is not correct. By the act of assignment he divested himself of a right of property and conferred that right of property upon his wife.

29. I have always considered the description of income used by Pitney, J. of the Supreme Court of the United States in Eisner v. Macomber [1919] USSC 119; (1919) 252 U.S. 189 to provide a most useful, even if picturesque, guide to an understanding of the concepts of capital and income and the relationships between them. The Judge (inter alia) said:-

"The fundamental relation of capital to income has been much discussed by economists, the former being likened to the tree or the land, the latter to the fruit or the crop; the former depicted as a reservoir supplied from springs, the latter as the outlet stream, to be measured' by its flow during a period of time.

"Here we have the essential matter; not a gain accruing to capital, not a growth or increment of value in the investment; but a gain, ' a profit, something of exchangeable value proceeding from the property, severed from the capital, however invested or employed, and: coming in being "derived", that is, received or drawn by the recipient (the taxpayer) for his separate use, benefit and disposal; that is income derived from property. Nothing else answers the description."

30. In recent years the High Court of Australia found the description helpful. In F.C.T. v. P.P. Smith [(1981) 147 C.L.R. 5781 - a case in which the Court was considering whether sums received under a personal disability insurance policy were to be considered as income - the Court said:-

"If the ability to earn is the tree, and income the fruit thereof, a policy of insurance against impairment of the fruit bearing capacity of the tree may well take the form of providing the fruit until such time as the tree recovers it proper role."

  1. That being so, it is helpful to consider in comparative terms the relative situations of the orchardist and Ron. For the orchardist his trees are the permanent productive asset (the capital) which generates a recurrent crop (the income). For Ron the capital asset was the medical practice and the "fruit" of that practice was the fees it generated. Just as the orchardist could determine when to harvest his crop, so too could Ron determine when to render his accounts. Just as the orchardist could dispose of the orchard and with it its unrealized crop, so too could Ron dispose of his practice and with it the entitlement to future fees. Just as the orchardist could dispose for value of the right to collect the crop when ready for harvest, so too Ron could dispose of his right to collect the fees generated from his practice. Just as the orchardist might choose not to harvest his crop and thereby to derive no income, Ron too might have chosen not to claim any reward for his services and thereby avoid deriving any income.
    1. So viewed the question arising out of the present circumstances is what would be the consequence for the orchardist if, instead of harvesting his crop, he authorised others to take the valuable crop without reward to himself. In the case of Ron the question is what are the consequences if, having established in himself a right to receive payment of fees, he then transfers the right to receive payment to another or others by way of gift. In my view, were the orchardist to do as has just been proposed then, quite independently of s.36 of the Act, he would have derived the crop as income at least upon it being severed from the capital - the trees - the crop then being available for his separate use, benefit and disposal subject only to his ratuitously assumed obligations to others. So too with Ron. In my view, when he parted with a beneficial interest in the debts he was parting with rights which had already come home to him. In so holding, I adopt comments made by Channell, J. in Smyth v. Stretton [(1904) 5 T.C. 36] in relation to salary and wages. By adapting his Honour's words to the facts of the present case one can say:-

"A sum receivable by way of fees for services is not the less fees taxable because for some reason or other the person who receives them has not got the full right to apply them just as he likes."

  1. But the question which then remains is whether by making such a disposition by way of gift Ron has "derived" the face value of the debts; the face value of the assigned interest in the debts; the present value of the assigned debts at date of assignment; or whether he is only to be considered as having derived in any particular year of income so much as is received by himself or his assignee in relation to the assigned debts. Considered in terms of an orchardist, the question would be whether the orchardist, traditionally assessed upon a cash receipts basis, would be liable to account for the amount due but unpaid to him in relation to the sold crop or only liable to account for so much as was received in due course; and to do so only when received. The latter view was the view taken by the High Court of Australia in Fainsworth v. F.C.T. [1949] HCA 27; (1949) 78 C.L.R. 504.
  2. In determining that question in relation to Ron it . is important in my view to bear in mind that historically Ron has claimed to be a person liable to assessment on a "cash receipts" basis and that the Commissioner has thought it appropriate to so assess him. In my view that was appropriate but for the transaction under consideration; and remains appropriate despite that transaction. In determining such questions one must be guided by a desire to fix upon a method of calculation of income which is "calculated to give a substantially correct reflex of the taxpayer's true income" [Commissioner of Taxes (S.A.) v. The Executor Trustee & Agency Company of South Australia Limited -Carden's Case [1938] HCA 69; (1938) 63 C.L.R. 108 at pp.152-4]. In my view that true reflex will be most nearly achieved if Ron is considered to have derived the fees paid by the patients whose indebtedness was assigned as and when, and only if, those fees are paid. In all the circumstances I accept that for all ordinary purposes it is a "cash receipts" basis of assessment which is most appropriate in relation to Ron.
  3. But, contrary to the submission made on his behalf, in the circumstances of a case such as this I do not understand that phrase is to be limited in its application to "cash" in any narrow sense, ignoring goods in lieu and other matters of value; or to amounts "received" rather than - as here - applied to the use of, or in accordance with the directions of, Ron.
  4. As the disputed amount ($27,806) exceeds the cash received ($13,642), the determination of the Tribunal on the above point will be that the decision of the Commissioner upon the objection under review be varied by reducing the taxable income of the Applicant for the year of income ended 30 June 1981 by $14,164.
  5. In relation to the years of income ended 30 June 1977, 1978, 1979, 1980 and 1982, by consent the applications of the Applicant will be dismissed notwithstanding the partial allowance of the objections in all years.
  6. I have been informed that amended assessments have already issued to allow in part other aspects of the objection of the Applicant in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1981. That being so, it seems that the appropriate Order to be made by the Tribunal should be that the decision of the Commissioner upon the objection of the Applicant under review relating to the year of income ended 30 June 1981 shall be further varied and the assessment of taxable income for that year reduced by a further $14,164.
