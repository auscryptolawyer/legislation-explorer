VT87/1647 and Commissioner of Taxation [1989] AATA 704; (1989) 21 ATR 3057; 89 ATC 919 (22 November 1989)

Last Updated: 17 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - "notional" rent included in assessable income -claim for deduction of expenses incurred in relation to the subject premises - daughter occupied premises without payment of rent - whether rates and taxes paid by daughter were rent -whether transaction resulted in receipt of income.

Taxation Administration Act 1963

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss.25, 51, 54

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Groser (1982) ATC 4478

DECISION & REASONS

Re:

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

No VT87/1647

AAT Decision No 5502

Tribunal: Mrs J R Dwyer, Senior Member

Date: 22 November 1989

Place: Melbourne

DECISION

Decision : The objection decision under review in relation to the year ended 30 June 1983 is affirmed.

(Sgnd) J R Dwyer

Senior Member

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL)

) No VT87/1647 TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

22 November 1989 Mrs J R Dwyer, Senior Member

1. The taxpayer claimed a loss of $1,561.00 in his income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1983 in respect of premises which he owns in a suburb of Melbourne ("the premises"). This loss was made up of the difference between the rent for the premises of $3,640.00, as shown in the tax return, and outgoings totalling $5,201.00 in respect of the premises, which were claimed as allowable deductions. The deductions claimed were:

Rates, taxes and insurance premiums $590.00

interest on borrowed money $3,803.00

depreciation $808.00

$5,201.00

2. The claimed loss of $1,561.00 was accepted by the Commissioner of Taxation ("the Commissioner") in the notice of assessment issued in respect of the year ended 30 June 1983. The taxpayer then wrote to the Commissioner seeking to correct returns he had made for the two previous years, so as to show a loss in respect of the premises for those years too. He claimed a loss of $1,912.00 in respect of the premises for the year ended 30 June 1982 and a loss of $2,544.00 for the year ended 30 June 1981.

3. While investigating the request for adjustment of tax paid in the two previous years, an officer from the Commissioner's office ascertained that the rental of $3,640.00, included in the tax return for the year ended 30 June 1983, was a notional rental only, as the premises were occupied by the taxpayer's daughter who paid no rent. The Commissioner therefore amended the assessment of income tax payable by the taxpayer as provided for in s.170 (1) of the Act. He issued an income tax adjustment sheet which added the sum of $1,561.00 to the taxpayer's taxable income for the year ended 30 June 1983. The explanation given was as follows:

"amended to disallow loss to property as it is not an arms-length transaction and therefore of a domestic nature."

  1. In evidence at the hearing, the taxpayer said that he purchased the property in about September 1980 as he expected that he would one day wish to move from the large home in which he presently lives, to a smaller home. He said that after he purchased the property, his daughter suggested that she would like to move out from home and live in it. He agreed, and originally intended that when she did so, she would pay a commercial rent. He asked the agent who sold the property what a realistic rental would be and in 1980 the agent suggested $65.00 per week. However, before moving into the premises, the daughter had a re-occurrence of anorexia. She was therefore unable to work and was consequently unable to pay the agreed rent. The figure of $3,640.00, included as income from rent in the year ended 30 June 1983, represents a weekly notional rent of $70.00, which the taxpayer calculated to take into account cost of living increases since the time of purchase.
  2. The taxpayer said that although his daughter had never paid any rent, she did refund to her mother some of the expenses of the house including the electricity account and the rates, land taxes and insurance premium which amounted to $590.00 in the year ended 30 June 1983. He said that his daughter derives her income from benefits under the Social Security Act 1947.

6. The Commissioner's reasons for disallowing the taxpayer's objection, as set out in the statement lodged with the Tribunal in accordance with s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, as modified by S.14ZG of the Taxation Administration Act 1953, read as follows:

"1. No part of the amount of $3,640 included by the taxpayer as assessable rental income derived during the year of income ended 30 June 1983 is assessable income under sub-section 25(1) or any other section of the Act.

  1. No part of the amount of $5,201 claimed as deductions for the year ended 30 June 1983 was incurred by the taxpayer in gaining or producing assessable rental income and no part of that amount is an allowable deduction under sub-section 51(1), section 53, section 54 or any other section of the Act."

7. It is therefore necessary to refer to the relevant provisions in the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). Sub-section 25(1)(a) so far as relevant reads as follows:-

"25. (1) The assessable income of a taxpayer shall include -

(a) where the taxpayer is a resident – the gross income derived directly or indirectly from all sources whether in or out of Australia;"

Sub-section 51(1) so far as relevant reads as follows:-

"51. (1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in training or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

It is not necessary to refer to s.53 as it relates to repairs and there was no deduction claimed in respect of repairs. Sub-section 54(1), which relates to depreciation, reads as follows:

“ 54. (1) Depreciation during the year of income of any property, being plant or articles owned by a taxpayer and used by him during that year for the purpose of producing assessable income,.... shall, subject to this Act, be an allowable deduction."

The emphasis in all these excerpts has been added by the Tribunal.

  1. Mr Rotunno, who appeared for the Commissioner, argued that as no income was derived by the taxpayer from the subject property, the sum of $3,640.00 being notional income only, there was no assessable income from the property under s.25. Therefore he submitted that the expenses of holding and maintaining the property were not losses or outgoings incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, and thus those losses and outgoings are not allowable deductions under s.51(1). The Commissioner submitted further that the depreciation in respect of the property was not an allowable deduction under s.54(1) because it was not depreciation of property used by a taxpayer during the relevant year for the purpose of producing assessable income.
  2. In his Notice of Objection dated 28 August 1984, the taxpayer claimed that the property had been treated by him in every way as if there were tenants other than his daughter in the property. He pointed out that the notional rent he included in his taxation return was the commercial rent for a house in that area at the relevant time. Mr Rotunno did not dispute this. The taxpayer also stated that he had not sought to gain any unjust or improper tax advantage in that he had only claimed the deduction he would have been allowed, if the property had been let on the open market.

10. In his Notice of Objection the taxpayer also stated:

"At what distance does this transaction become "arms-length"? If she married, and my daughter was, say 'Mrs Smith', this would be perfectly legal, even if she lost her husband and lived in the same house exactly as at present."

Mr Rotunno explained at the hearing that this was not correct. He referred to Income Tax ruling number 2167 which was tendered in evidence and said that the problem did not arise because it was the taxpayer's daughter who was living in the property, but because the terms on which she was living there were not consistent with normal commercial practices. Paragraph 13 of Taxation ruling 2167 reads as follows:

"13. Where property is let to relatives, the essential question for decision is whether the arrangements are consistent with normal commercial practices in this area. If they are, the owner of the property would be treated no differently for income tax purposes from any other owner in a comparable arm's length situation."

I accept that as a correct approach.

11. I consider that the submissions on behalf of the Commissioner are correct. As the taxpayer did not derive any income from the subject property, neither the notional income nor the expenses of holding and maintaining that property are relevant for the purpose of his taxation assessment. It is true that the taxpayer does not seek any tax advantage beyond his entitlements if the property had been let on the open market, but the Act does not deal with assessment of tax liability on that basis. It allows deductions in respect of losses and outgoings incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, or in carrying on a business for that purpose. In this matter there was only a notional income and no carrying on of a business, and that fact situation does not give rise to deductions under s.51(1).

  1. I have considered whether the taxpayer's evidence that his daughter refunded to his wife the rates and taxes paid on the property means that the amount of $590.00 should be treated as the annual rent received from the property. It is clear from the evidence that the arrangement to refund those items was not an arrangement to pay rent, but rather a step taken by the daughter to pay her own way to some extent, as she does not pay rent. The taxpayer said his daughter also refunds the electricity bills paid by his wife but electricity accounts are generally a tenant's responsibility and therefore of no relevance to this application.
  2. The Commissioner relied on Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Groser (1982) ATC 4478 ("Groser's Case"). The facts in that case were similar to those in the application for review. The taxpayer provided a home for his mentally retarded brother but the brother paid a sum of $2.00 per week to the taxpayer as rent. Jenkinson J of the Supreme Court of Victoria, after referring to the fact of the brother's mental retardation and concluding that it did not throw doubt on his contractual capacity in relation to the transactions between him and the taxpayer, said at p.4481:

"But I do regard that evidence and the respondent's evidence about his concern, and his sense of responsibility, for the protection and the welfare of his brother as throwing upon those transactions a light which emphasises strongly their familial character. My conclusion is that the arrangements which the respondent proposed and which his brother accepted ought to be regarded as the respondent's arrangements for his care of his brother, having nothing to do with transactions of the kind which result in the receipt of income as understood in ordinary usage."

14. Similarly, in this case the taxpayer's concern and sense of responsibility for the welfare of his daughter, are the reasons for the arrangement whereby she occupies the house rent free. The monies refunded by the daughter are not rent and are not called rent by the parties. The payment of rates and taxes by the daughter is part of a private or domestic arrangement between the taxpayer and his daughter.

  1. The taxpayer agreed that no commercial landlord would allow a tenant to remain in a property for many years, as his daughter has done, without ever having paid rent. He said in cross-examination that the reason why he had kept his daughter as the occupier of the property, in spite of her inability to pay rent, was a domestic matter rather than a commercial one. It is quite understandable that a father who is concerned for his daughter's welfare, should provide her with a home but, as in Groser's Case, that arrangement has a familial character and has nothing to do with transactions of the kind which result in the receipt of income as understood in ordinary usage. It therefore does not fall within the ambit of the Act.
  2. Thus I uphold the respondent's submission that there was no assessable income under s25(1) derived from the premises. There are therefore no allowable deductions under ss51(1) or 54(1). The objection decision under review in relation to the year ended 30 June 1983 must be affirmed.
