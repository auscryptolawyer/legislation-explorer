ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income tax - Company recoupment tax - Assessment against vendor shareholders for unpaid company tax - Division 7 Assessment raised against company - Section 260 assessments against vendor shareholders arising from same circumstances - Share options acquired as part of loan agreement - Whether profit on sale of options assessable - Income Tax Assessment Act 1981 secs. 25(1), 26(a), 26AAA; Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act 19B2 sec. 13(8).

In re V.G.M. Holdings Ltd [1942] 1 Ch. 235

Ord Forrest Pty Ltd v. FC of T [1974] HCA 57; (1973-1974) 130 CLR 124

FC of T v. Myer Emporium Ltd 87 ATC 4031

REASONS FOR DECISION

Before: Dr P Gerber (Deputy President)

Date: 31 July 19B9

1. The issue in this case comes to this; Does an amount of $795,892, being the nett profit from the sale of options in Far East Pty Ltd ("Far East") by Taree Pty Ltd ("Taree") in the 1981 tax year constitute part of the assessable income of Taree, a company whose business, as described in its first return in 1980, is "Investor"?

2. The history of Taree, as it emerged from the evidence, disclosed that the company was previously known as Mikado Pty Ltd and changed its name after it was acquired by Messrs Russell, Manners and Simmons some time in 1979. The paid up capital of the company was then $100, its shares being owned as to 50% by Mr Manners, 40% by Mr Russell and 10% by Mr Simmons. It is asserted in the 1980 return that "there was no trading of any description by the company until January 1980". In its profit and loss account attached to that return, the company disclosed as income some $138,226, which included an amount of $112,455 from the sale of options in Far East, undertaken during the dying days of the 1980 tax year. Claimed deductions amounted to $138,772, which amount included $850 and $124,000, described as "legal fees" and "management fees" respectively, the latter being paid as to half each to the Russell and the Manners family companies. Just what "management" Russell and Manners rendered to the company after its acquisition, from which time onwards it was virtually inert, was not made clear. The return thus disclosed a loss of $546, which was duly recorded as such in the Directors' Report signed by Russell and Simmons as directors. This return was lodged, it seems, some time in December 1980 and an assessment issued on 2 July 1981, accepting the loss as returned. It was not until February 1982 that an adjustment sheet was sent to the company, disallowing the management and legal fees, thus leaving the company exposed to a taxable income of $124,304. However, by that time, Taree's business had been sold to a company owned by Russell - Seventh Heaven - as trustee for the Eerat Unit Trust and, the company, nicely cashed up, was duly embalmed, ready for discreet disposal by the sale of its shares to the Funeral Directors who specialize in the disposal of "dead" companies. Undeterred by events, the Commissioner issued a further - and illegible - adjustment sheet which restored the claimed deductions for legal and management fees, thus leaving the company, by then in the hands of the liquidator, with a carry-forward loss of $546.

  1. In the I981 tax year, the remaining options in Far East which were not disposed of in the previous tax year, were sold by Taree, and it is the tax on this gain (less the carry-forward loss from the previous year) which the Commissioner now seeks to collect from Mr Russell as the shareholder nominated to represent the class of vendor shareholders under the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act 1982. Section 18(8) of that Act confers on the applicant the same rights of appeal under Division 2 of Part V of the Tax Assessment Act as Taree, thus allowing him to object to the above amount which was included in the assessable income of the company for the year of income ended 30 June 1981. The respondent seeks to tax the profit from the sale of the options in reliance on sees. 25(1), 26(a) and/or 26AAA of the Tax Act. The respondent also maintains an assessment under Division 7 of Part III of the Tax Assessment Act against the undistributed profits earned by Taree, as well as a sec.260 assessment raised against the applicant on 19 November, 1986.
  2. Taree's acquisition and disposal of its Far East options must be seen against the background of Far East's involvement in the development of a mining company - Creek Pty Ltd - ("Creek"), located in the Pilbara region in Western Australia with known deposits of tin and gold.
  3. Russell and Manners were first "introduced" to the Creek Pty Ltd Mine in May 1979 by one O'Hara whom they had met on previous trips to the area and who was heavily involved in the mining industry. O'Hara, with the help of some other investors, had put a great deal of money and effort into rehabilitating the mine, which they owned through a company - Portland Mining Pty Limited ("Portland"). However, by mid-1979, it became clear that a substantial injection of funds was required to bring the mine "on-line". The applicant and Manners both had considerable expertise as well as contacts in the leasing and finance industries and agreed to see if they could assist.
  4. Through his commercial contacts, the applicant became aware of Far East, a publicly listed company which, apart from some property interests in Bendigo, was at that stage "cashed up" looking for somewhere to go. By July 1979, it was agreed in principle that Far East would buy Creek from Portland for $514,000, payable on terms, together with 200,000 $1 shares in Far East. In addition, the applicant and two representatives from Portland O'Hara and Seal) were to join the Far East board. It is to be noted that up to that point, only $438,607 of Far East's authorised capital of $1 million had been issued. In the result, the allotment of 200,000 $1 shares gave Portland approximately a one-third interest in Far East. The applicant and Manners received as "brokerage" 10% of the issued capital of Portland (i.e. 10 x $1 shares) which shares were transferred, not to them personally but to Taree.
  5. Creek, however, continued to soak up funds and in late 1979 Far East began to cast around for further sources of finance. The issuing of further shares in Far East was not thought to be commercially realistic since the company's shares had not been traded on the stock exchange for quite some time, Indeed, trading in Far East was suspended in August 1979 at the company's request pending the outcome of the Creek acquisition. Nor were the principal shareholders in Far East (the Isaac family) interested in using their own financial resources to secure additional funds unless all the other directors provided personal guarantees - something they were not prepared to do. Portland, of course, was in no position to offer funds for no better reason than that its lack of capital was the very reason for the Far East/Portland "marriage" in the first place. It was in these circumstances that the applicant and Manners put forward a proposal, based upon their knowledge and contacts in the finance industry, to lend $250,000 to Far East per medium of Taree, which had until then been dormant. It was a term of that proposal that Far East issue the options to Taree which are the subject of this application. Since the applicant relies heavily on the time when, and for what purpose, these options were acquired, I propose to examine the surrounding circumstances in some detail.

8. This drama can, for present purposes, be said to have begun on 8 January 1980, when Taree wrote to Far East, offering $250,000 at 15% for 12 months in the following terms:

"Dear Sir,

Following on from the various discussions held between Far East, the major shareholders Wahrheit (the Isaac shareholders' holding company) and Portland, we have pleasure in outlining in general terms details of the proposed loan offer of $250,000 to Far East.

Loan of $250,000 to be made forthwith at an interest rate of 15% simple per annum calculated and payable monthly. Legal costs, stamp duty etc. to the account of the borrower,

Term of loan to be maximum of twelve months with the right of earlier repayment subject to the following conditions:

(I) Repayment of loan to be made from the sale of the company's land situated at Bendigo. Such land sale proceeds to be made from settlements less normal legal, stamp duty and selling expenses.

(II) Balance of monies owing to be due and payable at the end of twelve months from the date of loan.

Security, the documentation for which will be prepared by Messrs Carr & Bus to be a first ranking debenture over the company's assets and undertakings, including a registered first mortgage over the Bendigo land referred to above, and if thought necessary by Messrs Carr & Bus registered mortgage over the company's mining tenements situated in Western Australia.

In consideration for the granting of the loan and as conditions of the loan, Far East to issue Taree options to subscribe at par for shares having a total face value of $361,393, such options to be able to be exercised in full or part thereof at the discretion of Taree within a period of three years from the date of loan."

In his evidence, Mr Russell gave the following reasons for the inclusion of the options in the loan proposal:

"Do you recall what was the origin of that proposal? ---- There were two reasons. One is security for the transaction and the other reason was that we felt that Far East had a potential, if handled correctly, and it would give to Taree an option to purchase a share at a future point in time if Taree had - which it then did not have."

11. It was common ground that Taree did not provide the $250,0 00 from its own resources, but had by this stage already approached General Credits Limited, which agreed to finance the loan, provided that Russell and Manners would provide personal guarantees. Guarantees and indemnities were subsequently signed on 23 January 1983. The loan from General Credits was at the same commercial rate as the loan from Taree to Far East - hence Taree acted as a mere conduit from General Credits to Far East.

(4) The date of receipt of such offer to remain until Thursday the 23rd October. If the company makes such an offer prior to 23rd October, the basis of calculation to establish the price to remain as outlined in (3).

(5) Such an offer to be subject to tradable shares being in the hands of Taree no later than 29th November, 1980."

27. Mr Russell was closely examined and cross-examined as to the contents of the above correspondence:

Q. "There is a reference to the options and it says:

Originally the options would not have been listed on the Stock Exchange but as a result of the share and option issue referred to these can now be freely sold. This was not the original intention of the granting of the options, the Board accepting the advice of representatives of Taree that the options would be converted to shares and accepting the statement that in the future, the conversion of the options would create working capital for the company."

Now, what do you say about that paragraph?---It is half right. The original intention was to have an option to take up shares. And again, because the marketability of options and the options as we are now speaking in the general sense were not then conceived, the intention of Taree was that if the company did have a value, or the shares had a value on the Stock Exchange, it had the right to convert its options to those shares. Now, the next part I do not quite agree with:

"the conversion (of the options) would create working capital for the company".

That, I think, is the writer's journalese.

Q. Presumably, whenever an option is exercised then the power value is paid to Far East by the holder? ---- Correct, correct.

Q. Well, then it says:

The spirit of the original undertaking appears now to have been broken in that Taree are selling options without converting. The technical legal arguments put forward now did not appear to have been a deterrant at the time the options were originally agreed.

The Board believes that Taree should honour its obligations to convert. The conversion of 1,000,000 options would not reduce Taree's book profit. The Board undertakes that when Taree converts as we believe it is morally bound to do, the Company will use these funds for reduction of the Taree debt

The company also submits that Taree should give favourable consideration to rolling the outstanding balance of the debt when it falls due so that conversion of the options and repayment can take place in an orderly fashion."

Q. It sounds as if Far East was running a bit short of money again? ---It was.

Q. And it was only October 1980, the options ran until December 1982, Had it ever been suggested that Taree would be converting or might be converting the options within that 1980 year?---No, no.

Q. Well then, there is a reply by you dated 15 October 1980 saying:

"We have your letter and advise of its contents in regard to the loan made by Taree and specifically in regard to options associated with the Taree loan do not require a reply other than to say that Taree rejects out of hand its comments."

Now, what did you mean by that last statement?--- I was referring, Sir, that the options should be converted to shares. The earlier question there the third paragraph of the previous letter of 7 October, whereby the writer stated that the options should be converted to shares, and that (that) was the intention at the beginning and that such conversion would create working capital for the company - I was replying to that aspect of working capital to the company because the two did not go together.

Q. Yes. And what about the last paragraph in the letter of 7 October 1980, the suggestion that you should convert --- I felt that there was not a correlation to the loan and the Taree option and the writer was trying to say that the Taree options, by converting to capital, would repay the Taree loan, and I was saying - well, that I did not, that I did not agree with it. They were separate things.

Q. Well then, in going back to your letter of 15 October you say:

As Far East is exploring means to repay or rollover the Taree loan which currently stands at $209,840, Taree in an endeavour to be of assistance to the company invites an offer as per below which if accepted should be beneficial to the company and its shareholders.

The loan is due for repayment on the 23rd January, 1981. At this point in time it appears the company would have difficulty in meeting this commitment without raising additional capital by way of either a placement of shares or an issue to shareholders.

Taree would consider the following offer from the company. Fully paid tradeable shares be offered to Taree in sufficient quantity to equate the principal value of the loan owing at the date of registration of the shares.

And the number of shares to be calculated, in effect, on the same terms as the recent placement which was at 75 cents a share - well, that was calculated, as you said, by being 86 per cent of the last sale value. So the last sale value on the exchange when you wrote this letter was 85 cents, So in effect you are saying: 'Well, we will take more Far East shares at 86 per cent of 85 cents in satisfaction of our loan. Is that the substance of your proposal?'----Yes, indeed.

Q. And so at that point, although you had been selling some options, what was your approach to Far East in your view of its future?

DEPUTY PRESIDENT: Can I just ask, at that point in time, that is October 1980, how many options had been sold? ----In November 1980?

No, October?--- I am tracing it back, there were 300 - 350 to 400,000 left, so I would say at that point in time there may have been 600,000 left, so we would have sold approximately about 1.1 million.

MR FORSYTH: Well, what was your view of the future of Far East at this stage? ---- Well, we were not particularly enamoured with it because of the lack of performance, but felt that if the company was in difficulty, and I have something else, Sir, which you have not - in fact I never discussed it with you, but Universal Leasing Corporation had leased -which was my company - considerable items of plant and equipment to Far East, and when I say "my company", that is on behalf of our financiers, so I was somewhat wearing that hat as well at that period, and it was felt that if the company was absolutely desperate we would take shares to expel the debt, but I did not want to see conversion of options because we had decided earlier not to take up those options - not to take up stock as a result of those options. So it was to help Far East on this basis because already it was again short of cash and having difficulty making payments."

28. As already pointed out, Taree had begun selling its options in late May/early June 1980, and up to 30 June 19S0 some 300,000-400,000 options were sold, either because Manners was in need of funds, or predominantly (because of) disillusionment .. the fact that the mine was not the mine that we initially thought it would be

29. The period. July to December 1980 saw a continued sale by Taree of its options until all options held had been disposed of. During this time Far East made at least two further share issues and returned a monthly cash deficit. The share price, however, remained buoyant amidst prevailing optimism in the share market - an optimism which, in the case of Far East, seemed to vary in inverse proportion to the bottom line results of the Creek project. The price of tin, a somewhat volatile commodity, was also on the rise from late 1979 through the first half of 1980, thus adding further strength to the share price of Far East.

30, To conclude this historic narrative, the loan from General Credits to Taree was repaid sometime in October 1980, no doubt from the proceeds of the sale of options, thus releasing Russell and Manners from their personal guarantees on the loan. The nett assets purchased by Eerat for the sum of $532,105.30 on 21 November 1980 consisted of the following:

Debtors $ 30,215.30

Loan to Far East $209,840.00

Shares (Bluff Oil) $ 9,750.00

Shares (Portland) $ 90,000.00

Far East Options $192,300.00

$532,105.30

31. Also on 21 November, 19B0 $532,578.05 was deposited into the Eerat bank account as follows:

From Manners $251,313.05

From Russell $235,515.00

From Simmons $ 45,750.00

$532,578.05

This amount was presumably the major portion (if not all) of the proceeds from the sale of the Taree shares.

32. Are the proceeds from the sale of the options assessable income? I am satisfied that the fact that the proceeds from the sale of options were returned as assessable income in the 1980 tax year is neutral. I accept the explanation from the accountant who gave evidence on behalf of the applicant that he assumed that any purchase and sale of property within twelve months automatically attracted the application of the (then) provisions of sec. 26AAA, and that the possibility that there had been no "purchase" within the meaning of that section had not occurred to him. Mr Forsyth submitted on behalf of the applicant that the options were not purchased in the relevant sense, in reliance on authorities which have held that the word "purchase" is applicable only where the asset in question was in existence before the transaction - and if the transaction is one in which the asset is newly created, then it is not a purchase. Thus In re V.G.M. Holdings Limited [1942] 1 Ch 235 it was held that for the purposes of the Companies Act 1929 (UK), an acquisition of shares in a company by application and allotment did not constitute a "purchase" within the relevant sub-section. Again, in Ord Forrest Pty Ltd v. Commissioner of Taxation [1974] HCA 57; 130 CLR 124 Gibbs J (as he then was) cited In re V.G.M. Holdings as authority for the proposition that "before allotment, the share does not exist as a piece of property; it is only when it is allotted and issued that the rights which it confers are created " (pl48), although it was concluded in that case that the allotment of shares did constitute a disposition of property within the terms of the Gift Duty Assessment Act 1941-1967 (Cth).

33, I accept Mr Forsyth's well-reasoned submission that sec. 26AAA can have no application to these options. The proceeds from the sale in the instant case are not "caught" by that section.

34. I next turn to sec. 26(a). There can be no doubt that the options were "acquired"; the question is "when"? In an instructive submission, Mr Forsyth sought to persuade me that the operative date was January 8, 1980, or at the latest the following day. He points to the contractual obligations between the parties and submits that there was offer and acceptance, it being a term of the agreement that the loan would be granted on terms that the borrower would issue "these options" to the lender. Mr Forsyth put it disarmingly: "That was enough to be binding upon the parties, and, therefore, at that time, Taree became entitled to the benefit of 'these options'". However, with great respect, there is a fly in that ointment - the reference to "these options" assumes that there was a sufficient identity between the options said to have been acquired on January 8, 1980 and the options subsequently sold, so that the absence of an intent on that date to acquire "these options" for purposes of profit-making by sale would exclude the application of sec. 26(a). This argument is said to gain support from the fact that the only value "these options" had on January 8 lay in Far East's long-term future prospects as a tin mine, "with the bonus of having some element of gold".

35. Mr Forsyth's submission rests on the assumption that "these options" refer to the balance of the authorised capital of the Company at that point in time. There can be no doubt that it was within the power of the Directors to make such an allotment. Thus clause 7(a) of the Articles of Association of Far East provides as follows:

"Save as provided by Contract or these Articles to the contrary, all unissued shares shall be under the control of the Board which may grant calls or options thereon, issue option certificates in respect thereof, allot or otherwise dispose of the same to such persons on such terms and conditions and for such consideration and either at a premium or otherwise and for or at such time as it thinks fit; provided always that the power to issue shares at a discount and to grant options shall be subject to the provisions of the Act."

36. The short answer to Mr Forsyth's submission is that "these options" were not issued to Taree in January. I find as a fact that on January 8, 1980, there was no enforceable contract between the parties in place. What actually occurred is amply demonstrated by the Minutes of the Board of Far East on that day, which record that;

"It was agreed to accept the offer of the loan in principle provided that the Company restructure its authorised capital to five million dollars and that all shares were sub-divided into twenty cent fully paid shares and that the $350,000 options would apply after this restructure of the Company's capital. Final acceptance by the Company would be contingent on provision of advice from Brokers that the issue of $350,000 options would not-be a major detriment to the Company proceeding with a share issue".

37. In other words, the offer from Taree was conditional on (i) a capital restructuring of Far East and (ii) favourable advice from the company's brokers.

38. It is a matter of history that the capital restructuring of the company did take place, and that the granting of the options occurred, not on January 8, but on 24 March 1980, when the granting of the options was sanctioned by an Extraordinary General Meeting of Far East. It is also clear that the 350,000 options acquired on March 24 are different in kind and value from "these options" which Mr Forsyth claims to have been acquired on January 8. In short, if the documentation is confused, it is due to the fact that the relevant documents were drawn by laymen. This can avail the applicant nothing.

39. It follows that if a "purpose" for the acquisition of the options must be looked to, the punctum temporis is March 24 1980. It is significant that "these options" - whatever rights they may have conferred on Taree in January - were not only unsaleable, because they had no corporeal existence, but in any event had no commercial value since the $1 shares were trading at or around 70c, briefly hitting a high of $1.20 during that month (see appendix A). However, by February, they reached a high of $2.50 and a low of $1.12, thus making it clear that what had in the previous month appeared to be a "long shot", suddenly gave every indication of becoming a valuable and saleable piece of property. In the event, Taree began selling all their options as soon as they were able to do so, albeit in quantities nicely calculated not to flood the market, netting them a gain of approximately $1 million. Against this background, the state of mind of Taree's directors is significant. The facts, as they emerged, can be summarised as follows: Russell asserts in evidence that in January, "the intention of Taree was that if the company (i.e. Far East) did have a value, or the shares had a value on the Stock Exchange, it had the right to convert its options to those shares." The witness dismissed as "writer's journalese" Far East's assertion that "the spirit of the original undertaking was that Taree would not sell the options but convert them to shares to create working capital for the company". It is, of course, trite to point out that the Far East share options - in commercial terms - could be either converted into shares or sold; it can hardly be asserted that they could be viewed as a long term investment or a hedge against inflation, since their "use by" date was December 1982. I. am therefore satisfied, having observed both Mr Russell and Mr Manners in the witness box, that as far as they were concerned, there was never a point in time when they contemplated converting the options into shares; if the options could be sold at a profit, it was "money for jam", if not, Taree had nothing to lose. If additional support for this view of the evidence were needed, one need only have regard to the company's action. The moment the options became tradeable and could be sold at above par, Taree off-loaded its entire holding for a profit of close to $1 million and still insisted on recovering its $250,000 loan notwithstanding that it had already paid off General Credits from the proceeds of the sale of the options. This is not intended to be a criticism of the company's action, it is recorded merely to put the whole transaction into perspective, including Taree's letter to Far East dated 15 October 1980 in which it is piously asserted that "in an endeavour to be of assistance to the company", the lender offered to renegotiate the loan by accepting, in full settlement, "fully paid tradeable shares ... in sufficient quantity to equate the principle value of the loan owing".

40. Manners' explanation for the reason the options were acquired is even more fanciful. Thus he was asked by senior counsel for the applicant:

Q. "Well now, part of the proposal put to Far East was that it should grant an option to Taree over 361,393 shares. Were you involved in any discussions leading up to that term? ---- Yes, I was.

Q. Could you tell us about that? --- Russell and I had discussed the way in which we would go about seeking a loan from General Credits to fund ourselves with the $250,000 to on-loan to Far East and we thought of the kind of security that we would need to take. Now, my position was such that I wanted maximum security, so I think we initially agreed that we should take a registered mortgage out of the land that the public company had at Bendigo. That we should take a floating debenture or debenture deed over the activities of the company and then as an after thought, I suggested to Russell that it might be prudent to try and lock up the uncalled capital in the company so that they could not charge off in a different direction, transfer power or do something that might not have been in our interest because we had our all staked on this investment and so that figure, which I do not recollect numerically, I think you will find is the difference between the then issued number of shares when deducted from the authorised capital of the company.

Q. So your recollection is that it was your idea? ----Yes, I believe I added that in as what I considered to be additional security. ...

Q, Well, apart from the original idea that that option would impose a constraint upon Far East doing things you did not want them to do, what other benefits did it have for Taree? --- Well, I viewed - first of all I was very confident that O'Hara's judgment and the judgment of the other people involved was correct and that in fact this was going to be a substantial tin mining venture. The climate at that time, I recall, was that there was some kind of difficulty in Malaysia or one of the Asian tin consortiums and as a result there was a very high price being enjoyed for tin production and the prospects were indeed quite buoyant and the sort of feasibility study figures that were thrown around indicated that the war, if you can call it that, could be won economically and that the company would therefore prosper and grow and that there was an additional province area - I think that is the correct terminology - that would be available and could be exploited on a continuing basis in years to come. And so given that it was an opportunity to get in on the ground floor for what I thought was an excellent long-term investment, Taree having options gave me the opportunity to, I suppose, convert options at some time in the future when the company prospered and it was showing dividends and good performance generally. It gave me the opportunity candidly to put to one side an investment nest-egg to the benefit of my children. You see, I had been divorced, access or custody was granted to my former wife and I do not know whether it was guilt or self recriminations but I thought this was an excellent opportunity for the family to have a long-term investment."

41. I am satisfied that the oral evidence of Russell and Manners is at worst deliberately misleading and at best rosy reconstruction. In the end, having dismissed an intention by Taree to convert its options into shares - whether one looks at the directors' state of mind in January or in March 1980 - the acquisition of the disputed options can only be seen, after eliminating all other proffered "explanations", as part of a profit-making undertaking or scheme. Having cast their bread upon the waters, these directors prayed that it would return in the form of loaves and fishes, a faith duly rewarded. However, to consider this harvest to have arrived freed of all fiscal restraint would not only be a miracle, it would be against the evidence.

42. Having concluded that the profit on these options is "caught" by the second limb of sec. 26(a), it is strictly unnecessary to determine whether the profit is also assessable as "income" according to ordinary concepts. However, in case this matter goes on appeal, and in deference to the careful argument presented by both counsel, I will briefly state my reasons for concluding that, on the evidence, the stray profit on this transaction also constitutes assessable income in the hands of Taree pursuant to sec. 25(1).

43. The decision in FC of T v. Myer 87 ATC 4363 is ample authority, if such be needed, for the proposition that a receipt may constitute assessable income even though it arises from an isolated commercial transaction falling outside the normal business operation of a taxpayer, provided it was entered into with the purpose of making a relevant profit or gain from the transaction. Having concluded that Taree's purpose in the acquisition of the options was for no reason other than the obtaining of a profit from their sale, it follows that the gain in this case constitutes assessable income under both sec. 25(1) and the second limb of sec. 26(a). My approach in this case is thus, on one view, the obverse of that adopted by their Honours in the Myer case, where their Honours concluded that the gain was "income" pursuant to sec. 25(1) and went on to examine to what extent - if any - it was also income pursuant to sec. 26(a). Thus at p. 4373, their Honours concluded:

"A similar chain or reasoning would have led to the conclusion that the amount constituted assessable income under the second limb of sec. 26(a). The relationship between sec- 25(1) and 26(a) has been the subject of much previous discussion involving considerable differences of opinion about the extent (if any) to which the provision of the second limb of sec. 26(a) supplemented the provision of sec. 25(1) (see Whitfords Beach v. FC of T 82 ATC 4031 at 379-3B1). It is, however, unnecessary that we examine that question here since, as we have indicated, we consider that the amount in question in the present appeal constituted income of the taxpayer pursuant to both sec. 25(1) and sec. 26(a)."

44. I would uphold the Commissioner's decision on the objection.

45.
