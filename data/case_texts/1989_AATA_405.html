REASONS FOR DECISION

26 May 1989 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. There were several unusual features about these proceedings. One of them was the circumstance that reliance was placed upon the "assets betterment" method of calculation of taxable income and also on the "source and application of funds" ("the T-account") method of calculation. It is not often that both methods are relied on. Another feature was that it was not the Commissioner who was relying upon those methods of calculation, but rather the Applicant. His advisers had correctly perceived that the methodology of each approach is as much available to a taxpayer to prove that the contentions of the Commissioner are excessive as it is available to the Commissioner to prove that taxable income has been understated by a taxpayer.

The Background

  1. The Applicant is a medical practitioner. He had practiced alone on his own account for many years in a largecity as a general practitioner. He and his wife had reared a family of seven children. In 1978 he made the mistake of embarking upon a commercial venture for which he had no aptitude. I am satisfied that in undertaking the venture he hoped to profit. I accept that at the same time he was hoping to assist his daughter and her French-speaking husband in establishing themselves in the community which was new to the son-in-law. The young couple had some international experience in limited aspects of the fashion industry. With that background the Applicant thought them well suited to operate a fashion related business - marketing perfumes, cosmetics and the like in a large department store.
  2. On every view the decision was unfortunate. From October 1978, when the first store opened, to late 1986 when the project in its ultimate form was finally abandoned, it was a failure and a substantial drain upon the financial resources of the Applicant. Figures prepared for the Applicant were not available for all years but in so far as they were available through the income tax returns produced in evidence, they disclosed the following:-

Whether the outrage so expressed was the genuine protest of an innocent victim or the posturing of a cheat lies very much at the heart of the case. The Applicant freely and openly acknowledged his outburst. It was nothing to be proud of but, if he was an honest man accused of dishonesty despite his explanations, it was understandable.

Conceded Matters

  1. At the hearing the items B (overstated purchases) and C ("printing and stationery") were conceded by the Applicant. An explanation was offered as to the former item, but not the latter. The explanation offered was that the accountant had counted as "purchases" an amount advanced to the Applicant by his wife through her bank account to purchase goods and, in error, the same amount drawn on the bank account of the Applicant to reimburse her for the advance. Although no explanation was advanced by the Applicant as to the latter item, Counsel for the Commissioner did not seek to attribute any special significance to it.

The Sales Issue

  1. The crucial issue in this case is that relating to the understatement of sales. If there was such an understatement the possibilities were that staff or others misappropriated or stole the moneys without the knowledge of the Applicant; or that the Applicant knew of and concealed the relevant takings. If the former, the Applicant had no knowledge of it and the misappropriation did not result in any understatement by him of his taxable income. If the latter, he was plainly dishonest and generally unworthy of credit.
  2. The relevant evidence establishes the following. Commencing in November 1978 the Applicant took possession of a small, prime retailing site in a major store. He commenced marketing perfumes, cosmetics and the like. The store was staffed by his daughter and his non-English speaking son-in-law. Neither of them was experienced in retailing, and the Applicant had no commercial experience beyond being involved in running a one-man medical practice as a general practitioner. Trading conditions were substantially regulated by store policy. That being so, the conduct of the business was largely determined by outside influences in relation to marketing practices such as "sales" and discounting. The range of products which could be sold was also determined by the store-proprietors.
  3. From the nature of the business, trading was only strong in the Christmas period and before "Mother's Day". Early in 1980, on two weeks notice, the Applicant was required to vacate the premises. Having substantial stocks on hand and more stock on order and in course of delivery - but not yet "on hand" - he arranged another tenancy, but on this occasion chose a location in a suburban area. Neither of those major decisions had been made in consultation with his accountant. Had he taken advice, he might not have been dissuaded from his original decision, but he would certainly have been strongly counselled against any decision to move a retailing operation of the type in question into a suburban area such as he chose. The evidence of his accountant was that a store of the type being conducted was quite inappropriate to such a suburban centre, even if it marketed an extended range of goods.
  4. After one year, the initial tenancy of the suburban shop was renewed for another year. But, shortly after the second year of the term commenced, the daughter and her husband separated and the husband left the area. The daughter "dropped her bundle" and lost interest in the business. In consequence, the Applicant engaged a stranger to conduct the shop. Towards the close of that second year of trading a lady with some marketing experience came on the scene and persuaded the Applicant that she was capable of bringing the business to a profitable condition, if it could be conducted from a more appropriate site. That was arranged. But the delays in obtaining fittings for the new premises resulted in a cessation of selling between the closing of the suburban site in February 1982 and the opening of the new store in the central business district - but not on a prime retailing site - in May 1982.
  5. From the closure of the original site, great difficulty had been experienced in marketing the perfumes and cosmetics which had been intended as the principal trading commodities. Those difficulties continued, even though prices were substantially reduced. Furthermore, the stock deteriorated in condition, market-appeal and value to the point where a wholesale disposal was attempted with some success, but not sufficient to prevent the dumping of quantities of stock which had cost the Applicant several thousands of dollars. Bulk sales were effected to a local chemist in sums totalling $3,137 in the month of April 1982. Even though boosted by such sales, the gross sales figure for the year ended 30 June 1982 was only $18,099.
  6. Yet it remains a remarkable feature of this case that, in the same trading year and in the two years following, the cost of goods introduced to the business exceeded the total sales for the year. I consider that circumstance to be remarkable and I have no doubt that the investigators also thought so, and reasonably thought so. However, I am satisfied that the significance of the point was lost upon the Applicant. He seems to have always thought that the solution to his existing difficulties was to continue to trade and in doing so to increase sales, and to achieve that by expanding the volume and range of goods on hand available for sale. In the view of the matter taken by the Commissioner, he was not only correct in that theory, but was successful in implementing it.
  7. The new store did better. Initially it was under the control of the marketing lady who operated the shop with some assistance from daughters of the Applicant, at times such as late night shopping; Saturday morning shopping; and in rush periods, such as Christmas. The Applicant came to have suspicions as to the honesty of his employee. I make no finding whether the suspicions were well founded. But I do consider the suspicions to be indicative of the concern of the Applicant at the continuing failure of the business. In due course the service of that lady came to an end, and from that point the Applicant's third daughter came to be employed full time until the store finally closed.
  8. I am satisfied that there was at all material times an unsophisticated, and simple system maintained for co-relating sales and stock. I accept that there was no other record known to the Applicant than that produced in evidence in relation to the periods covered by the exhibits. I accept that stocktaking was carried out annually, with a detailed inventory being taken at 30 June 1982; a summary only being provided to the Applicant by the manageress at 30 June 1983; and a calculation done by reference only to stock records at 30 June 1984. I am satisfied that to the best of the knowledge of the Applicant the records provided to him were accurate. I am satisfied that the Applicant did not fully understand the figures provided, or the significance of them.
  9. The investigators took a different view of the matter. When they interviewed the Applicant they were told his version of the stock-mix and of the "mark-ups" sought to be obtained in the operation of the business. 30% on perfumes; 100% on jewellery. From that point they projected that he should have grossed far higher sales figures than had been returned. Furthermore, they took the view that there had been sales to substantially match the ambition. Taking a product-mix ratio of 51:49 for Perfume : Giftware; making some allowance for the forced sale; and allowing for profits of 30% on perfumes and 75.5% on giftware, they concluded

I observe that such an approach, unless supported by other evidence, is quite unreliable. If businesses always performed substantially in accordance with the expectations of their proprietors there would be few, if any, business failures.

  1. Having heard the evidence of the Applicant, I am satisfied that his evidence relating to shop operations was honest and accurate. The inconsistency between the calculations of the Commissioner's officers and the assetions of the taxpayer is not to be explained in this instance by finding the calculations to be so sound and persuasive as to impute dishonesty to the taxpayer. Having found the Applicant to be honest and in material respects accurate, it follows that I am satisfied that the calculations of the investigators were unsound. It is unfortunate that in due course the assessments which issued and which are now before me were based upon those speculations.
  2. I am confirmed in that assessment by a consideration of the evidence of the youngest of the daughters to give evidence and also by the calculations by way of "asset betterment" and "source and applications of funds" statements which were presented in evidence.
  3. However, before any assessments issued, further aspects of the affairs of the Applicant were examined by the investigators. Further adjustments were then made resulting in disallowance in part of claims to deductions in relation to wages paid to a friend-employee and to the second and third daughters of the Applicant.

The Employed Friend

  1. The friend employee was an unmarried lady. She died in 1986 in her 62nd year. She had lived with the Applicant's family for many years as a member of the family, having come to the city with them when they moved from the country. I accept the evidence of the Applicant that, although she participated in the life of the family as an adult member of the household, cooking meals occassionally just as did others, and generally sharing in household chores and contributing provisions, she did not do so in any sense as a servant. I find that she was in no way to be identified with any of the housekeepers who were retained from time to time on a part-time, live-out basis by the Applicant to perform domestic services for the family for reward. I also accept the evidence that the lady had her own rightful pride and desire for independence and for recognition of her self-reliance. She worked for many years as a cleaner of the surgery; as a laundress of "clean" used linen from the surgery; as a steriliser of equipment; and as a "girl Friday" running messages, attending to banking in relation to the surgery and the shop; and attending to all manner of minor duties. I find that the Applicant paid her for her services. I find that the rate of payment increased from time to time when award rates changed. The evidence of the Applicant was that she was paid according to the Award - no more and no less - for the hours she worked - no more and no less. I accept that evidence. Accepting it as I do, I can understand the sense of outrage felt by the Applicant when it was proposed that in substantial measure the remuneration he paid to the lady was for her services rendered as a domestic in his private household.

The Daughters

  1. At different times during the relevant periods four-daughters of the Applicant were employed by him. The investigators recommended that the amounts claimed and previously allowed as deductions for wages paid to the second and third daughters should be substantially disallowed. On the face of it that might have seemed to be reasonable, considering that both girls were full-time tertiary students at the time, and that one of them was residing away from the home while in attendance at her University. But those two women and their younger sister gave evidence before me as to what they did and how it was arranged. As to the daughter who was a full-time student at a distant university, I accept her evidence that throughout the relevant period, even when she was in "full-time" attendance at her university she was ordinarily home no later than the Thursday of each week, returning to University at the end of the weekend. As it is material to other questions yet to be considered, I accept that the regularity of her visits to the family home was as much prompted by her interest in her boyfriend as it was in being with the family. I accept that she did the typing work of the practice on the Friday and Saturday of each week and that for each hour she worked she was paid, strictly in accordance with the Award. I accept that throughout the relevant period there was always a typist available for the Applicant to enable him to perform the duties of his office; and that his daughter was that typist, In paying his daughter strictly in accordance with the Award she had the advantage that her father paid her at penalty rates for the Saturday work and, perhaps he had the advantage that, by reason of those payments, other demands that his daughter might have made upon him for financial support would have been reduced,
  2. In relation to the other daughter whose "wages" are in question, I find that she was undertaking her tertiary studies locally. That enabled her to follow a regular practice of working an early morning shift at the surgery before going off to her studies, returning in the later part of the afternoon to continue working as a receptionist and assistant to her father until the surgery closed. I accept her evidence that, on the rare occasions on which she was not able to adhere to that pattern of hours, she "made up" the missing time. I also accept that at all material times she was paid only for the hours worked and that she was paid strictly in accordance with the Award.
  3. As was held by Menzies J. in Stewart v F.C.T. (73 A.T.C. 4007), in considering the application of section 65 of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act"), it is not always appropriate to take as the measure of reasonable remuneration of family members payments provided for under industrial awards. However, in the particular circumstances of this case, having regard to the nature of the duties performed, and the circumstance that they were performed at the surgery under the same conditions as would have applied to any other person working for those periods at those times and about those duties, I am satisfied that it was reasonable that they should be paid as they were paid by the Applicant.

Home Telephone

  1. In the years of income in question the total cost of the home telephone was $844, $1390 and $1558. The Commissioner disallowed one-half of each claim. The Applicant challenges that disallowance as excessive and, in particular, points in necessarily general terms to the following considerations; first, that the telephone at home was treated as a matter of priority as a phone principally provided for convenience of patients. I accept that. It was also the evidence of the Applicant that he was concerned to restrict the children in their use of the phone in order to prevent them tying it up for prolonged intervals. I accept that. In a household so large as his that would be a necessary consideration anyway. However, I find corroboration of the evidence of the Applicant in a special way in a remark from one daughter - the one who came home for week-ends. She indicated that there was the public phone down the street available to be used for personal calls such as she seems to have often made. Secondly, the Applicant points to the circumstance that, when he became distrustful of the employed manageress, responsibility for ordering for the shop passed largely to his wife and that that resulted in her engaging in many interstate calls on the home phone. That evidence is consistent with the substantial increase in the cost of the home phone in the two later years.
  2. But I am not persuaded that it is appropriate to find - as the Applicant had claimed - that no portion of the cost of the home telephone should be considered "private". A finding of fact is called for. Understandably the evidence is imprecise and there must be an exercise of judgment. Bearing in mind the size of the household and the range of personal interests of the members of the household which were "private" in character, I consider that justice will be done if I reduce the amount disallowed to 40% of the total cost of the home phone in the year of income ended 30 June 1982 and to 25% of total cost in the years of income ended 30 June 1983 and 1984.

Additional Tax

  1. Section 226(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act") provided that penalties for incorrect returns should be imposed at 200% of tax avoided unless the Commissioner, in his discretion determined to remit those penalties in whole or in part (s.226(3)). The Commissioner did exercise his discretion in favour of the Applicant and reduced those penalties to figures reflecting a calculation whereby additional tax levied was to equal in each year 20% per annum of tax avoided until the date of calculation preceding the amended assessments; plus 40% of tax avoided as a "culpability factor". The result was that the penalties imposed were $17,329, $9,769 and $7,441; - a total of $34,759. It is to be noted that in effecting the remission no regard was had to factors which might have distinguished different elements of the alleged understatements or excessive claims in their gravity, or by reason of any other considerations. Although the allegations as to matters of understatements of gross sales, if true, indicated gross dishonesty on the part of the Applicant, that was not so in relation to some other items. In the event the Applicant has been successful on the more serious items (Items A & F) and in my view it is quite wrong that, in imposing any additional tax that might be imposed, he should be considered as a dishonest taxpayer.
  2. It therefore remains to consider what reduction if any of in additional tax there should be made by reason of the remaining factors:-
    • (a) the accounting error which resulted in a double claim for one item of purchases in the 1984 year of income. (Item B)
    • (b) the claim in relation to purchases of "art books, educational books and requisites for children" incorrectly claimed as "postage and stationery" as to which neither side offered any explanation. (Item C - On the part of the Applicant there was no explanation as to how or why the claim was made and, on the part of the Commissioner, there was no suggestion that, for example, the expenditure had not been incurred).
    • (c) the matters of adjustment relating to "motor vehicle" (Item D)" and home telephone" expenses (Item E); matters in relation to which there was no suggestion that the gross expenditure was not incurred, but rather only a difference in judgment on a matter of fact as to the proportion of the expenses to be allowed.

The Remission Decision

  1. The recommendation as to penalty made by an officer to his supervisor in March 1986 was as follows:-

"The level of co-operation experienced was somewhat less than is reasonable in respect of similar case situations.

Some minor aggravating factors were present such as taxpayer's evasive nature when questioned and somewhat varying stories. Taxpayer was also somewhat abusive on occasions.

Submit Incorrect Return Penalty be imposed in accordance with Income Tax Ruling 2012".

  1. The decision then taken was recorded as follows:-

"I would agree with the above. At the last interview I attended with the taxpayer he became more abusive. Myself and Mr B. were referred to at different times as "donkeys", "liars" and "thieves". Because (the Applicant's) co-operation on the whole was reasonable however there is no compelling reason for the imposition of an aggravation factor. Impose penalty at a rate of 40% plus 20% p.a. interest.

The history of section 226; Additional Tax.

  1. The statutory provisions relevant to the issue of additional tax for incorrect returns were constant from the enactment of the present Act in 1936 until 1984. However, the perceptions and practices of the Commissioner changed over the years. Prior to February 1983 the common practice of the Commissioner was to so effect remissions so as to fix upon penalties as a simple percentage of tax avoided. (Then, and now, references to percentages in this context are often confusing. The Act provided that in the absence of any remission, penalties would be 200% of tax avoided. Thus, if tax avoided was $1,200, penalties (in the the absence of remission) would stand at $2,400. However, if the Commissioner exercised his discretion to remit and actually imposed penalties at $900, it was common practice for the Commissioner to say that he had remitted 75% of statutory penalties whereas the taxpayer would say that the Commissioner had imposed penalties of 75%. The difference was occasioned by using the same percentage figures relative to different concepts. The Commissioner was referring to 75% of the statutory penalties, or 200% of tax avoided; The taxpayer was referring to 75% of tax avoided). But the practice of setting penalties in that fashion at a constant percentage over several years was deficient in that the longer the period of evasion, the less the sanction. If the period of evasion extended for from 5 to 10 years before assessment and penalties were set at 50% of tax avoided for each year (as was common place in days past) the penalties ranged from 5% per annum at the 10 year period to 10% per annum at 5 years.
  2. That deficiency in practice was overcome with the issue by the Commissioner of Income Tax Ruling I.T. 2012 on 25 January 1983 with effect from 14 February 1983. (For subsequent rulings see I.T. 2028, 2043 and 2206). From that point it was provided that, in "non-voluntary cases", the statutory penalty of 200% should be remitted to a figure represented by two components: a compensatory factor and a culpability factor. It was provided that the compensatory factor should be calculated at the rate of 10% per annum of tax avoided up to and including 13 February 1983 and 20% per annum thereafter. To that extent the compensatory rate was in line with the rate of interest payable by taxpayers on unpaid tax (cf. section 207). As to the culpability factor it was provided that there would be

"a basic penalty of ... 40% of the tax avoided ... subject to increase by a further percentage of the tax avoided ... depending the seriousness of the offence".

It was acknowledged that

"from time to time there will he cases were the appropriate circumstances warrant further remission ... these cases should be seen as limited and exceptional".

  1. Despite the recommendations that officers responsible for the determination of penalties should use their discretion in increasing or reducing penalties according to the gravity of the case or lack of it, unfortunately seemingly countless cases before the Tribunal have illustrated that the exhortation (such as it was) to exercise discretion was rarely heeded. 40% as a culpability factor simply became the norm - rarely exceeded and seldom reduced. As a result injustices were numerous; penalties were quite often disproportionate to the seriousness of the fault; and not infrequently the penalties imposed were quite draconian. I extensively criticized the lack of exercise of discretion in Case U36 (87 A.T.C. 266) - a case dealing with a variety of taxpayers whose "offences" were at the lower range of seriousness - as I have criticised the practice in many cases since. So have others. At the same time the appropriateness of having recourse to concepts of both compensation and culpability in determining the level of remissions has been recognised.
  2. For present purposes, in view of the issues to be considered I note that, in particular in Case U36, I considered the question of compensatory interest and I then said (ibid, p 269)

"I have referred to the assessment of interest at 10% p.a. up to February 1983 and at 20% p.a. thereafter as "compensatory". The rates are the same as those which have operated or now operate against taxpayers who delay in paying tax which has been assessed against them and which has fallen due for payment. To that extent use of the same rates against taxpayers who have had the benefit of causing a deferment of assessment seems not unreasonable. However, it should be noted that the rate of 20% p.a. is, for most people at most times, a high rate of interest, just as the 10% p.a. rate applicable in the years immediately preceding the change to 20% p.a. was low. No doubt the rate of 20% p.a. was chosen to deter taxpayers from delaying payment of tax.

"But it should not be overlooked that a 20% p.a. rate does work severely, and even harshly, against persons such as these applicants. None of them belongs to the world of high finance. None of them controls great wealth, or holds high office or exercises great power. Some of them did not, in any realistic sense, even have an opportunity to avoid liability during the period of accrual. Assuming that the moneys withheld were available to them for investment, rather than in meeting reasonably incurred expenses of living from day to day, it is unlikely that any of these persons could have earned any more than 12% p.a., which earnings would in turn have been liable to tax. The result is that persons whose gain from delay may be only about 10% p.a. after tax can be liable to pay 20% p.a. - non-deductible - on tax avoided. It is not always a major consideration, but it is a matter which I will take into account in assessing the appropriate culpability factor after bringing to account the "compensation factor" as calculated by the Commissioner in order to determine an appropriate figure for additional tax.

  1. Now, from effect from 15 February 1989, the Commissioner has issued a more comprehensive Ruling (I.T. 2517) to replace his former rulings I.T. 2012, I.T. 2028, I.T. 2043 and I.T. 2206. The ruling correctly stresses the need to exercise discretion and to do so "in a common-sense manner" recognising that,

"although section 223(1) is clearly intended to penalise heavily taxpayers who seek to evade their correct liability to tax, it is equally obvious that this legislation is not to be administered so as to be seen as oppressive by those taxpayers who, although caught by section 223(1), have made an honest attempt to fulfill their obligations under the income tax law. Sub-section 227(3) recognises that, in the context of sub-section 223(1), there are degrees of culpability. Some situations will require substantial additional tax, others less substantial. Some, although these will be exceptional, may not warrant any additional tax at all".

In paragraph 41 of the ruling the Commissioner has set out a "schedule of typical base criteria and "culpability component ranges", emphasising properly that:-

"The criteria and the additional tax ranges appearing below are intended to be a guide only".

The table appearing is as follows:-

  1. For the reasons expressed in Case U36 (ante) I consider the table to provide a much more reasonable guide. But, having said so much, the comment should not be considered as giving or withholding approval to the elements of the table. For the moment it simply suffices to say that the principles expressed are much more reasonable than those previously, commonly applied. I have mentioned these matters in order to lay a foundation for a consideration of two questions which must be addressed.
  2. The first is whether one should have regard to the new policy as warranting a reduction in additional tax as imposed on assessments which issued well before the issue of Income Tax Ruling 2517. In my view it is proper that one should do so. Nothing in the legislation has changed. Conventional legal theory has it that the Courts, and this Tribunal, have always had a clear understanding of the rights and obligations created by that legislation. So it is that, to the extent to which Ruling IT 2517 correctly reflects principles to be applied in the application of section 226, it merely correctly states principles of construction which have always been operative. That being so, to the extent which it accords with the reasons for decision in Case U36, it is appropriate to now acknowledge I.T. 2517 as relevant to the determination of all unresolved issues.
  3. The second question is whether, in circumstances where a more liberal policy of remissions is proposed by I.T. 2517 than was considered appropriate in Case U36 (ante), the more generous view of the matter of remissions as perceived by the Commissioner should be applied. In my view it should. I express that view not so much because I consider what was said in Case U36 to be incorrect, but rather because of the circumstance that the policies and practices of the Commissioner, operating as they do throughout the nation and operating on an on-going basis year after year, do form part of the total social order in which the legislative power applies and power of review has to be exercised. In my view it would be unjust to uphold the view expressed in Case U36 as to compensatory interest when as a matter of declared policy the Commissioner is being more lenient in that regard.

The Principles

  1. For the foregoing reasons, in exercising the discretion as to remission of penalties by way of additional tax I confirm the view previously expressed that the fixing of penalties does involve an act of discretion, and is not a mere mathematical exercise whereby the precision of mathematical calculations is used to give an illusion of certainty and precision to the exercise of judgment which that exercise lacks. None the less I record that, in my view, in addressing the questions of remission which I am about to address it is appropriate to have regard to a per annum rate of interest of the order of 14.026%. It is also appropriate to have regard to the "scales" appearing in para. 39 of these reasons.
  2. In considering the question of remissions it is also appropriate to consider whether the penalties otherwise appropriate should be increased by reason of the terms of abuse used by the Applicant towards the Commissioner's officers. To their credit they thought not. In the circumstances of this case I agree. Officers of the Commissioner going about their necessary duties should not be obliged to suffer abuse. But taxpayers going honestly about their affairs should not have to suffer unsound allegations reflecting on their integrity either.
  3. Whenever investigators ask questions suggesting dishonesty and, in particular when they persist with their inquiries, their endeavours are not only likely to be resented but quite often will meet with aggressive responses. They may be threatening or, less seriously, merely insulting. They may be responses of the sophisticated dishonest or the unsophisticated outbursts of the honest. But whichever it may be, investigating officers should not be overly sensitive. The officers in this case thought that the abuse in question did not warrant consideration as a factor of aggravation even though they considered the Applicant to be evasive in concealing the under-statement of his income. I not only agree with that but, a fortiori, do so when it is recognised that the suggestion underlying the inquiries which provoked the response was fundamentally unsound.
  4. It now remains to apply those principles to the several headings of which have been considered in these reasons.

(A) Understated Sales

I have found that there has been no understatement. Accordingly to the appropriate extent the impositions of additional tax are to be discharged. At this point it is also appropriate to observe that it was the Commissioner's incorrect perception of deceitfulness in this regard which lay a foundation for the imposition of a 40% culpability factor, not only in relation to this item, but in relation to all others. At the same time, I note that the "abusiveness" was not considered as a compelling factor warranting an increase of that 40% rate.

(B) Over-stated Purchases

The error was conceded on behalf of the Applicant. However, it was explained. It was explained as an error of the accountants making, rather than of the Applicant. It is deserving of only a nominal culpability factor such as is suggested by the categories "minor case of carelessness" or "inadvertent error, honest mistake, dependent on the degree of care", with a recommended culpability component of the order of 5-15% in the former case and 0-5% in the latter.

(C) Overclaim: Printing and Stationary

The error was conceded. No explanation was offered by the Applicant, nor was inability to or the failure to offer an explanation, exposed by Counsel for the Commissioner. A moderate penalty is called for. In the absence of an explanation a culpability factor appropriate to "carelessness" (15-30%) and "recklessness (30-40%) needs to be considered.

(D,E) Telephone and Motor Vehicle Expenses

As the evidence stands before me there is no question but that the expenditure was incurred and was accurately described in the returns of income. The default lies in the circumstance that the Applicant claimed the entirety of the relevant expenditure, rather than portion only. In my view, by reason of the decision of the full Federal Court of Australia in Rabinov v F.C.T. (83 A.T.C. 4437) and the circumstance that all returns were lodged prior to December 1984, there can be no imposition of additional tax for incorrect return. Accordingly, to the appropriate extent the assessment of additional tax will be discharged.

(F,G) Wages

I find there has been no default and, accordingly, to the appropriate extent, any imposition of additional tax will be discharged.
