Yeo and Commissioner of Taxation [1999] AATA 893; (1999) 43 ATR 1195; 99 ATC 2422 (26 November 1999)

Last Updated: 8 October 2009





DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION [1999] AATA 893

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No VT1999/4, VT1999/5 and

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION ) VT1999/98



Re KIAN BOH YEO

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member



Date 26 November 1999

Place Melbourne

Decision The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.



........(Sgd) B. H. Pascoe.....................

Senior Member

CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX – interest deductions – rented properties – alleged private unsecured loans – whether funds borrowed – whether used for purchase of properties – whether interest paid – burden of proof

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936

Taxation Administration Act 1953



REASONS FOR DECISION



26 November 1999 Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member



1. This is an application to review a decision to disallow objections against amended assessments of income tax for the years ended 30 June 1995, 1996 and 1997. The amendments were issued to disallow as deductions claims for interest expense relating to rental properties and said to be interest on unsecured private loans.

2. At the hearing the applicant, Mr Yeo, was unrepresented. The respondent was represented by an officer of the respondent. Evidence was given by the applicant and by Ms S, an auditor employed by the respondent. In addition, the Tribunal had the documents provided by the respondent pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (T1 – T32).

3. During the relevant years, the applicant owned one rental property in his own name and five rental properties in the joint names of himself and his wife. The disputed claim for interest related to the applicant's claimed share of interest on private loans and amounted to $21,375 in the 1995 and 1996 years and $20,966 for the 1997 year. Mr Yeo maintained that the loans for the majority of the period were from three private lenders who had provided the funds in cash and the interest was paid in cash monthly to the lenders or their representatives who called at his house to collect. He said that he did not know the lenders personally and could not recall who recommended them. He believed that he had correctly recorded the addresses of the lenders at the time of the loans but had no knowledge of subsequent or current addresses. Mr Yeo argued that a borrower does not need to know where a lender lives. All loans were said to have been unsecured, for ten years and interest only payments at the rate of 25% per annum. He stated that this was the normal market rate and no bargaining or negotiation was appropriate. In response to a request for information by the respondent in June 1997, the applicant included, with the information he provided, copies of documents titled "Term of Loan Agreement" for each of the claimed private loans. These were identical in their form and had signatures of Mr Yeo and his wife but no lenders' signatures.

4. Mr Yeo said that the private unsecured loans were repaid in 1997 by a new lender resident in Korea. He said that he told whoever came to collect the monthly interest that he wanted to consolidate his loans and gave them the address of a Mr Chang in Korea. He was not aware of any details of how the loans were settled but assumed that the original lenders were repaid or "they would have come after me". Mr Yeo was unable to provide any documentation of the new loan or discharge of the earlier loans other than three handwritten receipts dated 27 or 28 March 1997. He said that he was introduced to Mr Chang by his wife from whom he was now divorced and who made regular trips to Korea. Mr Yeo said that he had made some remittances to Korea but had not paid interest in recent times because of shortage of funds. He had since sold five of the six properties and repaid the loan principal although there may well be some unpaid interest. He said that the interest on the new loans was between 22% and 25% and unsecured.

5. While Mr Yeo maintained that all properties purchased were fully geared as he had no other funds available when they were purchased in 1991 and 1992, he was unable to explain some apparent timing differences between dates of purchase and dates of loans other than by assuming that he borrowed funds from other lenders whose identity he could not now recall. Similarly, he was unable to explain why he borrowed from the bank at 9.5% on five properties but borrowed the whole of the purchase price of the sixth at 25% from a private lender. He was unable to provide any explanation when it was put to him by the respondent that two of the three address given for the original private lenders did not appear to exist and that there was no record of the third lender ever having occupied the address given. The respondent placed in evidence a copy of a signature, on an income tax return, of the only alleged lender who could be identified. Mr Yeo could not explain why that signature differed significantly from a signature purporting to be of that same lender on a receipt for the repayment of principal which was dated 27 March 1997 and which was some three and a half years after the lender apparently departed permanently from Australia.

6. Mr Yeo submitted that the funds on which interest was claimed must have come from somewhere as he had no funds of his own. He argued that the basis of the loans was normal within his culture, the rate was market rate for such loans and the lack of documentary evidence should not preclude his entitlement to a deduction. He further submitted that any documentation he may have had may well have disappeared as a result of a burglary at his house on 27 April 1997 prior to the commencement of enquiries by the respondent.

7. For the respondent it was submitted that the applicant had failed to discharge the onus of proof imposed upon him by section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 in that he had failed to produce evidence of any weight to establish that funds were borrowed from private lenders and applied to the purchase of rental properties or that interest was paid on such purported loans. Alternatively, it was argued that the creation of the purported loans and interest thereon was a sham and not intended to create the legal rights and obligations which they gave the appearance of creating.

8. Section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 provides:

"14ZZK On an application for review of a reviewable objection decision:

(a) ...

(b) the applicant has the burden of proving that:

(i) if the taxation decision concerned is an assessment (other than a franking assessment) – the assessment in excessive;

(ii) ...

(iii) ..."

Here, Mr Yeo has the burden of proving, on the balance of probabilities, that funds were borrowed from private sources, these funds were utilised in the purchase of rental properties and the interest claimed was paid to such lenders.

9. The identity of the alleged lenders must be in doubt. Mr Yeo admitted that he did not know them personally, the addresses provided were either incorrect or false and no documentary evidence was produced other than a standard document which would appear to have been generated by Mr Yeo himself and not signed by any alleged lender. No corroborative evidence of the receipt of the funds, alleged to be by bank cheques or cash was provided. The best that can be said was that some funds from some source must have been available to enable the properties to be purchased. To attribute the source as private lenders relies on the oral evidence of Mr Yeo solely. He was unable to explain the flow of funds used to purchase a property in Maidstone which was purchased in March 1990. The date of the loan document from a Wee Shui Huat is May 1992 for $6,000 and a mortgage to the Bank of Melbourne for $84,000 was registered on 27 May 1992. Mr Yeo assumed that the original purchase price must have come from an unsecured loan but could not recall from whom or when the money was borrowed.

10. Mr Yeo was unable to provide any documentary evidence of the alleged new loan from Mr Chang nor of the discharge of the alleged loans from the three prior lenders. There was some evidence of subsequent remittance of funds to Korea but nothing in this evidence demonstrated that the remittances related to repayment of debt or interest thereon. While Mr Yeo was unable to identify the current whereabouts of his three alleged providers of unsecured finance, the Tribunal was asked to accept that somehow, Mr Chang from Korea was able to settle with each of them on 27 and 28 March 1997.

11. The alleged interest on private loans was said to have been paid in cash to the lenders or their associates who called at his house. Although purported receipts for such payments were produced there is no evidence that they were made out by an alleged lender or made on the dates shown thereon. Again there is only the uncorroborated evidence of Mr Yeo that such payments of interest were made in cash.

12. The alleged basis of the private loan arrangements leaves me in some doubt as the likely commercial nature of such borrowings. All alleged loans in 1995 and 1996 carried an interest rate of 25%. In each of the relevant years the alleged interest on private loans on four properties exceeded the gross rents derived from six properties. The interest on the new loan from Mr Chang was said to be between 22% and 25%. The then rate of interest charged on the secured loans from the bank was 9.5%. Mr Yeo was unable to convince me that borrowing funds at such high rates of interest could have yielded any commercial profit on the rented properties.

13. At the conclusion of the hearing, Mr Yeo said that he had done his best, provided what he could provide and could not produce any more evidence. Unfortunately for him, what was provided was not enough. Given the onus placed on him, I cannot be satisfied that the alleged funds were borrowed, were used to purchase rental properties or interest of the amounts claimed was paid. While not going so far as to say that I did not believe the evidence of Mr Yeo, it was necessary for him to provide some corroborative evidence. No other witnesses were called and there was too much of the evidence given which was vague, difficult to accept and lacking in commercial reality. It follows that I am not satisfied that Mr Yeo has discharged the burden of proving that the assessments in dispute were excessive and, consequently, must affirm the decision under review.

I certify that the thirteen (13) preceding paragraphs are a true copy of the reasons for the decision herein of



Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member
