ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member

Date 20 January 1997

Place Melbourne

Decision The Tribunal varies the decisions under review and remits the matter to the respondent with a direction that he amend the assessments in accordance with the findings and reasons for decision.



.....(Sgd) Mr B. H. Pascoe..........

Senior Member



CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - used car dealer - assessments amended as result of audit - deposits to various bank accounts assessed as income - whether non-assessable amounts included - whether further deductions allowable - whether certain bank accounts reflected business carried on by another person

Income Tax Assessment 1936

Taxation Administration Act 1953

Krew v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 71 ATC 4213

Martin v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 93 ATC 5200

Trautwein v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63

Ma v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 92 ATC 4373

REASONS FOR DECISION

20 January 1997 Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member



  1. These are applications to review decisions of the respondent to disallow objections lodged by the applicant against amended assessments for the years ended 30 June 1989 and 30 June 1990 and an assessment for the year ended 30 June 1991. The respondent had conducted an audit of the applicant’s affairs in 1991 and considered that the applicant had understated his income for the years ended 30 June 1989 and 1990. The applicant had not lodged a return of income for the year ended 30 June 1991 at the time of the audit but had supplied information to his tax agent for completion of the return. The respondent considered that the draft return understated the applicant’s income. The alleged understatements were as follows:

Year ended 30 June 1989 $499,460

Year ended 30 June 1990 1,804,357

Year ended 30 June 1991 663,114

Total $2,966,931

  1. At the hearing the applicant was represented by Mr G Ring, an accountant, and the respondent by Mr S McBurney of the Australian Government Solicitor’s Office. Evidence was given by the applicant; Mr L, a business associate of the applicant; Mr Ring and by an officer of the respondent. The initial hearing was held on 29 and 30 August 1996. At the end of the evidence the hearing was adjourned, prior to submissions, to allow the parties to meet and attempt to agree on adjustments to the amount in dispute. After resumption on 26 September 1996, the hearing was further adjourned till 1 November 1996 to allow the applicant’s representative to obtain further information from banks.
  2. At the completion of the audit, the respondent provided the applicant with a statement of the alleged additional income with a letter dated 22 January 1993. The alleged additional income consisted of unidentified deposits to various bank accounts, omitted interest income, unidentified receipts from a Mr N, rental income and some other adjustments. The applicant did not respond to this letter and the respondent wrote again on 29 April 1996, advising of an intention to issue assessments to include the additional income plus additional tax of 45% in respect to the 1989 and 1990 years and 50% for the 1991 year. The assessment and amended assessments were issued on 3 May 1993. An objection was lodged on 2 June 1993 but provided no detail as to any alleged errors made by the auditor. The objection simply alleged that the assessments were excessive. By letters dated 25 February 1996, the respondent disallowed the objections on the grounds that the applicant had failed to provide evidence to show that the assessments were excessive.
  3. The applicant gave evidence that he had been employed until 26 January 1990. During that time he had carried on a business as a used car dealer and, after resigning from employment, carried on that business on a full-time basis. During his evidence he reiterated, on a very regular basis, that the disputed assessments were excessive to a significant degree because had had never possessed surplus funds and he had struggled financially constantly during the relevant period. He said that he could not afford legal advice or representation and that the tax agent who had prepared the relevant returns of income wanted to charge large fees, which he could not afford. He accepted that he operated several bank accounts during the period. However, he argued that new accounts were opened either because his previous banker would not clear cheques quickly, or in order to borrow funds. He said that one account was an overdraft to purchase a workshop and yard, another for the purpose of borrowing to purchase a personal car, and other accounts for personal loans. He maintained that he took out personal loans to survive and needed to obtain money anywhere he could in his regular financial struggle. He admitted that he took bank statements from two bank accounts only, together with the relevant cheque butts, to his tax agent to prepare the income tax returns. He did not produce his sales book or any other records to his tax agent as he “had not been told to”. He said that he did not tell his tax agent anything else but left him to “to work it out”.
  4. Included in the bank accounts, to which unidentified deposits had been lodged, were accounts with State Bank which formed the largest item in each of the years in dispute. The amounts deposited with this bank were:

Year ended 30 June 1989 $257,519

Year ended 30 June 1990 1,377,140

Year ended 30 June 1991 344,994

Total $1,979,653

The applicant’s evidence was that these accounts were not his but belonged to an associate, Mr L. He said that Mr L wished to operate as a wholesale used car trader but did not have a motor trader’s licence. As the applicant had known Mr L for fifteen years and considered him a friend, he said that he agreed to help by allowing Mr L to open bank accounts in the applicant’s name, using the applicant’s business name and purchase book. The applicant maintained however, that all funds deposited to the State Bank belonged to Mr L. He said that, on occasions, he bought a car from Mr L or sold one to him, and funds would be transferred to, or from, the State Bank account to the applicant’s own bank account. He said that he had told his tax agent about the State Bank accounts and had said “surely you need to put it in” but the tax agent told him that they were not needed for the applicant’s income tax return.

  1. The applicant said that he had maintained a purchase book recording all motor vehicles purchased and sold but this had been lost, together with most of his records after a break-in at his car yard. Other records had been lost when moving between homes. He maintained that sales agreements had been provided to customers but that he could not now locate copies of such agreements. He said also that he had purchased and sold blocks of land over the years but had not made any profit and had not retained records of such transactions.
  2. Mr L provided a written statement in which he said that he commenced operating as a used car dealer in May 1989 and ceased in October 1990 as a result of financial difficulties. He said that he had allowed his trader’s licence to lapse some years before 1989 but that the applicant had allowed him to share the applicant’s car yard, and share expenses and use the applicant’s licence number and business name. He maintained that the bank accounts at the State Bank were under his control and that he was responsible for all transactions on those accounts. He acknowledged that his affairs had been audited by the respondent and that he had not lodged income tax returns for the years ended 30 June 1988, 1989, or 1990. He said that he suffers from depression and was unable to remember details of interviews with officers of the respondent. He denied saying to such officers that he had been pressured into making the written statement or forced to sign it. He also denied that settlement of his income tax liability was influenced by any different version of events given to such officers. He said that he had lost all of his records and was unable to provide any details of the transactions during the relevant period.
  3. Mr Ring acted as both a witness and advocate for the applicant. He said that he had become involved only after the assessment and amended assessments had been issued. He said that the previous tax agent had provided very meagre working papers and that he had attempted to reconcile the figures included in the income tax returns with bank accounts. He maintained that the respondent’s figures included some double counting, transfers between bank accounts and some specific items which were not income. In addition, he considered that significant amounts of interest paid, bank fees and other expenses, had not been allowed as deductions. He had not done any work in relation to the State Bank accounts as he accepted the applicant’s statement that these accounts related to Mr L and not to the applicant.
  4. An officer of the respondent gave evidence that he had commenced an audit of the applicant’s affairs in 1992. He said that the applicant had not produced any records, saying that they had been destroyed, stolen or lost. The tax agent had provided some bank statements and working papers and had said that the applicant was not good at keeping records. The tax agent had included income from one or two bank accounts and had no knowledge of other accounts maintained by the applicant. The officer said that the applicant had not provided any explanations nor sought discussions with him. He said that without the applicant’s cooperation, he had no alternative but to propose assessing the applicant on the gross receipts according to the various bank statements from some 12 bank accounts, and statements from two accounts with a finance company. He had also included payments from another car dealer, Mr N, and rent received, which he had not been able to identify among the bank deposits. He said that, after issue of the 22 January 1993 letter, he had expected the applicant to seek discussions and provide further information but no response was forthcoming. He was not prepared to give an opinion as to whether the adjustments could be considered as reasonable, where neither the applicant or any representative produced any explanations for the apparent discrepancy. The only information that the applicant had provided was a general complaint about his financial difficulties and the absurdity of the respondent’s assessments, but no specific details.
  5. After hearing the evidence, I advised the parties that I considered that the Tribunal had been left with an impossible task. I indicated that it appeared unlikely that any representative of the respondent, who had been involved in this matter, genuinely believed that the applicant had derived a taxable income totalling nearly $3 million over three years and that the assessments in dispute appropriately reflected his income. On the other hand, the evidence from the applicant, whilst indicating that the assessments were likely to be excessive, did not go anywhere near providing a basis on which the degree of such excess could be estimated. The applicant was reminded of the obligations imposed by section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 which provides that, on an application for review of a reviewable objection decision, the applicant has the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive. In discharging this burden, it is not sufficient for the applicant to simply show that the assessment is excessive, he must be able to demonstrate the amount which is more properly to be regarded as an appropriate reflection of his taxable income. Whilst estimates made on inexact evidence have featured in many decisions of this Tribunal and the Courts (see, for example, Krew v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 71 ATC 4213 and Martin v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 83 ATC 5200), the evidence in this case had not provided any real basis on which I could embark on such estimates. However, the parties were reminded of the words of Davies J in Martin (supra) where His Honour said, at page 5207:
“I regard it as unsatisfactory that the Taxation Office has not sought to come to a conclusion about Mr Martin’s costs of earning his income save to say that expenditure not proven would not be allowed. An unwilling taxpayer, even though he is a tax evader whose evidence is not reliable, should not be assessed to tax on gross income. The Act requires that a judgment be made as to allowable deductions. I have sought to undertake this task, doing the best I can on the inadequate information available, and keeping in mind that the burden of proof lies on the taxpayer, Mr Martin.”

In this case, the assessments had included as assessable income, all deposits to bank accounts, in particular the State Bank Accounts, without any consideration as to whether the payments from such accounts were allowable deductions. Given the lack of any cooperation by the applicant, this is not necessarily a criticism of the respondent but it had left the Tribunal with a difficult, if not impossible, task. Consequently, I adjourned the hearing and urged the parties to attempt to agree on some reduction in the wide disparity between them.

  1. At the resumed hearing on 26 September 1996, Mr Ring sought a further adjournment. He advised that the parties had met and agreed on a number of items to be deducted from the respondent’s figures, but that he needed more time to obtain information from banks and to reconcile the various accounts. He explained that a lack of funds and resources had limited the amount of work which was needed. The respondent opposed any further adjournment on the grounds that, although meetings had been held and some concessions made by the respondent, there remained substantial sums in dispute, and only since the hearing, had any factual and detailed evidence been produced. It was said that the applicant had been given numerous opportunities since 1992 to provide information and it was inappropriate to give further time after the hearing had commenced. Mr McBurney said that items totalling less than $300,000 had been agreed, leaving substantial amounts still outstanding. After consideration, the applicant’s request for further adjournment was granted.
  2. On resumption on 1 November 1996, Mr Ring handed up handwritten schedules which purported to list adjustments for each of the years which would reduce the alleged understatements of income. Such adjustments were bank interest and charges not claimed previously, transfers between bank accounts, other deductions not claimed previously, non taxable deposits or other adjustments to the respondent’s figures. The totals of the schedules were:

Year ended 30 June 1989 $440,893

Year ended 30 June 1990 1,743,496

Year ended 30 June 1991 505,265

Total $2,689,654

Mr Ring submitted that, if further time and resources had been available, further adjustments would have been found to eliminate the remaining discrepancy. He believed that further transfers between accounts had taken place where, for example, the applicant had drawn $5,000 from one account, kept out $1,000 and banked $4,000 to another account. He submitted that the State Bank accounts were not the applicant’s and produced photocopies of cheques signed by Mr L alone. He argued that the respondent had adopted a narrow approach, had not attempted to come to grips with the applicant’s unorthodox treatment of money, had made many errors and taken a rigid, pedantic view in issuing the assessments. He maintained that scrutiny of the applicant’s finances demonstrated that he had continuously operated to the limit of resources, had increasing debt and many cheques drawn by him had been dishonoured by the banks. Mr Ring said that Tribunal should remit the matter to the respondent to prepare, and base assessments on, an asset betterment statement or, preferably, set aside the decisions under review and allow the objections in full.

  1. It was submitted for the respondent that, if there were any errors or omissions in the original calculations by the respondent, any problems were due to the applicant’s failure to honour his responsibilities under the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”). Mr McBurney confirmed that certain adjustments listed by Mr Ring had been conceded by the respondent, notwithstanding the lack of source documents. He also advised that the respondent would not contest any item of less than $1,000 in the interests of expediency. It was said that, in spite of the adjournment, the handwritten schedules of alleged adjustments was received on the day before the resumption of the hearing with no further details and the respondent was not in a position to agree to further concessions. It was pointed out that unsubstantiated deductions claimed in the returns had been allowed and there was no evidence that amounts shown by Mr Ring on his schedule were not included among those deductions. Mr McBurney submitted that the evidence had clearly shown that proceeds of car sales had been deposited to bank accounts other than those for which statements had been provided to the tax agent. He drew attention to the fact that the tax agent had not been called to give evidence and no records had been provided to the respondent or the Tribunal. He said that the applicant’s evidence showed that he had not attempted to provide all necessary information to his tax agent, had avoided his responsibilities and clearly sought to evade income tax. The 1991 tax return was said to be an example of the applicant’s irresponsibility, when that return was based on transactions of one bank account only, notwithstanding that the return was still in the course of preparation when the audit commenced.
  2. In relation to the State Bank accounts, Mr McBurney again referred to the fact that no records were produced. He said that Mr L could not lawfully trade without a licence, the applicant opened the bank accounts in the name of his registered business name and Mr L operated from the applicant’s premises. It was submitted that the logical inference was that Mr L was employed by the applicant in the applicant’s business but the parties sought to avoid the deduction of tax instalments and the liability for superannuation. Mr McBurney accepted the views of Davies J in Martin (supra) that it would be appropriate to allow some deductions against the gross receipts deposited to these State Bank accounts but was unable to assist the Tribunal to arrive at an appropriate level of such deductions. He urged the Tribunal to err on the side of a modest deduction so the applicant would not benefit from his failure to keep records.
  3. Notwithstanding the two adjournments, the applicant must bear the responsibility for leaving the Tribunal in a very difficult position. It was not so much a matter of not believing the evidence of the applicant but of the applicant producing very little factual information on which the Tribunal can make a finding. If his statements relating to his alleged financial struggle through the relevant years and the injustice being done to him by the respondent are put aside, there was very little value in his evidence. I believe that Mr Ring had conscientiously sought to make sense out of the little he had to work on, with I suspect, little financial reward and minimal cooperation from his client. The comments of Latham CJ in the case of Trautwein v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63 are well-suited to this matter, where he said, at pages 87 and 88:
“... The circumstance that the facts are (or were) peculiarly within the knowledge of one party is a relevant matter in considering the sufficiency of evidence to discharge a burden of proof. (See cases cited by Isaacs J. In Williamson v. Ah On [1926] HCA 46; (1926) 39 C.L.R. 95, at pp. 113-115). Obviously the facts in relation to his income are facts peculiarly within the knowledge of the taxpayer.

In the absence of some record in the mind or in the books of the taxpayer, it would often be quite impossible to make a correct assessment. The assessment would necessarily be a guess to some extent, and almost certainly inaccurate in fact. There is every reason to assume that the legislature did not intend to confer upon a potential taxpayer the valuable privilege of disqualifying himself in that capacity by the simple and relatively unskilled method of losing either his memory or his books.

The application of sec. 39 is not, in my opinion, excluded as soon as it is shown that an element in the assessment is a guess and that it is therefore very probably wrong. It is prima facie right--and remains right until the appellant shows that it is wrong. If it were necessary to decide the point I would, as at present advised, be prepared to hold that the taxpayer must, at least as a general rule, go further and show, not only negatively that the assessment is wrong, but also positively what correction should be made in order to make it right or more nearly right.”
  1. The only appropriate approach to take in this case is to look at three areas. The first and relatively easy area to deal with is the concession by the respondent as to amounts which have been accepted as either not assessable income or allowable deductions from the schedule prepared by Mr Ring. For the benefit of the parties it is appropriate that I set out the amount which I understood to have been accepted.

Year ended 30 June 1989 1990 1991



Bank Charges $2,867 $1,697 $1,116

Interest Paid 7,933 24,412 2,373

Adjustment for errors 23,063

Payments by Mr N 32,925 78,250

Contra amount 6,000

Capital receipt 15,000 11,814

Voluntary Departure Package 42,000

Loan - Mother 29,000

Other expenses 2,000

Rent 14,040

Amounts under $1,000 52 804 2,129



Total $81,840 $198,203 $17,432

  1. The second area for consideration is whether, on the balance of probabilities, other adjustments proposed by Mr Ring should be accepted as reducing the amount of alleged omitted income. Several amounts have been listed as “contras”, that is, amounts said to have been transfers from one bank account to another and, therefore, not constituting assessable income. It may well have been the position that the respondent would have accepted all or many of these, if the information and documentation had been provided at an earlier date. However, despite concerns at the production of this information some 18 months after the application for review by this Tribunal was lodged, I am prepared to accept Mr Ring’s statement that the items listed were transfers from one bank to another and so find. For the year ended 30 June 1989, the respondent had included deposits to an ANZ bank account which Mr Ring stated had been an account for which statements had been provided to and used by the tax agent in the preparation of the return lodged. Again, I am prepared to accept that this adjustment is appropriate. For the year ended 30 June 1991, there were five proposed adjustments listed by Mr Ring on his schedule which I am prepared to accept also. These were the receipt of proceeds of the sale of the applicant’s residence, the correction of an error in the amount included from a finance company account, cheques from customers banked by the applicant but subsequently dishonoured, cheques drawn by the applicant by subsequently returned and credited back to his account and the reversal of a journal entry made by the tax agent which had increased sales revenue shown in the return lodged. Whilst there was no confirmatory documentation provided for these adjustments and the respondent did not have adequate time or detail to consider their appropriateness, I am prepared to rely on and accept Mr Ring’s evidence that the alleged omitted income calculated by the respondent should be reduced by the items referred to above. Again, for the benefit of the parties, the amounts to be allowed in this category are as follows:

Year ended 30 June 1989 1990 1991



Contra amounts 39,096 118,015 60,370

Deposits to ANZ Account 46,998

Sale of residence 6,804

Correction of error 4,000

Dishonoured cheques 7,789

Returned cheques 14,280

Journal entry 25,031



Total $86,094 $118,015 $118,274

  1. Excluding the amounts deposited to the State Bank accounts, which is the third area for consideration, the only remaining items on Mr Ring’s schedules which I am not prepared to accept are amounts shown as additional purchases of cars or other expenses. No evidence, documentation, detail or explanation has been provided in relation to these items. It is not clear whether any attempt has been made by the applicant, or Mr Ring, to ensure that these expenses were not included in the deductions claimed and allowed in the returns lodged. Consequently, I am not prepared to find that such amounts represent allowable deductions over and above the deductions previously allowed. It is possible that they do but, having given the applicant the benefit of the doubt in the previous category of adjustments and recognizing the applicant’s onus of proof obligations, I am unable to say that, on the balance of probabilities, that these particular amounts should be allowed. I am somewhat influenced in this category of items that, in the main, Mr Ring has based his assertions on the applicant’s memory of what particular cheques were related to car purchases, etc. Given the evidence of the applicant, I am not prepared to rely on his memory. For completeness these proposed adjustments of Mr Ring, which I do not accept, total $15,440 for the year ended 30 June 1989, $50,138 for 1990 and $24,565 for 1991.
  2. The third and most difficult area to consider is the deposits to the State Bank accounts. Both the applicant and Mr L maintained that these accounts related solely to a business carried on by Mr L. However, the accounts were opened by the applicant and under his registered business name. Mr L said that all transactions on the account arose out of the use of the applicant’s licence and from the applicant’s premises. No detail was given as to how expenses were dealt with and whether expenses were paid direct out of this account, or whether Mr L reimbursed the applicant for a share of the expenses. As there was no evidence of what items were included in the applicant’s income tax returns, it is not known whether expenses claimed by him related to the alleged business carried on by Mr L. The evidence of Mr L has to be regarded with some caution. He is a long term friend of the applicant and had reached a settlement with the respondent in relation to the audit of his affairs. His evasiveness in cross-examination left me with a strong impression that the evidence he had given in this case, differed from the explanation given previously to the respondent in relation to his own affairs. He had also inexplicably lost all records of the alleged business transactions. I am prepared to accept that these accounts were used in motor vehicle trading activities and that Mr L was an authorised sole signatory to the accounts. However, I am not prepared to accept that the applicant had no interest or involvement in the transactions on these accounts. If the applicant’s evidence is accepted at face value, that the account related solely to a separate business carried on by Mr L, why would he have expected his tax agent to include the transactions in his return of income? On balance, I have come to the view that the applicant did have an equity or interest in these transactions. At the very least, it is likely that the transactions which were reflected in these accounts resulted from a joint venture between the applicant and Mr L in which wholesale, as opposed to retail, trading in motor vehicles was carried on. While Mr L may have managed the business and signed cheques, it is more likely as a partner or employee of the applicant, than as a independent sole trader.
  3. Having accepted that the State Bank accounts were used for motor vehicle trading and that the applicant had an interest in the transactions, the question on which no evidence was led by either party, is the likely quantum of profit that could reasonably have been expected. I am in a position similar to that of Davies J in Martin (supra) without even the limited evidence available to His Honour. When I put to Mr Ring that, if I was to find against him on the question of these bank accounts, it would be necessary to consider an appropriate profit margin, he ventured that, in his experience, profit margins of used car dealers varied from 10% to 20%, depending on economic conditions. The respondent was not prepared to venture a guess. No evidence in relation to this question was led at all. Nevertheless, some deduction from the gross income is appropriate. In Ma v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 92 ATC 4373, Burchett J said, at page 4379:
“...the making of estimates upon inexact evidence, which is so much a feature of both judicial and administrative decision making, cannot be uniquely excluded from appeals against betterment assessments.”

Here I do not have inexact evidence. I have no evidence. A guess has to be made. In this case I will allow a deduction equal to 70% of the gross deposits to the State Bank accounts. Further, and based on my view that there was a joint arrangement between the applicant and Mr L, I will allow a further 15% as the share of Mr L. The result is to include 15% of the deposits in the taxable income of the applicant or, in other words, to reduce the alleged omitted income included by the respondent by 85% of these deposits.

  1. The end result of the foregoing findings is that the taxable income of the applicant in each of the three years in question should be calculated as follows:

  1. The remaining issue is the matter of additional tax. The respondent imposed additional tax, at the rate of 45%, on the income tax payable under the amended assessments for the year ended 30 June 1989 and 1990. Additional tax, at the rate of 50%, was imposed on the income tax payable under the assessment for the year ended 30 June 1991. Such additional tax was imposed pursuant to sections 222 or 223 of the Act. Both these sections allow for additional tax equal to double the amount of tax payable, and the respondent remitted the amount of the above percentages under the discretion contained in section 227 of the Act. It is clear that the applicant deliberately omitted income. It is not clear to me, and I doubt whether the applicant knows the true extent of that omitted income. He accepted that receipts from the sale of motor vehicles were banked to accounts other than those for which statements were produced to his tax agent. He made no attempt to maintain adequate records and did not produce all of the limited records he did have to his tax agent. As indicated, I did not accept the applicant’s evidence relating to the State Bank accounts and came to the view that income banked in these accounts had been omitted deliberately. It might be said that the applicant has been treated generously by me in reaching the conclusions that I have on the basis of limited or no details, and the seeming lack of interest by the applicant in cooperating to arrive at an appropriate settlement of his taxation liability. His former tax agent may have been in a position to throw some light. In the circumstances, I see no reason to vary the percentages adopted by the respondent for calculation of additional tax. Further additional tax, referred to as the “per annum” component, was also levied but the Tribunal has no jurisdiction to vary this component, even if I was moved to do so.
  2. As a result of these findings, the decisions under review are varied by reducing taxable income in each of the years concerned to the amounts set out in paragraph 21 above. The matter is remitted to the respondent to amend the relevant assessments in accordance with the findings and reasons.
