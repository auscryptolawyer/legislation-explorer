NT88/238 and Commissioner of Taxation [1989] AATA 8; (1989) 20 ATR 3263; 89 ATC 511 (20 January 1989)

Last Updated: 3 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Assessable Income - whether moneys in accounts in false names conducted by taxpayer constituted assessable income of taxpayer - burden of proof - false explanations preferred to explain control of moneys.

INCOME TAX - Practice and Procedure - burden of proof - false explanations proffered to explain control of moneys.

INCOME TAX - derivation by agent - liability of agent - s.254 Income Tax Assessment Act considered.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - ss. 25, 254;

Jones v. Dunkel [1959] HCA 8; (1959) 101 CLR 298;

Applicant COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

No. NT88/238

AAT Decision no 4881

Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

20 January 1989

Sydney

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT88/238

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re: Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

DECISION

Tribunal : Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

Date : 20 January 1989

Place : Sydney

Decision : The Tribunal decides that the determination of the Commissioner the subject of review be varied by reducing the assessment of taxable income from $899,405 to $58,462 and additional tax for late return in accordance with the reasons for decision published herewith.

(Sgd.) PETER M. ROACH,

Senior Member.

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEABS TRIBUNAL)

) No. NT83/833

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re :

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

30 January 1989 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. In determining the issues arising upon this reference it is important to remember that the issues are not about honesty as such; about crime, or the proceeds of crime; or about the confiscation of the proceeds of crime. The question is whether the taxable income of the Applicant for the year of income ended 30 June 1986 has been assessed by the Commissioner at an excessive amount and, if so, to what extent. That question must be judged in light of the evidence which the parties have chosen to present to the Tribunal, bearing in mind the witnesses whom it would have been reasonable to expect might have been called by one party or the other but who were not called. [Jones v Dunkel [1959] HCA 8; (1959) 101 CLR 298] and also the fact that the Applicant - and not the Commissioner - is in a position of having direct knowledge of the relevant facts.

2. By notice of assessment dated 7 May 1987 the Commissioner assessed the Applicant as a person who had derived a taxable income of $899,405 in the year of income ended 30 June 1986. That being so, the Commissioner assessed the Applicant as liable to pay "$529,989.25 in income tax, and $8,994.05 by way of Medicare levy. In addition, the Commissioner imposed additional tax for late return in the sum of $269,491.65. In consequence, on 10 June 1987, there came to be immediately due and payable to the Crown the sum of $808, 474.95. In assessing the Applicant as he did the Commissioner took into account transactions with a firm of Stock & Share Brokers, Roach Tilley Grice ("RTG") and matters arising therefrom. In the proceedings before me the Applicant argued that his taxable income for that year did not exceed $53,462. The elements of the calculations respectively relied on by the parties were as follows:-

Item Commissioner Applicant

$ $

Unemployment Benefits 9,661 9,661

Deposits to RTG 848,800 NIL

Gains Bank Bills: RTG 26,449 NIL

Interest: RTG 9,495 NIL

Deposit to Bank Account 5,000 NIL

Salary 3,801

Commission 40,000

TOTAL 899,405 53,462

3. In effect, the Commissioner's contention was that amounts deposited to RTG constituted assessable income, with no allowance being made for any "losses or outgoings" which might have been incurred in the derivation of that assessable income. He also treated as assessable income gains on bank bills and interest credited through the account with RTG and a deposit to another account of the Applicant. The Applicant gave evidence that no part of the moneys deposited with RTG or derived in or through dealings with the moneys so deposited was beneficially owned by him. On that basis he neither argued that the deposits did not constitute assessable income nor that any expenses were incurred in the derivation of those moneys, should they be found to have the character of assessable income. He also denied that the $5,000 cash deposit to the bank account constituted untaxed assessable income.

4. The evidence of the Applicant was that he is a licensed hotel broker and valuer aged in his late forties; that he has been twice married; that his first marriage resulted in a divorce in the early 1970's; that, associated with the breakdown of that marriage, there was a dissolution of partnership between the Applicant and his then father-in-law as hoteliers carrying on business in leasehold premises in the City of Sydney; that thereafter the Applicant continued as sole proprietor of that business until -he sold out and, with another, purchased in partnership a suburban hotel; that the purchase price of the suburban hotel was said to have been about $800,000, of which approximately $500,000 was borrowed; that the Applicant had a 40% interest in that business; and that he continued in that business until bought out by his partner in about 1981 at which time he received something of the order of $150,000. By that date the Applicant was in his early forties; he had been married to his second wife for about eight years; he was responsible for the support of his wife and two children; and they were living in a suburban home which had been purchased by the Applicant and his wife as joint tenants in about 1980 with the assistance of substantial mortgage borrowings. No detailed evidence was presented by either side in support of, or in refutation of, that account.

  1. The Applicant claims that by mid-1988 the $150,000 realized on the sale of the suburban hotel had been exhausted. There had been losses on share dealings late in 1981 which, when measured against those resources, were substantial. He asserted that the home, valued in mid-1988 at something of the order of $150,000, was still substantially encumbered so as to leave the couple with an equity in the premises of the order of $90,000. No evidence was presented by either side in support of, or in refutation of, that account or to explain how or why such a situation could have arisen. The only other asset of significance acknowledged by the Applicant was a 1984 Ford LTD sedan purchased in his wife's name in November 1986 at a cost of $22,500, but at all times used by him and now said to be worth some $14,000 to $15,000.
  2. Having ceased business as a suburban hotelier, the Applicant came to be involved in hotel-broking. He worked for a hotel broker on commission from sometime unspecified after the hotel sale until about mid-1985. For a period, from July to October 1985, he was employed as a real estate salesman by a suburban real estate agency and, while so employed, earned the $3,801 acknowledged to constitute assessable income of the year of income ended 30 June 1986 together with a further $1,677 in "car and phone" allowances not acknowledged by him as contributing to taxable income and not alleged to do so by the Respondent. Having come to be interested in real estate, in 1983 the Applicant commenced courses of study which led him to become a licensed real estate agent and hotel broker from mid-1986 and a registered valuer from 1987. No evidence was presented to establish the extent of his attendance at courses of study or as to his commitment to private study, or even as to the expectations of those presenting the courses as to what would constitute sufficient diligence in application to course-work to succeed in those courses. I am not persuaded that the courses called for any measure of commitment which could fairly be called "full-time study".

7. His own account of the months immediately following the cessation of employment with the suburban real estate agent in October 1985 are quite vague and imprecise. He said he did not "do anything" until February 1986. His evidence was that his course of study for his real estate licence was completed in late 1985 and that his licence was granted in June 1986; that, in about February 1986, he became a sub-tenant of another real estate agent in a small city office at which they shared facilities; and that, from that date until June 1986, when he became entitled to carry on business on his own account, he was basically "getting stock for sales". He called no evidence in support of those propositions. Following the issue of the assessment in dispute, he was to acknowledge having earned "commissions" from the hotel broker in the years or income ended 30 June 1983 and 1984. $5,200 was acknowledged for each year and no expenses were claimed. I am sure that the admissions were not accurate and that they did not result from any responsible attempt to be accurate. For the next year the only acknowledged income was by way of Social Security payments ($7,928). In the 1986 year, in addition to income from the last-mentioned employer, he was also to concede that he had drawn unemployment benefits during the year of income ended 30 June 1986 in the sum of $9,661. In addition, he claimed that his wife was working and earning but no other evidence was advanced to establish when, or in what amounts moneys were received, or how the moneys were applied, or why a person with the experience and capacity and vigour of this man at his age should have been so unproductive as to be reduced to the "very poor" situation he claimed he and his family were in, in late 1985. I accept that the period leading up to that date was a difficult one for many people who were seeking employment. But I do not accept that work was not available to people with the experience, personal capacities, qualifications and initiative of the Applicant if they sought it. Further, as to the fact of his claim to, and acceptance of, unemployment benefits, I do not accept that that was an unwanted condition forced upon him by unavoidable circumstances. I find that he was sufficiently content with his lot to prefer it to any regular, ordinary employment.

8. Then, on 14 November 1985, there commenced a series of deposits with RTG of moneys which the Commissioner asserts constituted assessable income of the Applicant. By 30 June 1985 the Commissioner alleges that, in all, $848,800 had been deposited. That that sum had been deposited by him with RTG is not disputed by the Applicant. The Commissioner also alleges that by 30 June 1986 interest amounting to $9,495 had been derived through the investment of the moneys so deposited and credited to the relevant account and that realized gains on bills of exchange dealt with through that account amounted to $26,449. The final figure which was taken into account in the Commissioner's calculations was the sum of $5,000 which constituted an opening deposit made to a new bank account opened by the Applicant as a current account with his bankers on 20 May 1986.

  1. Counsel for the Applicant has argued that, if the Tribunal is persuaded on the balance of probabilities that beneficial ownership of the moneys deposited with RTG did not lie with the Applicant, it follows that the moneys so deposited did not constitute assessable income in the hands of the Applicant and that it follows, almost inevitably, that the moneys credited by RTG as gains on bills of exchange and interest flowing from the use of the moneys through the account with RTG did not constitute assessable income of the Applicant.
  2. Counsel for the Commissioner contends that there are two answers to that. The first is that the Tribunal should not be persuaded that beneficial ownership of the moneys deposited did not lie with the Applicant. It then follows, it is contended, that the moneys deposited, and the interest, and the gains on bills of exchange are properly brought to account and that the Applicant has failed to persuade the Tribunal that to that extent the assessment is excessive. The second argument advanced by Counsel for the Commissioner is that it does not matter whether in relation to these transactions the Applicant was a principal or an agent because of the provisions of s.254. I propose to deal with the latter argument immediately.

THE S254 ARGUMENT

11. S.254 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") provides:-

"(1) With respect to every agent and with respect also to every trustee, the following provisions shall apply:

(a) He shall be answerable as taxpayer for the doing of all such things as are required to be done by virtue of this Act in respect of the income derived by him in his representative capacity, or derived by the principal by virtue of his agency, and for the payment of tax thereon.

(b) He shall in respect of that income make the returns and be assessed thereon, but in his representative capacity only, and each return and assessment shall, except as otherwise provided by this Act, be separate and distinct from any other.

(c)

(d) He is hereby authorized and required to retain from time to time out of any money which comes to him in his representative capacity so much as is sufficient to pay tax which is or will become due in respect of the income.

(e) He is hereby made personally liable for the tax payable in respect of the income to the extent of any amount that he has retained, or should have retained, under the last preceding paragraph; but he shall not be otherwise personally liable for the tax.

(f) He is hereby indemnified for all payments which he makes in pursuance of this Act or of any requirement of the Commissioner.

(g)

(h) For the purpose of insuring the payment of tax the Commissioner shall have the same remedies against attachable property of any kind vested in or under the control or management or in the possession of any agent or trustee, as he would have against the property of any other taxpayer in respect of tax.

(2) In this section, "tax" includes income tax, and tax of a similar nature to provisional tax, under a State income tax law."

Although the section has existed in the present Act substantially in its present form since the enactment of the Act in 1936, and although s.89 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922 was to similar effect, there have been no decided cases touching the interpretation of either provision.

12. My own view of the matter is that the submission is unsound. In my opinion s.254(1)(b) provides for the assessment of an agent qua agent. The section contemplates an assessment which shall be "separate and distinct from any other (assessment)": that means (inter alia) an assessment separate and distinct from the assessment of the agent in his own right. It follows that, if it was the intention of the Commissioner to assess the Applicant under the provisions of s.254, he should have done so by the issue of an assessment which, by its terms, made plain that the Applicant was being assessed as an agent and thereby assessed as a person accountable for payment of the tax of another and liable for that tax to the extent to which he should have made provision from assets in his hands [s.254(1)(e)] and with rights to be indemnified [s.254(1)(f)]. In my view the assessment before me is not an assessment raised pursuant to the power conferred by s.254. That being so, I now turn to consider whether or not the Applicant has persuaded me that the moneys deposited with RTG were not beneficially owned by him.

THE S.25 ARGUMENT

13. According to the Applicant, at a time when he was working towards the finalization of his courses of study; when he was engaged as a commission salesman with a suburban real estate agent earning an extremely modest living; and with his savings from several years before nearly exhausted, he happened to answer the phone and found himself talking to a third person. (I shall refer to that third man as "Harry"). Harry, it was said, indicated that he was phoning in response to an advertisement. He enquired about real estate investments. Having introduced himself, Harry indicated that he was interested in purchasing any commercial property in Sydney showing a good return. Later, the Applicant said, he took a second call at his employer's office when Harry said that he had had a hotel in Melbourne; that he was being divorced; that he was moving to Sydney; that he would like to have a hotel in Sydney; and that he would like to look at a hotel in the "million dollar range". The Applicant said that he mentioned a particular hotel known to (be on the market in suburban Sydney, but nothing came of it. A third call (he said) came some two weeks later and, on this occasion, Harry asked the Applicant whether the Applicant would "act as an agent for him in Sydney". The Applicant said that he agreed to do so, although at that point of time nothing was discussed about terms. The Applicant was unclear as to whether he was still in the service of the suburban agent when the third call was made. When asked to be specific as to what Harry had asked, the Applicant said that Harry then stated "I have money I want to send to Sydney in case we (sic) buy a hotel. Could you arrange to have that money put on a short-term money market account". (I attribute no significance to the use of the term "we"). Harry did not specify with whom the account would be conducted or on what terms the investment would be made. Those matters, the Applicant said, were left to him, but the proposal was that Harry would pay the Applicant 1% of moneys deposited in the form of commission. That was most acceptable to the Applicant even if the sums to be invested, which had not yet been specified or quantified, were small in amount because he was, he said, in extreme need of funds at that time. No explanation was offered as to why such a potentially high rate of commission should have been acceptable to Harry.

  1. His evidence then was that, shortly before 14 November 1985, he met with Harry on a face-to-face basis for no more than the second time. He said they met in the city in the Regent Hotel in George Street, Sydney and that the only description he could give of the person he met that day was that "he was a man about 5'10" to 6', medium build and greying hair, fair". He said that, he - the Applicant - was only known to Harry as a person who had answered the phone in the office of a suburban estate agent and whom he had possibly met on a face-to-face basis once before. According to his own account, without Harry knowing anything more about the status and reputation of the Applicant, Harry provided him with funds to invest. His evidence initially was that the moneys were given to him in cash, although there "may have been some cheques there too" payable to RTG, possibly in the form of bank-cheques.
  2. According to the account of the Applicant, from that point Harry periodically contacted him and arranged that they would meet at the Regent. At such meetings, additional moneys would be given to the Applicant, principally in the form of bank cheques, and the moneys would be entrusted to him to invest. There were no personal cheques, according to the Applicant. Initially, he said that he would take the moneys and cheques as handed to him and take them to RTG and there deposit them, giving instructions to RTG as to the application of the moneys. The selection of RTG was made by the Applicant and was based on a schoolboy association with a principal of RTG. I find that that account of the depositing is in many respects untrue.

16. I find that the moneys deposited with RTG comprised:-

* 8 bank cheques on 14 November 1985 - 1 by $25,000, 3 by $20,000, 4 by $10,000 - in all $125,000;

* 10 bank cheques on 21 November 1985 - 9 by $10,000 and 1 by $9,000 - in all $99,000?

* 14 bank cheques on 22 November 1985 - each of $10,000 - in all $140,000;

* 4 bank cheques on 26 November 1985 - 3 by $10,000 and 1 by $5,000 - in all $35,000;

* 14 bank cheques on 6 December 1985 - each of $10,000 - in all $140,000;

* 1 bank cheque of $109,800 on 16 December 1985; and

* 1 bank cheque of $200,000 on 19 December 1985;

I find that each of those cheques was procured by the Applicant and paid for in cash. I further find that the cheques were so obtained from a wide range of banks, for the most part banks with which the Applicant had no association. I find that, for fees of no more than $2, banks such as the Commonwealth, Westpac, ANZ and National were prepared to lend their aid to the desire of unidentified strangers to convert cash funds into seemingly innocuous bank cheques. The evidence of the Applicant was that he was the only person to deposit moneys into the account with RTG at any time prior to the closure of the account in the early months of 1986.

17. It is not disputed that the total of the amounts deposited prior to 30 June 1986 was $848,800. In the subsequent year, it is said that something like a further $2,000,000 was deposited. If the logic underlying the assessment for the year ended 30 June 1986 is correct, and there are no new circumstances operating such as would warrant a different treatment from that year in the year following, it seems likely that further assessments might be pending for the year of income ended 30 June 1987. For that reason, if for no other, I do not propose to embark upon a detailed consideration of the evidence presented before me in relation to the latter year.

18. Explanations for some of the deposits made after 30 June 1986 were quite remarkable. For example, on 6 January 1986 and on 2 January 1987, on both occasions while in Surfers Paradise with his family on vacation and there visiting with his "in-laws", he made deposits at the Surfers Paradise branch of a bank for transmission to the credit of RTG in Sydney. The amounts so deposited were $150,000 and $450,000. The former deposit was of cash: and the latter by a bank-cheque drawn on a Sydney bank. As to the cash, his evidence was that he had carried it with him when he travelled by car from Sydney. I simply do not believe his account of the matter.

19. As to withdrawals, the evidence in chief of the Applicant was that, from time to time, Harry would contact him and give him instructions to pick up a cheque at RTG. Harry's instructions would indicate what he wanted done with it. Despite early denials, I am satisfied that withdrawals were all effected by the Applicant. I am not persuaded that RTG or any other institution concerned in these matters dealt with, or knew of any other person in these transactions than the Applicant.

20. Withdrawals can be said to fall into three categories:-

* moneys remitted overseas;

* moneys transferred to others within Australia; and
  • * moneys credited to other possibly fictitious account-holders or drawn in cash.

The amounts were all substantial, and the remittances overseas particularly so. In all cases it was possible that the moneys remained under the control of or were applied to the benefit of, the Applicant. Some explanations were certainly untrue. For example, on 3 February 1986 he withdrew and remitted $229,586 to the credit in London of a named individual on the basis, according to the Applicant, that the transferee had been named and identified by Harry. In that instance the named individual bore the same surname and initials as the father-in-law of the Applicant. Despite the fact that his father-in-law bears a surname which only appears five times in the current Sydney telephone directory, the Applicant's evidence was that the "coincidence" that Harry's transferee should have happened to bear the same quite rare surname and initials as the Applicant's father-in-law did not even occur to him. I consider the latter assertion to be completely false. But that falsity does not establish that the moneys were those of the Applicant.

  1. I am satisfied that the Commissioner, very reasonably, suspected the moneys in question to be the proceeds of crime and, more particularly, to be "drug moneys". If they were that is a matter of great importance to the community - greater than any question of taxation only. But it is not the responsibility of the Tribunal to determine any such question. It has to decide whether, on the balance of probabilities, the Applicant has satisfied it that the assessment of the taxable income of the Applicant for the year ended 30 June 1986 is excessive.
  2. I regard the Applicant as an outrageous and not very competent liar. I utterly reject the story that Harry parted with these large sums to an unaccredited stranger - such as the Applicant says he was - in circumstances leaving the Applicant incapable of tracing, identifying or even contacting Harry and such as to leave Harry's executors with no known evidence of payment to the Applicant. I think it quite likely that the Applicant was involved in, or was on the fringes of, criminal activity. However, I make no finding in that regard. But, despite the lack of personal merit in the individual, I am satisfied that, except to the extent to which his principals allowed him to draw on the funds entrusted to his care and to appropriate them to himself, the moneys dealt with were not his property and that the income generated by their use was not his beneficially. In reaching that conclusion I have been fully conscious of the circumstance that the truth must have been fully known to the Applicant; that it could only rarely be known to the Commissioner in circumstances such as these; and that I am satisfied that in all matters touching the identity of Harry, the Applicant has chosen to lie to me.
  3. But in reaching that conclusion I have also taken into account evidence which indicates that the bulk of the money has been remitted overseas; that there is nothing in the evidence to suggest that he has access to it, or has control over it overseas; that the evidence does not indicate that he has either the resources or a lifestyle such as might be expected of a person enjoying levels of earnings which have been attributed to him by the assessments; and that those who aspire to great wealth are not likely to be persons who will forego both the pleasures to be had by the acquisition of symbols of wealth and the satisfactions which come from an affluent lifestyle and at the same time also forego control of the money and the power which access to such money enables them to exercise. I recognize that the remittances overseas may have been at the direction of, and to the advantage of, the Applicant as the person having ultimate control of, and authority over, the moneys. But on balance I am persuaded that that was not so. In my judgment the Applicant was the agent of another or others, rather than a principal. He was a puppet: not the puppeteer.
  4. As a result I am satisfied, on the balance of probabilities, that in so far as the deposits to RTG; the gains on bank bills; and interest from RTG are concerned, the assessment is excessive. I am not so persuaded in relation to the $5,000 taken into account by the Commissioner. I am not persuaded that it did not constitute a reward to the Applicant for his services. Accordingly, that amount will be added to the figure for taxable income now admitted by the Applicant.
  5. It may be that by not upholding the assessment made by the Commissioner others, and possibly even the Applicant, will enjoy the advantages of the moneys while avoiding tax on their earnings. It may be that those earnings are themselves the fruits of anti-social activity, and even criminal activity. However, if the legal system is only available to protect those who wholly abide by the law, all too soon the community as a whole will be the loser.

26. As the evidence stands before me, I have been persuaded that the taxable income of the Applicant as determined by reference to the evidence presented before me, did not exceed $58,462 for the year of income ended 30 June 1986.

  1. No argument was addressed as to remission, or further remission, of additional tax for late return. In the circumstances I am not persuaded that the Commissioner erred in the exercise of his discretion to remit other than by taking into account excessive figures for taxable income and, in consequence, tax. For that reason I will merely direct that, as to additional tax, the assessment be varied in accordance with these reasons for decision.
  2. The order of the Tribunal will be that the assessment of the Commissioner under review be varied by reducing taxable income to $58,462 and by reducing additional tax in accordance with these reasons for decision.
