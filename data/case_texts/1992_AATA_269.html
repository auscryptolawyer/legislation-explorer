DECISION

Having prepared documents described before me as T Account Worksheets the respondent made amended assessments of the applicant's taxable income and tax payable for the years of income ended 30 June 1984, 30 June 1985, 30 June 1986 and 30 June 1987. The applicant objected against those amended assessments. Those objections were disallowed by the respondent and the applicant sought review of those objection decisions by this Tribunal. The documents filed pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 were put into evidence before the Tribunal.

2. Section 167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") provides, inter alia, that if the respondent Commissioner is not satisfied with the return of income furnished by any person then the Commissioner may make an assessment of the amount upon which, in his judgment, income tax ought to be levied. That amount becomes the taxable income for the purposes of making an assessment under section 166 of the Act.

3. Sub-section 170(1) of the Act provides that the Commissioner may amend any assessment at any time subject to other provisions of the section. Although the provisions of section 170 have been amended with effect from 1 July 1986 no issue arose before the Tribunal in respect to these amendments to the law.

4. Section 190 of the Act as then enacted provided that in proceedings under Part V of the Act on a review before this Tribunal the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive shall lie upon the taxpayer. That provision has been replaced in effect by section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953. The new provision does not apply to these applications (s.112 of Act No. 216 of 1991).

5. In McAndrew v FCT [1956] HCA 62; (1956) 98 CLR 263 the High Court decided that the word "excessive" in paragraph 190(b) refers to the quantum of the assessment, not any incorrect or unauthorised step in the process of calculating the taxable income.

6. The decision of the High Court in Trautwein v FCT [1936] HCA 77; (1936) 56 CLR 63 dealt with a factual situation similar to the present which arose under the predecessor to the Act which included different statutory provisions. The earlier Act did not contain a provision equivalent to paragraph 190(b) but did provide in paragraph 39(1)(b) that the production of the notice of assessment shall be prima facie evidence of the correctness of the assessment in proceedings on appeal against the assessment. Notwithstanding the different statutory provision the dicta of Latham C.J. at pages 87-8 is instructive. The Chief Justice said:

"Sec. 39 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922-1934

provides (inter alia) that the production of any notice

or copy notice of assessment under the hand of the

Commissioner shall be conclusive evidence that the

assessment has been duly made and that the amount and

all the particulars of the assessment are correct,

except in proceedings on appeal against the assessment,

when it shall be prima facie evidence only. Isaacs J.

said in Moreau v Federal Commissioner of Taxation

[1926] HCA 28; (1926) 39 CLR 65 at p 70 that sec.39 'throws the burden

on the appellant to establish his right to the benefit

he claims'. This statement, if strictly construed,

means that the taxpayer appellant does not rebut the

presumption created by sec.39 merely by showing that

there is an error in it - and thereby 'creating a

blank' - he must go further and show either that there

ought to be a 'blank' - a complete omission of the item

in question - or that something else should be

substituted for that item. The circumstance that the

facts are (or were) peculiarly within the knowledge of

one party is a relevant matter in considering the

sufficiency of evidence to discharge a burden of proof.

(See cases cited by Isaacs J. in Williamson v AH On

[1926] HCA 46; (1926) 39 CLR 95 at pp 113-115. Obviously the facts in

relation to his income are facts peculiarly within the

knowledge of the taxpayer.

In the absence of some record in the mind or in the

books of the taxpayer, it would often be quite

impossible to make a correct assessment. The

assessment would necessarily be a guess to some extent,

and almost certainly inaccurate in fact. There is

every reason to assume that the legislature did not

intend to confer upon a potential taxpayer the valuable

privilege of disqualifying himself in that capacity by

the simple and relatively unskilled method of losing

either his memory or his books.

The application of sec.39 is not, in my opinion,

excluded as soon as it is shown that an element in the

assessment is a guess and that it is therefore very

probably wrong. It is prima facie right - and remains

right until the appellant shows that it is wrong. If

it were necessary to decide the point I would, as at

present advised, be prepared to hold that the taxpayer

must, at least as a general rule, go further and show,

not only negatively that the assessment is wrong, but

also positively what correction should be made in order

to make it right or more nearly right. I say 'as a

general rule' because, conceivably, there might be a

case where it appeared that the assessment had been

made upon no intelligible basis even as an

approximation, and the court would then set aside the

assessment and remit it to the Commissioner for further

consideration."

7. A practical application of this dicta is illustrated by what happened in Hines v FCT (1952) 9 ATD 413 where a Full Court of the High Court upheld an appeal from Webb J. That case also involved the contest between assessable income and alleged gambling wins. In that case Mr Hines had been forthcoming about his gambling wins and had adopted the practice of advising estimated wins. Eventually the Commissioner conducted an investigation which resulted in substantially increased taxable incomes being assessed. The Full Court upheld the taxpayer's appeal on the basis that he had established that the assessments in question were excessive. In the course of their reasons their Honours (Dixon C.J., Williams and Fullager JJ.) said at page 420:

"Is it possible, in this state of affairs, to

justify a refusal to accept Hines' statement on oath

that in this year he had no other source of income than

the business? In our opinion, it is not. Three

important factors come to Hines' aid at this point.

The first is that there is nothing in the evidence that

seriously suggests any source of income other than the

business. The second is that, as soon as Hines'

betting wins become substantial, the taxpayers

voluntarily directed the Commissioner's attention to

the position, and, in each year in which an increase of

assets took place, voluntarily placed the figures

before the Commissioner. It is not perhaps impossible,

but it is in the highest degree unlikely, that this

course would be adopted if Hines was really concealing

a secret source of substantial income. The third is

that the Commissioner had in 1947 conducted a full

investigation and inquiry, which had failed to reveal

anything worthy of being adduced in evidence or even to

suggest any definite line of cross-examination. It may

be added that, generally speaking, it is not altogether

an easy matter to conceal from all the world a secret

source of substantial personal exertion income. The

burden of proving a negative is not, as a rule, easily

discharged, but in this case it could not fairly or

reasonably be said that Hines had failed to discharge

it. The case must be decided on the evidence,and on

the evidence there is a very definite preponderance of

probability in his favour."

8. In FCT v Elton 90 ATC 4078 the Commissioner appealed against a decision of this Tribunal. The basis of the appeal was that the Tribunal had failed to take into account sub-section 177(1) and paragraph 190(b) of the Act and had placed an onus on the Commissioner. It follows, said the Commissioner, that the Tribunal was required to assume that the assessments of income tax were prima facie correct until the taxpayer proved that the assessment was wrong and by how much it was wrong. The Federal Court (Davies J.) decided that paragraph 190(b) only comes into operation when neither party has convinced the Tribunal that its contention is more likely than not the correct one.

9. The application of the law in relation to cases of the present kind is clearly set out in Krew v FCT 71 ATC 4213 by Walsh J. at pages 4216-7:

"It is necessary to consider the question of the

burden of proof, which I think is a question of much

importance in this case. I leave aside for later

discussion some submissions in relation to the opinion

of the Commissioner mentioned in sec.170(2) of the Act.

I think it is clear that the onus of proof in relation

to the main issues of fact in the case is on the

appellant. Furthermore, it is not sufficient, in my

opinion, for the appellant to make it seem probable

that the overall discrepancy of about 70,000 pounds disclosed

by the betterment statement is not wholly correct.

Each year must be considered separately and it must be

shown in respect of a particular year that the

challenged assessment was wrong. If it is not proved

that the whole of the amount of income which is in

dispute has been wrongly included as taxable income,

then material must be provided upon which the amount of

income which should have been excluded can be

ascertained. What I have just stated is subject to the

qualification that in some cases it may be enough for a

taxpayer to show that the assessment has proceeded upon

a wrong basis or upon wrong principles: cf. Henderson v

F.C. of T. (1970) 70 ATC 4016; 44 ALJR 115. But, in my

opinion, the qualification has no operation in the

present case.

It is plain that when the matters were before the Board

of Review sec.190(b) of the Act applied. The appellant

had to show in respect of each assessment that it was

excessive. Even if it be accepted that sec.190(b) has

no direct application in an appeal to this Court from

the Board of Review (see F.C. of T. v Finn (1960) 103 CLR

at p 170), it remains true that in this Court the

appellant must show that the Board of Review's

decisions were wrong and, more particularly, he must

show that the assessments as affirmed or as varied by

it were wrong and must produce evidence which is

accepted and which provides material for determining

the amount by which they should be held to be wrong.

In McAndrew v F.C. of T. [1956] HCA 62; (1956) 98 CLR 263, the Court was

concerned with an appeal to the Court against the

Commissioner's assessment and not with an appeal from a

Board of Review. But the opinions I have stated are

supported by the reasons given by the Court in that

case. I refer to the joint judgment of three members

of the Court in which, at p 271, the view is expressed

that even without sec.190(b) the burden of establishing

his objections would be cast upon the taxpayer and I

refer, also, to what was said by Kitto J., at p 273.

I think that some of the submissions made to me failed

to recognise the foregoing considerations. It was said

that, where there is a surplus of assets, over those

which would be explained by returned income, there is

no presumption that that surplus is income, or is

assessable income. In a sense that is true: cf. Elsey

v F.C. of T. (1969) 69 ATC 4115; [1969] HCA 48; 43 ALJR 415 at p 422,

but, in my opinion, that is of no assistance in

deciding the present case. It was argued, also, that

the basic question (described as a question of law) was

whether in the circumstances the surplus was income or

was capital and that there was no presumption that it

was income. In my opinion this misconceives the

problem which was before the Board of Review and the

problem which is now before this Court. The case is

not one in which nothing more is known than that there

was a surplus. It is a case in which it appears that

in every year over a long period the appellant's

position improved in a way for which his disclosed

income cannot account. He has offered the explanations

that the opening figures taken in the betterment

statement as to his assets (although correct so far as

they went) were wrong in that they did not include the

sum of about 50,000 pounds then held by him in cash, and that

this had been derived from successful gambling which

continued throughout the period, and that all these

funds had been so used that they were absorbed into his

known assets. His total assets at the beginning of the

period (apart from the money said to have been in the

safe) were worth about 20,000 pounds and consisted mainly of

money in bank accounts and stock in hand. It was

natural that the appellant's account of his gambling

should be regarded with suspicion and it was not

accepted by the respondent or by the Board of Review.

In each year the respondent treated the betterment for

that year as being income. He did not prove how it had

been earned. He could not provide evidence showing

affirmatively that it was assessable income. If the

onus had rested upon the respondent he would have

failed, but when the respondent made assessments which

included the betterment funds as assessable income and

disallowed the appellant's objections, the matters were

taken to the Board of Review and the onus was then on

the appellant to show that the assessments were wrong.

He gave evidence of his ordinary business activities

and of his gambling activities and of the way in which

these were interrelated. If his account of the matter

had been accepted in full, it would have been shown

that the disputed accruals to his wealth were not

assessable income and that the assessments were wrong.

I say that this would have been shown because it was

not contended (and it is not now contended) by the

respondent that the funds in dispute would have been

assessable as being the profits of a business of

gambling carried on by the appellant. If his evidence

had been accepted in full it would have been proved

that, at any rate in the earlier years of the period,

his activities in horse-racing and in betting and other

forms of gambling were so regular and extensive that it

might well have been thought that the proceeds of those

activities were taxable, but that can be put on one

side. Counsel for the respondent had not sought to

uphold any of the assessments on that basis. But the

explanations of the appellant were not accepted and

that meant that he had not discharged the onus of

showing that the assessments were wrong.

It is one thing to say that the failure of the

respondent to produce positive evidence of activities

of the appellant, which produced money not recorded in

the books of his business of dealing in metal and other

goods, is a matter to be taken into account when

deciding the question whether his evidence should be

accepted. It is a different thing to say that as a

matter of law the appellant must succeed if the

respondent has not proved affirmatively that the

disputed receipts were taxable income, because (as was

submitted) there is no presumption that they were. The

former proposition is correct. The latter proposition

is, in my opinion, plainly wrong."

10. The dicta of Walsh J .is consistent with the decision of the High Court in FCT v Dalco [1990] HCA 3; (1990) 168 CLR 614.

11. In summary then the onus lies on the applicant to show that the assessments are excessive and he will do this by establishing that his taxable income was less than the amount assessed. It is not for the respondent to prove the correctness of the assessment but that is not to say that the Commissioner need not meet the material put forward by the taxpayer. Clearly the onus can shift to the Commissioner if the applicant leads material which supports the claims made in the objection as was explained by Davies J. in the Elton Case.

12. I turn now to the facts of this case. The applicant had been employed in the hospitality industry over many years. Because section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act requires that I prepare these reasons so as not to disclose the identity of the taxpayer, it is not possible to identify the establishment where he was employed during the income years ended 30 June 1984 to 30 June 1987 other than to say it is a well known hotel well located to participate in both regular and seasonal trading.

13. During the relevant period the applicant was the manager/licensee of the hotel with the owner living interstate. The applicant resigned his position shortly after a change in ownership of the establishment. The applicant described in some detail the method of accounting for each day's takings. Suffice it to say for present purposes that the takings were kept in a safe controlled by the applicant but the reconciliation of the cash to the cash register tapes was done by office staff. However, the initial balancing of the till to the machine tape was done at close of business by the applicant or the bar manager and sometimes the owner if he were there.

14. In his evidence in chief the applicant stated that he only had two sources of income - the salary package paid by the hotel and his winnings on horse racing. His wife was employed by a government agency. His salary package was made up of a weekly salary plus payment of superannuation. Petrol was also paid for and meals at the hotel were free although the applicant's evidence was that he normally ate at home. The hotel also paid the applicant's domestic telephone and electricity accounts. During the course of cross-examination the applicant admitted that he was also paid a quarterly bonus, an annual travel allowance of $2,000 and a bonus for extra work during the peak season of $2,000. In response to questions in cross-examination the applicant said that these amounts were paid by cheque but not banked and were cashed in the hotel in the same way that the quarterly bonuses of $500 were also cashed in the hotel. He also said that he may have given this money to his wife. In relation to the travel allowance it was said to be in the nature of a holiday bonus and not for travel in the course of his employment.

15. The applicant said in evidence that he had been punting on horses from an early age. During the relevant period he regularly backed horses on the basis of tips supplied by contacts and preferred to limit himself to no more than $250 on the day with generally no resort to additional funds on the day. It was his practice to ring his contacts on a regular basis.

16. The applicant described his method of financing his betting in these words during evidence in chief at page 31 of the transcript:

"Q. How did you used (sic) to finance your bets?

A. Finance my bet?

Q. Yes. Where did the money come from to place the bet

in the first place?

A. Well, I used to get my money - Marie might give me

my starting balance. If I ever lost it I would not ask

for any more off her until such time as I'd back a

winner. And whenever I had winnings I used to punt out

of my winnings. And when I had some winnings if I had

winnings, I would just keep them in a drawer at home.

If Marie wanted any extra money I would give her some

but I always used to make sure that I had enough money

there for my punting,

Q. And in terms of punting referred that your average

bet is in the order of - or daily bet in total was in

the order of $150. Is it on average would that occur

twice a week, once a week?

A. Of $150 betting?

Q. Yes.

A. No, I'd only punt - if I was punting I'd only sort

of - that $150 would be my weekly allowance for

punting, whether I was punting mid week or if I took a

trifecta on a Wednesday and I had $24, I'd only have

the balance for Saturday.

Q. Is it reasonable then to take that average weekly

amount and multiply by the number of weeks to obtain

your total annual commitment to betting?

A. For betting, yes, but there would be some weeks

when, as I said, some weeks in the winter time when

you're flat out, you don't get a chance to bet.

Q. Well then, in that case, the maximum amount which

you would've committed to betting in an average year

would be this $150 by 52 weeks in that year?

A. Not always because, as I said, some weeks you would

not have a bet.

Q. And what was the biggest win that you can recall you

ever received?

A. The biggest win - I've got many trifectas which have

paid over $10,000, $11,000. In one month alone I won

over $30,000 in trifectas.

Q. Can you recall when that occurred?

A. Well, when I was interviewed with the taxation

people when I first - I told them at that stage I had a

win over $30,000 for one month then and since then I've

had plenty of wins of over $10,000, $11,000 in

trifectas.

Tribunal: Perhaps you might just answer the question.

The question was, can you recall when you had that win

of over $30,000?

A. No, sir, because at that stage the TAB wasn't giving

out the customer balance tickets. As Mr Ham knows ...

Tribunal: Just answer the question. Can you recall the

month in which you had the $30,000 win?

A. No.

17. Funds described as surplus proceeds from the betting were said to be kept at the applicant's home but funds were sometimes paid into the cheque account at the bank if it was overdrawn. It seems, on the evidence, that winnings were not normally banked and were kept in the applicant's home. It was his practice to use this money for betting and not to resort to either his own or his wife's wages. Exceptions to this rule were large winnings when the winnings were paid out at the TAB by cheque. There must be considerable doubt about this evidence of keeping money in the house. On the one hand the applicant said his wife was keen to maximise interest derived on their money and on the other hand the applicant refused to identify how and where he kept the money in the house. Along the same lines was the evidence that the applicant won $30,000 during a period of four weeks in the relevant period but he was unable to say what happened to the money. However, when cross-examined in relation to specific bank accounts he stated that the various deposits put to him were proceeds of gambling wins.

18. Exhibit 1 is a nondescript handwritten document introduced into proceedings by the respondent. To some extent it took the applicant's representative by surprise but more importantly it is a document without any evidentiary value. It is merely the musings of the applicant and his wife. It seems to me that little weight should be given to the document and the evidence relating to it. Put at its very highest the document is a calculation of a salary claim to be put to the owners of the new hotel. It has not been suggested that the applicant was remunerated in accordance with this document or on any similar basis. It reflected an ambit claim which came to nothing except the applicant's resignation.

19. The applicant's evidence indicated, but did not establish, that the cash register system in the hotel was not secure. In particular although the individual registers were reconciled at the end of each day it seems the registers were not functioning correctly and could easily be manipulated. I gathered that the respondent was hoping to convince the Tribunal that the applicant was able to supplement his salary from these tills. There is no basis on the evidence before me for coming to such a conclusion.

20. In relation to $60,000 deposited to a credit union account the applicant explained that amount was proceeds from the sale of a house left under a Will to the applicant's wife. With respect to other significant deposits in various bank and credit union accounts the applicant was generally unable to explain the deposits saying they had been made by his wife.

21. Overall the applicant displayed either a remarkable lack of interest in his family's financial affairs or was not prepared to give frank answers. I prefer the latter view because I am not satisfied that the applicant did not participate in his family's financial affairs. The following exchange in cross examination displays a lack of consistency in answers that indicates that the applicant was dissembling in answering questions (pages 80-81 transcript) - (the emphasis is mine):

22. Evidence by the then proprietor of the hotel established that only the amounts paid in cash for wages on a weekly basis were included in the assessable income disclosed on the group certificates completed by the hotel. His evidence also corroborated the applicant's evidence in relation to successful bets placed in respect of a horse owned by the proprietor.

23. A superintendent of police was also called to corroborate the applicant's betting activities. That evidence was to the effect that the applicant obtained good information in relation to horse races. The evidence in relation to specific incidents was confined to two incidents when the applicant had substantial wins on the TAB. However, the superintendent also said that it has been his practice to contact the applicant for tips over many years. In the light of his evidence about the quality of the applicant's information it is easy to accept this evidence.

24. The applicant also called his wife ("W") to give evidence. Throughout the relevant period she was employed by a government agency which involved working five days a week from 8.30 am to 4.30 pm. W identified the various banking accounts operated by herself and set out at folio 15 of the T documents.

25. W gave evidence about major assets during the relevant period as follows. In the year ended 30 June 1985 there was a sale of real estate inherited by W in an amount of $67,653 according to Exhibit 2. During the year ended 30 June 1986 two motor vehicles were traded in and replaced by a Ford Fairlane acquired by the applicant and W jointly and a Ford Laser acquired by W.

26. In the year ended 30 June 1987 W sold the Ford Laser for $7,700 and purchased a new Ford Laser for $14,900 on her own account. Also in that year the applicant purchased a number of rights to shares in BHP Gold at a cost of $5,658. W said this purchase was paid for by the applicant from betting wins. Towards the end of the relevant period the applicant and W purchased a business.

27. W acknowledged a build up of funds in the banking accounts during the relevant period. In consequence of this she adopted a practice of transferring available funds to high interest accounts. Those available funds included the applicant's weekly pay from the hotel but did not include his betting wins which, she said, he kept as his own kitty.

28. Regarding the acquisition of the new business after the applicant resigned from the hotel, I accept W's evidence that the business was leased and subsequently purchased. $60,000 was withdrawn from a credit union account and was borrowed from a bank. Also of relevance is the fact that to refurnish their home they had to borrow $30,000 from a bank.

29. Following the applicant's resignation from the hotel he was paid a termination payment approximating $20,000. W said that amount was not banked but kept at home for living expenses during the three months the applicant was unemployed.

30. W has lodged income tax returns for the relevant period and paid tax in respect of assessments made by the respondent. I was told from the bar table that the respondent accepts that W's returns of income and the resulting assessments are accepted as being correct. The returns of income for the relevant years of income lodged by W are Exhibit 3 in these proceedings.

31. W also said in her evidence that the amounts attributed to family living expenses by the respondent were too high in respect of her family but she was unable to provide an estimate of the actual family living expenses. It is fair to note that given the expenses paid for by the hotel an average family could have lived on the taxable income reported by W without hardship.

32. Evidence was given by a chartered accountant called for the applicant. He criticised the methodology of the T accounts used by the respondent (Exhibit 2) as the basis for forming a judgment as to the amount of the applicant's income. In particular he criticised the inclusion of income derived by W which had not been banked in W's banking accounts. He also criticised the respondent's treatment of all unexplained withdrawals from banking accounts as being for personal living expenses. Exhibit H is an analysis of the banking records of the applicant and W described it as a "Bank Movement Report". The evidence of the accountant satisfied me that movements of funds between accounts, something to which both the applicant and W referred in their evidence, had not always been recognised as such and have been treated as unexplained deposits and unexplained withdrawals deemed to be personal living expenses.

33. The criticism of the methodology is well founded on the evidence of the applicant and W. In particular W was quite clear in her evidence that private living expenses (excluding those paid by the hotel) were paid from the two cheque accounts. The remaining banking accounts were used for savings and investments. The failure to recognise this means that the methodology of the "T" statements is flawed. That this is so is made clear by comparing Exhibit H with the bank analysis prepared by the respondent which is Exhibit J. Exhibit K is a reconciliation of adjustments so as to disclose the true amounts of unexplained deposits. The reconciliation was prepared by the accountant and considered necessary to rectify the errors in the methodology.

34. The evidence of the accountant established two fundamental flaws in the calculations made by the respondent. Both resulted in the taxable income being overstated in the calculations. The first is the adjustment to include income derived by W but not banked. There is simply no basis for making this adjustment in relation to the applicant. The second aspect is the attitude taken in relation to deposits and withdrawals by the respondent which ignores many transactions between accounts as demonstrated by Exhibits H and J and the evidence of the accountant and the taxation auditor. That evidence when considered a whole leads me to the conclusion that the calculations which form the basis of the amounts of taxable income assessed by the respondent are not correct and seriously overestimate the amount of the taxable income in each year.

35. A director of the company which at that time owned a chain of hotels and which purchased the hotel in 1987 gave evidence about the takeover of the hotel. In particular he said that eight cash registers were not functioning properly and had to be replaced immediately. He also expressed the view that there had been poor stock control in the bars and that the normal records of the hotel were missing. He formed the view that the applicant resented the takeover of the hotel. The evidence about the records was explained before me - the hotel accounts were kept by a firm of accountants in Melbourne. As to the cash registers I cannot come to any conclusion about this evidence. As I said during the hearing, the tills can be left open for periods of trading without the need to register transactions. The registers provide a sound control if properly operated but are very easy to abuse. There is no evidence before me that would justify a finding that the applicant falsified the registers in some way. The witness also gave evidence about salary negotiations conducted with the applicant shortly after the takeover. He said in evidence that the applicant's claims were too high and that he did agree to go to $1000 per week salary only - an amount far more than his organisation paid to anybody else as manager and licensee of an hotel.

36. An auditor employed by the respondent also gave evidence. He said that at the initial interview with the applicant the applicant said that his income was derived from interest, wages and punting wins, referring in particular to some good wins.

37. The difficulty experienced by the auditor and by this Tribunal is the lack of records in relation to banking transactions and also the cash transactions. That problem was resolved by the auditor in the now familiar way of treating unexplained deposits and unexplained withdrawals as, in effect, income; the unexplained withdrawals being treated as personal living expenses. That treatment flies in the face of the uncontested evidence of the applicant and W about their family lifestyle if the applicant's gambling activities are put to one side. The auditor admitted in evidence that he made no attempt to quantify the family's living expenses.

38. The auditor also gave evidence about a discussion he had with the applicant's then tax agent. The agent advised of the undisclosed income from the hotel as admitted by the applicant in these proceedings.

39. In making the amended assessments the respondent simply ignored the claims about betting wins. No adjustment was made for the wins alleged and no adjustment was made for losses. The auditor seems, on the basis of his evidence, to have assumed that the applicant lost more than he gained from his betting activities. No attempt was made to quantify the private expenditure paid for by the hotel. Nor did the auditor attempt to reconcile interest returned as income with interest shown as derived in the various banking accounts.

42. The evidence before the Tribunal is such that I must accept that the applicant regularly bet on horses during the relevant years of income, that he deliberately understated his returns of income during those years and that the applicant was prepared to lie to defend his position. The basis for the last conclusion is to be found in evidence about cheque butts - he denied having cheque butts until he realised it may be to his advantage to produce them.

43. However, I have no reason to doubt the evidence of W, the superintendent of police and the former owner of the hotel. I have taken into account the fact that the former owner may have had a position to defend but have concluded that his evidence was honestly given. The result is that I am satisfied on the balance of probabilities that the applicant was a successful punter on racehorses during the relevant years of income. In particular I accept that the applicant had good sources of information in racing circles and was therefore likely to be an overall winner.

44. Because the respondent has not taken the unquantified gambling wins into account in forming his judgment under section 167 of the Act the assessments in dispute must be excessive. This is evidenced by the way in which banking deposits and withdrawals have been dealt with and also by the quite extraordinary amounts attributed to personal living expenses. I have no reason to doubt the evidence of the applicant that he and W were not big spenders. There is nothing before me to suggest otherwise.

45. The overall impression gained from the material before me is that there has been a deliberate and contrived arrangement for the understatement of the applicant's assessable income. There has been no explanation as to why part of the remuneration paid to the applicant by the hotel was not included on his group certificates. There is absolutely no doubt in my mind that the applicant deliberately understated his assessable income. On the other hand I am satisfied that the assessments are excessive i.e. the amounts of assessed taxable income and tax payable are greater than they should be.

46. Faced with this situation, I can either set aside the objection decisions under review and remit the matter back to the respondent with directions or I can set aside the objection decisions and form a judgment as to the amount upon which income tax ought to be levied (s.167 of the Act and s.43 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975). I propose to follow the second course. In doing so I propose to make specific adjustments to the taxable income as returned. I have accepted that the amounts returned by the applicant are the correct amounts of interest derived by the applicant. 49. I turn now to the question of additional tax assessed in accordance with section 223 of the Act. Sub-section 1 of that section provides, inter alia, for the imposition of additional tax, by way of a penalty, where the taxpayer omits from a statement to a taxation officer any matter or thing without which the statement is misleading in a material particular. The Act imposes additional tax at double the amount of the tax avoided on the basis of the omission. Sub-section 227(3) provides for remission in whole or in part of the additional tax imposed by section 223.

52. As I have said previously it should be a rare case where the tax and additional tax assessed exceed in total the taxable income assessed (Case Y12 91 ATC 186 at 191). Furthermore any consideration of a remission requires that attention be directed to remitting whole or part of the additional tax imposed by the Act. To do this regard must be had to the amounts involved and not to a mathematical formula based upon percentages.

53. In making the assessments the respondent applied one of its taxation rulings. I was told in evidence by the auditor that it was ruling IT.2206. I well understand why the respondent makes these rulings so as to obtain consistency of decision making in a large organisation. They are, however, only internal guidelines which need to be adapted to the circumstances of the case.
