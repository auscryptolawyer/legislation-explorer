QT86/2644 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 817; 87 ATC 1113 (23 October 1987)

Last Updated: 29 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Allowable deductions - Concessional rebates -whether taxpayer entitled to dependants rebate for de facto spouse - whether unemployed workman a "prescribed person" -penalty for late lodgement - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 -1984 - secs. 159J, 251U.

In re Bethel (1887) 38 Ch D 220

In re Goodman's Trusts (1881) 17 Ch D 266

And:

RESPONDENT

AAT Decision No 3882

Dr P Gerber, Senior Member

Pursuant to subsection 43(1)(a) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the tribunal affirms the objection decision in relation to the assessment for the year ended 30 June 1984.

(Sgd.) P Gerber

SENIOR MEMBER

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

APPLICANT

And:

RESPONDENT

REASONS FOR DECISION

Before: Dr P Gerber

Date: 23 October 1987

Place: Brisbane

The taxpayer in this application is a qualified tradesman who was employed at his trade for the first half of the 1984 financial year and, due to the exigencies of the industry, was made redundant for the second half of that year and received unemployment benefits from 1 February 1984 until he found new employment in the following tax year.

2. In issue are:

(i) a rebate in respect of a de facto spouse pursuant to sec 159J of the Tax Act;

(ii) relief from the Medicare levy on the basis of being a "prescribed person";

(iii) relief from additional tax for late lodgement (levied at 20 per cent).

3. Re (i) The applicant's evidence was to the effect that he was living in a stable de facto relationship with one Elizabeth C and that both were ideologically opposed to the vows and ceremonies by which other lads and lassies change their status from unencumbered sprites to matrimonial mortgagors without the equity of redemption. The applicant viewed the Commissioner's refusal to allow him his rebate as a form of discrimination. He put it poignantly in his objection:

"I have no doubt that if I were of a different ethnic group and married by local custom my claim would have been allowed and this I consider totally discriminatory."

4. "Local custom" is no guarantee of recognition of marriages contracted abroad. One need only recall the sad fate of the late Christopher Bethel1, an Englishman who died on 30 July 1884 fighting for the Boers in Bechuanaland. Before his death, Christopher had married one Teepoo according to the custom of the Barralong tribe. The marriage was not recognized in England and his child was held to be illegitimate (In re Bethell. Bethell v Hildyard (1885) 38 Ch D 238. It was this kind of jurisprudence which caused Lord Justice James to declaim in a case involving the question legitimation per subsequens matrimonium:

"England has been for centuries a country of hospitality and commerce. It has opened its shores to thousands of political refugees and religious exiles, fleeing from their enemies and persecutors. It has opened its ports to merchants of the whole world, and has by wise laws induced and encouraged them to settle in our marts. But would it not be shocking if such a man, seeking a home in this country, with his family of legitimated children, should find that the English hospitality was as bad as the worst form of persecution from which he has escaped, by destroying his family ties, by declaring that the relation of father and child no longer existed, that his rights and duties and powers as a father had ceased, that the child of his parental affection and fond pride, whom he had taught to love honour and obey him, for whom he had toiled and saved, was thenceforth, in contemplation of the law of his new country, a fatherless bastard .... because of the mythical legend that the English barons and earls many centuries ago cried out in Latin, Nolumus leges Angliae mutare?" (In re Goodman's Trusts (1881) 15 Ch D 266, 297-298)

  1. The applicant, who ably argued his own case, echoed Lord Justice James' sentiments. In addition, he sought some comfort from the Oxford English Dictionary, which provides, as one illustration of "marriage" an "intimate union". If intimacy were a true illustration of "marriage", a lot of people would be disagreeably surprised on waking up in the morning. On the other hand, the applicant's submission is not wholly devoid of merit. It is true that our laws recognise marriage by cohabitation and repute if recognised by local law (lex loci celebrationis), In re Bethell notwithstanding. However, I very much doubt whether that rule applies to an exchange of vows on Bribie Island, a mere 80 kilometres north of Brisbane. To succeed on his argument the taxpayer and his dependant would have had to go to Scotland and contract a marriage, either per verba de praesente or by written consent followed by subsequent consummation. Unfortunately for this taxpayer, this arrangement would have had to be executed before the end of 1939 because these marriages have been rendered ineffective as of 1 January 1940 by Scots law.
  2. I am satisfied that I must give the word "spouse" in the Tax Act its ordinary meaning, that is, there is a presumption that there has been a ceremony solemnized by or in the presence of an authorized celebrant (cf Marriage Act 1961, sec 41). This view achieves even greater force when it is realized that the Tax Act recognizes de facto relationships for some other purposes of the Act, as, for example, the rebate provision for home loan interest; (cf sec 159ZA). Furthermore, the fact that the definition of "spouse" was enlarged by amendment as of 1 July 1984 (cf sec 159H(3)) and henceforth provides a dependant rebate where a man and woman live together as husband and wife on a domestic basis although not legally married to one another strongly suggests that if the applicant's argument were correct, • the amendment would be surplusage. In short, where there is evidence of an absence of a marriage celebration which complies with the Marriage Act, the rebate provided for by the section can have no application in the year now under review. This aspect of the claim must therefore fail.
    1. Re (ii) - the applicant asserted that by virtue of his unemployment he became the holder of a Health Care Card and thus a "prescribed person" exempt from the Medicare levy.
    2. Section 251U(1) states: "Subject to this section, a person shall be taken to be a prescribed person for the purposes of this Part and of any Act imposing levy through a particular period if -

(a) ....

(b) ...

(c) the person was, through the whole of that period, the holder of a Card issued by the Commonwealth and known as a Health Care Card.....and the amount of the income derived by the person or by his or her spouse was not taken into account in determining the eligibility of the person for the Card."

9. Whether a person is a "prescribed person" entitled to exemption from the levy must be determined by circumstances existing in the five month period from 1 February 1984 to 30 June 1984 (sec 251R(8)).

  1. The respondent submitted that the applicant was not a "prescribed person" because his Health Care Card was subject to an income test notwithstanding the fact that when he received his Health Care Card, he was during the whole of that period below the relevant levy threshold.
  2. To understand this somewhat existentialist concept requires a detailed knowledge of the application of the Social Security legislation, a minefield of unexploded booby traps. I am grateful for the assistance of Mrs Jocelyn Green, Assistant Director of the Department of Social Security, who, on short notice, came to the Tribunal's assistance and enabled me to find my way through to the end without blowing up. Her evidence comes to this: There are some conditions (for example blindness) which entitle such a person to a Health Care Card which is not subject to an income test. Unemployment is not such a condition and an unemployed person is only entitled to a Health Care Card if his/her income falls below the statutory amount. It is not to the point that this applicant would have qualified under the income test (his only income was the Social Security benefit), it is the quality or characteristic of the Health Care Card which is made the discrimen for the status of the "prescribed person" in the Tax Act. Since the applicant's Health Care Card was subject to an income test, this aspect of his claim cannot succeed.
  3. Re (iii) Since the penalty in this case does not exceed 20 per cent, I have no power to interfere with the exercise of the Commissioner's discretion. The applicant explained, both before me and in his objection, that the delay in lodging his return was due to a series of factors, none of which would, in my opinion, introduce a "culpability" component, a term so beloved by the respondent, who adopts the view that a failure to lodge a return must be regarded with the same venom as a failure to pay tax when due. I am not persuaded that these two run in parallel streams.
  4. It is perhaps timely to point out that in the case of late lodgement, the Act provides for a discretion with respect to the imposition of additional tax and that this discretion is not exercised by applying an arbitrary amount, culled mutatis mutandis from another section of the Act dealing with late payment. The instant case would seem to me to have been an appropriate one for imposing merely a nominal penalty, calculated on an interest only basis. In parenthesis, it is interesting to observe that when Parliament decreed that penalties, struck at 10 per cent or below, were not reviewable, the imposition of 10 per cent became the standard amount for late or non-lodgement of returns. After Parliament increased the penalties which are not reviewable from 10 per cent to 20 per cent, late lodgement penalties automatically rose from 10 to 20 per cent. It seems that these kinds of penalties follow the U tube principle, where the level of remission will always be at the same height as the level which cannot be reviewed. Late lodgement by busy people can, at times, have an innocent explanation. It therefore seems appropriate to remind the respondent that he has a discretion and that this discretion is not exercised by imposing a knee-jerk penalty of 20 per cent whenever a taxpayer has overlooked - for what may be the most innocent of reasons - to lodge his return on time.

14. For the above reasons the application is dismissed.
