Last Updated: 16 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Taxation - eligible deductions s54 - motor vehicle depreciation - employee of AIS - claims relating to 3 vehicles – excessive corrosion - rates of depreciation for various plant ITO 1217 - necessity to request individual depreciation rate - "one off" claims for loss not recognised as depreciation.

Taxation - eligible deductions s51(1) - operating expenses of fishing vessel - taxpayer amateur fisherman in the relevant income years - vessel used to survey fishing grounds - nature of expenses - expenses not contemporaneous with derivation of income - acquisition of a structural asset.

Income Tax Assessment Act ss 51(1), 54(1), 55(1)

Maddelana v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 71 ATC 4161

Ronpibon Tin NL and Tongkah Compound NL v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1949) 78 CLE 47

Softwood Pulp and Paper Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 76 ATC 4439

REASONS FOR DECISION

  1. The applicant's claims for deductions fall naturally into two parts. For the years ended 30th June 1979, 1980, 1981 and 1982 he claimed deduction for depreciation on three different motor vehicles. For the years ended 30th June 1981 and 1982 he claimed depreciation and operating costs in respect of a certain fishing vessel.
  2. In 1973 he purchased the first motor vehicle, which was then a twelve months' old Volvo stationwagon. He bought it while in good condition for a price of $5,500. At the time, he was employed with Australian Iron and Steel Pty Limited as a metallurgist. He drove the car to work but was also required to use it to transport himself around the company's vast works which covered several square miles. As a senior technical officer (raw materials), his job was to inspect iron ores, limestones, sands and other components intended for the blast furnaces. In the course of this employment, he had an intimate involvement with a substance that was referred to as filter cake. This was a fine iron ore precipitate which was the end result of a dedusting system and which contained between 5% and 8% chloride. It was necessary from time to time to transport filter cake from the works. In the course of this activity, it dribbled out of the trucks and came to cover a considerable area of the works and associated roads. Although it was particularly thick near the office block where the applicant worked, it was necessary for him to drive his car through it everyday.
  3. The security and parking arrangements within the works were such that a person with the applicant's classification was given access through every gate within the works 24 hours per day. However, the area designated for his parking was one that was covered with filter cake. This area was well over one square kilometre.
  4. For many years the corrosive quality of this mud-like substance was not suspected. However, by 1978 excessive rust began to appear in the Volvo and by 1979 the vehicle was totally corroded and unroadworthy. The vehicle was advertised for sale in the local paper with no success and was ultimately sold for spares to a dealer for $450.
  5. The applicant claimed as depreciation the difference between what the Volvo should have been worth had it been driven the normal mileage over the vehicle's normal life on the one hand, and the sale price of the vehicle on the other hand. The difference of $3,050 was claimed for depreciation in the year ended 30th June 1979. The claim is based on the excessive actual depreciation specifically caused by conditions associated with the gaining of assessable income. The applicant's case is that the underbody of the car was particularly corroded by this substance which he could not avoid. It was necessary not only to pass over it and to park on it, but also to move amongst it. It was part of his job to supervise the condition of this material and "to decide what should be recycled and what should be dumped. As a result of this, his duties involved him in an intimate connection with it.

6. After the Volvo was sold, the applicant bought a Landrover, hoping that it would be more suitable. The underside of the vehicle was then soaked with heavy oil. The applicant hoped that this would be more effective than the anti-rusting inhibitor which the Volvo had. It did not prove to be so. Within 12 months, excessive corrosion became noticeable and the second vehicle was sold. An application of the same formula resulted in a difference of $450 which was claimed for depreciation in the year ended 30th June 1980. In the years ending 30th June 1981 and 1982 depreciation was claimed in respect of a third vehicle. In those cases the amounts of $700 in each year did not represent the difference between a sale price and the "bluebook" value (determined according to trade guidelines). They represented the difference between the then actual value as determined by a local dealer and the "bluebook value". The applicant's case was that the total difference in value in each case was caused by corrosion which by then was quite evident, even in the third vehicle. These claims for motor vehicle depreciation are in a different category from the claims to which I will now refer.

7. In 1978 or 1979 the applicant bought a vessel designed for fishing. She was a 17 foot aluminium boat with console configuration and a 70 horsepower outboard motor. The applicant had been a keen amateur fisherman and was an official of local sporting fishing clubs. The vessel was bought with the intention ultimately of using it for the purpose of professional fishing. In 1980 and 1981 the applicant used it for the purpose of surveying local waters to establish the likelihood of profitable fishing in the future. With the use of echo sounders, he tried to identify the ocean bottom and fish bearing structures such as the edges of reefs. Commercial charts merely gave a coarse overview. They did not have sufficient detailed information to justify the setting up of a commercial fishing operation.

  1. During the two years in question the applicant claims to have spent $600 and $700 for fuel and oil for the vessel. He claimed these as operating expenses of the vessel. He also made a claim of $1,600 in the year ended 30th June 1981 and $1,365 in the year ended 30th June 1982 for her depreciation.
  2. During the whole of the period in question he did no fishing of a commercial nature. In fact he was still employed by Australian Iron and Steel Pty. Ltd. and would not have been able to obtain a commercial fisherman's licence. The survey work was carried out on weekends and rostered days off. He obtained his licence in April 1982 and ultimately resigned from Australia Iron and Steel Pty Ltd in July 1982. From then until the present day he has continued to work as a fisherman in the Wollongong area.
  3. 10. He gave evidence that the survey work was of considerable assistance to him and that he still uses the information he obtained in those days. As he put it "reef areas don't shift". He is a successful fisherman and attributes a good deal of his success to the ground work that was laid during those years.
    1. He agrees that there was some private use of the vessel during the survey period but for reasons which will later appear, it does not seem to me to be relevant to consider any possible apportionment.
    2. In his final address, the applicant (who was unrepresented) continued to emphasise the difficulty he experienced in understanding why expenses of the survey should be regarded as capital expenditure. He pointed out that the expenses that he claimed were in the nature of operating expenses for which he is now entitled to a deduction. He emphasised that he understood that claimable operating expenses should be claimed in the year in which they were incurred, but as he pointed out he was unable to earn money in the years in which these expenses were incurred because of his lack of a licence. He understood that the capital cost of the vessel would not be deductible, but considered that the monies he expended and the expenses he incurred by way of depreciation were necessary for his present business.

13. The claims in respect of the vessel can be conveniently- dealt with first. It is well settled that expenses which are not contemporaneous with the derivation of income or expenses which relate to the acquisition of a structural asset are capital expenditure and are therefore excluded by section 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act") from eligibility as deductions.

14. In Maddalena v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 71 ATC 4161 for example, the question was whether the applicant, a professional footballer, was entitled to deduct expenses incurred by him in seeking and obtaining a new contract with a new club to play football. In the view of the court, an applicant who, at the time, had no business of providing services had no income earning activity of providing services until he had an employment contract. The expenses therefore could not be viewed as expenses of an activity of performing services from which he might derive rewards that were income. At the time that these present expenses were incurred, the applicant was not a fisherman and was not deriving income from that activity. No income flowed from the use of the vessel in question. The same phrase occurs both in section 51 dealing with general deductions, and in section 54 dealing with general depreciation.

15. As was said in Ronpibon Tin NL and Tongkah Compound NL v FC of T [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at pages 56-7:

"For expenditure to form an allowable deduction as an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, it must be incidental and relevant to that end. The words 'incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income' mean in the course of gaining or producing such income. Their operation has been explained in cases decided under the provisions of the previous enactments

16. I would respectfully add that in order to determine whether plant or articles are used for the purpose of producing assessable income so as to found a claim for depreciation under section 54, it must be shown that the plant or articles are used in the course of gaining or producing such income. Preparation for or anticipation of income is not sufficient where a new venture is to be created.

17. In Softwood Pulp and Paper Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation, 76 ATC 4439 expenses such as travel, legal and accounting expenses, the cost of testing raw material, the cost of professional fees for the conduct of feasibility-studies by expert consultants and the acquisition of some raw materials were held to be capital outgoings. Menhennitt J said at page 4450:

"In my opinion none of the amounts claimed in this case, assuming that all or any of them were incurred by the applicant and some of them undoubtedly were, was incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable income.

Everything that was done in this case, up till the time when the project ceased, was in my view entirely preliminary and directed to deciding whether or not an undertaking would be established to produce assessable income."

18. The expenses incurred by the applicant in the present application were preliminary to the establishment of his business as a professional fisherman. The structural asset that he acquired through those expenses, namely the knowledge of the seas in his area, has been capitalised and turned to good advantage. The expenses incurred in arriving at the stage when he could safely decide to fish professionally must be regarded as "setting up" expenses. After the structural asset becomes a revenue asset the same type of expenses will assume a different nature and will be, as the taxpayer has since discovered, deductible. The claim for excessive depreciation on the motor vehicles due to excessive corrosion caused by circumstances related to the gaining of assessable income now falls for consideration.

19. It seems to me that this claim is based on a misunderstanding of the statutory nature of depreciation. The starting point of the statutory scheme is section 54(1) of the Act which is in the following terms:

"Depreciation during the year of income of any property, being plant or articles owned by a applicant and used by him during that year for the purposes of producing assessable income, and of any property being plant or articles owned by the taxpayer which has been installed ready for use for that purpose and is during that year held in reserve by him shall, subject to this Act, be an allowable deduction."

  1. Without such special statutory provision, depreciation, not being a loss or outgoing, would not be deductible. The only basis upon which a taxpayer can rely for such a deduction is the basis specifically set out in the relevant sections of the Act.
  2. It is clear that the word "depreciation" does not refer to any actual demonstrable loss in value. The word bears a specific meaning spelt out by the structure and terms of the relevant sections. Under section 55(1) the Commissioner is bound to make a calculation of the effective life of the unit, assuming it is maintained in reasonably good order and condition and the annual depreciation rate percentum is to be fixed accordingly. Depreciation, in other words, is not a one off expense. It is expressed in statutory terms only as a rate per centum per annum.
    1. In the exercise of his statutory function under section 55(1), the Commissioner has drawn up a list of standard basic rates of depreciation for various types of plant used under normal working conditions. This list is contained in Income Tax Order No. 1217. It lists the various types of plant and equipment that can be the subject of claims for depreciation and sets a basic rate both according to the prime cost method and according to the diminishing value method. There are special statutory provisions where these rates may be increased by loadings or by acceleration but none of these statutory provisions has relevance to the present circumstances.
    2. It is obviously administratively impossible for the Commissioner to have regard to each specific item of property. However, in any particular case an applicant may expressly ask him to make an estimate of the effective life of any particular piece of equipment, and thus determine the rates of depreciation appropriate to that item. But thereafter, there is no provision in the legislation for depreciation to be assessed otherwise than on a percentage per annum basis. If the amount claimed for depreciation at the end of the year does not truly represent the diminution in value of the item of plant, then an error may have been made in the calculation of the appropriate percentage rate. There is no provision, however, in the legislation for claiming that diminution in value as such, particularly when the diminution in value is realised on sale. It will be recalled that the Volvo vehicle of the applicant was in use for many years before it was sold below what its market value might otherwise have been. That difference in value is not recognised in the Act as a deductible item. The only recognition given is a percentage fixed at the outset, which is intended to relate to normal equipment over its normal working life. If the equipment is not normal or if the working life is expected to be unusually short then special application may be made to the Commissioner. He can, however, go no further than his statutory obligation of fixing an annual depreciation rate. In the absence of any such request, the percentage rates specified in Income Tax Order 1217 will apply.

24. After the experience with the Volvo, it might have been possible for the applicant to have approached the Commissioner to fix a special rate of depreciation for each of the two Landrovers, the subject of the second and third claims. This was not done however. Claims were made once again on a "one off" loss basis. For reasons which I have outlined, such claims cannot be regarded as depreciation in the way in which that term is used in the Act.

25, I have not dealt with the question whether the diminution in value of the vehicles was caused by non-work related causes. For example, it was suggested that each of the vehicles was parked at the seaside for lengthy periods while the applicant was fishing. I have also not found it necessary to deal with the question whether the vehicles were wholly engaged in the gaining of assessable income. The applicant admitted, and it is evident in any event, that there is a substantial element of private use involved. Whatever the situation, it appears to me that no part of the amounts claimed may be treated as depreciation. They simply do not fall within the concept of that term as understood in the Act.

26. Accordingly, the decisions under review are affirmed.
