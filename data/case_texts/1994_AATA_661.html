DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISIONS



FRINGE BENEFITS TAX - car used for business purposes - garaged at place of business and residence - whether available for private use by employee - whether car an exempt benefit - principal purpose to carry passengers - statutory formula applied to determine taxable value of benefit - decision affirmed - culpability penalty remitted.

REASONS FOR DECISION



7 November 1994 Deputy President B.M. Forrest



This is an application for review of a decision to disallow the applicant’s objection to the Commissioner’s fringe benefits tax assessment of the applicant for the year ending 31 March 1991.



The applicant, a family company, conducts an art gallery outside the Hobart metropolitan area. It owns a station wagon which during the whole of the relevant period was garaged at the premises from which the applicant conducts its business. The premises are also the residence of the applicant’s sole shareholders and directors who are also employed by the applicant in its business. Following an audit of the applicant it was determined by the Commissioner that the applicant was liable to pay fringe benefits tax in respect of the station wagon.



In its 1990-1991 income tax return the Commissioner accepted the applicant’s claim for running expenses and depreciation for the station wagon as 100% business.



The fringe benefits legislation is a comprehensive framework whereby a wide range of fringe benefits provided to employees are liable to tax. Fringe benefits tax is not an income tax but a tax levied on employers for the fringe benefits provided to employees.



Section 66 of the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 “the FBT Act” imposes a liability on the employer to pay tax on the Fringe Benefits Taxable amount. In s. 136 numerous expressions used in the FBT Act are defined. The term “benefit” is given a wide meaning:



“benefit” includes any right (including a right in relation to, and an interest in, real or personal property), privilege, service or facility...”



“Fringe benefit” is also defined. In general terms it refers to any benefit other than salary or wages. The definition lists a number of other payments and benefits which are not a fringe benefit for the purposes of the FBT Act.



The provisions relating to car fringe benefits are to be found in Division 2 of Part III of the FBT Act. The car fringe benefits tax is levied on the value of the private use of the car by the employee or on the value of the availability for private use of the car. It is assessed on the value of the benefit provided by an employer to an employee in connection with his or her employment. Section 7 provides:

“7. (1) Where:



(a) at any time on a day, in respect of the employment of an employee, a car held by a person (in this subsection referred to as the “provider”):



(i) is applied to a private use by the employee or an associate of the employee; or



(ii) is taken to be available for the private use of the employee or an associate of the employee; and



(b) either of the following conditions is satisfied:



(i) the provider is the employer, or an associate of the employer, of the employee;



(ii) the car is so applied or available, as the case may be, under an arrangement between:



(A) the provider or another person; and



(B) the employer, or an associate of the employer, of the employee;



that application or availability of the car shall be taken to constitute a benefit provided on that day by the provider to the employee or associate in respect of the employment of the employee.



(2) Where, at a particular time, the following conditions are satisfied in relation to an employee of an employer:



(a) a car is held by a person, being:



(i) the employer;



(ii) an associate of the employer; or



(iii) a person (other than the employer or an associate of the employer) with whom, or in respect of whom, the employer or an associate of the employer has an arrangement relating to the use or availability of the car;



(b) the car is garaged or kept at or near a place of residence of the employee or of an associate of the employee;



the car shall be taken, for the purposes of this Act, to be available at that time for the private use of the employee or associate, as the case may be.”



If the conditions of s. 7 are met then by virtue of the definitions of “car benefit” and “car fringe benefit” in s. 136, there will be a car fringe benefit, unless an exemption applies under s. 8. “Car” is also defined in s. 136 to include a station wagon.



Broadly speaking where an employer’s car is used by an employee for private purposes or is available for the private use of the employee a car fringe benefit arises.



At the hearing the applicant was represented by the public officer of the company who also gave evidence in respect of the use of the motor vehicle. The Commissioner was represented by Mr Giles a departmental officer.



It was submitted that the applicant should not be liable for fringe benefits tax in respect of the station wagon on the basis that the vehicle was used exclusively for business use. The fact it was garaged at the place of residence of the directors of the applicant was said to be simply a consequence of the residence also being the applicant’s place of business. It was alternatively submitted that the vehicle should be seen as an exempt benefit in that it was purchased because of the nature of the applicant’s business, and it provided sufficient space for the carriage of works of art and goods used in the applicant’s business.



The difficulty with the first submission is that the test of whether a vehicle may be the subject of fringe benefit is not only whether it is applied to private use but also whether it is taken to be available for private use by the employee. Because the vehicle is garaged at the residence of the applicant’s employees it will be taken to be available for their private use. In such circumstances a fringe benefit arises whether or not the vehicle is actually applied to private use by the employee. The garaging of the car at the employees’ place of residence and its availability for their private use are determinative factors.



The fact that the applicant’s business premises also happens to be the employees place of residence does not allow a different result even though in general terms a car will not be taken to be available for private use by an employee when it is garaged at the business premises.



Accordingly a car fringe benefit arises in respect of the subject motor vehicle.



The applicant’s second submission that the availability of the motor car be treated as an exempt benefit cannot succeed. Notwithstanding the evidence of use to which the vehicle is put in the applicant’s business, the vehicle as a station wagon is designed for the principal purpose of carrying passengers and is specifically excluded from the category of motor vehicles to which the exemption can apply; s. 8(2).



Having determined that a fringe benefit arises in the availability of the car for private use the next question is the determination of the taxable value of the benefit. This may be by one of two methods; the statutory formula contained in s. 9 or the operating cost basis in s. 10.



The statutory formula will apply unless the employer elects to adopt the operating costs basis. However, if as a result of an election to use the operating cost basis the taxable value of the car fringe benefit exceeds the value determined under the statutory formula than the latter value applies.



Under the former the taxable value is determined by applying a statutory percentage which varies according to the kilometres travelled by the car in the tax year to the base value, generally the original cost of the vehicle and applying to the result the number of days the car is privately used or available for private use during the relevant tax year.



Under the later method the private kilometres travelled by the car during the FBT year is valued to arrive at the taxable value which is reduced in proportion to the business percentage determined in accordance with s. 10(2). It is determined by reducing the total operating costs of the car in the proportion of private kilometres travelled to the total kilometres travelled during the year. To use this method a log book must be kept for 12 consecutive weeks.



The Commissioner submitted that the operating costs method could not apply as the log book requirements to enable this method to be used have not been met.



The records supplied by the applicant (T7) do not contain sufficient particulars to satisfy the requirement for log book entries: see definition of “log book records”, s. 136(1).



The records supplied do not indicate the number of kilometres travelled on each journey, the name of the driver on each journey, the name or signature of the person making the entry or whether the odometer reading given is at the start or finish of the journey.



It was not in dispute that the value of the car was $16,000 (its original cost in 1981) and that the annualized number of kilometres for the year was 2805. The kilometres travelled were taken from records kept by the applicant.



The taxable value of the car fringe benefit has been correctly calculated in accordance with the statutory formula in s. 9(1).

Base value

2/3 of cost price 10,666.00

vehicle more than 4 years old: s. 9(2)(a)(i)



Statutory fraction 0.24

Less than 15,000 kilometres: s. 9(2)(c)(ii)



It should be said that if the employer was able to demonstrate that there was no actual private use, bearing in mind that the total kilometres travelled is low and that the Commissioner has in some years accepted 100% business use of the vehicle for income tax purposes, the use of the operating cost method may result in little if any taxable value. However, to use this method requires compliance with the log book provisions of the FBT Act.



From the perspective of the applicant as an operator of a small family business, coming to grips with the complexities of the FBT Act is a matter of understandable concern. The records furnished by the applicant, though inadequate for fringe benefit tax purposes, satisfied the Commissioner as to the business usage of the vehicle for income tax purposes. This indicates a spirit of compliance with the FBT Act and in the circumstances the whole of the culpability penalty imposed on the applicant should in my opinion be remitted. Otherwise the objection decision under review will be affirmed.
