CATCHWORDS

FRINGE BENEFITS TAX - child care benefit - facility on jointly leased premises - whether exempt benefit - whether facility located on business premises of employer - intention of Parliament

Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986

Richardson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (unreported 1273 FCA 25 November 1997)

Cooper Brooker (Wollongong) Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation (1980-1981) [1981] HCA 26; 147 CLR 297

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Swift and others (1989) [1989] FCA 413; 18 ALD 679



REASONS FOR DECISION

24 April 1998 Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member



  1. This is an application to review a decision of the respondent to disallow an objection against a Notice of Private Ruling issued on 19 February 1997. This ruling was that child care benefits provided by Esso Australia Ltd (“Esso”) under the arrangements set out in the request for ruling were fringe benefits as defined by section 136(1) of the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 (“the Act”) and not exempt under section 47(2) of the Act. In an earlier private ruling dated 8 January 1993 and expressed as applicable to the years ended 31 December 1993, 1994 and 1995, the respondent had ruled that the child care benefits were exempt under section 47(2). The ruling which was the subject of this application was expressed as being applicable to the years ended 31 December 1996, 1997 and 1998 and was based on views expressed in Taxation Ruling TR96/27 which had been issued on 18 December 1996.
  2. At the hearing the applicant was represented by Mr J. De Wijn of counsel and the respondent by Mr M. Moshinsky of counsel. Given that any review of an objection decision can be based solely on the facts and details of an arrangement on which the ruling was based, no evidence was led.
  3. The sole question in issue was whether it could be said that the child care facility is located on business premises of the employer, Esso. It was not in dispute that the provision of child care for an employee by Esso was a benefit as defined by the Act and a residual benefit under that Act. Section 47(2) of the Act states:
“47(2) Where:
(a) a residual benefit provided to a current employee in respect of his or her employment consists of:
  • (i) the provision, or use, of a recreational facility; or
  • (ii) the care of children of the employee in a child care facility; and


(b) the recreational facility or child care facility, as the case may be, is located on business premises of:
  • (i) the employer, or
  • (ii) if the employer is a company, of the employer or of a company that is related to the employer;
the benefit is an exempt benefit.”

Pursuant to section 136(1) of the Act “business premises” are defined in the following terms:

“ ‘business premises’, in relation to a person, means premises, or a part of premises, of the person used, in whole or in part, for the purposes of business operations of the person, but does not include:
(a) premises, or a part of premises, used as a place of residence of an employee of the person or an employee of an associate of the person; or

(b) a corporate box; or

(c) boats or planes used primarily for the purpose of providing entertainment unless the boat or plane is used in the person’s business of providing entertainment; or

(d) other premises used primarily for the purpose of providing entertainment unless the premises are used in the person’s business of providing entertainment.
In this definition, provision of entertainment has the same meaning as in section 51AE of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.”

Whilst the term “business operations” is included in the list of definitions in section 136(1) it relates solely to a government body or a non-profit company and has no relevance to this matter.

  1. The basic facts relating to this matter are relatively straight forward and can be taken from the statement of facts filed with the Tribunal by Esso prior to the hearing:
“1. The Applicant, together with Lend Lease Corporation Ltd. (Lend Lease) and BP Australia Ltd. (“BP”), has established and maintains a child care centre available for the use of employees of the respective companies. The child care centre forms an integral part of the Applicant’s Work and Family Program, in recognition of which the Applicant was the inaugural winner in 1992 of The Australian Financial Review/Business Council of Australia Corporate Work and Family Awards and again a winner of this award, which is also sponsored by the Commonwealth Department of Industrial Relations, in 1995.
  1. The Applicant’s Work and Family Program is explained in the following extract from the Work and Family Program policy document:
‘At Esso we are committed to helping our employees achieve successful and satisfying careers. We also realise that the demands of a family can sometimes conflict with those at work. Balancing the two is not easy but necessary. It means having the flexibility to meet family needs while ensuring the work gets done.’

The aims of the program are met by establishing policies in a number of areas including: family care/parental leave, personal leave, sick leave/income security, flexible work arrangements, job sharing, working at home, employee assistance programs and health and fitness programs. A key feature of the program has been the provision of quality work based child care and related support.
  1. The child care policies of the Applicant have seen a substantial reduction in the number of female employees who do not return to work after taking parental leave so that now 92% of female employees return to work with the Applicant. This high return rate has led to a shift in the company culture; it is now assumed that women who have babies will return to the workforce, either in a full time or part time capacity.
  2. The Applicant’s child care centre is located in South Melbourne, a five minute drive from the Applicant’s head office and is open from 7:15am to 6:30pm. The premises are leased by the Applicant, Lend Lease and BP as joint lessees from The Uniting Church in Australia Property Trust (Victoria). The lease is for a period of 3 years (with options for three further terms each of 3 years).
  3. The Applicant, Lend Lease and BP have entered into an agreement with KU Children’s Services (“KU”) to provide child care management services. The agreement with KU relates exclusively to the provision of child care services. KU has no rights or obligations in respect of the lease held by the Applicant, Lend Lease and BP.
  4. The child care centre was opened in January 1993, when it was leased to the Applicant and Lend Lease only. Employees of the Applicant have from that time been entitled to make an election to forgo salary in consideration of the Applicant providing the employee with a child care benefit. BP became a joint lessee on 1 April, 1993.”
  5. It was submitted for the applicant that the child care centre constituted premises of Esso in its capacity as joint lessee. Whilst it was accepted that they could be regarded also as premises of the landlord and premises of the other joint lessees, it was argued that the definition did not require exclusive possession. It was further argued that the provision of child care was an integral part of the business operations of Esso and the premises were used for the purpose of its business operations. It was said that, if Parliament had intended the exemption to apply only where an employee had exclusive possession, then it would be expected that it would have said so. Mr de Wijn instanced many provisions of taxation legislation which used the words “wholly and exclusively” and noted that the definition in section 136 used the words “in whole or in part” instead. He submitted that the purpose of section 47(2) was to encourage the provision of child care facilities by employers and should be interpreted beneficially and not read down unless the words of the provision specifically required exclusive possession.
  6. For the respondent it was submitted that the ordinary and natural meaning of the words “business premises of the employer” and “premises...of the person used...for the purposes of business operations of the person” in the context of the legislation is business premises of the employer exclusively. It was argued that the words do not accurately describe business premises of the employer and other employers. Mr Moshinsky submitted that, had Parliament intended the provision to apply to cases of joint ownership or joint possession, it would have said so by including words such as “whether occupied exclusively or jointly with others”. He maintained that the legislative intent was to subject a child care benefit to fringe benefits tax with section 47(2) providing a narrow exemption available only to certain employers in specific limited circumstances. It was argued that the acceptance of the applicant’s interpretation would exempt arrangements set up by groups of employers in circumstances where the benefit was clearly intended to be subject to the tax.
  7. There was no dispute between the parties that premises used in the provision of child care facilities, particularly in circumstances such as these, constitute a purpose of business operations of the employer. In addition there was no dispute that leased premises can constitute premises of the employer, it being accepted that such premises can be properly regarded as premises of both the landlord and of the tenant. The sole question in dispute is whether the provision requires exclusive occupation with the employer having full management and control over the premises in question. In simple terms, the applicant says that if Parliament had intended to require exclusive possession, as contended by the respondent, it would have said so specifically and the respondent says that if Parliament had intended the exemption to apply to joint possession of premises it would have said so. The Explanatory Memoranda to the Fringe Benefits Assessment Bill 1986 is of little assistance. The relevant extracts state:

(at page 16):

“ Recreational and child minding facilities

(Clause 47)

Recreational or child minding facilities provided on an employer’s business premises for the benefit of employees will be exempt from fringe benefits tax. Facilities of these kinds provided on business premises of a related company in a wholly-owned company group will be similarly exempt.”

(at page 75):

“ By sub-clause 47(2) the provision of recreational or child care facilities on an employer’s premises for the benefit of employees will not give rise to a taxable fringe benefit.

For these purposes ‘child’ is defined in sub-clause 36(1) to include an adopted child, step-child or ex-nuptial child of the employee. ‘Child care facility’ is also defined in that sub-clause and, broadly, means a facility where children under the age of 6 are provided with care or education, but not with residential care. ‘Recreational facility’ is defined in that sub-clause to exclude facilities for accommodation or for drinking or dining.

Where the employer is a company, recreational or child minding facilities provided on a ‘related’ company’s premises – see notes on clause 158 but meaning, broadly, another company within a wholly owned company group – for the benefit of the employer’s employees will be similarly exempt from tax.”

(at page 125):

“ ‘business premises’ means premises or a part of premises used wholly or partly for the purpose of business operations, but does not include any part of premises used as a residence.

‘child care facility’ is defined to mean a facility where a person may receive 2 or more children under 6 years away from their own homes to mind, care for or educate but not provide residential care.”

Neither party could direct the Tribunal to any other extrinsic material to assist in ascertaining the intention of Parliament.

  1. Whilst it may be said that a first reaction to the words of section 47(2) by a reader may well be that a jointly leased building, distinct and separate from Esso’s normal business premises would not appear to be appropriately described as “business premises” of Esso, it is necessary to consider the actual words of the definition in section 136(1). If these words are incorporated in lieu of “business premises” in section 47(2) and the name of the applicant substituted for “employer” and “person”, then the relevant words would be:
“Where a residual benefit provided to a current employee in respect of his or her employment consists of the care of children of the employee in a child care facility and the child care facility is located on premises or part of premises of Esso used, in whole or in part, for the purposes of business operations of Esso, the benefit is an exempt benefit.”
  1. The first consideration is whether the premises on which the child care facility is situated can be correctly described as premises of Esso. It is clear that sole tenancy results in the premises being appropriately considered as premises of the tenant. It is equally clear that the subject premises can be described as premises of Esso, Lend Lease and BP jointly. Can they be described as premises of Esso, premises of Lend Lease and premises of BP? Mr de Wijn contended that the natural meaning of “of” does not require the relevant property to be held to the exclusion of all others as submitted by Mr Moshinsky. Both counsel referred to the same dictionary definition of “of”. The Macquarie Dictionary (3rd Ed.) reads:
“7. Belonging or possession, connection or association : the queen of England; the property of all.”

Extending the example used here it is correct to describe the Queen as Queen of England, Scotland, Wales and Australia. It is equally correct to describe Her Majesty as Queen of Australia. Is it also correct to describe property of Esso, Lend Lease and BP as property of Esso? It was submitted by Mr de Wijn that the nature of joint tenancy is that each joint tenant has the right of possession of the whole of the premises subject to the rights of the other joint tenants. He suggested that, if lines were drawn on the premises so that each of the three joint tenants had exclusive use, or each tenant leased adjacent premises with the one contracted child care services provider managing the three facilities, then the respondent’s ruling would be satisfied. Mr de Wijn submitted that this would be an anomalous result.

  1. The problem of statutory construction where the words are not clear and unambiguous has been commented on in several decisions of the Courts. In a recent case of Richardson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (unreported, 1273 FCA 25 November 1997) Merkel J, after referring to various authorities, adopted a construction of section 97(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 which produced a different approach to the operation of the section depending on whether the trust income is less or greater than the trust’s assessable income. His Honour considered that the different approaches were warranted to avoid capricious, arbitrary and clearly unintended consequences. The decision of the High Court in Cooper Brookes (Wollongong) Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 26; (1980-1981) 147 CLR 297 was concerned with the interpretation of section 80C(3) in its application to section 80B(5) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. In an often quoted passage of the judgement of Mason and Wilson JJ it was said (at pages 320 and 321):
“Generally speaking, mere inconvenience of result in itself is not a ground for departing from the natural and ordinary sense of the language read in its context. But there are cases in which inconvenience of result or improbability of result assists the court in concluding that an alternative construction which is reasonably open is to be preferred to the literal meaning because the alternative interpretation more closely conforms to the legislative intent discernible from other provisions in the statute.

...

On the other hand, when the judge labels the operation of the statute as ‘absurd’, ‘extraordinary’, ‘capricious’, ‘irrational’ or ‘obscure’ he assigns a ground for concluding that the legislature could not have intended such an operation and that an alternative interpretation must be preferred. But the propriety of departing from the literal interpretation is not confined to situations described by these labels. It extends to any situation in which for good reason the operation of the statute on a literal reading does not conform to the legislative intent as ascertained from the provisions of the statute, including the policy which may be discerned from those provisions.

Quite obviously questions of degree arise. If the choice is between two strongly competing interpretations, as we have said, the advantage may lie with that which produces the fairer and more convenient operation so long as it conforms to the legislative intention. If, however, one interpretation has a powerful advantage in ordinary meaning and grammatical sense, it will only be displaced if its operation is perceived to be unintended.”
  1. In relation to this, is there a “natural and ordinary sense of the language read in its context” or an “ordinary meaning and grammatical sense” of the words used in section 136(1) of the Act? If so, can it be perceived that its operation is unintended? It is not easy to see a natural and ordinary sense of the language. One difficulty is the reference to “part of premises” and to the use, “in whole or in part”, for the purpose of business operations. It is not easy to conceive of premises of Esso used only partly for the purpose of business operations. For what other purposes would such a company use premises? In Taxation Ruling TR96/27 the respondent provides examples of situations in which, in his view, the exemption either applies or does not apply. Some of these examples indicate the difficulties which the two interpretations of section 47(2) and section 136 produce. In example 5 it is envisaged that a child care centre operator conducts the business on leased premises and sub-leases an undivided share in the premises to each of several employers. This example highlights a difficulty in the interpretation espoused by the applicant. Under this interpretation, is there a limit to the number of joint lessees or joint sub-lessees before it can be said that the premises cannot be properly regarded as premises of any one of them? Can each of twenty sub-lessees of premises on which a child care facility is located claim an exemption of the benefits of provision of employee child care at that facility? Acceptance of this interpretation seems to involve the view that the intention of Parliament was to exempt a child care benefit where the employer has accepted some liability for rent of the premises rather than the total cost being a fee paid to a child care centre operator.
  2. Example 3 in TR96/27 envisages two wholly owned operating subsidiaries of one holding company. It is considered by the respondent that, if the two subsidiaries lease premises as a child care facility jointly, then no company in the group would be entitled to an exemption as none would have exclusive possession of the premises. However, if one subsidiary leased the premises and offered places to children of employees of the holding company and its sister company, all companies would be entitled to the exemption. This appears an anomalous result and it is difficult to consider that this could have been the intention of Parliament.
  3. Another possible interpretation which was not canvassed by either party at the hearing is that the exemption does not apply to premises which can be described as used for business operations only by virtue of and subsequent to the establishment of a child minding facility thereon. That is, it is first necessary to establish that premises are used for business operations other than the provision of child care and that the child care facility is then located on those business premises.
  4. As an administrative review body, the Tribunal’s role is not simply to be guided on the issues by the way the parties conduct their cases but, rather, the Tribunal is required to arrive at the correct or preferable decision. In order to provide procedural fairness to the parties, the Tribunal held a Directions Hearing by telephone and advised the parties that this third, additional interpretation was proposed to be considered by the Tribunal prior to finalising this decision and reasons, and after considering the submissions made by the parties at the hearing. Given that no submission had been directed to such an interpretation, the applicant was given 14 days in which to make any such submission, with the respondent having a further 7 days to respond prior to the Tribunal finalising its decision. Both parties provided submissions.
  5. In summary, the applicant submitted that:
  • The respondent and the Tribunal are bound by the Public Ruling TR96/27 and the Private Ruling based on that Public Ruling pursuant to section 74A of the Act.
  • The provision of child care forms part of the business operations of the applicant and the relevant premises are “business premises”.
  • If the third possible interpretation was adopted it would produce a consequence that the section 47(2) exemption would have no or little practical operation because it is not generally possible or practical to have child care facilities in office buildings, factories and other places where the actual income earning activities take place. The applicant argued that the standards and requirements of the Children’s Services Centres Regulations 1988 made under the Health Act 1958 typically require purpose built buildings or adapted houses in residential areas.
  1. The submission for the respondent did not contend that any interpretation other than that adopted in TR96/27 should be accepted. However, the respondent argued that the Tribunal is not bound by the public rulings issued by the respondent (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Swift [1989] FCA 413; (1989) 18 ALD 679 at pages 691-692) and that section 74A of the Act cannot be invoked by the applicant if the Tribunal decision produces the same result, that the exemption is not available, but for different reasons.
  2. Section 74A of the Act provides:
“74A(1) Expressions used in this section have the same meanings as in Part IVAAA of the Taxation Administration Act 1953.

74A(2) Subject to section 74C, if:

(a) there is a public ruling on the way in which a fringe benefits tax law applies to a person in relation to an arrangement (‘ruled way’); and

(b) that law applies to that person in relation to that arrangement in a different way; and

(c) the amount of fringe benefits tax under an assessment in relation to that person would (apart from this section and section 74C) exceed what it would have been if that law applied in the ruled way;

the assessment and amount of fringe benefits tax must be what they would be if that law applied in the ruled way.”

Section 74C applies where there is conflict between public and private rulings which is not in issue here.

  1. The review function of the Tribunal and the relevance of the respondent’s rulings was set out by French J in the Federal Court decision in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Swift and others [1989] FCA 413; (1989) 18 ALD 679 at pp 692 and 693:
“Section 43(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act provides, inter alia, that ‘For the purpose of reviewing a decision the Tribunal may exercise all the powers and discretions that are conferred by any relevant enactment on the person who made the decision...’. The Tribunal had, in this case, the same discretion as is conferred on the Commissioner by s 5(4) of the TUCTA Act. Its duty in the exercise of that discretion was not limited to a consideration of the matters that were before the Commissioner, nor to a determination whether his decision was the correct or preferable one. It was, rather, bound to consider the relevant facts proved on the evidence before it and to decide on the basis of those facts what was the correct or preferable decision: Drake v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1979) 2 ALD 60; 24 ALR 577 at 589 (Bowen CJ and Deane J); Nevistic v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs [1981] FCA 41; (1981) 34 ALR 639 at 640 (Franki J); Commonwealth of Australia v Ford (1986) 9 ALD 433; 65 ALR 323 at 328 (Wilcox J); Freeman v Secretary, Department of Social Security [1988] FCA 294; (1988) 15 ALD 671 (Davies J); Fletcher v FCT (1988) 84 ALR 295 at 306 (Full Court). In that process it was neither entitled nor required to place weight upon the fact that the Commissioner had exercised his discretion in a particular way: Collins v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs [1981] FCA 147; (1981) 4 ALD 198; 36 ALR 598 at 603 (Full Court).

In the absence of any statutory direction, the Tribunal is not bound to apply the administrative policies by which the exercise of the discretion under review is regulated at the primary decision-making level. In particular, it was not bound in this case to apply taxation ruling IT 2063 to which reference has already been made. It is generally entitled to take administrative policy into account as a relevant factor in the achievement, inter alia, of a desirable consistency in decision-making: Drake v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs, supra, at ALR 591. For as Brennan J said in Re Drake and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (No 2) [1979] AATA 179; (1979) 2 ALD 634 at 639: ‘Inconsistency is not merely inelegant: it brings the process of deciding into disrepute, suggesting an arbitrariness which is incompatible with commonly accepted notions of justice.’

That observation is subject to the caveat that decision-makers can be consistently wrong or consistently unjust and that consistency is safely sought by reference to policy only when the policy is appropriate and acceptable: Nevistic v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs, supra, at 646 (Deane J). This does not involve the Tribunal in reviewing policy or deciding what policy a primary decision-maker should adopt: Re Aston (1985) 8 ALD 366; 4 AAR 65 at 76 (Davies J). And, as in that case, which involved the exercise of a discretion to limit the terms of fishing licences under a national quota scheme, a policy which is developed in the political arena after consultation with the relevant industry ought generally to be given great weight. Taxation ruling It 2063 does not appear to be in that category. Nevertheless, the magnitude of the task involved in carrying out original decision-making and supervising and regulating the exercise of delegated authority under the taxation laws, is a powerful indicator that appropriate guidelines are essential for the avoidance of administrative chaos and for the achievement of reasonable consistency. The need to maintain the perception and reality of equal treatment is an important factor in the administration of all laws and not least in those relating to taxation. But such considerations go to the weight to be attributed to policy and that is ultimately a matter for the Tribunal: Nevistic v Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs, supra, at 647 (Deane J); Re Aston, supra, at AAR 78 (Davies J). ”
  1. Having considered the submissions, it is my view that this third interpretation is the appropriate view to adopt of the intention and application of section 47(2). There is significant logic in the view that the exemption applies where the child care facility is located on premises used for business purposes other than the provision of child care. I do not read the section as applying to premises which may be described a premises used for business operations only because of their use as a child care facility. It is reasonable to believe that the intention of Parliament was to make the provision of employee child care an assessable fringe benefit except where the facility is located on the normal business premises of the employer. Thus the exemption is not intended to apply where the child care is provided in separate premises used solely for child care. The use of the words in section 136 “...used in whole or in part, for the purposes of business operations” support this view. If the provision of child care is excluded from “business operations” then it is appropriate to refer to premises used, in part, for business operations thus maintaining the premises as being “business premises” after the location thereon of a child care facility. In arriving at this view, I am not seeking to argue that the provision of child care for employees is not for the purposes of an employer’s business operations, but that the premises must be business premises other than those which may become business premises solely as a result of containing a child care facility.
  2. It is accepted that this interpretation excludes an “offsite” child care centre from the exemption, contrary to the respondent’s view expressed in TR96/27. While some may see this as an anomaly, the interpretation which I have adopted is, in my view, a clearer and more logical interpretation of the words used by Parliament and much less anomalous than the potential results of the previous two interpretations espoused by the parties at the hearing. In my view if any further words are to be read into the existing words of the section they are to be added after the words “business premises” in section 47(2) as “...other than premises which may become business premises solely as result of the location of a child care facility thereon”. While being conscious of the admonition by French J that the Tribunal should consider the need for consistency in maintaining appropriate guidelines, I am of the view that the respondent was incorrect in his interpretation of section 47(2) in TR96/27. The requirement of exclusive possession appears to exclude a number of situations where premises may be appropriately described as business premises of the employer. This decision may lead the respondent to reconsider his public ruling. However, as criticised by the applicant, the respondent altered his previously held view when that ruling was issued in December 1996.
  3. I accept also the view of the applicant that there may well be many situations in which the standards and requirements of local legislation may make it impossible or impractical to locate a child care facility on existing premises of the employer. However, these requirements may make it equally impractical to establish the facility in other premises. It may well not be possible to locate suitable premises readily accessible to employees. It may not be practical or possible for an employer to join with other employers, as in this case, to justify a viable child care facility. It may not be possible or practical to obtain exclusive possession of suitable premises. I am unable to see that the intention of the exemption in section 47(2) was that it be readily available to all employers in whatever situation was possible and practical based on the quality or type of business premises. In my view the interpretation of section 47(2) which I have adopted does not frustrate the purpose and object of the exemption provided by that section. Read within the context of the Act, I am of the opinion that the intention of section 47(2) is to provide a limited exemption in specific situations from the general expectation that benefits provided by way of child care for children of employees will be subject to fringe benefits tax.
  4. I agree with the submission of the respondent that section 74A provides no assistance to the applicant in this case. In both my view of the law and that conveyed in TR96/27, Esso is not entitled to the exemption from tax albeit for different reasons. Consequently, the amount of fringe benefits tax does not exceed what it would have been if the law applied in the ruled way.
  5. It follows from my findings that the decision under review should be affirmed.
