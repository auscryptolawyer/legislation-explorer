    (1) Your assessable income includes income according to ordinary concepts, which is called ordinary income.

    (2) If you are an Australian resident, your assessable income includes the *ordinary income you *derived directly or indirectly from all sources, whether in or out of Australia, during the income year.

  3. Section 167 of the ITAA36 provides:
    Default assessment

    If: (a) any person makes default in furnishing a return; or

    (b) the Commissioner is not satisfied with the return furnished by any person; or

    (c) the Commissioner has reason to believe that any person who has not furnished a return has derived taxable income; the Commissioner may make an assessment of the amount upon which in his or her judgment income tax ought to be levied, and that amount shall be the taxable income of that person for the purpose of section 166.

    Reliance by Commissioner on returns and statements

    (1) Where a return of income of a taxpayer of a year of income is furnished to the Commissioner (whether or not by the taxpayer), the Commissioner may, for the purposes of making an assessment in relation to the taxpayer under this Act, accept, either in whole or in part, a statement in the return of the assessable income derived by the taxpayer and of any allowable deductions or rebates to which it is claimed that the taxpayer is entitled and any other statement in the return or otherwise made by or on behalf of the taxpayer.

The Onus of Proof

  1. Section 14ZZK of the TAA 53 provides:
    Grounds of objection and burden of proof

    On an application for review of a reviewable objection decision:

    (a) the applicant is, unless the Tribunal orders otherwise, limited to the grounds stated in the taxation objection to which the decision relates; and

    (b)the applicant has the burden of proving:

    (i) if the taxation decision concerned is an assessment—that the assessment is excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessment should have been; or

    (ii) in any other case—that the taxation decision concerned should not have been made or should have been made differently.

  2. In order to discharge the onus of proof, the Applicant must positively prove their actual taxable income and in doing so, show that the amount of money for which tax is levied by the assessment exceeds the actual liability. [9] The Full Court recently reinforced this accepted position in McPartland v Commissioner of Taxation[10]
    A taxpayer’s burden in challenging an assessment made pursuant to s 167 is to establish on the balance of probabilities their “actual taxable income” and, in so doing, show that the amount of money for which tax was levied exceeded their actual substantive liability
  3. The rationale for the onus is that the facts relating to a taxpayer’s taxable income, are within the taxpayer’s knowledge and they must be taken to know what their income is and how it was derived.[11] In this context, there is no inherent unfairness in requiring a taxpayer, to bear the onus of establishing their true taxable income for each relevant income year.[12]
  4. The taxpayer bears the onus on all issues, save where the Commissioner and taxpayer agree to confine the issues to a particular point of law or fact, in which case the taxpayer bears the onus only in respect of the disputed matters.[13]
  5. There is no particular method to be adopted by taxpayer in order to discharge their burden. The method will vary according to the circumstances of the case.[14] In Commissioner of Taxation v Liang[15] the Full Court considered the onus, both in practical and legal terms, when a taxpayer was assessed on deposits of an unknown origin into a property trust. In that case, the Full Court said:
    ...it was for the taxpayers to disprove the basis of that assessment by establishing the relevant facts. It was not for the Commissioner to posit or prove a basis from which it might be inferred that the Deposits were income but for the taxpayers to place the relevant facts before the Tribunal.
  6. In the circumstances of this case, the Amended Assessments were made under s167 of the ITAA 36, which allows the Commissioner to make an assessment of the amount on which, in his judgement, income tax ought to be levied. That amount becomes the taxpayer’s taxable income for the purpose of s 166.
  7. Section 167 is used by the Commissioner when a person defaults on lodging a return, the Commissioner is not satisfied with a return lodged or he has reason to believe a person who has not lodged a return has derived taxable income. Assessments issued under s 167 are referred to as ‘default assessments’.
  8. The power contained within s 167 is necessarily different to that contained within s 166 which involves a process of calculating the taxable income on the assessable income minus deductions which are able to be determined by the Commissioner. A default assessment, however, is an estimation of the taxpayer’s income when the Commissioner is unable to make an accurate assessment in accordance with s 166 of the ITAA 36. The Courts have accepted default assessments are a guess and, can be inaccurate in fact.[16]
  9. In the case of Commissioner of Taxation v Ross[17] the Court made a number of observations as to the general principles established from the authorities in respect of onus of proof in the context of challenging a default assessment. Those principles can be summarised as follows:
    • Taxpayers burden: it is plainly the taxpayers burden of proving that an assessment is excessive and what their correct assessment should be. The taxpayer cannot shift the burden of proof to the Commissioner by merely challenging the assessments accuracy that must actively demonstrate the correct taxable income.[18]
    • Insufficiency of identifying errors: merely identifying errors in the Commissioner’s methodology or demonstrating that certain amounts included in the asset betterment statement are not assessable income is insufficient. The taxpayer must provide evidence establishing their actual income. This involves more than just disputing specific items it requires presenting a coherent and substantiated picture of the income for the relevant periods. [19]
    • Requirement to explain unexplained wealth: in cases involving default assessments based on the asset betterment method, taxpayers must satisfactorily explain the source of any unexplained wealth and demonstrate that these sources are not taxable. Providing explanations for some transactions or assets without accounting for all unexplained wealth does not satisfy the burden of proof Failure to do so means the onus of proof remains unmet. [20]
    • Asset betterment method validity: the asset better method, is a legitimate approach for the Commissioner to estimate taxable income when records are inadequate. The taxpayer must counter this with substantive evidence.[21]
    • Requirement of full disclosure: full entry disclosure of all material facts is essential emissions are in accuracies in the taxpayers records can undermine their position
    • Importance of contemporaneous records: maintaining accurate and contemporaneous financial records is crucial the absence of such records can significantly hinder the taxpayers ability to discharge the burden of proof.[22]
  10. These principles underscore the importance for taxpayers to maintain thorough and accurate financial records especially when challenging assessments based on indirect methods like asset betterment.
  11. The standard of proof that the taxpayer must meet is the civil standard, being the balance of probabilities. The decision maker, may in evaluating the evidence as a whole, be cautious of a taxpayer’s self -serving statements.[23] This does not necessarily mean that a self -serving statement should not be believed or accepted, but some other corroborative evidence would normally be required.[24]

Evasion

  1. The term evasion is not legislatively defined. It is best expressed by Dixon J in Denver Chemical Manufacturing v. Commissioner of Taxation[25]:
    I think it is unwise to attempt to define the word ‘evasion’. The context of s210(2) shows that it means more than avoid and also more than a mere withholding of information of the mere furnishing of misleading information. It is probably safe to say that some blameworthy act or omission on the part of the taxpayer or those for who he is responsible is contemplated. An intention to withhold information lest the Commissioner should concede the taxpayer liable to a great extent that the taxpayer is prepared to concede, is conduct which if the result is to avoid tax would justify finding evasion.
  2. Evasion falls between an innocent mistake and fraud, and involves some blameworthy act or omission. Whether a taxpayer’s acts (including omissions) can be said to be blameworthy is an objective test based on what a reasonable person would do in any given situation.
  3. The mere fact of a shortfall is not enough to establish evasion. Reasons provided by the taxpayer and/or adviser in connection with the acts or omissions that gave rise to the tax shortfalls are relevant but not determinative. If there is evidence of the taxpayer having acted reasonably and honestly such action could not be considered as blameworthy. This is to be contrasted with situations of intentional omission.[26]
  4. Blameworthy conduct may also include an abdication of personal responsibility or failure to meaningfully engage with a taxpayers taxation affairs.[27] Further the power is not limited it circumstances where the evasion is by the tax payer personally, but extends to where it is also attributable to a tax agent.[28]
  5. On review, the Tribunal’s task is not to consider whether an opinion had been formed in accordance with s 170(1), but rather whether there was, in fact, evasion on the part of the tax payer. The onus of proof imposed by s 14ZZK places on the taxpayer the burden of disproving fraud or evasion.
  6. In the matter of Binetter v Federal Commissioner of Taxation, Perram and Davies JJ observed that[29]:
    Although the Tribunal re-examines whether, on the evidence before it, there was an avoidance of tax due to fraud or evasion, and is able to substitute its opinion for that of the Commissioner, the issue for the Tribunal is whether the taxpayer has discharge the onus of showing that the opinion of fraud of evasion should not have been formed, and therefore, the statutory condition for the power to amend is not satisfied.
  7. A failure by the Commissioner to have formed an opinion that there was fraud or evasion prior to, or at the time of, amending an assessment does not invalidate that assessment. It does, however go to whether the amended assessment is excessive because the statutory condition to the power is not exercised.
  8. In circumstances where the amended assessment is valid, the Commissioner can form an opinion on fraud or evasion at the objection stage. Subsection 169A(3) of the ITAA 1936 will apply to deem the opinion to have been made when the amended assessment was made. This results in the amended assessment being treated as appropriately authorised in terms of the requirement for the Commissioner to form an opinion on fraud or evasion.
  9. There is no court authority as to whether the Tribunal, standing in the shoes of the Commissioner, can also rely upon s 169A(3).

CONSIDERATION OF ISSUES

The operation of section 169A(3)

  1. This existence of the evasion opinion operates as a statutory precondition to the exercise of the power to amend an assessment outside the ordinary amendment period of review. While the Applicant was informed during the audit process that a finding of evasion was under consideration, the opinion itself was formed by the Commissioner until after the Amended Assessments were issued.
  2. The Commissioner says the Tribunal is entitled to rely on s 169A(3) of the ITAA 36 to form the opinion that there had been evasion within the meaning of item 5 of the table in s 170(1) of the ITAA for the first time in conducting a review in proceedings brought under Part IVC of the TAA 53. The Applicant disagrees. The issue is of some significance as only the 2017 and 2018 Amended Assessments fell within the period of review.
  3. Each of the parties made extensive oral and written submissions in respect of this issue, which included an in depth analysis of the legislative history, extrinsic materials and limited authority on the application of s169A(3). The Tribunal acknowledges and commends the parties for the quality and detail of the submissions which were of great assistance in considering this issue.
  4. Neither party (or the Tribunal) was able to identity an authority which has considered whether s 169A(3) applies in the case of an evasion opinion. The authorities relied upon and considered by the Tribunal primarily concern the application of Part IVA and determinations pursuant to s177F of the ITAA 36.
  5. The Applicant encouraged the Tribunal to adopt a narrow construction of s169A(3). Although the Applicant acknowledged the apparently broad text of the provision, this is an example of when general or broad language needs to be down to meet context. They submitted that having regard to the policy and legislative history, statutory purpose and judicial commentary on the section, a distinction is drawn between the operation of section 169A(3) for the purposes of calculating a liability to tax, and the time limits that inform the period of review that would otherwise prevent the Commissioner from amending an assessment.
  6. The Applicant says this distinction is significant because the opinion formed for the purposes of s 170(1) of the ITAA 36 Act, is not relevant to the calculation of taxable income but to whether or not the Commissioner is prevented from amending an assessment because of the period of review.
  7. That is to say, the reference to ‘determination,’ ‘opinion,’ or ‘judgment’ in section 169A(3), requires that the relevant opinion be one that concerns the calculation of taxable income as relevant to determining the tax liability of a taxpayer. Accordingly, the Tribunal cannot form for the first time an opinion that there has been fraud or evasion. All the Tribunal can do is decide whether or not an opinion that has been formed should have been formed. That requires that the Commissioner to have formed an opinion that there has been fraud or evasion in the first place.
  8. The Respondent says the Tribunal should adopt a broad construction s169A(3) of the ITAA. It is uncontroversial that the Tribunal’s task is not evaluate whether the Commissioner held the requisite opinion, but rather to form its own opinion as whether there had been fraud or evasion. Section 169A(3) is a deeming provision, that enables the Commissioner to form an opinion as to evasion at the objection stage, and in turn, the Tribunal standing in the shoes of the Commissioner, on review. The opinion having been formed by the Tribunal is taken to have operated at the time when the relevant Amended Assessments were made.
  9. Further the Respondent says s169A(3) is engaged in circumstances where the task of the Tribunal is to determine whether the Amended Assessment is correct. In determining whether the amounts upon which the applicant has been assessed are correct, the Tribunal is also determining the necessary statutory preconditions for the assessment being issued. This is necessary to the determination of the correctness of the assessment because if it had not been for the evasion, the assessment could not have been revised upwards. The words of the statutory text, legislative history and the case law are consistent with a broad interpretation.
  10. Statutory construction must begin with the text itself. The language employed in the text of the legislation is the surest guide to the legislative intention.[32] The section relevantly provides that:
    in determining whether an assessment is correct, any determination, opinion or judgement of the commissioner made, held or formed in connection with the consideration of an objection against the assessment shall be deemed to have been made, held or formed when the assessment was made.
  11. The provision is engaged whenever the Commissioner is required to determine whether an assessment is correct. The language deployed does not explicitly distinguish between the particulars of an assessment (such as the amount of the applicants taxable income) from the existence or satisfaction of any statutory conditions for the issuing of an assessment that go to excessiveness (such as the formation of an evasion opinion). It also does not explicitly distinguish between determinations, opinions or judgments that can be made by the Commissioner. Further, there is nothing in the Explanatory Memorandum to the Taxation Laws Amendment Act 1986 (Cth) (EM) which suggests that the reference to opinion excludes an opinion on fraud or evasion. I also acknowledge the language of the provision and the EM do not exclude the Applicant’s construction either.
  12. In Commissioner of Taxation v Addy [2022][33] the Court found the Commissioner could rely on 169A(3) to form the state of satisfaction concerning whether a person’s usual place of abode was outside of Australia, and therefore not a resident for tax purposes. Residence is relevant to the calculation of the person’s assessable income. This was the case notwithstanding s169A does not specifically refer to a state of satisfaction, but rather determination, opinion or judgment.
  13. In Commissioner of Taxation v BHP Billiton Finance[34] , one of the issues raised was whether the Commissioner was entitled to rely upon section 169A(3) of the 1936 Act, in making its determination under section 177F of the 1936 Act. In that case, an assessment had issued that did not give effect to a determination under 177F. The Commissioner then subsequently made the determination in the course of considering the taxpayer’s objection but instead of issuing the amended assessment, the Commissioner relied upon 169a(3). The Full Court in that case rejected a narrow construction of the section.
  14. While the authorities support a broad interpretation and application of s 196A(3), those decision were made with a key difference in the factual matrix. The Commissioner had formed the requisite opinion, determination or judgment on objection. That is not the case here.
  15. The arguments advanced by each party in this matter are finally balanced. However, I prefer the construction advanced by the Commissioner. I am not persuaded that the plain meaning of the words in s169A(3), which explicitly refers to any determination, opinion or judgment, should be read down to exclude the formation of an evasion opinion under s 170(1) of the ITAA, I am also not persuaded that it should only apply to determinations, opinions or judgments which are relevant to the particulars of as assessment, especially in light of the legislative history and recent authority.
  16. I am satisfied the Commissioner can rely upon section 169A(3) of the ITAA 36 Act to deem the opinion which was formed by the Commissioner as operating at the time when the relevant assessments were made. The Tribunal, standing in the shoes of the Commissioner, has the powers and functions of the Commissioner as at the objection stage. Accordingly, the Commissioner is entitled to rely upon 169A(3) at the objection stage, so too is the tribunal.
  17. Noting there is a valid assessment, if the Tribunal was precluded from forming an opinion on evasion for the first time at review in this case, then the Applicant would succeed not because they have established the Amended Assessments are incorrect or excessive because was no evasion, but merely because the Commissioner had failed to form the necessary state of mind at the time of the Amended Assessment.

Are the Amended Assessments Excessive?

  1. The Applicant submitted in respect of the Amended Assessments that:
    • although the Applicant’s evidence was imprecise, this was not surprising given he was being asked to recall events going back as far as 2006.
    • he tried his best to give honest answers and he should not be criticised for attempting to recreate events from the witness box.
    • on occasion disagreed with propositions that were put to him but importantly offered an alternative explanation and did not engage in self serving reconstruction of events.
    • he is not sophisticated in financial or accounting matters and that he relied heavily on others to assist him. Although he is the director of a number of entities, he delegated his director responsibilities to others whom he relied on to assist competently.
    • the Applicants position in respect to his relationship and interactions with Mr N has been consistent. He saw an opportunity to build a relationship with Mr N and to increase business connections both in Australia and overseas. Unfortunately. he was used by Mr N for his own personal purposes;
    • there could be no blameworthy act because the Applicant failed to return amounts in his tax return that he knew belonged to someone else and he otherwise relied on professional advice.
  2. The Applicant’s evidence including in cross examination, demonstrated a consistent pattern of limited record keeping and reliance on oral instructions or third parties to manage financial matters.
  3. The Applicant accepted that, in many instances, he did not maintain documentary records of transactions and instead relied on memory or informal recording methods (for example recording information on a mobile phone which was no longer available). The Tribunal therefore did not have the benefit of formal business records or documents that would ordinarily exist in a business environment, such as loans, salary records, emails, notes, receipts or correspondence.
  4. The absence of contemporaneous documents was particularly significant given the volume of cash transactions, and that many of those transactions did not involve Mr N and therefore were not explained by that relationship.
  5. This, in turn, placed increased significance on the oral evidence of the witnesses and in particular the Applicant. In considering the Applicant’s evidence as a whole, I found the Applicant’s evidence difficult to accept without corroboration.
  6. The Applicant was frequently unable to recall transactions, financial matters or relationships. As an example, he was unable to recall whether written loan agreements existed, the terms of loans, whether interest was payable, details of repayment, the purpose of numerous transactions or deposits across the relevant years, the name of his bookkeeper or the process in which his returns and other financial documents were prepared by Mr Lahoud.
  7. There was ample evidence of the Applicant receiving payments from his related entities which were described in the bank records as pay or wages. Not only is the terminology consistent with payments being for wages, the transactions were also received on a recurrent basis in repeated increments for similar amounts of money. The assertion that these reflected loan arrangements was unsupported by documentary evidence such as loan agreements or reliable financial records.
  8. There was also evidence of significant personal expenses in the accounts of the Applicant’s related entities with no explanation of how various expenses were incurred in gaining or produce assessable income.
  9. I place little weight on the Applicant’s evidence that he maintained informal records from his mobile phone. That explanation was not raised prior to the hearing and no phone records were produced in evidence. In circumstances where such records would have been directly relevant to the issues in dispute, the absence of those records significantly undermines the reliability of that explanation.
  10. In a number of instances, when confronted with adverse documentary material, the Applicant did not provide clear explanations but instead offered speculative possibilities for the circumstances of a transaction. In one instance, the Applicant sought to explain expenses incurred at theme parks on the Gold Coast as a result of a lost or overdrawn personal credit card. In other instance he sought to explain a payment to a private school attended by his children a donation. Speculative explanations are not a substitute for evidence.
  11. There was an unexplained inconsistency between the Applicant’s reported taxable income in each year and his demonstrated financial capacity during the same period. The Applicant reported a very low taxable income in a number of years but in the same period he:
    • advanced substantial funds into business ventures – as an example, the Applicant gave evidence that he advanced approximately $300,000 to RA Pty at a time when he reported very low taxable income and had previously lived at home and not large tax returns for earlier years. The Applicant did not provide credible explanation as to the source of those funds.
    • acquired real property - in loan documentation before the Tribunal the Applicant stated an income position significantly higher than the income reported to the Commissioner. Further, the bank statements indicate the loans was not only paid down but discharged in its entirety. This is inconsistent with the submission he was reliant on equity to meet expenses.
    • purchased other assets - the Applicant purchased shares and motor vehicles, for amounts higher than or disproportionate to the taxable income reported.
    • travelled overseas extensively – especially in the 2011 and 2012 income year

The Applicant did not provide satisfactory evidence explaining the source of the funds enabling those activities. I find this is because the Applicant had a higher income than that disclosed.

  1. The Applicant's evidence was that funds received from Mr N were held for Mr N and used for gambling and related expenditure while Mr N was in Australia. The Applicant asserted that he held those funds as constructive trustee.
  2. I do not accept the Applicant’s evidence that the funds deposited into accounts within his control were held on behalf of Mr N or otherwise not his income. This conclusion is based on a combination of the objective documentary evidence, the limited corroboration of the alleged arrangement and the improbability when assessed against the surrounding circumstances.
  3. The Applicant’s explanation requires acceptance that very substantial funds were entrusted to him by person with whom he had only recently formed a relationship, without any formal documentation or clear structure. It also requires acceptance that the Applicant was also prepared to advance substantial funds relative to his declared taxable income to someone with who he had no established relationship.
  4. The Applicant accepted that part of his relationship with Mr N involved developing overseas business opportunities and that he held directorships in overseas entities connected with those activities. That evidence is inconsistent with the characterization of the relationship as purely personal based arrangement, and supports the inference that funds transferred were connected to those business activities.
  5. Further, the transfers were not made directly by Mr N but entities associated to Mr N and the Applicant. This is inconsistent with the characterization of the funds as personal funds provided by Mr N for gambling or personal expenditure.
  6. In addition, there was evidence that gambling activity occurred using funds said to belong to Mr N during periods when Mr N was not in Australia. This is inconsistent with the Applicant's evidence that he only gambles at Mr N’s discretion or benefit while present.
  7. Finally, the Applicant accepted that his accountant advised him to cease accepting deposits from Mr N. Despite that advice, the deposits continued. This is inconsistent with the Applicant's evidence that he believed he was merely holding funds on behalf of another person and supports an inference that the Applicant considered himself entitled to continue receiving the funds.
  8. There was no contemporaneous documentation of any trust relationship or other corroborating evidence notwithstanding the scale and frequency of the transfers. In light of those matters, I consider the more probable explanation is that the funds were connected with the Applicants business interests and represented business related income rather than funds held on behalf of Mr N.
  9. At an earlier stage in the compliance activity the Commissioner was told there were hundreds of text messages between the Applicant and Mr N. However, only a small number of messages were produced, primarily to demonstrate that the Applicant was attempting to have Mr N acknowledge ownership of the funds.
  10. On their face, those messages do not demonstrate that M N accepted ownership of the funds or acknowledged any trust or agency arrangement. In circumstances where a large volume of communications were said to exist, the limited and selective production of messages reduces the weight that can be placed on the communications that were produced.
  11. I place limited weight on the document said to have been prepared by a lawyer which was produced late in the proceedings despite its apparent significance. There was limited evidence as to its provenance and its structure did not did not reflect a formal legal document recording a trust or agency arrangement. I do not consider it provides reliable corroboration of the Applicants explanation.
  12. The evidence concerning the Hummer was also unclear, including the timing and nature of the payments. The lack of clarity is consistent with the broader absence of reliable documentation concerning financial dealings said to involve Mr N.
  13. The Applicant’s original income tax returns records very low taxable income in years where the objective evidence demonstrates access to, and use of, substantially greater financial resources. I do not accept that a reasonable taxpayer in the Applicant’s position could have believed that the returns accurately reflected his income.
  14. The Tribunal does not accept the Applicant’s characterisation of himself as naive, financially unsophisticated, or wholly reliant on others. That characterisation is inconsistent with the evidence that over a sustained period the Applicant operated multiple businesses, engaged in complex commercial activities such as importing and exporting goods, and had the benefit of professional advice as to his taxation obligations, including his duties as a director.
  15. The Tribunal also considers that the Applicant’s knowledge and financial sophistication and acumen would reasonably have increased over time through his business activities and exposure to professional advisors. Further, there is no evidence that any language or comprehension difficulty impeded his ability to understand taxation matters.
  16. It is also significant that there is evidence the Applicant was prepared to provide false or leading misleading information in circumstances where he perceived it to be advantageous to do so. This includes misleading Mr N as to compliance activity and, to the extent his evidence is that income was not concealed, providing information in loan applications which was not accurate. This conduct further undermines the reliability of the Applicant's evidence.
  17. I do not consider the deficiencies in the Applicants reporting to be isolated errors. The evidence demonstrates a pattern of significant financial activity which was not reflected in the Applicant's reported income over multiple years. I do not accept those in accuracies arose solely from innocent mistake or oversight.
  18. I also place significant weight on evidence from Mr Lahoud that the Applicant did not provide him with material financial information concerning overseas business interests, his personal bank statements or reliable evidence as to loans. I consider it more probable than not that this information was knowingly withheld.
  19. I also place weight on the evidence that the Applicant continued to receive deposits after being given explicit advice those transactions were likely to be assessed as income. The Applicant was knowingly (from 2011 onwards) in receipt of income which he understood would be characterised as such.
  20. Taking these matters together, I have formed the view there was an avoidance of tax due to evasion in each of the 20006 to 2016 income years. Further I am satisfied the omission of income was the result of blameworthy conduct.
  21. The Applicant’s case suffered from multiple key difficulties, including:
    • extensive unexplained cash and cheque transactions;
    • evidence of recurrent payments described as pay or wages from related entities;
    • personal expenditure incurred by related entities; and
    • an unsatisfactory explanation of the relationship Mr N and the associated bank deposits and withdrawals and the app asset acquisitions were inconsistent with the level of taxable income disclosed.
  22. Overall, the Applicant’s evidence and recollection were insufficient to fill evidentiary gaps in the documentary evidence such that he was able to establish the Amended Assessments were excessive and what they should have been. Therefore, I am not persuaded the Amended Assessments are excessive in light of :
    • the inadequate condition of the record keeping and documentary evidence;
    • the unreliability of the Applicant’s own evidence,
    • the failure to lead evidence in chief capable of discharging the Applicant’s onus,
    • the failure to put on evidence from witnesses with actual knowledge of the transactions undertaken and the accounts which could have corroborated the Applicant’s evidence; and
    • the omission of income was as result of blameworthy conduct

The Penalty Assessments

  1. A taxpayer is liable to administrative penalty where a statement is made to the Commissioner that is false and leading misleading in a material particular. Where a shortfall arises from intentional disregard of a taxation law, the base penalty amount is 75% of the shortfall amount, with a lower based penalties applying for recklessness or a failure to take reasonable care.
  2. Intentional disregard means that something more than reckless disregard of or indifference to a taxation law. Intentional disregard of the law can be inferred from the facts and surrounding circumstances.[35]
  3. Reliance on professional advice may support remission only where the taxpayer provides all relevant information and follows that advice. Safe harbour does not apply when the agent did not receive all relevant taxation information.
  4. Mr Lahoud was the tax agent for only some of the relevant income years. Regardless, the the Applicant did not fully disclose his affairs to Mr Lahoud including directorships and business activities, personal bank account statements and the full extent of business transactions and loan arrangements. Mr Lahoud substantially relied on what the Applicant told him and he did not review bank statements or independently verify loan balances.
  5. Mr Lahoud’s evidence also confirmed that the Applicant’s income tax returns were only submitted after the Applicant agreed they were correct and signed. He also said that he had given general advice to the Applicant about his obligations as a company director. Mr Lahoud told the Tribunal the Applicant went against his advice to stop the payments from Mr N.
  6. I find that safeharbor does not apply in this case because Mr Lahoud was not a registered tax agent for some of the years, he was not provided all relevant information by the Applicant and the Applicant failed to follow his advice.
  7. Further, I am satisfied the for the reasons traversed in paragraphs 165-172 of these reasons that there was intentional disregard of a taxation law and the Penalty Assessments were correctly imposed at the BPA of 75%.
  8. There is Federal Court authority[36], that the uplift contained in s 284-20 of Schedule 1 of the TAA 53 is imposed automatically and is not a matter for the exercise of discretion by the Commissioner. This is the case even where a series of penalty assessments, each involving a base penalty amount worked out using the relevant items in s 284-90 are made contemporaneously. The Tribunal has no power to set aside the imposition of the uplift of the base penalty amount.
  9. The Commissioner has a discretionary power to remit a penalty, including for a false or misleading statement. Whilst the discretion is framed without limitation, the exercise of that discretion is guided by Practice Statement Law Administration 2012/5 ‘Administration of the false or misleading statement penalty – where there is a shortfall amount’ (PSLA 2012/5).
  10. Consistent with the principles in PSLA 2012/ 5 remission in full or in part of a shortfall penalty arising from a false or misleading statement will generally only occur where the result is unjust having regard to all the circumstances.[37] There was no evidence before the Tribunal to suggest this was the case.

The SIC

  1. Remission of SIC is limited and generally requires satisfaction of the statutory thresholds, including where the SIC exceeds 20% of the additional tax.[38] Annexure A of these reasons sets out the percentage of the sum of tax shortfall and penalty. Only the 2013 to 2018 income years are reviewable by the Tribunal.
  2. Without repeating the matters traversed in the proceeding paragraphs, the Applicant has failed to discharge his onus of establishing grounds of remission. This is consistent with Practice Law Administration Statement 2006/8[39] which provides that remission is not appropriate in circumstances of fraud or evasion, which is the case here.

DECISION

  1. For the reasons outlined, the Tribunal is satisfied on the balances of probabilities that:
    • the Applicant has engaged in blameworthy conduct in each of the income years consistent with evasion and that it is open to the Tribunal to form that opinion for the first time on review;
    • the Applicant has failed to discharge his onus of proof in establishing the Amended Assessments and the Penalty Assessments are excessive or incorrect, and in the case of the Penalty Assessment, should otherwise be remitted; and
    • the Applicant has failed to discharge his onus of proof that the SIC should be remitted in full
  2. Accordingly, the objection decision of the Respondent is affirmed.
