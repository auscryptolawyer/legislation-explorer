Re Mt Newman Mining Co Pty Ltd; Amax Iron Ore Corporation; Pilbara Iron Ltd; Seltrust Mining Corporation Pty Ltd and Mitsui-C Iron Pty Ltd and Collector of Customs [1985] AATA 346 (9 December 1985)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: MT. NEWMAN MINING CO. PTY. LTD.; AMAX IRON ORE CORPORATION; PILBARA IRON

LTD.; SELTRUST MINING CORPORATION PTY. LTD. and MITSUI-C. IRON PTY. LTD.

And: COLLECTOR OF CUSTOMS

No. W85/99

Customs and Excise

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

The Hon. G.D. Clarkson Q.C. (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Customs and Excise - rebate on diesel fuel - purchased for use in mining operations - fuel purchased to generate electricity for town adjacent to mining site - whether for use in an operation connected with mining for minerals - need for sufficient connection

Diesel Fuel Taxes Legislation Amendment Act 1982

Customs Act 1901

HEARING

PERTH

9:12:1985

ORDER

The decision of the Tribunal is to set aside the decision under review and to remit the matter to the respondent to determine in accordance with the reasons of the Tribunal.

DECISION

The first applicant manages, on behalf of the joint venturers the other applicants, the iron ore mining operations carried on at Mount Whaleback in the Pilbara region of Western Australia.

In the course of their operations the applicants use diesel fuel to generate electricity for a variety of purposes including the supply of electricity to the nearby town of Newman.

Section 78A of the Excise Act provides for the payment by the Collector of a rebate of excise duty to a person who purchases diesel fuel on which duty has been paid for use by that person in mining operations as defined.

A claim by the first applicant on behalf of the joint venturers for a rebate of duty in respect of fuel purchased for use at the minesite to provide power to the town from 18 August 1982 to 31 March 1985 was refused by the respondent. This application is to review that decision.

A question similar to that arising here was considered recently in relation to the town of Pannawonica, also a mining town in the Pilbara. A joint venture of companies, different from those involved here, mined ore near Pannawonica and railed it to Cape Lambert on the Western Australian coast for crushing and shipping.

The Full Court of the Federal Court concluded that it was open to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal to find, as it had, that the generation of electricity for the township was an operation connected with the mining for minerals, a finding which brought that activity within the definition of "mining operations" as used in s.78A of the Excise Act thus giving rise to an entitlement to rebate of excise duty.

That section provides that "mining operations" is to have the same meaning as it bears in s.164 of the Customs Act, sub-section (7) of which provides:

"'mining operations' means -

(a) exploration, prospecting or mining for

minerals; or

(b) the dressing or beneficiation (at the

mining site or elsewhere) of minerals,

or ores bearing minerals, as an integral

part of operations for their recovery,

and includes -

(c) other operations connected with

exploration, prospecting or mining for

minerals that are carried out in, or at

a place adjacent to, the area in which

the exploration, prospecting or mining

occurs;

(d) where minerals, or ores bearing

minerals, are dressed or beneficiated,

at a place other than the mining site,

as an integral part of operations for

their recovery - the transporting of the

minerals or ores from the mining site to

the place where they are dressed or

beneficiated;

(e) the liquefying of natural gas;

(f) where natural gas is liquefied at a

place other than the mining site - the

transporting of the natural gas from the

mining site to that place; or

(g) the production of common salt by means

of evaporation.

but does not include quarrying operations

carried on for the sole purpose of obtaining

stone for building, road making or similar

purposes."

At the present hearing the applicants did not concede that paragraphs (a)

and (b) as set out above did not apply to them but the dispute in the present review concerns firstly the scope in its context of the phrase "other operations connected with . . . mining for minerals. . . ." in paragraph (c) above.

This conceded that the generation of power was carried out in an area adjacent to the area in which the mining for minerals occurred.

Where, as here, the statute provides its own definition what has been said in other cases should be approached cautiously, but from what was said regarding the same provision by the Federal Court and by the Tribunal in Cliffs Robe River (WAG 110 of 1984) Full Court, Federal Court, 28 March 1985 unreported) and the authorities therein referred to some assistance may be gained.

The phrase "connected with" requires that there be a "sufficient" connection between the "other operations" and the "mining for minerals". The one "having to do with", the other. The phrase suggests a "relation between things, one of which is bound up with or involved in another". Federal Commissioner of Taxtion -v- Broken Hill Pty. Co. Ltd. [1968] HCA 16; (1969) 120 CLR 240 at 277. Re: Nanaimo Community Hotel Ltd. (1944) 4 DLR 638 at 639.

In its present context the connection is one that is more than geographical for that is dealt with later in the paragraph which requires the "connected" operations to be carried out in or at a place adjacent to the area where the mining occurs.

The applicants pointed to the facts which had been accepted as establishing a sufficient connection between the mining and the generating of power for Pannawonica in Cliffs Robe River and to the similarities which arose in the circumstances of the present case. The respondent was prepared to concede that in the early stages of its development Newman may have been in the same sort of relationship with the mine nearby as had been found to exist between Pannawonica and its neighbouring mine, but maintain that in this case by the time the relevant period commenced, Newman had developed to such a stage and size and the joint venturers' responsibilities for it had become so remote or assumed by others that the generation of power for the town's many and diverse needs could no longer be properly described as an operation connected with the joint venturers' mining for minerals.

The issues thus raised led into an examination of the history and development of Newman in which some comparisons with Pannawonica were made.

At this stage the establishment of a beneficiation plant at the mine should

be described.

The Company under the original agreement was bound to continue investigation of the feasibility of a plant for secondary processing and after about ten years operations the beneficiation plant was constructed. Its function can be briefly described. Ore at the mine consists of high grade hematite bands with shale bands in between. "Contact" ore as it is loosely called consists of high grade ore, downgraded by waste or shale and the purpose of the beneficiation plant is to extract the high grade ore from this mixture. In the past few years it has produced about ten percent of the total production.

As operations grew, the town of Newman kept pace. The involvement and

influence of the first applicant as manager for the joint venturers was pervasive. It provided the houses in which all residents of the town lived and all services such as roads, sewerage, water and power, an airport, parks and accommodation for government services, such as police and schools. In effect it owned and operated the town.

This situation was seen by the Company and others to have undesirable effects and steps were taken towards what was described as "normalization" in which a gradual transfer of functions from the Company to a local government authority and to government agencies was planned and put into effect.

Some aspects of this move should be noted.

The original agreement set up a procedure whereby the development of the whole project was controlled. The Company from time to time submitted proposals for future action. When these proposals, sometimes with variations, were approved by the appropriate Minister, both the Company and the State respectively took whatever action was necessary to put the proposals into effect.

In the development of the town emphasis has been placed on the provision of

family housing. Single persons' quarters are not the basis of the housing scheme, as it could have been in the early development of an isolated mining venture.

An examination of the figures set out earlier indicates that there are over 1300 houses or flats occupied by tenants or purchasers who are Company employees. Only about 250 single persons quarters are occupied by employees. The population would therefore have a greater mix involving both sexes and all ages than a development site where little or no accommodation is provided for dependents. This in turn means a greater demand for more diverse facilities and services.

This appears to be confirmed by the list of commercial, government and shire premises to which power is sold. There appear to be between 15 and 20 commercial enterprises including two supermarts, a boutique; a restaurant, a liquor store, a butcher and a fruit and vegetable store.

There are over 20 government and shire services including a hall, oval and aquatic centre, police station, infant health centre, St. John's Ambulance, fire station, kindergarten and schools, hospital and medical centre. Some twenty sporting bodies are listed and one church.

Major contractors who provide direct services to the mine, as for example, a vehicle service company which maintains the 250 light vehicles or cars in the mine fleet, engineering contractors, air conditioning contractor, Telecom workshop and a depot for the company which supplies transport of parts and supplies from Perth to Newman are accommodated in industrial areas.

The Company is the only supplier of electricity to the town. Each employee tenant in a house or flat has a quota of electricity which few exceed. Employees purchasing houses or flats have free power for air conditioning for nine months of the year and pay for other power consumed. Government, shire and commercial occupants of premises pay for power. Sporting organizations are not charged. Any charge for electricity is at current State Energy Commission rates.

The evidence is that the mine is dependent on electric power. The drills used to prepare for blasting, the mechanical shovels which dig the ore and the lights attached to them are all electrically powered as is the distribution of water for dust control. The crushers and the conveyor systems are operated by electric power which is used also for purposes such as lighting of the general administrative areas, air conditioning and workshops.

The extensive metering and control system enables reasonably accurate records to be kept of the power used in each operation of the venture including the town.

It is common ground that approximately 30% of the power generated is used in the town and for the supply of water, approximately one half of which is used at the mine.

How much power is used in the extraction of ore dressing, beneficiation or other operations connected with mining for minerals specified in the definition of mining operations is in dispute, the respondent suggesting 18% and the applicant 70%. It is unnecessary to resolve this question although it should be noted that all the power generated is used for purposes connected with the mining venture, to use a neutral description, and the town.

The Company maintains, and I did not understand it to be contested that the deposit at Mount Whaleback is one of the largest single deposits of hematite ore in the world, and that the present venture produces 32,000,000 tonnes of ore per annum from a total of 80,000,000 to 90,000,000 tonnes of material moved and is capable of producing more than 40,000,000 tonnes for export. The magnitude of this production is such that Port Hedland, the port through which the ore is shipped, is one of only a few ports in the world capable of handling ore carriers up to 225,000 tonnes dead weight and it exports a greater tonnage than any other port in Australia.

For the year to 31 March 1985, the end of the period to which the claim relates, the operating cost of generating the power used for all purposes was $13,965,000 of which $10,399,000 was the cost of diesel fuel.

A convenient starting point in assembling the issues involved in the present review is the Company's letter of 14 May 1985 to the Collector which states:

"We refer to the Federal Court case No. WAG110

of 1984 - Collector of Customs -v- Cliffs

Robe River Iron Associates, which resulted

from an appeal by the Collector of Customs

against a Decision handed down by the

Administrative Appeals Tribunal.

The reasons given in the Decision, which was

in favour of Cliffs Robe River Iron

Associates parallel the situation that exists

at Newman in respect of the generation of

power for the town. We therefore hereby

lodge a claim for rebate of duty paid on

diesel fuel purchased for the period 18

August 1982 to 31 March 1985."

The Collector's reply was in his letter of 20 May 1985, part of which is as

follows:

"Current policy relating to the payment of

rebate under the category of mining requires

the applicant to substantiate that the

operations being claimed for, are connected

with the mining for minerals.

It is considered that power generation for

the township is not sufficiently connected

with the mining operation to allow the

payment of rebate."

This is elaborated in the s.37 statement as follows:

"22. The town is one of considerable size

(approximately 6,000 people) and its

inhabitants engage in a wide range of

occupations and activities including not only

mining of minerals but also beneficiation and

dressing of minerals. Further, a sizeable

proportion of the population is engaged in

activities totally unrelated to mining

operations, for example, retailing, service

industries and tourism. The electricity

supplied to the town is consumed by people in

all of these categories. The responsibility

for the town's amenities and services no

longer rests with the applicant but has

passed to the relevant local authorities. It

was accordingly considered that, in this

case, the provision of power for the town of

Newman was not an operation sufficiently

connected with the mining of minerals in

terms of the definition of "mining

operations" in Section 78A."

The Company's attitude was:

"Under the Agreement we had, and continue to

have, an obligation to the State of Western

Australia to, inter-alia, provide power

supplies to the townsite. In furtherence of

this obligation, the Agreement specifically

makes provision for the Company to generate,

transmit, supply and charge for electricity

in relation to the townsite.

Our mineral leases and rights to mine

pursuant thereto are only secured to the

extent that we fulfil all our obligations

under the Agreement. If we were to cease the

supply of electricity to the townsite, the

State would be entitled to determine our

entitlement to mine. Both operations are

inextricably interwoven. It is not plausible

to suggest that one operation can be carried

on without the other given our obligations

under the Agreement."

At the hearing counsel for the Collector did not challenge the decision in

Cliffs Robe River but sought to distinguish it. He claimed that "mining for minerals" in the relevant definition is restricted in its meaning to the extraction of ore from the ground and that the generation of electricity was neither mining for minerals (para. (a) of the definition) nor was it dressing or beneficiation (para.(b)). It was further argued that applying the test accepted by the Federal Court in Cliffs Robe River the generation of electricity may be an eligible use if sufficiently connected with mining for minerals, but that the generation of electricity for a use connected with dressing or beneficiation is not an eligible use.

Central Norseman Gold Corporation Ltd. W84/118 - 23 August 1985 was also referred to. There the Tribunal considered a similar problem relating to the mining company which mined near Norseman, an isolated town of approximately 1,800 people, situated between Kalgoorlie and Esperance in Western Australia.

Most of the workers resided in Norseman but the Company had 48 houses and 84 single persons quarters on the mining lease.

Its powerhouse at the mine supplied power for these premises.

The Tribunal said that "it was necessary in a business sense to establish residential accommodation on the mining lease so as adequately to house the fluctuating population of workers. . . . The applicant's housing was constructed for the purposes of the mine and the housing was part of the infrastructure of the mine".

In that case the residents of the accommodation were persons associated with the mine, including two policemen, member of the gold stealing detection staff, the manager of the mess and private contractors. The Tribunal held that the Company was entitled to a rebate on the price of diesel fuel purchased for use in providing electricity to the accommodation on the mining lease.

The respondent's argument here is that there were a number of distinctions between Newman on the one hand and on the other a small "company" town like Pannawonica or the minesite accommodation at Norseman.

One such distinction was size. At Pannawonica there were approxiamtely 1,200 people and on the Norseman minesite about 200 or less, but at Newman there are 6,000. There was also a demographic distinction in that a greater proportion of the population of Newman, being engaged in activities not directly related to the mine, Newman had ceased to be a "closed" or "company" town and supported other activities such as tourism.

Further the respondent pointed to the process of normalization to which reference has already been made in which the town begins to achieve its own independence and separate entity, its ties to the mine diminishing as the number of non-company employees grows. There was said to be some 60 organizations at Newman which carried on business and in the process employed people not in any form of mining.

Counsel for the respondent claimed that only about 33% of the population of Newman were Company employees and that even if dependents of employees were included with employees there were still 17% of the town's population not in that classification. It appears that at Pannawonica there were only 10 or 15 people who were not Company employees or dependents and at the Norseman mine site only 3 and some private contractors.

It was also pointed out that some 16,000 to 20,000 people visit the mine in a year and that this indicated a tourist activity of some size.

As to the use of electricity in the town, the facts set out in the decision relating to the Norseman minesite and to Pannawonica strongly suggest that in this case a substantially greater proportion of the total power generated is used in Newman.

The respondent also said that in regard to the transfers of assets and responsibilities in the normalization process, many services and facilities have been handed over to the shire and to government agencies. Freehold titles had been issued to the Company in respect of much of the townsite area and the respondent says it is therefore no longer valid to judge the Company's involvement in the town by reference to the Company's obligations under the 1964 Agreement which had to a large extent been discharged or transferred to others.

It was put that the town was no longer an integral part of the infrastructure of the mine.

Another distinction relied on was that the dressing and beneficiation processes which exist at Newman were not used at Pannawonica. This leads to the argument that crushing is dressing and that therefore only 18% of the power generated is used in mining for minerals while 35% is used in dressing and beneficiation, a non-eligible use, and 30% in the town.

Further it was said that when the mining for minerals constituted such a small part of the total demand for power it is less likely that the generation of power for the town is an operation connected with mining for minerals.

Both the applicant and the respondent referred to the factual situation as the Tribunal had found it in Cliffs Robe River and sought to show similarities and differences respectively in the present case.

The applicant referred to a passage in the decision of the Tribunal as follows:

"The town was built pursuant to the agreement

of 18 November 1964 expressly and solely to

enable mining to be carried out in the Robe

River Valley. No other town was available.

Nor was any other source of power available.

The generation of electricity for the town is

vital to the existence of the town and the

town is vital to the extraction of ore. The

persons who live in the town do so because

they are employees of Cliffs or members of

the families of employees or because, in

those few other cases, they provide services

for the employees and their families. The

town serves no purpose other than to

facilitate the mining of iron ore in the Robe

River Valley. To say, as the Collector

submitted, that electricity is generated for

the comfort and convenience of the residents

of Pannawonica does not deny a sufficient

connection between the purchase of fuel and

the extraction ore."

The applicant said that all of this was apposite in the present case except

the reference to the "few" other cases who were neither employees of the Company nor members of the family of employees.

The respondent said, however, that the important distinction in the present case is one referred to by the Federal Court which pointed out that Pannawonica was not only constructed by the Company but was "operated" solely for the purposes of enabling it to mine minerals and that while the town of Newman was admittedly constructed by the applicant, the Company no longer "operates" the town because of the terms and effect of the normalization agreements.

This points to an essential difference between the parties, the applicant saying that the only significant difference between the factual situation in the two cases is one of scale, while the respondent says that the continuing withdrawal of the Company from the direct management or operation of the town, the size of the town and the variety of interests of its inhabitants make the connection between the town and mine more tenuous as the town develops its own dependence and autonomy.

It is helpful to note the factors which led the Tribunal in Cliffs Robe River to find a sufficient connection and the facts to which the Federal Court referred as supporting its conclusion that it was open to the Tribunal to hold what it did.

The relevant portion of the Tribunal's decision are set out earlier. The Federal Court said:

"(i) the construction of the Town of

Pannawonica

and the supply of electricity to

it was an essential part of the scheme

to establish the mine and was a

condition of the agreement pursuant to

which the mine was established;

(ii) the township forms part of the

infrastructure

of the mine, its sole function

being to provide a place of residence

for the mine workers, there being no

other available;

(iii) the township was constructed and is

operated by the respondent solely for

the purpose of enabling it to mine the

minerals on the adjacent land;

(iv) the township has no alternative source

of electricity and without electricity

it could not provide a place of

residence for the mine workers and the

mine could not operate."

The similarities in the development of Pannawonica and of Newman are marked

but not surprising. Each commenced in the course of an isolated mine being opened up in the Pilbara pursuant to an agreement between the State Government and the operating consortium. But the similarities aside it is desirable to look at the overall picture of Newman alone as disclosed by the evidence in order to assess its function and its relationship to the mine.

The town and the mine exist side by side and constitute an undertaking established in a most isolated and remote situation. The basic reason for development of the undertaking was to win iron ore. This required mining equipment, the power to drive it, and the labour to operate it. Any two without the other would be useless. There was no other possible source of power and there being no other accommodation available the project could not have proceeded unless the Company provided the accommodation for its employees and for those reasonably necessary to support those employees in the marked isolation of the project. Furthermore the accommodation and its environs had to be reasonably congenial, otherwise workers would not come or would not stay.

Consider now the generation of electric power for the mine and the town. As to the mine, the evidence is that it was necessary to use electric power at the mine because of the high consumption of energy by the powerful equipment used. A power house was therefore a basic requirement in the plan for development and operation of the mine.

As to the town, if the people concerned were to be accommodated in the congenial circumstances referred to, electric power was undeniably necessary for domestic purposes and for air conditioning, street lighting, water treatment and a reasonable range of other amenities and recreations. To satisfy this demand from the same source as that of the mine seems an obvious course.

I do not think that the degree and kind of connection which gives rise to eligibility can be measured solely in quantitative terms, such as the size of the town or the proportion of power used in it.

The object of the overall venture was the winning and export of iron ore from the isolation of Mount Whaleback. The location of the town was determined by the location of Mount Whaleback. The size of the town was determined basically by the size of the workforce needed to service the operation being undertaken and that operation was huge.

Throughout the relevant period in the inhospitable area where the operation is, the mine and the town were indispensable parts of the one operation. Each was essential to the other.

Further I do not think that during that period this mutualism was lessened by the number of persons in the town who were not employees.

In the course of establishing and operating the venture the Company chose to encourage employees to have their families with them and provided the additional accommodation, amenities and services required by these additional people and for those others such as school teachers, for instance, whose services met the wider range of interests in the enlarged community.

Because residents must ordinarily find their entertainment and sporting interests in the vicinity of Newman, there is a wide range of such activities but the evidence does not reveal any substantial activity in the town which could be described as not related to the ordinary needs and relaxations of those associated with the mine.

It was suggested that a tourist activity was developing but the evidence did not support this. Each year some 15,000 to 20,000 people undertake a short inspection of the mine which takes about one to one and a half hours. Bearing in mind the size of the undertaking I am not surprised that it attracts visitors who pass through by bus, car, and plane. But these inspection tours are at the mine. There is little accommodation available in the town for visitors and little to see apart from the mine. The mine visits are conducted by an employee of the company who acts as a part-time bus captain.

The respondent points to the fact that more employees appear to be employed on beneficiation than on the extraction of ore and that operations "connected with" beneficiation do not fall within the relevant definition of mining operations.

This does not persuade me that the relationship between the town and the mining for minerals is in some relevant way lessened. I am not satisfied that mining for minerals and operations connected therewith on the one hand and beneficiation on the other hand are mutually exclusive in all circumstances, but further the fundamental process on which the whole operation is based is the extraction of the ore. This operation can be, and was for some years, carried on without there being a beneficiation plant, the process at which - to the extent it does not overlap the process of extraction - cannot be used unless ore has first been extracted.

If prior to the building of the beneficiation plant the generation of power at the mine site for use in the town was sufficiently connected with the mining for minerals at the mine to justify eligibility for rebate for fuel purchased for such use - and it appears clearly that it was - then that eligibility was not lost merely because after some years persons employed at the beneficiation plan and their dependents joined the town because in my assessment the basic reason for the town's creation and continued existence remained substantially the same.

It will be noted that in the definition of "mining operations" the "other operations" referred to in paragraph (c) refer back to the words of paragraph (a) and not also to those in (b). It is suggested that therefore if an operation is connected with an operation in (b) it necessarily cannot be connected with an operation set out in (a).

The structure of the definition suggests that "mining for minerals" would not ordinarily include dressing or beneficiation since "mining" in its ordinary meaning is restricted to the extraction of ore. That "mining" is so restricted was the view taken by the Tribunal in Cliffs Robe River (W84/14 - 2 November 1984) and was not questioned by the Full Court of the Federal Court (WAG 110 of 1984 - 28 March 1985).

For the purpose of this legislation, however, "mining operations" is given a wider meaning by the addition of dressing or beneficiation when it occurs as an integral part of operations for the recovery of minerals. Paragraph (c) then goes on to widen the scope further by including "other operations" - that is operations other than dressing or beneficiation - connected with any of the activities set out in paragraph (a). It may then be said than an operation is not excluded from the definition because it is "connected with" dressing or beneficiation if it can also be fairly described as "connected with" exploration, prospecting or mining for minerals. A connection with beneficiation for instance, does not necessarily exclude it from having a relevant connection with mining for minerals. The word "connection: is after all both wide and imprecise as the Federal Court said in Cliffs Robe River (supra).

As I understand that decision, the Federal Court did not say than an operation "connected with" beneficiation, for instance, could never because of that connection also be "connected with" mining for minerals. What the Court said was that beneficiation itself, being an operation described in a preceding paragraph was not one of the "other operations" referred to in paragraph (c).

However, it is sufficient to say as the Federal Court did that "paragraph (c) widened the scope of "mining operations" by introducing "other operations" which do not answer any of the descriptions used in the preceding paragraphs so long as those operations are connected with one of the operations mentioned in paragraph (a)".

The respondent also relied on the process of normalization which commenced to be applied after the agreement with the East Pilbara Shire in 1981 and continued thereafter with transfers of properties and public services to the Shire and to the State Government, the granting of some freehold titles to the Company and the introduction by the Company of the home ownership scheme for its employees.

The respondent says these developments in which other authorities took over responsibilities in the town and the Company's pervasive influence was lessened led to the town being sufficiently independent of the mine so as to be regarded as no longer operated by it and that the crucial connection is therefore broken.

One can envisage circumstances in which a settlement established to serve a mine and dependent on it gradually attracts other producers and activities which in due course are sufficiently substantial to themselves support a settlement. Indeed, as indicated earlier such a development is contemplated in the 1981 normalization agreement. In such and perhaps other cases the generation at the mine of electricity for the town may lack the appropriate connection with the mining for minerals.

I have come to the conclusion however that in the present case the respondent has underestimated the degree of mutualism to which I have referred and has overestimated the degree of independence which in the relevant period the town attained.

The Company itself is still substantially involved in the town and its activities. As shown earlier, notwithstanding the home ownership scheme it owns most of the housing in the town. It still employs over 70 people in its functions in the town including the administration of all accommodation, catering, building maintenance, servicing of air conditioning equipment and similar activities and as noted earlier, it still has the substantial liabilities of maintaining the three basic services for the town of providing power, water and sewerage.

The transfer of responsibility and title to the hospital, schools and other services to the appropriate State authorities has already been noticed and that of course makes the town less a Company town as that expression is commonly understood.

The local government functions which have been transferred to the East Pilbara Shire are numerous, but the substance of the matter is that instead of providing these facilities and services by its own employees the Company pays for them by paying the special supplementary and transitional rates. The Shire operates in Newman from premises and with equipment which were transferred by the Company to the Shire for a nominal consideration and it is the Company and not the Shire which determines the standard of services to be provided in the town. The Shire is obliged to maintain services at the standard which the Company adopted and now pays for.

It should also be noted that the electric power used in the town is supplied by the Company at a significant financial loss.

If one bears in mind what the Company still provides for the town and the amounts it pays to the Shire it is reasonable to conclude that while the appearances of a closed company town may well have been softened, the cost to the Company of maintaining the town remains high.

But further, the essential link between the town and the mine still remains. It arises from their respective functions in relation to one another. The town provides the living conditions for the mine's workers. The mine provides the material support for the town's residents. These functions and the consequent close relationship are not changed because some of the mining company's responsibilities in the town are taken over by a local government authority.

The continued dependence of the town and the mine on one another is demonstrated by the evidence of Mr. Mackintosh, the mine manager responsible for the overall venture at Mt. Newman who said, "the only reason for any of the operations at Newman is associated directly with the mine".

It is fair to conclude from the evidence that the closure of the mine would see the end of the town of Newman as it now exists. With no employment by the Company except perhaps for a few on maintenance tasks it is difficult to forecast exactly what would survive. If the closure of the mine resulted in the present supplies of electricity and water being discontinued little activity could remain. The exodus of mine workers with their families would be followed by the closing down of many of the services and amenities now provided with a further consequent reduction in population.

It should also be noted finally that for the whole of the relevant period power has been generated to service the town and that apart from the practical necessity of continuing to do so for as long as the Company wishes to continue the mining venture, it is an obligation which the Company undertook as part of its arrangement with the State under which it was permitted to develop and operate the mine.

I conclude in terms of the test propounded by the Federal Court in Cliffs Robe River that the generation of electricity for the township of Newman was so bound up with and involved in the mining for minerals at Mount Whaleback that it was an operation connected with the mining for minerals and that therefore a rebate is payable in respect of diesel fuel purchased during the relevant period for use in supplying power to the town.

This conclusion makes it unnecessary to consider the other matters which were argued and in particular whether any apportionment of fuel purchased would be appropriate. I merely note that the parties appear already to have accepted an apportionment by the exclusion from the fuel purchased of a proportion of it estimated to have been used in diesel road vehicles.

The decision of the Tribunal will be to set aside the decision under review and to remit this matter to the Collector to determine in accordance with the reasons of the Tribunal.
