Claasz and Commissioner for Superannuation [1995] AATA 540 (21 June 1995)

Claasz and Commissioner for Superannuation [1995] AATA 540 (21 June 1995)

Last Updated: 24 September 2008

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



Superannuation - Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme - second late election to preserve superannuation entitlements - whether jurisdiction to recognise more than one late election - whether desirable to extend time for making election a second time - whether extensions of time should be allowed so as to revive first late election which had ceased to have effect.



Superannuation Act 1976 - ss.137(1), (3), 140(2), 157(1)

Acts Interpretation Act 1901 - s.33(1)

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 - s.43



Livingstone v Westminster Corporation [1904] 2 KB 109

Re War Damage Act 1943; Re 56, Denton Road, Twickenham, Middlesex [1952] 2 All ER 799

Rootkin v Kent County Council [1981] 2 All ER 227

Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239

Chalk v Commissioner of Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 19 AAR 450.

Re : MANFRED HANS CLAASZ

REASONS FOR DECISION



21 June 1995 Mr A M Blow (Deputy President)

Mr N J Attwood AO (Member)

Mr H G Julian DSC (Member)



This is an application pursuant to s.154(6) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (“the Act”) for the review of a decision by a delegate of the respondent which was made on 13 December 1993. That decision confirmed an earlier decision by another delegate for the respondent on 27 May 1992. Both decisions relate to a letter written by the applicant on 22 December 1991 whereby he sought to preserve his superannuation benefits under the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme (“CSS”) following his retirement from the Commonwealth Public Service on 21 April 1987.



Under s.137(1) of the Act, a person in the applicant’s position has until 21 days after his or her resignation takes effect to elect to preserve his or her superannuation benefits. Under s.157(1) of the Act, the Commissioner for Superannuation has a discretion to treat a late election as if it had been made in the period allowed. The decision of 27 May 1992, which was confirmed by the decision under review on 13 December 1993, was a decision not so to treat the late election of 22 December 1991.



The applicant was an “eligible employee” for the purposes of the Act from its commencement on 1 July 1976, when he was employed at the Australian National University. On 7 May 1982 he left that University and commenced employment at the Australian National Gallery. He continued to be an “eligible employee”. He resigned from that employment with effect from 21 April 1987 to take up employment with the Snowy Mountains Engineering Corporation (“SMEC”). The terms of his engagement with the SMEC meant that he no longer had continuing “eligible employee” status for the purposes of the Act.



On or about 21 April 1987 the applicant completed, and had someone forward to the respondent’s office, a form whereby he applied for a refund of his accumulated superannuation contributions. In completing that form, he deleted two other options, one of which was an election of preservation of his superannuation rights. On or about 1 June 1987 a refund of contributions amounting to $25,738.33 was accordingly paid.



On 7 July 1987 the applicant wrote to the respondent saying, inter alia, “Would you please consider my late election for deferred benefits with the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme.” He explained that he had joined the SMEC Superannuation Scheme, had found that he was the only member of that Scheme, and was aware of circumstances that could seriously disadvantage him. He requested the respondent to allow him an extension of time to make his application for deferred benefits with the CSS. He wrote “The reason for my extension is to give me time to reconsider my position while I ascertain the real disadvantages of remaining with the SMEC Superannuation Scheme.”



After a delay of over four months, which appears to us to have been inappropriate, a delegate of the respondent directed pursuant to s.157(1) of the Act that the election of 7 July 1987 be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed. The applicant was notified of that decision by letter dated 18 November 1987, wherein he was requested to forward a cheque for $25,738.33 “as soon as possible.” When such a late election is allowed, s.140(2) of the Act requires refunded contributions to be returned. The applicant was also requested by that letter to complete and return a form giving notice of details concerning his employment and superannuation scheme membership as at 21 July 1987, and to return it “as soon as possible”. Notification of such details is required by s.137(3) of the Act.



On 18 January 1988 a delegate of the respondent, pursuant to s.137(3) of the Act, allowed the applicant until 18 February 1988 to give notice of the required details pursuant to that sub-section. The applicant was written to on 19 January 1988, advised of that extension of time, and also told (inter alia) “Failure to repay $25,738.33 immediately will result in your late election lapsing.”



The applicant did not ever refund the sum $25,738.33 or any part thereof to the respondent. He did not ever complete and return the s.137(3) notice, or otherwise comply with that sub-section.



The applicant had no further contact with the respondent‘s office until his letter of 22 December 1991.



It is now contended on behalf of the respondent that s.157(1) of the Act empowers him to treat only one late election as being within the time allowed. If that is correct, then the respondent had no power to treat the second late election of 22 December 1991 as having been made within time. It would follow that this Tribunal has no power to do so either. The powers conferred by s.43 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the AAT Act”) do not exceed those possessed by the respondent.



It is necessary to consider the wording of the relevant provisions of the Act.

Section 137(1), which allows resigning employees to elect to preserve their superannuation benefits, reads as follows:-



“A person who:



(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which paragraph (b) does not apply and, upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or



(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue of which the person is to be deemed, under sub-section 58(3), to have retired involuntarily;



may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner, that this Division apply in relation to the person.”



Section 157(1), which confers the discretion to allow a late election, reads as follows:



”Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognized, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly.”



There is nothing in the Act which expressly prohibits the Commissioner from recognising two or more late elections in respect of the same resignation by an eligible employee. Ordinarily recognition of a single late election would be sufficient for a former employee to arrange preservation of his or her superannuation benefits. A former employee might desire recognition of a second or subsequent late election if and only if the first late election has become ineffective, by reason of the expiry of time limits imposed or extended pursuant to ss.140(2) and 137(3), to which we have referred. It is necessary to consider the wording of those provisions.



Section 140(2), which requires the repayment of any refunded superannuation contributions reads as follows:-



“(2) Where:



(a) a person referred to in subsection 137(1) has been paid:



(i) in a case where the person is a person to whom paragraph (a) of that subsection applies - an amount by way of lump sum benefit to which the person has become entitled under section 80 or Part VIA; or



(ii) in a case where the person is a person to whom paragraph (b) of that subsection applies - an amount or amounts by way of pension or by way of pension and lump sum benefit to which the person has become entitled under section 55 or 59 or Part VIA or an amount by way of lump sum benefit to which the person has become entitled under section 62 or Part VIA; and



(b) after that amount was, or those amounts were, paid, the person makes an election under section 137;



the election does not have effect unless an amount equal to that amount or to the aggregate of those amounts, as the case requires, is paid to the Commissioner within 7 days after the date of the election or within such further period as the Commissioner, in special circumstances, allows.”



Section 137(3), which provides for notification of an employee’s new employment and superannuation scheme membership, provides as follows:



“(3) Subject to subsection (4) an election under this section is of no effect unless the person who made the election gives notice in writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, after the expiration of the period that is the prescribed period in relation to him:



(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of the prescribed period and, if so, the name and address of his employer; and



(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the prescribed period a member of the superannuation scheme applicable in relation to that employment and, if so, specifying the scheme concerned.”



The provisions of s.137(4) are not relevant to this case.



It can be seen at once that an employee can, as the applicant did, get into a situation where a late election has been recognised, but time limits expire and it becomes too late to take advantage of that late election.



It was submitted for the respondent that the power to recognise a late election conferred by s.157(1) is of such a nature that it can only be exercised once, and that the Commissioner thereafter is functus officio.



Some statutory powers are of such a nature that they may not exercised more than once. We were referred to some reported cases concerning statutory powers of that nature. In Livingstone v Westminster Corporation [1904] 2 KB 109, Buckley J held that, once a local council had exercised a statutory power to determine the amount to pay an employee by way of compensation for abolition of his office, it had no power to re-assess the amount to be paid. In Re War Damage Act 1943; Re 56, Denton Road, Twickenham, Middlesex [1952] 2 All ER 799, Vaisey J held that once a particular authority had classified damage to premises as “not total loss” it had no power to impose a different classification.



That line of authority was distinguished by the English Court of Appeal in Rootkin v Kent County Council [1981] 2 All ER 227. That case concerned a decision to grant a schoolgirl a bus pass entitling her to free travel to and from school. It was later discovered that she lived closer to the school than the decision-maker had thought, and should not have been granted the pass. It was held that a decision cancelling the pass could be made. The two earlier cases that we have referred to were distinguished. Eveleigh LJ (at page 235) distinguished them on the basis that they concerned duties, not discretions.



We consider that the position is governed by s.33(1) of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901, which reads as follows:-



“Where an Act confers a power or imposes a duty, then, unless the contrary intention appears, the power may be exercised and the duty shall be performed from time to time as occasion requires.”



There is nothing in the legislation to indicate a contrary intention. The Act permits eligible employees to request an indulgence. There is no reason why they should not be permitted to request a second indulgence.



Further, in this case, the time allowed for the applicant to repay his refunded superannuation contributions had expired, with the result that, to use the words of s.140(2), his first late election did not have effect. Also, because he had not completed and returned the s.137(3) notice within the period allowed, his first late election, to use the words of that sub-section, was “of no effect”. We fail to see how the Commissioner could be functus officio by reason of his having treated as having been made within time an election that did not have effect or was of no effect. In saying that, we appreciate that such a late election is not a nullity, since its effect is capable of being revived by extensions of time under ss.140(2) and 137(3).



For these reasons we conclude that the respondent had a discretion to recognise the second late election pursuant to s.157(1), and that this Tribunal has jurisdiction to consider whether to reverse the decision under review.



Before going on to consider the merits of this application it is appropriate that we consider the Tribunal’s powers in relation to the first late election.



The refunded contributions amounting to $25,738.33 were not paid by the applicant to the respondent within seven days of 7 July 1987 as required by s.140(2). Nothing done by or on behalf of the respondent amounted to the allowing of a further period within which to pay that amount pursuant to that sub-section. The letter of 18 November 1987 requested payment as soon as possible. The letter of 19 January 1988 told the applicant of the consequences of failure to repay that amount immediately. However neither communication advised the applicant of a period within which payment had to be made. A decision should have been made fixing a date by which payment had to be made, and the applicant should have been notified of that date. Those things were not done. No further period for late payment was allowed.



On 9 January 1995 a delegate of the respondent purported to confirm pursuant to s.154(4) of the Act “the decision conveyed to Mr. Claasz by letters dated 18 Nov 1987 and 19 Jan 1988 that, pursuant to sub-section 140(2) of the Act, special circumstances did not exist to justify allowing him a period beyond 18 Mar 1988 to repay the sum of $25,138.33, being the amount of the lump sum previously paid to him under the provisions of the Act.” No decision allowing the applicant a period expiring on 28 March 1988 to repay the sum in question was ever made. The purported determination of 9 January 1995 is therefore a nullity.



As s.43 of the AAT Act confers on this Tribunal all the powers and discretions possessed by the respondent, we now have the power, under s.140(2), to allow the applicant a further period beyond 7 July 1987 in which to repay the sum refunded to him.



In our view s.43 of the AAT Act also gives us the power to allow a further period for the applicant to comply with s.137(3) in connection with his first late election. It is true that that power has once been exercised in the applicant’s favour. However, we consider that s.33(1) of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 also applies to that discretion, and that there is no reason why a second extension of time may not be allowed in an appropriate case.



In 1987 the applicant invested the sum refunded to him in a fund or funds managed by the ANZ Bank. His case is that he did not pursue his first late election because he encountered such great difficulties and delays in obtaining information as to both the CSS and the ANZ funds that he eventually gave up, and left his money with the ANZ. He made no contact with the respondent between writing a letter dated 28 December 1987 and writing the letter of 22 December 1991, to which we have referred. He did not respond to the letter dated 19 January 1988, to which we have referred. He was prompted to write his letter of 22 December 1991 by discussions he had been having in the course of his work concerning SMEC superannuation arrangements.



The applicant is a certified practising accountant. He has been since 1983. From August 1982 until April 1987 he was the Financial Manager of the Australian National Gallery. After moving to SMEC, he was the Manager, Financial Services, of SMEC’s Finance Division. He was no doubt well equipped and well placed to make informed decisions as to his superannuation and his investments. He must have been well aware of the consequences of abandoning his opportunity to pursue his first late election.



If we make a decision in the applicant’s favour now, he will be able to have his CSS benefit restored upon refunding the sum $25,738.33, with the respondent having received no interest or investment income in respect of that sum for over seven years.



Rejection of this application would not cause the applicant any significant hardship in our view. It appears that he owns two houses, has a well paid job, has new superannuation arrangements, and has received the benefit of the monies paid to him in 1987.



In our view, there is little that can be said in support of the applicant’s application. We accept that he must have been under a certain amount of work pressure on moving to the SMEC, but we do not accept that that pressure, either alone or in combination with other factors, justified his decision not to pursue his original late election once it had been recognised. We accept that the delay on the respondent’s part between receiving the late election of 7 July 1987 and deciding to recognise it on 18 November 1987 was inappropriate. However the applicant’s decision not to pursue that late election appears to have resulted at least in part from his deciding to apply a substantial part of his refund of contributions to the reduction of a home mortgage debt. He thereby placed a substantial sum out of convenient reach. We also accept that it was inappropriate for the respondent not to have fixed a deadline for the repayment of the refunded amount by allowing a “further period” pursuant s.140(2) of the Act. However the applicant did nothing by way of seeking an extension of time. If he had explained that he had paid a substantial sum off the mortgage debt, and therefore needed time to reorganise his affairs, that would have been a fact we could have taken into account in his favour. We accept that the applicant encountered some difficulty and delay in finding out from the ANZ Bank whether any of his money had been invested in a growth fund at about the time of the 1987 stock market crash. However the bank has compensated him for losses suffered in its growth fund. We accept that the applicant might have had to sell one of his houses to raise the money to repay the refund of contributions in 1987 or early 1988. However the applicant had a chance to profit through both houses escalating, or possibly escalating, in value over the years.



We have to consider the applicant’s letter of 22 December 1991, whereby he sought to make his second late election, standing in the shoes of the respondent. Under s.157(1) of the Act, we therefore have to consider whether we are “satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised”. In deciding what is desirable in all the circumstances of this case, we need to do justice between the applicant, who is seeking to make the late election, and the requirements of the administration of the superannuation fund: Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239 per von Doussa J at 247. We bear in mind the observations made by Davies J as to the considerations relevant to the exercise of this discretion in Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 19 AAR 450.



On balance, we believe that the factors favouable to the applicant are outweighed by those unfavourable to him, particularly his delay and his expertise in financial matters. We are therefore satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election of 22 December 1991 should not be recognised. Further, we are not prepared to allow extensions of time under ss.37(3) and 140(2) in connection with the applicant’s first late election because we would regard such a course as inappropriate because of his delay between December 1987 and December 1991, and because of his financial expertise.



For these reasons, we have decided to affirm the decision under review.
