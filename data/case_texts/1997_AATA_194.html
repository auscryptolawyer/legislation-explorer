ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

Applicant



And DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal Associate Professor R D Fayle, Senior Member      

Date 10 June 1997

Place Perth      

Decision The decision under review is affirmed.     

..........(Sgd..R D Fayle).........

Senior Member



CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Application to lodge objection against assessment arising from a tax audit and after period for lodgement has expired - Lengthy delay between receiving assessment and making request - Agent failed to lodge objection within time limit - Guidelines for exercise of discretion - Taxation Administration Act 1953, Part IVC.



Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 3 FCR 344

Case W23, 89 ATC 253

Lucic v Nolan (1982) 45 ALR 411

REASONS FOR DECISION

10 June 1997 Associate Professor R D Fayle

(Senior Member)



  1. This is an application by Mr J (“the applicant”) pursuant to s.14ZX(4) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (“the Administration Act”) for the Administrative Appeals Tribunal to review a decision of the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (“the respondent”) refusing an extension of time in which the applicant may lodge an objection pursuant to s.14ZW of the Administration Act in relation to an assessment of income tax, issued on 9 December 1994 for the year of income ended 30 June 1992.

FACTS AND EVIDENCE

  1. The applicant was represented at the hearing by his tax agent, Mr Erich Veder who gave evidence. The applicant did not attend the hearing.
  2. The applicant left Australia in November 1989 and returned in December 1991. He again departed for Indonesia on 21 February 1992 to manage a business. The business did not succeed and he returned to Australia on 3 April 1992.
  3. After a tax audit of the applicant’s income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1992, the respondent issued the notice of assessment in question on 9 December 1994. Mr Veder was present at the several interviews between the applicant and the respondent’s two auditors. Mr Veder had extensive discussions with the auditors about the efficacy of the proposed adjustments which had been advised in writing. The applicant was informed at a final interview on 27 September 1994, at which Mr Veder was present, that an assessment would be issued making the adjustments foreshadowed.
  4. The respondent’s records indicate that there was no contact, between the applicant or Mr Veder, concerning any dissatisfaction with the assessment from that meeting until it received a letter, by facsimile, on 23 January 1996 from Mr Veder. That this is so is supported by folios T5, T6 and T7 of the documents filed by the respondent with the Tribunal pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the T documents”). That letter requested an extension of time in which to lodge an objection against the notice of assessment mentioned. The purpose of the letter was to defer recovery action apparently then under way, to recover the income tax assessed, that having been due for payment on 13 January 1995. The letter contained the following paragraph:
“The taxpayer contends that the overseas earnings were taxed at source in the UK. Other monies included as income included wedding gifts and monies belonging to his wife.”


  1. Pausing there for a moment, whilst the respondent has submitted that no notice of objection accompanied the request for an extension of time, as is required by s.14ZW(2) of the Administration Act, the Tribunal is of the view that the above paragraph is sufficient to alert the respondent to the nature of the objection and, except for it having been lodged out of time, could be taken to be a taxation objection in terms of s.14ZU of the Administration Act.
  2. Notwithstanding possible compliance with the requirements of s.14ZW(2), the applicant did not comply with the requirements of s.14ZW(3) in that the letter of 23 January 1996 did not “state fully and in detail the circumstances concerning, and the reasons for, the person’s failure to lodge the objection with the Commissioner within the ... 60 day period.”
  3. A file note of the respondent dated 22 January 1996, (T8), indicates that an officer of the respondent telephoned Mr Veder’s office to inform him of the need to provide an explanation of the circumstances of and reasons for the failure to lodge the objection within time. Apparently Mr Veder was not then available but did call back later that day. The file note goes on to state:
“ ... advised the T/A re s188(3) [which is a reference to the predecessor provision of s14ZW(3), contained in the Income Tax Assessment Act]. His view is that if the matter can be settled then there is no need for the time and expense. I advised I am not in a position to state the matter will be settled and that he needs to consider the protection of his client’s rights. He will lodge full s188 application. (Signed by the respondent’s officer at 2.45pm.)”


  1. This is consistent with the evidence of Mr Veder that in his experience, as a tax agent of many years and before that, as an employee of the respondent, it is better to negotiate a settlement than to go through a process of formal objection. His contention, based on his experience, is that objections are never allowed and matters are usually settled after having gone through a process of referring a disallowed objection to a “Board of Review” for review. Mr Veder told the Tribunal that he had over 13 years experience as a Registered Tax Agent and had dealt with a cross-section of income tax returns on behalf of clients. He had also assisted several clients with audits and investigations conducted by the respondent and lodged objections in relation to seven of these matters. In every instance he was able to reach a negotiated settlement without the need for a formal hearing. He considered the cost of lodging a formal objection unnecessary if the matter could be negotiated, his preferred method of resolving any dispute.
  2. The Tribunal is minded of Deputy President Gerber’s remark in Case W23 89 ATC 253 when considering a similar situation:
“Pausing here, and looking at the events in slow motion, the first lesson clearly emerges: while much is to be said for the practice of attempting to resolve a dispute of this kind informally and it is obvious that both parties accepted such a ‘shorthand’ procedure in this case - it is unwise to deal with the Commissioner without a ‘protective’ objection.” (P.255).


  1. Mr Veder provided the Tribunal with a copy of what he purports is a letter sent on 19 February 1996 to the respondent which has all the hallmarks of a formal notice of objection (Ex. A5). The respondent’s evidence, given by its witness Mr Erich Krispler, an officer of the respondent and also its representative in these proceedings, is that the respondent has no record of ever having received the above letter. Mr Krispler told the Tribunal that the first occasion on which the respondent received a copy of the purported letter was on 18 April 1997, when it received copies of the applicant’s responses to the Tribunal’s directions made on 14 March 1997.
  2. The T documents disclose that in April 1996 the respondent made several attempts, to speak to Mr Veder by telephone (T9) to no avail and wrote to him on 17 June 1996 requesting a detailed statement of reasons for failing to lodge a notice of objection in time. No reply appears to have been received to this letter. The respondent again telephoned Mr Veder on 3 and 9 October 1996 (T11 and T12) in this regard, again to no avail. In the result, on 15 October 1996, the respondent wrote to the applicant, care of Mr Veder, informing him of its decision not to grant an extension of time in which to lodge an objection against his 30 June 1992 notice of assessment (T14).
  3. The Tribunal is satisfied that the statutory period in which to lodge a taxation objection in relation to the year ended 30 June 1992 is 60 days as stated in s.14ZW(1)(c) of the Administration Act, as amended by Act No 101 of 1992.
  4. The only written notification of reasons for the delay in lodging the objection are contained in documents filed with the Tribunal pursuant to its directions made on 14 March 1997. In essence these submit that the taxpayer and his wife have been under continuing stress and ill health. But that refers to November 1993, a considerable time prior to the issue of the notice of assessment in December 1994.
  5. It was further submitted by Mr Veder, (bearing in mind that the taxpayer did not give evidence despite the Tribunal, during the hearing, offering to adjourn to allow the taxpayer to give evidence either in person or by telephone), that the taxpayer does not have the means to pay for professional assistance in this respect. The Tribunal finds this submission rather curious in that Mr Veder was present at the hearing representing his client.
  6. The third limb to the explanation is that the taxpayer was unable to obtain the necessary documentary evidence to support his contention about the foreign source nature of the money assessed as income. When asked about the prospect of obtaining such evidence Mr Veder was somewhat equivocal and left the Tribunal with the impression that such evidence may never be obtained.
  7. The next reason, that the respondent had the power to obtain the evidence, hardly bears comment since it simply ignores the provisions of s.14ZZK(b) of the Administration Act which places the onus of proof on the applicant taxpayer.
  8. The final reason, which is consistent with the evidence of Mr Veder, is that the applicant relied entirely on him to manage his income tax affairs and negotiations with the respondent.

DISCUSSION

  1. The Tribunal is satisfied that in its role of reviewing the decision of the respondent under review, to disallow the request for an extension of time to lodge the objection, it may consider evidence before it of technical conformity with the provisions of s.14ZW(3) and (4)[1]. This holds despite clear evidence that those provisions had not been complied with at the time the respondent made its decision and that, in those circumstance, the respondent was justified in making the correct and only decision open to it. Nevertheless it will emerge that that is little comfort to the applicant in the circumstances of this case.
  2. The judgment of the Federal Court (Wilcox J) in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 3 FCR 344, provides useful guidelines to discern whether an extension of time to comply with a statutory provision to protect ones’ rights should be granted. His Honour suggests, at pages 348 and 349, six matters for consideration in this respect. Each of these is now considered in context.
  3. The first is that an application should not be granted unless it is proper to do so. In this regard, one cannot ignore the “prescribed period”, 60 days in this instance. The prima facie rule is that proceedings commenced outside this period will not be entertained (Lucic v Nolan (1982) 45 ALR 411 at 416).
“Indeed, it is a pre-condition to the exercise of discretion in his favour that the applicant for extension show an ‘acceptable explanation of the delay’ and that it is ‘fair and equitable in the circumstances’ to extend time (Duff at 485; Chapman v Reilly, unreported Federal Court of Australia, Neaves J, 9 December 1983 at 7).”


  1. Almost 14 months passed between the time the applicant received the assessment and the first occasion on which the respondent was alerted to some dissatisfaction on the part of the applicant. The assessment arose after a considerable period of discussion between the applicant and the respondent’s auditors. In the opinion of the Tribunal, none of the reasons mentioned above, either alone or together, provide a satisfactory explanation for the delay. Further, in January 1996 Mr Veder was encouraged by an officer of the respondent, to protect his client’s rights. Given Mr Veder’s background he would have appreciated the significance of this advice, to lodge an objection and an explanation of the delay with a request for an extension of time in which to lodge.
  2. The Tribunal is not convinced that the purported copy of the objection, bearing the date of 19 February 1996, was created and mailed to the respondent about that time. The preferred view is that the copy of the letter bearing the date of 19 February 1996 was first received by the respondent on or about 18 April 1997, some two years and four months after receipt of the notice of assessment. That is, following the commencement of proceedings before this Tribunal in March 1997.
  3. The second of Wilcox J’s guiding principles, stated in this context, is consideration of any action taken by the applicant in alerting the respondent to dissatisfaction with the assessment. This is discussed immediately above and there is no evidence that the respondent was put on alert until at least 14 months after the issue of the assessment.
  4. The third guiding principle concerns any prejudice which might be suffered by the respondent if the extension was granted. Mr Krispler submitted that the respondent would be seriously prejudiced if it had to delve into files long since archived and where the personnel involved, who might be called to give evidence, may no longer be employees. Even if they were, there would be some doubt about their accurate recall of events which occurred over three years ago when the audit was carried out. The Tribunal acknowledges that these are real concerns. Further, since the statutory period for objecting was 60 days, any extension of time beyond that limit would be prejudicial to some extent, the increasing degree of which is likely to be a positive function of the time lapse.
  5. The fourth guiding principle concerns public considerations in granting the extension. The effect of granting an extension after such a long delay for which there is no apparent justifiable reason would seriously impede the fair and efficient conduct of the business of the respondent - a serious public concern.
  6. The fifth guiding principle concerns the merits of the application. It would be a fruitless exercise to grant an extension of time to contest a matter having no merit. In the present case, Mr Veder did not accept the invitation of the Tribunal to hear from the applicant. The applicant’s case depends on both credibility and fact. The Tribunal was not given the opportunity to make any judgement in relation to the first. Also, there is considerable doubt that the applicant can produce evidence to dispel his onus of proof in relation to his assertions represented in documents filed by Mr Veder at the direction of the Tribunal. Mr Krispler, whilst under oath gave evidence that the applicant has been inconsistent in his explanations for having the money in question which has been assessed as income. For these reasons the Tribunal is unable to conclude that the applicant’s case has sufficient merit to warrant re-examination by it.
  7. The final guiding principle is that of considerations of fairness between the applicant and other persons otherwise in a like position. Taxpayers represented by Registered Tax Agents at all stages of an income tax audit and assessment process have a reasonable expectation that if there was any serious disagreement with the culminating assessment then their representative would take whatever protective action was required. If none was taken then they would have good reason to assume the matter finalised. No doubt, in retrospect, some might wonder whether they received the best advice or were assessed in the most favourable terms. It would therefore be perceived as unfair for them and the authorities if this Tribunal granted one of this class of taxpayer an extension of time after the matter had long since been regarded as finalised.

CONCLUSION

  1. Taking all of the above into consideration it is clear that the Tax Agent and, by inference, the applicant, failed to lodge the objection as soon as circumstances reasonably permitted. There is nothing to indicate that it would have been unreasonable for the Tax Agent, on behalf of the applicant, to lodge an objection within the prescribed time limit set by s.14ZW(1)(c) of the Administration Act.

DECISION

  1. For the above reasons the decision under review is affirmed.
