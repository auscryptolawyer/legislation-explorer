Re Canberra Fuel Distributors Pty Limited and Commissioner of Business Franchises [1985] AATA 120 (21 May 1985)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: CANBERRA FUEL DISTRIBUTORS PTY LIMITED

And: COMMISSIONER FOR BUSINESS FRANCHISES

No. A.85/7

Business Franchise

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE TRIBUNAL

Mr Justice J.D. Davies (President)

E. Smith (Senior Member)

D.R.S. Craik (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Business Franchise - petroleum wholesaler - petroleum products licence - licence fees - fees calculated by reference to value of fuel sold in course of interstate trade - ACT deemed a State - whether fuel sold in course of interstate trade - terms of contract of sale - whether first sale after importation from NSW in course of interstate trade - authorities interpreting s.92 Constitution applied - relevance of transient passing of property

Business Franchise (Tobacco and Petroleum Products)

Ordinance 1984 (ACT) ss.3(5), 3(9), 31(1), 31(8)

The Constitution (63 & 64 Vict. c.12) s.92

HC Sleigh Ltd v State of South Australia (1977) 136 CLR 475

Wragg v State of New South Wales [1953] HCA 34; (1953) 88 CLR 353

Harper v Victoria [1966] HCA 26; (1966) 114 CLR 361

SOS (Mowbray) Pty Ltd v Mead (1972) 124 CLR 529

Mikasa (NSW) Pty Ltd v Festival Stores [1972] HCA 69; (1972) 127 CLR 617

North Eastern Dairy Co Ltd v Dairy Industry Authority of New South Wales [1975] HCA 45; (1975) 134 CLR 559

Permewan Wright Consolidated Pty Ltd v Trewhitt [1979] HCA 58; (1977) 145 CLR 1

Ex parte H. Brazil & Co Pty Ltd [1978] HCA 25; (1978) 138 CLR 194

The Commonwealth v The State of South Australia [1926] HCA 47; (1926) 38 CLR 408

Vacuum Oil Company Proprietary Ltd v The State of Queensland [1934] HCA 5; (1934) 51 CLR 108

HEARING

CANBERRA

21:5:1985

ORDER

The decisions under review are affirmed.

DECISION

This is a review of decisions of the respondent assessing licence fees under the Business Franchise (Tobacco and Petroleum Products) Ordinance 1984 (ACT) ("the Ordinance") for the months of December 1984 and January 1985 in respect of sales of petroleum products made by the applicant, Canberra Fuel Distributors Pty Limited, during October and November 1984.

2. The issue is whether petroleum products sold by the applicant to Georges Jet Gas Pty Limited ("Georges") during those months were sold in the course of interstate trade. For the purposes of its provisions, the Ordinance requires the Australian Capital Territory to be treated as a separate State of the Commonwealth.

3. Section 31(1) of the Ordinance provides:

"31.(1) Subject to this section, the fees to be

paid for petroleum products licences shall be the

specified amount together with the prescribed

amount calculated in accordance with sub-section

(2) by reference to the value of motor spirit and

diesel fuel sold in the course of interstate trade

in the relevant period."

Section 3(5) of the Ordinance provides:

"3.(5) A reference in this Ordinance to interstate

trade is a reference to trade carried on in the

Territory that would be trade and commerce among

the States within the meaning of section 92 of the

Constitution if the Territory were a State."

This provision is expanded by s.3(9) which provides:

"3.(9) Where petroleum products are, or tobacco is,

sold in the Territory for the purpose of resale

outside the Territory, the first-mentioned sale

shall, for the purposes of this Ordinance, be

taken to have been made in the course of

interstate trade."

Conformably with these provisions, s.31(8) provides:

"31.(8) The value of any petroleum products sold

for delivery and consumption inside the Territory

shall be disregarded in assessing fees payable

under this section."

4. The applicant is a wholesaler of petroleum products and carries on business as such at Fyshwick in the Australian Capital Territory. The applicant also conducts, or has an interest in, two retail outlets but we are not concerned with them. The applicant is a distributor of Caltex products and an agent of Caltex Oil Australia Pty Limited ("Caltex Oil"). The applicant sells Caltex petrol principally as agent for Caltex Oil and effects sales between that company and retailers. It also acts as agent for Caltex Oil by servicing a number of Caltex depots. In addition, the applicant makes sales of Caltex petrol on its own behalf. Thus, it makes sales to the retail outlets which it conducts or in which it has an interest and, from time to time, it makes sales to other retail outlets and to other wholesalers. In such circumstances the sales are made by the applicant on its own invoices and its arrangement with Caltex Oil permits the transient passing of property from Caltex Oil to the applicant and from the applicant to the purchaser when a sale is made. All sales of diesel fuel are made in the applicant's name, although the fuel is Caltex fuel. Necessarily in such sales there is also a transient passing of property through the applicant. Motor oils and other Caltex products are likewise sold by the applicant either as agent for Caltex Oil or in its own name. In addition, the applicant acts as a consultant with respect to petroleum products and generally acts as the agent for Caltex Oil in the Australian Capital Territory. Most of the applicant's transactions take place in and relate to the Australian Capital Territory but it does service some retail outlets and depots in New South Wales and, from time to time, makes sales in or for delivery in New South Wales.

5. The Caltex petrol, which is the product with which we are concerned in this review is brought into the Australian Capital Territory by rail from New South Wales. Mr B.A. Grelis, a director of the applicant, said that each Monday 16 railcars loaded with fuel leave the Banksmeadow yard in Sydney arriving on the Tuesday in Canberra, where the fuel is discharged by the applicant and put into its bulk store. Fuel is likewise received from the Banksmeadow yard on Thursdays and on Saturdays.

6. Georges is also a wholesaler of petroleum products. In late 1984, after negotiating a special discount rate with the applicant, Georges made arrangements to purchase fuel from the applicant's premises at Fyshwick. During the relevant period, tankers pulled into the Fyshwick premises and took on fuel on behalf of Georges. The tankers had no markings which are of significance for the present proceedings. Officers of the Commissioner for Business Franchises followed such tankers from the Fyshwick premises and observed them proceed out of the Australian Capital Territory into New South Wales.

7. There is no evidence that it was an express term of the contract that the goods would be taken out of the Australian Capital Territory. Nor is there evidence of facts which would give rise to a necessary inference that the goods were acquired for that purpose or would be used to that end. Nor is there evidence that the destination of the products was communicated to any person in the applicant's organisation.

8. Although Mr Grelis in his oral evidence said, in answer to one question, that he thought that all the fuel purchased by Georges was to be resold in the Australian Capital Territory, the crucial part of the evidence adduced on behalf of the applicant which we accept is that the applicant was not informed and was not aware of the destination of the fuel once it left the applicant's Fyshwick premises. Mr Grelis made clear in his evidence this lack of knowledge was seen by the applicant to be an advantage as the applicant wished to avoid any difficulty which might otherwise arise under s.45 of the Trade Practices Act 1974 (Cth) and under the Laidley Agreement between the Transport Workers' Union and the Australian Petroleum Distributors Association with respect to the supply by wholesalers of petroleum products. Mr Grelis and others of the applicant's office presumably may also have had in mind the provisions of the Ordinance when they deliberately refrained from obtaining knowledge of the use to which the purchaser would put the petroleum products acquired from the applicant company. But there is no reason to doubt the evidence that they did not wish to know and were not informed what would be the destination of the petroleum products purchased by Georges from the applicant.

9. Indeed, we assume that Georges would have regarded its business as its own and would have been inclined to keep to itself the details of its own activities. Georges and other companies with which it was associated carried on wholesaling and retailing activities widely in South Eastern Australia. There is no reason to infer that the applicant had any clear understanding of the ambit of their activities, or that Georges and the associated companies would have wished persons outside their organisation to understand their business.

10. In the light of these facts, it is clear that neither the inclusion provision of s.3(9) nor the exclusion provision of s.31(8) of the Ordinance can apply. Section 3(9) speaks of goods "sold ... for the purpose of resale outside the Territory" and s.31(8) speaks of products "sold for delivery and consumption inside the Territory". Neither provision speaks of the purpose for which the goods were purchased but rather of the purpose of the sale. Products are not sold for consumption either inside or outside the Territory unless that purpose arises from an express term of the contract, or by necessary implication from the terms of the contract, or by implication from the circumstances surrounding the sale.

11. A purchaser may make known to the vendor prior to the sale the purpose to which the goods will be put. The sale may proceed on that basis. In such circumstances, products may be sold for the purpose of resale outside the Territory or for the purpose of delivery and consumption within the Territory. But, no such circumstances prevailed in the present case. The goods were simply bought at and delivered at the Fyshwick premises. The use to which the purchaser intended to put the goods was not made known to the applicant.

12. The Commissioner drew an inference adverse to the applicant from an endorsement upon the invoices of the subject sales, namely, "Franchise fee not paid - responsibility of purchaser" or "No franchise fee paid. Responsibility of purchaser if sold outside the ACT." However, Mr Grelis explained that such an endorsement which was standard upon the applicant's invoices when petroleum products were sold on behalf of the applicant and in circumstances such that a franchise fee was not payable. We accept his explanation and see no significance adverse to the applicant in the endorsement.

13. The case therefore turns upon the issue whether the goods were sold in the course of trade that would be trade and commerce among the States within the meaning of s.92 of the Constitution, assuming that the Territory were a State. See ss.3(5) and 31(1).

14. If one were to concentrate solely upon the circumstances of the arrangement between the applicant and Georges it would be clear that the sales were not made in the course of interstate trade. There was no express or implied term of the contracts between the applicant and Georges and no mutual understanding between them that the goods would be taken out of the Australian Capital Territory. It is sufficient to refer to the remarks of Gibbs J (as he then was) in HC Sleigh Ltd v State of South Australia (1977) 136 CLR 475 at pp.494-5 :

"... The fact that the sales by Mobil and Esso to

the plaintiff of petroleum products refined at

Port Stanvac are made pursuant to the refinery

exchange system does not convert them into

transactions of interstate trade. They are sales

made within South Australia of petroleum products

produced within South Australia, and are part of

the domestic trade of South Australia. Some of

the products so sold are delivered by Mobil or

Esso or transported by the plaintiff from Port

Stanvac to distribution points situated in other

States, namely Victoria, Tasmania and New South

Wales. That, however, is not enough to convert

the sale to the plaintiff into an interstate

transaction. For that result to ensue it would be

necessary that the agreement for sale to the

plaintiff should contain a stipulation that the

petroleum products should be despatched from the

refinery in South Australia and delivered by the

seller to the plaintiff in a State other than

South Australia : see W. & A. McArthur Ltd. v.

Queensland [1920] HCA 77; (1920) 28 C.L.R. 530 at pp.540, 559-560

which on this point has often been cited with

approval.

The plaintiff further alleges that the

circumstances of certain of the sales which it

makes give them the qualities of interstate trade.

Those sales are as follows : (a) sales made in

South Australia, the product sold being delivered

by the plaintiff from a distribution point in

South Australia to buyers in Victoria; (b) sales

made by the plaintiff pursuant to orders received

by an agent in South Australia from purchasers

resident in South Australia, delivery being made

by the plaintiff by transporting the petroleum

products from Victoria to South Australia; (c)

sales made by the plaintiff in South Australia or

New South Wales pursuant to orders received from

purchasers resident in South Australia, delivery

being made by the plaintiff from Broken Hill in

New South Wales to South Australia. In none of

these cases was it alleged that it was a term of

the contract that petroleum products should be

despatched from one State and delivered in

another. W. & A. McArthur Ltd. v. Queensland

[1920] HCA 77; (1920) 28 CLR 530 is authority for the

proposition that these sales did not form part of

interstate trade."

15. But it is necessary to give closer attention to the circumstances of the applicant's trade. The applicant was the Australian Capital Territory agent for Caltex Oil and the fuel which it marketed, either on its own behalf or on behalf of Caltex Oil, was fuel which was, and was known to be, imported from interstate. It was public knowledge that the Caltex refinery was in Sydney, that there was no Caltex refinery in the Australian Capital Territory and that the Caltex products which the applicant sold, whether on its own behalf or on behalf of Caltex Oil, were goods which were imported into the Australian Capital Territory. In these circumstances, were the subject transaction sales made in the course of the interstate trade carried on by Caltex Oil and the applicant?

16. At certain times in the development of the jurisprudence relating to s.92 of the Constitution, it may well have been thought that such sales would not have been sales in the course of trade and commerce among the States. In Wragg v State of New South Wales [1953] HCA 34; (1953) 88 CLR 353, the Court took a restricted view of trade and commerce among the States and held that it did not comprehend the sale in New South Wales of potatoes which had been grown in Tasmania and imported into New South Wales for the purpose of sale there. Thus, the Chief Justice, Sir Owen Dixon, at pp.385-6, said:

"... It is of course clear that in the case of most

imported articles sales which take place in the

course of distribution to the consumer are, when

considered by themselves, entirely intra-State

transactions. If they take on the character of

inter-State commerce it must be in virtue of some

inseparable connection with the importation of the

article from another State. The distinction which

is drawn between inter-State trade and the

domestic trade of a State for the purpose of the

power conferred upon the Parliament by s.51(i.) to

make laws with respect to trade and commerce with

other countries and among the States may well be

considered artificial and unsuitable to modern

times. But it is a distinction adopted by the

Constitution and it must be observed however much

inter-dependence may now exist between the two

divisions of trade and commerce which the

Constitution thus distinguishes. ...".

At p.388, his Honour concluded in the particular case:

"Does this mean that the sale by the primary

wholesaler is an inseparable part of the

inter-State transaction? In my opinion the facts

stated are insufficient to justify that

conclusion. I think that they show no more than a

course of business in which it is convenient to

make the first intra-State sale from the wharf."

See also Harper v Victoria [1966] HCA 26; (1966) 114 CLR 361.

17. Other views, however, were expressed subsequently in S.O.S. (Mowbray) Pty Limited v Mead (1972) 124 CLR 529 and in Mikasa (N.S.W.) Pty Limited v Festival Stores [1972] HCA 69; (1972) 127 CLR 617. And, in North Eastern Dairy Co. Limited v Dairy Industry Authority of New South Wales [1975] HCA 45; (1975) 134 CLR 559, there was a major change of view with respect to interstate trade. At p.579, Barwick CJ said:

"There can be no doubt whatsoever, in my view, that

the movement of the first plaintiff's milk and

milk products from Victoria to New South Wales was

part of a commercial transaction and itself

constituted a part of interstate trade and

commerce. Further, the possession for sale and

the sale of the milk in New South Wales as the

purpose and end point of that interstate

commercial movement would be itself, in my

opinion, part and parcel of that interstate trade.

Indeed, that purpose established the commercial

character of the transportation."

At p.599, Gibbs J said:

"... There can be no doubt that in taking milk over

the border for the purpose of selling it the first

plaintiff was engaging in interstate trade. On

the other hand, in my opinion the sales made

within New South Wales to customers who made no

stipulation that the milk should come from

Victoria, and who were indeed quite indifferent as

to the origin of the milk, were, considered by

themselves, intrastate sales. However, as Walsh

J. said in S.O.S. (Mowbray) Pty. Ltd. v. Mead

(1972) 124 CLR at p 582, in a passage to which I

referred in Pilkington v. Frank Hammond Pty. Ltd.

[1974] HCA 13; (1974) 131 CLR 124 at p 176, in many cases an act

may have a double aspect, being in one aspect an

act which forms part of intrastate trade, and in

another aspect an act which forms part of

interstate trade. Viewed from the point of view

of the plaintiffs' trade, each sale was at least

'an inseparable concomitant or consequence of that

transaction' (cf. Fergusson v. Stevenson (1951) 84

CLR 421 at p 435). The trade in which the first

plaintiff engaged did not consist merely in

bringing the milk across the border - it carried

the milk from one State to another to sell it in

the latter State. Trade is not limited merely to

carriage. The essential object of s.92 would be

defeated if a State could, by the enactment of

legislative provisions such as those now in

question, entirely exclude from sale the products

of another State. ...".

Mason J did not express a considered view on this point. Jacobs J said, at p.633:

"For the reasons which appear from what I have

stated earlier I am of the opinion that the

plaintiff North Eastern Dairy Co. Ltd. directly

and through its agent Carroll's Food Mart Pty.

Ltd. engages in a transaction of trade, commerce,

and intercourse between the States of Victoria and

New South Wales when it transports milk from

Victoria to New South Wales for the purpose of

selling the milk in New South Wales either

directly or through its agent and when it sells

the milk to purchasers in New South Wales. The

transport, the purpose thereof and the

effectuation of the purpose are not severable.

...".

McTiernan J dissented on the s.92 issue.

18. The principles enunciated in the North Eastern Dairy Case were re-stated with greater clarity in Permewan Wright Consolidated Pty Limited v Trewhitt [1979] HCA 58; (1979) 145 CLR 1 and earlier cases including Harper v Victoria, cited above, were not followed on this issue. Permewan Wright Consolidated Pty Limited had acted as an agent for an egg producer in New South Wales and had sold in Victoria by retail, eggs brought down from New South Wales into Victoria. It was held by the Court that Permewan Wright Consolidated Pty Limited was engaged in trade and commerce between the States. Barwick CJ followed his views as to the ambit of interstate trade and commerce which he had earlier expressed in his dissenting opinion in Harper v Victoria and in the North Eastern Dairy Case. Gibbs J said, at p.17:

"I shall assume that in the light of North Eastern

Dairy Co. Ltd. v. Dairy Industry Authority of

N.S.W. [1975] HCA 45; (1975) 134 CLR 559, it should be held in

the present case, notwithstanding Harper v.

Victoria and Cantarella v. Egg Marketing Board

(N.S.W.), that the sale by the appellant, as agent

for Bartters, was an inseparable concomitant of

Bartters' interstate trade."

At p.19, Stephen J said:

"The sales here in question form a part of the

interstate trade of the present appellant's

principals, a group of New South Wales egg

producers trading as Bartters Enterprises

('Bartters'). It transports some of its eggs into

Victoria where its agent, the appellant, disposes

of them on its behalf by retail sales. ...".

At p.34, Mason J said:

"It has been evident, at least since the delivery

of judgment in North Eastern Dairy Co. Ltd. v.

Dairy Industry Authority of N.S.W. (1975) 134 CLR

559, that the ground which was common to each of

the majority judgments in Harper v. Victoria can

no longer be sustained. In the North Eastern

Dairy Case the Court decided that the first sale

made by the importer within the State after

importation from another State formed part of the

importer's interstate trade or was inseparably

connected with it and was therefore protected by

s.92. All the members of the Court arrived at

this conclusion though there was some difference

in the reasoning which led to the result. It is

neither necessary nor profitable to identify the

different approaches then taken. It is quite

sufficient to say that the first sale by an

importer within a State may be protected on the

footing that it is part of, or that it is

inseparably connected with, his interstate trade."

At p.40, Murphy J said:

"... The conduct which constituted the offences was

part of trade and commerce among the States.

...".

At p.60, Aickin J, after considering the passage from Gibbs J's judgment in the North Eastern Dairy Case, which we have set out above, said:

"Although I do not think it matters for present

purposes I am, with respect, unable to accept that

the distinction which is there drawn between the

Mowbray Case (1972) 124 CLR 529 and North Eastern

Dairy Case [1975] HCA 45; (1975) 134 CLR 559 is a satisfactory

one. It appears to me that the situation in each

case was that the vendor of the goods in New South

Wales or in Tasmania, as the case may be, had

either himself brought the goods across the border

or had arranged for them to be sent to him across

the border, in each case for the purpose of

selling them in the State of destination. I can

see no difference between those two situations.

...".

At p.62, Aickin J concluded:

"I am satisfied that neither the reasoning nor the

decision in Harper's Case [1966] HCA 26; (1966) 114 CLR 361 can

be reconciled with the reasoning and the decision

in the North Eastern Dairy Case (1975) 134 CLR

559."

19. There has been no significant development on this issue since the Permewan Wright Case, cited above, and this Tribunal must follow the principles there enunciated.

20. In the above judgments, there is perhaps a divergence of view to be perceived on the question whether the first sale made by the importer is a sale made in the course of interstate trade or merely an inseparable concomitant or consequence of that trade. For present purposes, we think that no distinction should be drawn between a sale which is of the essence of interstate trade and a sale which is merely an inseparable concomitant thereof. If a sale cannot be separated from interstate trade, it is a sale made in the course of interstate trade. The course of a trade comprehends not merely the essential ingredients thereof but also the ordinary and indispensable incidents thereof. As Dixon CJ said in Wragg's Case, in the passage cited above, a transaction may take on the character of interstate commerce by "...virtue of some inseparable connection with the importation of the article from another State". See also the judgment of Gibbs CJ, with whom Stephen, Mason, Jacobs and Aickin JJ agreed, in Ex parte H. Brazil & Co Pty Ltd [1978] HCA 25; (1978) 138 CLR 194.

21. On the facts of the present case, we think that the conclusion should be drawn that the sales to Georges made at the applicant's Fyshwick premises were sales made in the course of the interstate trade of Caltex Oil and of the applicant. This conclusion is consistent with and supported by The Commonwealth v The State of South Australia [1926] HCA 47; (1926) 38 CLR 408 and Vacuum Oil Company Proprietary Limited v The State of Queensland [1934] HCA 5; (1934) 51 CLR 108. It seems to us that no distinction ought properly to be drawn between a case where the applicant sold as agent for Caltex Oil and a case in which it sold on its own behalf and there was a transient passing of property through it. In either event, there was a sale of goods imported into the Australian Capital Territory for the purpose of being traded by one or other of those means. That trade was part of the course of interstate trade and the end step thereof.

22. There was nothing in the particular circumstances of the sales to Georges which would lead one to conclude that those sales had an intrastate character whatever the character of the applicant's sales generally. Georges would have known that the applicant was the Australian Capital Territory agent of Caltex Oil and that it was dealing as such with the petroleum products of Caltex Oil which had been imported into the Australian Capital Territory.

23. For these reasons, therefore, we are satisfied that the subject sales were sales made by the applicant in the course of interstate trade within the meaning of s.31(1). As we have already mentioned, the sales were not excluded from assessment by virtue of s.31(8) for they were not sales "for delivery and consumption inside the Territory". Accordingly, the assessments of licence fee were correct.

24. In our opinion, the decisions under review should be affirmed.
