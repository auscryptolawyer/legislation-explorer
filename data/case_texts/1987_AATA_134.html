Re Taxation Appeals [1987] AATA 134 (9 April 1987)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. NT85/16682

AAT No. 3393

Income Tax

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

P.M. Roach (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Allowable Deductions - Personal disability insurance plan - Wife as insured - Solicitor-husband and employer as insurer - Whether premium deductible - Whether premium received assessable - Whether a "sham" - Whether s. 260 applied.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) s. 260

Fitzgerald v. Masters [1956] HCA 53; 1956 95 CLR 420

Newton v. F.C.T. [1958] UKPCHCA 1; 1958 98 CLR 1

Gulland: Watson: and Pincus 85 ATC 4,765

Oakey Abattoir Pty Ltd 84 ATC 4,718

HEARING

SYDNEY

9:4:1987

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review of the Commissioner of Taxation.

DECISION

During the year of income ended 30 June 1980, the Applicant (A), a successfully married woman then approaching the age of 40 years, derived personal exertion income by working as a Secretary and Law Clerk to a Solicitor. On 20 June 1980, she paid out monies in support of a proposal for "disability insurance" which she completed that day. She delivered the proposal and her cheque to the proposed insurer who thereupon immediately accepted the payment and the proposal. She claims an income tax deduction pursuant to the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act") in the year of income ended 30 June 1980 in relation to a "premium" of $1,620. As the "policy" only provided for "income benefits", without more the claim would appear to be quite unremarkable. However, there were further relevant circumstances.

2. The "insurer" was her husband (H), a solicitor in private practice, practicing alone on his own account. He was also her employer. She was his only employee. On 20 June, she paid $6,480 to him as a "premium". That amount was received by him and placed to her credit in his solicitor's trust account. From there he transferred it to his business account, thereby appropriating the monies as his own property. With his funds so augmented, he contemporaneously paid to her $6,480 as a "premium" on a policy he was effecting with her that day.

3. At that point the policies were complete in all respects save one: the amount of "monthly benefits" had yet to be specified. The appropriate amounts were to be "actuarially calculated" by the promoters, having regard only to the information in the proposals. H and A were agreed that "monthly benefits" should be so determined. In consequence, H that day sent all the forms to the promoters in Melbourne together with his cheque in favour of the promoters for $480. The policies were identical save for any difference to be specified in the amount of monthly benefits. By the end of the day A's funds had been restored to the position they were in at the beginning of the day. His funds were too. However, it is claimed, their contingent liability for claims was in each case increased. H might have become liable for more than $2,000,000. A might have become liable for more than $1,600,000. Neither sought to spread the risk. Neither investigated the cost of acquiring disability insurance - or the terms upon which it would be available - from any insurer registered under the Insurance Acts 1973. Neither knew at that time what the monthly benefits would be, although H thought that they would approximate the annual premium.

4. Those transactions occurred against a background of earlier events and within the context of a successful matrimonial relationship in which for over eighteen years each partner had contributed fully to the well-being of the marriage and in providing for the children of the marriage. They enjoyed complete and mutual trust and support. The husband was successful in his practice and was expecting to derive an income approaching $80,000 for the year of income ended 30 June, 1980. All this occurred in a period when, in the words of H, "a plethora of tax minimization schemes" were being marketed. There came to his attention a proposal marketed from Melbourne. Details of that proposal were not evidenced but, from the understanding of the proposal expressed by H in the witness box and from what is to be inferred from the documents placed in evidence, the "scheme" was based around two concepts: the first, that an employed person who insured against loss of income from employment would be entitled to a tax deduction for the premium paid; the second, that a person did not need to be registered pursuant to the Insurance Acts to effect a "one-off" policy; and that such a person, upon writing a "one-off" policy, would receive the premium as a capital sum not liable to be brought to account as assessable income.

5. The promoter brought these ideas together and in essence proposed that H and A could both qualify for tax deductions, without disturbing the immediate financial resources of either of them, by effecting mutual insurance. The only difference in their affairs (apart from income tax) would be that, if the policies were in force and one spouse became disabled (in the relevant sense) and thereon sought to enforce his or her entitlement, there could have been catastrophic results for the insurer-spouse. It seems that the parties did not even consider what would have happened if both had become totally and permanently disabled at the one time, with the result that each might have become substantially dependent upon the "insurer" for income at a time when the "insurer" had lost all capacity to generate income by personal services. But even if only one had been disabled, the other either could not have satisfied the entitlement then arising in the other or could only have done so by becoming at least a partial dependent of the other.

6. No explanation was given during the hearing as to why the "premium" - and the tax deduction said to be available in consequence of it, should have been fixed - as it was in the forms provided from Melbourne - at $6,480; or why it was limited to $6,480, rather than increased so as to eliminate for one spouse the potential of being liable at all to income tax; and for the other effect an even greater reduction in pending liability to income tax.

7. Then at some date between 20 June, 1980 and 5 February, 1981 (the date upon which the return of income claiming the deduction for $1,620 was signed), H reflected on the action taken in establishing the policy. He decided, and his wife agreed, that the cover should be reduced to something closer to her level of income as an employee and that the premium should be adjusted accordingly. Pending the return of her policy from Melbourne, when for the first time A and H would learn of the amount of monthly benefits specified in the policy as a result of the "actuarial calculations" of the promoters, that decision could only be in general terms. Until it was known to what extent monthly benefits would be reduced, it was impossible to effect a pro rata reduction in premium. The evidence of H was that the decision to reduce was reached on or before 30 June 1980. In so far as the decision was only in general terms I am prepared to accept that, but I do not accept that the decision was made more precisely within that period because the evidence was that the policy was not returned until some time in July. It was only then that a decision having contractual force and effect could be made.

8. When the policy was returned it was altered. That was done by the hand of H alone. Both the premium and monthly benefits were "reduced" by 75% - the former from $6,480 to $1,620; the latter from $6,642 to $1,660.50. No evidence was presented to explain what happened to the reduction in the premium: whether it was refunded, or treated as a pre-payment of future premiums. That decision having been made and implemented, the only amount claimed as a premium deduction in the return of income signed on 5 February, 1981 was $1,620. The receipt of a premium of $6,480 from H was not referred to in A's return of income.

9. From the foregoing it appears to be quite possible that the "plan" in question (as originally entered upon) was the same as that considered in case P. 68 (82 ATC 324). When one also takes into account spelling errors common to the documents considered in each case (e.g. "prostrate" for "prostate"; and "veneral" for "venereal") that possibility becomes a near certainty. In case P. 68, the Board took the view that the arrangement was "a fiscal nullity" and "sham". Further, the taxpayer in that case was personally the subject of quite trenchant criticism. I make no such criticism of this Applicant or of her husband. I think that they were honest in their evidence and in their intentions. However, they were naive in their acceptance of the promoter's proposals. He was also naive in his perception of the relevant legal principles - something which afflicted many in the legal profession at the time - and in his endeavours to rearrange what had initially been done so as to make effective for tax purposes what might have been ineffective. I am satisfied that what was done was done with honesty of purpose and with the intention that it should be legally effective.

10. The first contention for the Commissioner was that the alleged contractual bargain between A and her husband was not complete until after 30 June 1980, the consequence being that no loss or outgoing was incurred by A during the year of income. I reject that. By 30 June 1980, their bargain was complete even though a very important term of the agreement was not precisely known to them: they had mutually entrusted the determination of terms as to the quantum of monthly benefits to a third party. If parties choose to do so they are at liberty to bind themselves contractually in that way (cf. Fitzgerald v. Masters [1956] HCA 53; (1956) 95 CLR 420).

11. The next contention for the Commissioner was that the arrangement was a nullity, either because there had been no intention between the parties to bring into existence a legally enforceable relationship - so that an essential ingredient of the concept of "contract" was missing; or because the arrangements represented by the documents and other actions were merely a facade presented in an endeavour to conceal the true arrangement between the parties. However it is put, I reject the submission. Having heard the evidence of both A and H, I am quite satisfied that it was their intention to do whatever was necessary and to undertake such obligations as were required by the terms of the documents, so that they would bring about an alteration in their legal relationship which would qualify each of them as an insured person to claim an income tax deduction. Much was made of the lack of prudent practicality in entering into the arrangement and of the improbability of the "insurer" requiring "claims" to be presented with the formality contemplated by the policy and supported by independently procured evidence. However, it must be recognised that the parties were not dealing with each other at arms length and that, so long as they continued to enjoy a successful marriage, that situation was likely to continue.

12. Before proceeding to consider the argument for the Commissioner based upon the provisions of Section 260 of the Act it is appropriate to consider an even more basic issue. In my view the plan presented by the promoters was founded on a fallacy. In my opinion the premium received by A was assessable income in her hands. It was a reward for the service to be rendered by her of providing insurance cover. It constituted income on ordinary principles. It was no less income because it was a "one-off" transaction or because she was not registered under the Insurance Acts. It was a commercial transaction and it was just as much so when she received a premium as the "insurer" as it was when she outlaid a premium as the "insured". As it was not - it was claimed - received in the course of any business, deductions on account of claims, actual or contingent, would not be appropriate.

13. That being so, I find that on 20 January, the $6,480 expended by A constituted "a loss or outgoing" (in the sense required by section 51 of the Act) and the $6,480 she received that day was derived as assessable income. Further, the later events whereby the arrangement was altered could not operate retrospectively to alter what was done on 20 June, 1980. Had the relevant facts been fully disclosed to the Commissioner prior to his assessment and the return then been assessed according to the view I have stated, it would not have been necessary to consider arguments based upon section 260. Either the arrangement would have been viewed as a nullity, in which case there would have been neither derivation of income nor the incurring of loss or outgoing; or the transactions would have been identified as effective and self-cancelling, with the result presently contended for by the Commissioner.

14. In short, in my view H wasted the $480 paid to the promoter for services in providing forms and carrying out "actuarial calculations". I appreciate that that view leaves both A and H exposed to the risk of an amended assessment which could work unjustly against them by increasing their taxable incomes by $6,480 in each case by bringing to account as assessable income the premiums received. In the case of A that injustice could arise because she has only claimed $1,620 as a deduction; and in the case of H because, as he indicated in his evidence, his own objection to the claimed deduction has been abandoned. I hope that no such amended assessments will issue, because the correct result has already been achieved.

15. As to section 260 of the Act, I adopt the principles laid down by the Privy Council in Newton v. F.C.T. ((1958) [1958] UKPCHCA 1; 98 CLR 1) as recently affirmed by the decisions of the High Court of Australia in Gulland: Watson; and Pincus (85 ATC 4,765). At the outset it is appropriate to consider the question as to what was comprehended by the "arrangement". Was it limited to the action of A in effecting a policy with her husband with the intention that she should claim a tax deduction? or, did it extend to the mutuality of insurance arising by each spouse effecting insurance with the other with the intention that each could claim a tax deduction? I am of opinion that the latter approach is correct. It is the view which clearly demonstrates that the events of 20 June 1980 left the then financial position and prospects of both A and H quite unchanged except in two respects: the stage was set for both to claim a tax deduction; and each had assumed remarkably large contingent liabilities. I also observe that a particular facet of the "arrangement" was that the fact of the receipt of premium income should not be disclosed to the Commissioner, a course which, if carried to fruition, would both achieve the "purpose" and also have the "effect" of avoiding tax as contemplated by section 260.

16. That being so, I find that I am compelled to the view that I am "able to predicate - by looking at the overt acts by which (the arrangement) was implemented - that it was implemented in that particular way so as to avoid tax". I also find the transactions to be quite incapable of explanation by reference to ordinary business or family dealing, without necessarily being labelled as a means to avoid tax. Accordingly, I am satisfied that what was done was "absolutely void, as against the Commissioner, ....."

17. I would uphold the determination of the Commissioner upon the objection.

Postscript

18. The foregoing reasons for decision were prepared in draft following the conclusion of the hearing. They were not finalised because the question I had raised during the hearing as to whether the premium received by the Applicant constituted assessable income took the parties by surprise. I invited written submissions on the basis that the Applicant would have an opportunity to respond to any submission on behalf of the Commissioner. The Commissioner's submission came to hand but instead of a submission by the Applicant in reply, notice was given withdrawing the application for review advising that the Applicant concurred with the original decision of the Commissioner. No reasons for that decision were advanced. However, as the decision in P 68 (ante) was based on a concept of "fiscal nullity" and "sham"; as the former doctrine has not found favour with the Courts (Oakey Abattoir Pty Ltd 84 ATC 4,718); as the draft reasons were at an advanced stage; and as this may be but one of many similar applications before the Tribunal, I have decided to place these reasons on record and to direct that the Commissioner's decision be affirmed.
