Re Taxation Appeals [1993] AATA 148; (1993) 25 ATR 1159 (19 May 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. VT92/69

AAT No. 8717

Number of pages - 11

Income Tax

[1993] AATA 148; (1993) 25 ATR 1159

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

B.H Pascoe(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Icome Tax - amended assessment - excess expenditure method as a result of audit - increased wealth claimed to result from gambling - onus of proof

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936

Ma v FC of T 92 ATC 4374

Krew v FC of T (1971) 2 ATR 230, 71 ATC 4213

FC of T v Dalco 90 ATC 4088[1990] HCA 3; , 168 CLR 614

Ousley v FC of T 92 ATC 4486

Case 81 15 CTBR

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 18-19 February; 15, 18 March 1993

19:5:1993

Counsel for the Applicant : Mr J.W. de Wijn

Solicitors for the Applicant : Woodhams O'Keefe and Co

Counsel for the Respondent : Mr J. Lenczner

Solicitors for the Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in substitution therefor decides that the applicant taxpayer's objection should be allowed to the extent of excluding $134,577 of alleged omitted income and the remission of additional tax applicable thereto.

DECISION

B.H. PASCOE This application is for the review of the decision to disallow an objection against an amended assessment of income tax in respect of income of the year ended 30 June 1986.

2. In July 1989 the amended assessment was issued to include additional income of $143,067 as a result of an audit of the taxpayer's affairs. The audit commenced in August 1988 with particular reference to the years of income ended 30 June 1985, 1986 and 1987. The additional income assessed resulted from the preparation of an Excess Expenditure Statement, sometimes referred to as a 'T account', which compared the funds expended for the year with the identified funds available and assumes the shortfall of funds available to have been income.

3. The taxpayer maintained that the alleged shortfall could be accounted for by the following items:

Cash on hand at 1 July 1985 $40,000

Proceeds from sale of lining boards 6,000

Gambling winnings on horses 95,000

Gambling winnings on dogs 25,000

Incorrect entry in 'funds expended' 8,100

174,100

Less cash on hand at 30 June 1986 15,000

$159,100

4. The Excess Expenditure Statement prepared by the Australian Taxation officers listed on the expenditure side the balance of various bank accounts as at the end of the financial year, including an account in another name in Adelaide, and added expenditure shown in the income tax return, loan repayments, purchase of real estate, income tax paid, including a residential unit in the same name in Adelaide, and private expenditure. On the funds available side it listed bank account balances as at the commencement of the year, gross receipts shown in the income tax return, one specific gambling receipt from the TAB and paid by cheque and a loan from the taxpayer's brother. The expenditure added to $332,309 and the funds available to $189,242, leaving the discrepancy of $143,067.

5. In his evidence the taxpayer explained that until late 1984 he was employed as a painter and docker on the waterfront. However, during the year ended 30 June 1986, he carried on business as a proprietor of tea rooms and a gift shop in a Victorian country town. He had had a lifetime of gambling with a particularly successful period in 1985 and 1986. His gambling was always by cash and he maintained a store of cash secreted beyond a concealed panel under the staircase in his business premises. He was unable to provide any objective evidence or anything other than vague estimates of the amount of cash secreted at any time and stated that it 'might have got up to $50,000 or $60,000' and the figure of $40,000 at the commencement of the relevant year was 'about what I reckon I had'. His principal betting activities were on horse racing, primarily at Victorian country race meetings, but sometimes metropolitan and interstate meetings. Although not a consistent gambler at dog races ('I don't like the dogs'), he maintained that during the relevant period his brother was part-owner and trainer of a particularly successful dog and, based on information provided by his brother, he attended several dog race meetings specifically to place bets on the particular dog. He stated that at one particular meeting his brother had pre-arranged with their bookmakers to accept bets totalling $6000, which won $18,000. He had also gambled at casinos, particularly Adelaide, and at various 'swy' or two-up games.

6. The taxpayer had not maintained any record of his gambling activities and only after issue of the proposed adjustments of taxable income in 1989 did he seek to reconstruct an estimate of the relevant year's results. With a friend and the use of a number of race books from the period which he had retained, he prepared an indicative list of winnings. He also approached four bookmakers with whom he had bet regularly to provide him with an estimate of the net winnings during the year. As a cash bettor, no specific record of his winnings was available and the four bookmakers each signed a statement setting out an estimate of the net winnings of the taxpayer from each of them in the particular year ended 30 June 1986.

7. After purchasing a property in the country town in 1982, the taxpayer renovated the building prior to commencing a business of a gift shop and tea rooms. The building was originally built in 1870 and the taxpayer removed lining boards, hand made nails, doors, staircase and banister and parcelled them for sale. He maintained that this parcel was sold for $6000 during the relevant year.

8. The claim for an incorrect entry on the expenditure side of the 'T account' related to the inclusion by the tax auditor of $8100 being 12 instalments of $675 transferred from the taxpayer's cheque account to the credit of a separate fully drawn advance account with the same bank. As the balances of both accounts were included in the 'T account' at the beginning and end of the financial year, it was submitted that this item represented double counting. Mr de Wijn, for the taxpayer, offered to 'sit down with apples and oranges on the desk' to assist the Tribunal in understanding the accounting involved but this offer was declined, somewhat reluctantly.

9. Given that the most significant amounts within the taxpayer's explanation for the discrepancy related to alleged gambling winnings, it was not surprising that the bulk of the time of the hearing was devoted to the issue. In total five bookmakers gave evidence. Four were called by the applicant and one by the respondent. All provided sworn evidence that the taxpayer had been on a winning streak during the relevant period. The major difficulty with their evidence was that the taxpayer had always bet in cash and consequently no record was available of the amount or results of his betting activities with these bookmakers. At best, their evidence was based on estimates as to amounts and dates arrived at, some years after the event. Four of the bookmakers were those who, in 1989, provided the taxpayer with statements of his estimated net winnings from them during the year ended 30 June 1986. Although each of them had inserted the figure in his own handwriting, it was not clear whether in all cases the figure was their own independent estimate or was the figure suggested to them by the taxpayer. The figures provided in these statements varied from 'between $10,000 and 20,000' to '$30,000' but collectively added to between $95,000 and $100,000. Apart from the one bookmaker who was called by the respondent, they could not be shaken from their testimony that the taxpayer had been a consistent winner in this period and that the estimate of winnings in the period was reasonable. As submitted by Mr Lenczner, for the respondent Commissioner, it is difficult to accept their evidence without question when each had difficulty in now remembering when and where he signed the statement, no records were available and it is possible that, having committed to a signed statement in 1989, each was reluctant to provide any different view in a witness box in 1993. On the other hand, as Mr de Wijn submitted, none of these bookmakers had any social or other connection with the taxpayer, the only association had been with the taxpayer as a regular cash punter at the racecourse and each had appeared as result of a summons issued to him. Each also maintained in his sworn evidence that there had been no pressure applied to complete the signed statement.

10. The one bookmaker who was prepared to vary his view at the hearing from that provided in his signed 1989 statement was a Mr O, who was called by the respondent. In his case he had been requested by the Australian Taxation Office to extract from his records details of all cash bets in excess of $400 during a period of the relevant year and the results of those bets. This amount was selected by Mr O as the taxpayer had always placed substantial bets and the arrangement, as was the arrangement with all the bookmakers who gave evidence, was that the bookmaker would only take bets to a maximum of $3000 potential payment. The result of this exercise was that the punters concerned had lost a net $863. The respondent sought to use this as evidence that it was more likely than not that the taxpayer had lost rather than won during the period. Mr O had signed a statement in 1989 that, in his opinion, the taxpayer had won between $10,000 and $20,000 from him during the year ended 30 June 1986. In the witness box Mr O agreed that, if he now had the opportunity to change his earlier statement, he would amend it to 'from nothing to $20,000'. Nevertheless he maintained that his memory was that the taxpayer was a winner at that time and that it was not possible for him to say with any certainty which of the bets over $400 extracted from his records related to the taxpayer.

11. The fifth bookmaker, Mr E, was only summoned to give evidence some two days before the hearing and had not been approached by the taxpayer, as were the other four, to sign a statement subsequent to the tax audit. He was able to be relatively specific as to the time during which the taxpayer bet with him based on the dates of obtaining his licence in 1984, obtaining a rails position at relevant race meetings in 1985, leaving his other full-time employment in late 1986, and losing a large amount on the stock market in October 1987. His evidence was that the taxpayer placed bets with him over a period of approximately 10 months during the relevant year. At the end of that period Mr E approached the taxpayer at a race meeting and asked him if he would consider selling his 'tips' and also asked if he would not place any further bets with Mr E as he could not afford to lose any further money to the taxpayer. He maintained that the taxpayer had been a consistent winner over the period and it was likely that the taxpayer had won some $25-30,000 from Mr E during the period.

12. The brother of the taxpayer also appeared as a witness. His evidence was directed primarily at the part ownership by him of a racing dog which had a very successful record during the relevant year. In total the dog had won 35 times out of 44 starts. He stated that the taxpayer regularly attended race meetings in which the dog was participating and that he was aware that the taxpayer was a substantial bettor. At one particular race meeting he had, at the request of the taxpayer, made prior arrangements with three bookmakers to accept bets of $2000 each on the dog which won at odds of 3-1. As he was required to attend the dog prior to the race preceding the one in which the dog was participating, he was unable to give direct evidence of actual bets placed by the taxpayer and any winnings from those bets.

13. In relation to the alleged sale of lining boards, etcetera, an antique dealer who operated from a shop close by the shop acquired and renovated by the taxpayer gave evidence of the removal of the lining boards, the approximately quantity and possible value. He considered the alleged sale price as being high but conceivable. He was unable to give any evidence as to the date of sale, the sale price or the purchaser. From the evidence of the taxpayer and the antique dealer, it seems clear that the lining boards were removed some time before the commencement of the year ended 30 June 1986. The taxpayer stated that he purchased the shop property in 1982 and commenced renovations soon after. The lining boards were removed, denailed, tied in packets of 20 and stored in an old garage. His memory was that the sale price was $6000 including some doors, the staircase, banister and hand made nails. It is clear that he assumed the proceeds were received in August 1985 and from a person named 'Meeds' or 'Mees' because a bank cheque for $6000 purchased in such a name was deposited to the taxpayer's bank account at that time. Unfortunately, subsequent evidence, accepted by the taxpayer, demonstrated that the particular amount had come from a nephew with a similar sounding name for the purchase of exotic birds owned by the taxpayer. Consequently there is no evidence that the proceeds were exactly $6000 or were received in the particular financial year.

14. The accountant who prepared and lodged income tax returns for the taxpayer, attended the original interview of the taxpayer by two officers of the Australian Taxation Office and assisted in providing subsequent information to those officers gave evidence at the hearing. The taxpayer was a small client of the accountant who saw him on an annual basis. He knew little of the taxpayer's affairs other than the business details provided in the income tax return. He took a strong view that the amount of the alleged discrepancy could not have arisen from the tea rooms and gift shop business carried on by the taxpayer during the relevant year as the business could not have generated an income of that size. He was questioned by counsel for the respondent about a discrepancy between the totals of sales and purchases recorded in the books of account and the figures shown in the income tax return for the year ended 30 June 1986. The return figure for sales was some $24,000 higher than shown in the books and the return figure for purchasers some $30,218 higher than the book figure. Although there were no clear working papers produced to explain these discrepancies, the accountant explained them as having arisen from cash purchases, the bulk of which would have been paid for out of cash sales. The accountant had limited recollection of the interview in November 1988 but recalled the taxpayer being resentful of being asked questions which he considered unnecessary or unjustified.

15. Both Australian Taxation officers appeared as witnesses. Neither provided any evidence or opinion as to the likely source of the alleged understatement of income. They described the taxpayer's attitude during the November 1988 interview as being a mixture of evasive, aggressive and cooperative. Mr Lenczner, in his submission and his line of questions, was pursuing the argument that the non-disclosure of the taxpayer during the interview or at any later time prior to the issue of the excess expenditure statement of the bank account and property in a false name, any large balance of cash held and the quantum of betting wins in a particular period, went against the credit of the taxpayer. It is clear, however, that the record of interview supported by memory of happenings nearly five years ago did not provide any accurate record of the precise questions asked of the taxpayer or his answers. Certainly, it is not clear what was asked or commented on relative to gambling winnings. The standard questionnaire used in the interview contains nine questions relating to betting and gambling. Against one question, 'Can you estimate a total amount which you consider you were winning or losing overall at any particular time in prior years', was noted '$23,000, $10-11,000 in 1985 TAB winnings'. From the evidence it appeared that the $23,000 was one big win by the taxpayer in 1982 and the $10-11,000 was a particular win on the 1985 Melbourne Cup. The Australian Taxation officers could not recall any detail of questions asked and responses on the subject of gambling transactions, the taxpayer's accountant considered that horse racing activities occupied more time at the interview than anything else and the taxpayer's recollection was that he had been asked to name some of his winnings. No responses were noted against any of the eight other questions in this section of the questionnaire.

16. The three remaining witnesses were bank officials who provided documentation and evidence of the transfers of funds to the Adelaide bank account and to the Adelaide solicitors who acted in the purchase of the residential unit.

17. It is the transfer of these funds which provides one of the difficulties in assessing the evidence in this case. Within a time period of less than two weeks, the taxpayer transferred to the Adelaide bank account and to the solicitors a total of almost $94,000 in seven separate transactions. The transfers to the bank account were in three amounts of $12,000, $28,000 and $5000 lodged at branches of the bank in Sydney, Glen Waverley and Glenferrie. Two bank cheques from two different banks in Stawell were obtained for cash on the same day and forwarded to the solicitors in the amounts of $10,000 and $12,000. An amount of $24,000 was transferred to the solicitors through Westpac, South Australian Head Office and the taxpayer handed $2860 in cash to the solicitors. Initially, the taxpayer, when asked how he put the money together to purchase the unit, responded 'It was funds drawn out of the Adelaide account and I took it in cash and I remember something about getting bank cheques. I just can't hit it on the head'. When the various deposits were individually put to him he was unable to recollect the specific circumstances and details and could only assume that the varying locations of deposits such as Glen Waverley and Glenferrie were on the way to race meetings at Sandown and Caulfield respectively, at Sydney while visiting friends and at Stawell en route to a race meeting. He could only assume, also, that the decision to purchase the unit was made because he had won substantial amounts at the relevant time and had sufficient funds available to meet the cost of the unit. Given the very short period in which the transfer of funds was made, it must be assumed that the decision to purchase and the execution of a purchase contract was made some time prior to the first transfer of funds. The taxpayer was put on notice of an audit of his affairs in the latter part of 1988 and was aware of the alleged shortfall in disclosed income by May 1989, some three years after the purchase of the unit. It is somewhat surprising that he was unable to recollect the circumstances which led to having some $94,000 available at the particular point of time and the transfer of those funds to Adelaide. Only when it was raised at the hearing did he recollect that $18,000 of the $28,000 deposited through the Glen Waverley branch of the bank was a bank cheque purchased by the mother of his then de facto wife. He maintained that this was the repayment of a loan made to her some three months earlier to allow her to purchase a motor vehicle prior to receiving money due on retirement from employment.

18. The taxpayer stated that the reason fo the purchase of the unit in Adelaide under an assumed name was his desire to have a 'hideaway' which nobody knew about, particularly people from the waterfront and ex wives. The need to maintain secrecy about the purchase was put forward as the reason for transfers of funds away from his home town. One of the transfers through a Stawell bank was by purchase for cash of a bank cheque where the taxpayer made out the requisition in another assumed name and false address. He maintained that 'I didn't want anyone to know I was buying the flat'. He admitted to having used several aliases whilst working on the waterfront and when asked by counsel for the respondent 'Does that mean, that if it suits your interest, you are quite prepared to tell an untruth', he answered simply 'Yes'. Counsel for the taxpayer submitted that this admission should be taken in the context of questions relating to use of an assumed name and banking transactions, and not in a more general sense.

19. The taxpayer admitted to several convictions for criminal offences during the 1970's and a conviction when aged 16 for a minor offence involved with SP betting. Apart from the respondent's view that these convictions have some relevance to the matter of the taxpayer's credit, no other relevance to the particular issues of this case was apparent. If anything, the earliest conviction adds to the taxpayer's evidence of having been involved with gambling on horse racing from an early age.

20. From the evidence given by the taxpayer, it is clear that the amount included in the Excess Expenditure Statement for personal living expenses was considerably less than the likely actual expenses of the taxpayer. He visited the Adelaide casino regularly prior to and after acquiring the residential unit, visited Sydney and travelled regularly to distant race meetings. He stated that after early wins from his betting system at the Casino he then commenced losing. It was not clear whether the subsequent losses were in excess of the original winnings or equal to them. During cross-examination it was put to the taxpayer that, to the $95,000 alleged to have been won on horse racing:

'You would have to add another 25,000 or 20,000, whatever is the

figure Mr E nominated?- - - No, you wouldn't. Why not?- - - I was

trying to impress a young lady at the time. So you were spending

money?- - - Bloody oath. Well it is a lot of money to spend on a

young lady?- - - More than one.'

In answer to other questions on likely quantum of winnings and the possibility of having more than $15,000 in cash at year end, the taxpayer responded:

'Well, not necessarily because I don't mind spending money when

I've got money.'

The Tribunal was not invited to increase the expenditure and resulting shortfall in funds available, but counsel for the respondent suggested that if the Tribunal accepted that the taxpayer had derived money from gambling wins during the relevant year it would also need to consider whether that money had been applied by the taxpayer in the expenditure shown on the 'T account' or in other ways not so included as expenditure when the statement was prepared. It is clear that the expenditure shown in the 'T account' was conservative and the taxpayer must have had funds available additional to the net discrepancy indicated.

21. The decision in this case turns on whether or not the Tribunal is satisfied that the taxpayer has adequately discharged the burden of proof contained in section 190(b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (the 'Assessment Act') which requires that 'the burden of proving that the assessment is excessive shall lie upon the taxpayer'. The not unusual difficulty here is that the taxpayer relied wholly on estimates of cash on hand and gambling wins made nearly three years after the end of the particular year of income and evidence provided to the Tribunal nearly seven years after that year. This reliance on estimates was commented on by Burchett J in the case of Ma v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 92 ATC 4373, where he said (at 4379):

'Furthermore, the making of estimates upon inexact evidence, which

is so much a feature of both judicial and administrative

decision-making, cannot be uniquely excluded from appeals against

betterment assessments. To refuse to consider the credit, not only

of the applicant, but also of his independent and unchallenged

witnesses, simply because the effect of the evidence was to support

his accountant's generalisations about double-counting rather than

to hit upon a precise figure, was to fall into an error of law. If

authority be needed for this proposition, it may be found in the

decision of Walsh J in Krew v FCT (1971) 2 ATR 230; 71 ATC 4213, to

which Mr Gibb, with his usual frankness, referred me. That was a

betterment case, bearing some similarity to the present, in which

Walsh J, in various parts of his judgment, acted on evidence that

"substantial" portions of sums of money represented the proceeds of

gambling; expressly eschewed (at ATC 4223) any attempt to reach a

"precise result"; and acknowledged (at ATC 4224) that his decision

as to certain "large payments" lacked "satisfactory evidence of a

specific kind", concluding nevertheless that it was "probable that

a large part of the money was derived from gambling", with the

result that "a somewhat arbitrary decision" was required, to some

extent in favour of the taxpayer.'

That case was an appeal against an earlier decision of this Tribunal where the taxpayer argued that the alleged understatement of income arose primarily from betting transactions. Burchett J discussed the interpretation of the Tribunal of the decision in FC of T v Dalco 90 ATC 4088[1990] HCA 3; , 168 CLR 614. In relation to this interpretation he said (at page 4378):

'The applicant contended that the tribunal also erred in law in

the propositions which it considered were to be derived from Dalco,

supra. I think that ground too is made good. Dalco insists that a

taxpayer in the position of the applicant must show, according to

the civil onus, that the assessment is excessive. But it does not

hobble him in the ways that the tribunal's propositions suggest.

If, by any means, he can show the assessment is excessive, he is

entitled to seek relief.

In Dalco (at CLR 621) Brennan J (with whom and with Toohey J the

other judges agreed, Deane J qualifying his agreement as "general")

declared:

(T)he ultimate question for the court hearing the appeal is not

whether the grounds have been made out but whether the amount

assessed as taxable income is wrong. The burden which rests on a

taxpayer is to prove that the assessment is excessive and that

burden is not necessarily discharged by showing an error by the

Commissioner in forming a judgment as to the amount of the

assessment. (Emphasis added.)

Toohey J (at CLR 631) said that the taxpayer may discharge the

onus under s 190(b) "by pointing to some error of computation", and

added:

A taxpayer does not necessarily discharge the onus of showing that

an assessment is excessive, merely by showing that moneys treated

by the Commissioner as income are in truth not the income of the

taxpayer, through that may be a step in demonstrating his or her

taxable income to be less than the assessment. (Emphasis added.)

These carefully qualified statements cannot be read as enunciating

the absolute rule favoured by the tribunal. Indeed, as Toohey J (at

CLR 629) made clear, Dalco was a very special appeal:

(I)t was not the taxpayer's case that he had demonstrated to

Yeldham J that his taxable income for the years in question was

less than the assessments concerned.

So Mr Dalco was contending for an extremely narrow and technical

rule, the rejection of which does not imply that the High Court

embraced an equally narrow and technical rule in an opposite sense;

rather it adhered to broad and familiar principle. Brennan J (at

CLR 624) said: "The manner in which a taxpayer can discharge (the)

burden (of proof) varies the circumstances." This was also the

point Deane J emphasised in his brief comment, beginning (at CLR

626) with the words: "In the circumstances of the present

case . . .". It should not be overlooked that the error alleged

in Dalco did not deny that the very sums erroneously characterised

as income might in fact have been received in another character as

income; here, by contrast, the error alleged, if made out, denies

the receipt of the sums in question, being (at worst for the

applicant) simple re-banking, in any character but that of capital.

As was pointed out in argument, the position before the tribunal,

when the taxpayer has proved, in the words of Brennan J, that "the

amount assessed as taxable income is wrong", is neither exactly as

it is before a court (the situation in Dalco itself), nor exactly

as it was before the Boards of Review. The tribunal, under s 43 of

its Act, is expressly empowered to set aside the assessment and

remit the matter to the Commissioner for reconsideration. If the

taxpayer has shown by evidence that the only receipts which could

possibly be income were the amounts paid into the bank account, and

that many of those amounts represented the same capital reinvested

after previous withdrawals, he has shown that an assessment based

upon the counting as income of all the payments (save certain, which

the argument set on one side) must be excessive. The question,

then, is whether he has proved sufficient to entitle him to the

setting aside of the amended assessments and their replacement by

assessments in the amounts of the original assessments, assuming

assessments had earlier issued upon his returns, or to entitle him

only to the setting aside of the amended assessments and a referral

back to the Commissioner, or to some other decision.'

Mr de Wijn submitted that this case is one which fits the description stated by Burchett J in Ma (supra) (at page 4377) where the learned Judge stated:

'But if a taxpayer denies any undisclosed source of income, provides

acceptable evidence of how he spends his time, and demonstrates a

reasonable explanation for any appearance of the possession of

assets, he will generally discharge his burden of proof unless some

positive reason is shown why he is to be disbelieved. Any other

view would introduce a degree of arbitrariness into liability for

tax.'

22. It was difficult to accept the taxpayer as a completely reliable and honest witness. His memory was somewhat selective and it is difficult to believe that he had no clear memory of how some $94,000 was produced in a little over one week to satisfy an obligation to purchase a residential unit made prior to the banking of the funds. There was doubt on the accuracy of the figures shown in his income tax return for sales and purchases of his declared business. He stated that it was unlikely that he had retained more than '$50,000 or $60,000' in cash at any one time. Nevertheless, I am satisfied that he had been and was in the relevant year a regular gambler at horse racing and dog racing meetings and more probably than not won substantial sums during the year of income. This view was clearly supported by the sworn evidence of five bookmakers and the taxpayer's brother. The difficulty is that neither the taxpayer or any of his supporting witnesses could provide adequate objective evidence of the precise quantum of those winnings.

23. One feature of this case which lends support to the view that the year ended 30 June 1986 produced abnormal amounts and it is more likely than not that the evidence of the taxpayer and the bookmakers that he had an abnormal 'winning streak' in that year should be accepted, is the fact that notwithstanding the audit of at least three years and the size of the alleged shortfall of disclosed income in the year ended 30 June 1986, amended assessments were issued for that one year only. The Australian Taxation officers stated that Excess Expenditure Statements were prepared for the years ending 30 June 1985 and 1987 but resulted in an overstatement of income in 1985 of approximately $27,000 and an understatement in 1987 of approximately $16,000. No further attempt was made to pursue the possible issue of amended assessments for these or any other year notwithstanding the relatively large alleged shortfall in the one year ended 30 June 1986. In the absence of any specific or even implied proposition of an alternative source of income, it is difficult to contemplate an additional business or other income earning source which was likely to have generated the amount alleged within one income year. The position is somewhat akin to that considered in the High Court by Walsh J in Krew v FC of T 71 ATC 4213 where the appellant himself was not accepted as a reliable witness but His Honour then went on to say (at page 4219):

'That view of his testimony is very damaging to the appellant's

case. But it would be wrong to treat it as leading necessarily to

the conclusion that his appeal must fail. A witness upon whose word

one cannot rely may yet give evidence which is true in part. There

are some features of the evidence which assist the appellant in his

assertion that the funds in dispute came from gambling. The

evidence does not exclude the possibility that the appellant made

money from dealings of the same kind as those with which his regular

business was concerned but which were not recorded as transactions

of that business. Further the evidence does not exclude the

possibility that the appellant made profits from some enterprise

other than his regular business, which does not seem to have

occupied his time fully. But the evidence does show that it is

improbable that unrecorded transactions were occurring regularly

in his ordinary business of such magnitude that they would explain

all the accruals to the appellant's wealth. But they may have

accounted for some of them. The circumstance that the respondent

has been unable to produce any positive evidence as to the sources

of additional income is, I have indicated, a matter of significance

in considering this case. It may be acknowledged that, generally

speaking, it is not easy to conceal a secret source of substantial

income derived from personal exertion.'

In that case the learned Judge was considering alleged understatements of income in each of sixteen consecutive years where the taxpayer argued that the increased wealth arose from successful gambling. Here we are concerned with one year only.

24. Mr de Wijn also referred the Tribunal to an old Board of Review decision reported at 15 CTBR Case 81 where the Chairman, Mr R.R. Gibson, commented on the difficulty of dealing with estimated gambling winnings and said (at page 516):

'If the taxpayer's onus were a matter of sheer quantitive proof of

the amount of his net punting gains during each year it could not

be held that the onus was discharged. But if a similar onus were

placed on every person whose receipts for any year were, because of

successful punting, in excess of such of those receipts as were

definitely of an assessable nature, I do not see how any arbitrary

assessment based on the whole of those receipts could ever be proved

to be excessive unless the person concerned confined his punting to

booked bets and could produce documentary evidence of the results

of his betting from every bookmaker concerned. My opinion is that

where the Commissioner arbitrarily assesses the whole of a

taxpayer's receipts or estimated receipts from whatever source they

may have been derived, and the taxpayer establishes to the

satisfaction of the Board that none of his receipts in excess of

those proved to be assessable income could have been derived from

a source which is productive of assessable income, he has discharged

his onus of proving that the assessment is (pro tanto) excessive.

If strict proof of the actual amount of non-assessable receipts were

essential any assessment which treated as assessable income the

whole of the receipts of a taxpayer who during the year of income

was a successful punter would be practically invulnerable.'

25. It is appropriate to consider the claim of cash on hand at the commencement of the year and the end of the year together with the alleged gambling winnings. Because the evidence of the gambling winnings was not precise as to time, the taxpayer's claimed 'winning streak' could well have commenced prior to 1 July 1985 and may have ceased or even continued after 30 June 1986. The evidence from the bookmakers was that his betting was nowhere near as successful in later years. If the view is accepted that it was more likely than not that the taxpayer won the amount of $120,000 during the year, then it would seem to follow that the net $25,000 of cash reserves allegedly used during the year also came from betting winnings. Having regard to all the evidence, I am of the view that the taxpayer should be given the benefit of any doubt as to the quantum. In the absence of any other suggested source of revenue and the acceptance that substantial sums were, on the balance of probabilities, won during the period from gambling activities, I am satisfied that the taxpayer has discharged the onus of proof that the amended assessment of income tax in dispute is excessive. To find otherwise would be to ignore the sworn evidence of five bookmakers attesting to an extraordinary winning period. There was no valid suggestion of collusion or coercion in relation to their evidence. Whilst recognising that their evidence consisted of estimates as to quantum and timing made some time after the relevant period, I am of the view that their views were properly held and add significant weight to the taxpayer's testimony. This case can be contrasted with that considered by Olney J in Ousley v Commissioner of Taxation 92 ATC 4486, where he said (at page 4495):

'The case the applicant mounted was that he had won money gambling

as a punter but the tribunal did not believe him. That was not

necessarily the end of the appellant's case. Obviously, if there

was other evidence upon which the necessary finding could be made,

then the appellant would have been entitled to succeed. But there

was no other evidence probative of the facts asserted.'

26. The respondent sought to allow a possibility that the taxpayer's activities in keeping and breeding birds may have been more significant than admitted by the taxpayer and could have resulted in the production of assessable income. I doubt that this was a serious suggestion and, apart from evidence of two sales of birds to a nephew, one prior to the relevant year and one within it, there was no evidence to suggest that the taxpayer's involvement with birds was any greater than the hobby put by him in his evidence.

27. Prior to setting out any decision in relation to the effect of my conclusions on the question of gambling winnings, it is appropriate to deal with the two specific claims of the receipt of proceeds from the sale of lining boards and the double counting in the calculation of expenditure in the excess expenditure statement.

28. In relation to the alleged sale of lining boards, I cannot be satisfied that the taxpayer received $6000 or, if he did receive that particular amount, he did so during the year ended 30 June 1986. It is clear that the taxpayer's memory of amount and date was assisted primarily by the knowledge of a bank cheque for $6000 received in August 1985. When subsequently proved that this cheque had nothing whatsoever to do with the sale of lining boards, the taxpayer was left with no objective evidence of the sale and, if anything, the evidence available suggested that sale and receipt of any proceeds occurred prior to 1 July 1985.

29. The taxpayer claimed that an entry of $8100 in the list of expenditure was included incorrectly. The actual entry showed:

'LOAN REPAYMENTS

(675 x 12) 8100

-2490 5610'

The 12 instalments of $675 were transferred from one bank account to another and the balances of both were also included in the statement. The $2490 represented interest on the fully drawn advance account which was claimed as a deduction in the taxpayer's income tax return. This was previously included in the excess expenditure statement included within the total expenses 'as per return'. Neither of these amounts were correctly included under the loan repayment heading and the net increase of expenditure of $5610 should be excluded. During the hearing, the Australian Taxation officers acknowledged that this adjustment should be made.

30. The question of an appropriate finding in relation to the taxpayer's gambling winnings poses difficulties. Whilst satisfied that, on the balance of probabilities, the taxpayer did win substantial sums from betting on horse and dog racing, there were some very unsatisfactory aspects of the evidence. The taxpayer was very relaxed, sometimes flippant, during his time in the witness box. I am sure he would not object to the expression of view that he produced an impression of being 'a likeable rogue'. Much of his testimony was the result of 'best guesses' although quite unshaken by cross-examination. No positive reason for disbelieving him was shown. Mr Lenczner sought to emphasise that the taxpayer had conscientiously and deliberately concealed information from the tax auditor and, if he had nothing to hide he would have disclosed the assets acquired under an assumed name and the extent of his gambling winnings. There is no doubt that a considerable amount of time and expense would have been saved if he had done so. It was suggested that the method of transferring funds to Adelaide indicated an activity designed to hide something. For the taxpayer, these submissions were explained as a desire to keep the knowledge of his Adelaide 'hideaway' secret and the approach of a person who was confident that there was no undisclosed income and who disliked Government agents asking questions.

31. The result of all of this is that the Tribunal is faced, in the words of Burchett J in the case of Ma (supra), with 'the making of estimates upon inexact evidence'. Given that 'a somewhat arbitrary decision' (Krew supra, page 4224) is necessary in this case, I propose to regard the taxpayer's answer to the alleged additional income of $143,067 as follows:

Result of Gambling Winnings $

Cash on Hand at 1 July 1985 40,000

Winnings during year 120,000

160,000

less Cash on Hand 30 June 1986 15,000

145,000

less additional private expenditure $

not included in 'T account' 16,033 128,967

Sale of Lining Boards 6,000

Incorrect entry under 'Funds Expended' 8,100

$143,067

If the taxpayer's arguments were accepted in full, the amended Excess Expenditure Statement for the relevant year would result in an excess of funds available of $16,033. Based on the taxpayer's evidence, it is clear that private expenditure in the year must have been well in excess of the amount allowed for in the Statement. Given that all of the evidence was based on estimates and the unlikely possibility of the taxpayer's income being overstated, I consider it appropriate to limit the taxpayer's claims of additional funds available to the actual amounts claimed reduced by additional expenditure equal to the purported excess of $16,033.

32. I, therefore, find in favour of the taxpayer in his claim for betting winnings, against the taxpayer in his claim for proceeds of sale of lining boards and in favour of the taxpayer to the extent of $5610 in his claim for an incorrect entry. The result is that the amended assessment of income tax should be reduced by the excision of $134,577 of alleged omitted income. Given the unsatisfactory way in which his business income was arrived at for his income tax return, it is not unreasonable to believe that the balance of $8490 could well have arisen from that business.

33. The taxpayer's objection to the amended assessment included objection to the quantum of additional tax imposed under section 223 of the Assessment Act. No argument or submission on this question was put at the hearing. I am of the view, therefore, that the method of calculation of the additional tax imposed, being a per annum component at the rate of 14.026 per cent and a culpability component of 45 per cent, is appropriate. The additional income of $8490 remaining should have additional tax at these rates imposed.

34. The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in substitution therefor decides that the applicant taxpayer's objection should be allowed to the extent of excluding $134,577 of alleged omitted income and the remission of additional tax applicable thereto.
