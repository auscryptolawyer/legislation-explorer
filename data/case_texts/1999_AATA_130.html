REASONS FOR DECISION



8 March 1999
J Block, Senior Member






1. The objection decision in this matter is the disallowance by the Respondent of the Applicant’s objection dated 5 January 1996 against an amended assessment dated 7 December 1992 and referable to the year ending 30 June 1992 (“relevant year”).

2. (a) The Applicant was represented by Mr Roger Hamilton of counsel, instructed by Doherty Partners, while the Respondent was represented by Mr Ian Young of counsel, instructed by the Australian Government Solicitor.



(b) The Tribunal had before it the T documents and also Supplementary T Documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act; the Tribunal also accepted into evidence a number of exhibits which will be referred to more fully later in these Reasons.



3. (a) I intend in this clause 3 to refer to a number of aspects of these Reasons designed to make them easier to read. This Decision must be confidential; it is usual in these circumstances to refer to persons (both natural and corporate) by designated letters; I think that this method has the effect of making the Reasons difficult to follow. For this reason I intend to allot pseudonyms to all of the persons and companies who are relevant to or figure in these Reasons; at the same time I intend at this early stage to set out certain abbreviations which will be used throughout these Reasons. It is convenient also in this clause 3 to set out certain relevant corporate structures.



(b) The Applicant was the purchaser of the Assets under an agreement entitled “Sale of Assets Agreement” (“Sale Agreement”) made on 18th October 1991 (“Completion Date”); the term “Assets” was defined in the Sale Agreement, and that definition is set out later in these Reasons.



(c) The Information Memorandum (“IM”) which is Annexure B to Adam’s statement (Exhibit A3) sets out the corporate structure referable to the Vendors; each of the Vendors had a name which was prefaced by the tradename of important products imported and sold in the Businesses; I have (throughout) substituted the neutral word “Product” for the tradename; on this basis, Product International Limited (“International”) was the owner of all of the issued shares in each of Product Manufacturing Pty Limited (“Manufacturing”), Product Corporation Pty Limited (“Corporation”), Product Distributing Pty Limited (“Distributing”) and Product Franchising Pty Limited (“Franchising”); page 3 of the IM states that:



(1) International was a holding company;

(2) Manufacturing supplied products to Corporation, dealers and agents;

(3) Corporation was a retailer of spare parts, vacuum cleaner bags etc, and provided a service facility;

(4) Distributing was an importer of finished goods and components for vacuums and electronic products for supply to Manufacturing;

(5) Franchising was non-operating; moreover and in respect of Franchising, a Receiver was not appointed.

(d) The diagram at page 3 of the IM also refers to another manufacturing subsidiary in Singapore; although the Applicant contemporaneously acquired the Singapore operations through Ladder Singapore, the Singapore operations are not relevant to this Decision.



(e) (1) The term “Vendors” (and each a “Vendor”) or the “Group” refers collectively to International, Manufacturing, Corporation and Distributing.



(2) The purchaser under the Sale Agreement was the Applicant, a company associated with Ladder Pty Limited (“Ladder”), and also Ladder Pte Ltd incorporated in Singapore (“Ladder Singapore”).



(3) The Guarantors under the Sale Agreement were Ladder, Ladder Singapore and each of Cross and Ramsey.



(f) The Sale Agreement was preceded by a number of offers made by Ladder; in relation to the Sale Agreement, Ladder procured that the Applicant was the purchaser and that it changed its name to incorporate the word “Product”; in respect of the Applicant, Cross was (and is) a director.



(g) International was owned as to 52.51% by Australian Corporation Limited (“Australian”); Silver was the chairman of Australian.

(i) Hay (who is sometimes referred to as the “Solicitor”) acted as such for the Applicant in relation to the Sale Agreement; (Adam told the Tribunal in his evidence that he and Hay had offices in the same building and had frequent contact with each other).



(j) The term “Receiver” refers to Hanson, who was appointed as Receiver and Manager in respect of each of the four Vendors on 5th July 1991 (the “Appointment Date”) at the instance of a bank (referred to as “the Bank”) which held a first charge over the assets of the Vendors; the Receiver was a member of a leading firm of accountants and auditors in Sydney; he was assisted by Playfair, a member of the same firm.



(k) In respect of the Bank, Simon (whose title was “Senior Manager Corporate Recovery”) dealt with the Bank’s claim against the Group.



(l) The term “Liquidation Accountants” refers to another leading Sydney accounting firm who specialise in liquidations and who prepared certain documents referable to Manufacturing; (Exhibit A5).

(n) Certain definitions contained in the Sale Agreement are reproduced later in these reasons.

8. The legislation which is relevant for the purpose of this matter comprises sections 36(1), 36(8) and 36(9) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, which are set out in this clause 8 as follows:

“36(1) Value of property assessable

Subject to this section, where:

(a) a taxpayer disposes by sale, gift or otherwise of property being trading stock, standing or growing crops, crop-stools, or trees which have been planted and tended for the purpose of sale;

(b) that property constitutes or constituted the whole or part of the assets of a business which is or was carried on by the taxpayer; and

(c) the disposal was not in the ordinary course of carrying on that business;

the value of that property shall be included in the assessable income of the taxpayer, and the person acquiring that property shall be deemed to have purchased it at a price equal to that value.

36(8) Value of trading stock and profit on disposal

For the purposes of this section and section 36AAA:

(a) the value of any property or live stock shall be:

(i) the market value of the property or live stock on the day of the disposal; or

(b) (not relevant).

36(9) Value of “property”

Notwithstanding subsection (8), the value for the purposes of this section of any property disposed of by the taxpayer after 7 April 1978 shall, if the Commissioner so determines, be such value as the Commissioner considers reasonable, having regard to:

(a) the cost to the taxpayer of the property;

(b) if, in any agreement entered into in connection with the disposal of the property, an amount was specified as the value of the property or as the consideration received or receivable in respect of the disposal - the amount so specified;

(c) if, before the property was disposed of, an agreement or arrangement (whether or not enforceable by legal proceedings and whether or not intended to be so enforceable) was entered into, or an understanding was reached, as a result of which, at any time after the disposal took place, there has been, or there could reasonably be expected to be, a substantial reduction in the value of the property - that agreement, arrangement or understanding;

(ca) if, before the property was disposed of by the taxpayer, an agreement or arrangement (whether or not enforceable by legal proceedings and whether or not intended to be so enforceable) was entered into, or an understanding was reached, under which, or by reason of which, the person or persons who acquired the property from the taxpayer was or were under an obligation, or could reasonably be expected, to dispose of the property to another person or other persons (whether or not that other person was, or those other persons included, the taxpayer) for a consideration less than the market value of the property at the time when it was disposed of by the taxpayer - that agreement, arrangement or understanding;

(d) if the disposal of the property by the taxpayer or the acquisition of the property by the person or persons who acquired the property arose out of, or was made in the course of, a transaction, operation, undertaking, scheme or arrangement that was entered into or carried out for the purpose, or for purposes that included the purpose, of securing that a person who, if the transaction, operation, undertaking, scheme or arrangement had not been entered into or carried out, would have been liable to pay income tax in respect of a year of income would not be liable to pay income tax in respect of that year of income or would be liable to pay less income tax in respect of that year of income than that person would have been liable to pay if the transaction, operation, undertaking, scheme or arrangement had not been entered into or carried out - that transaction, operation, undertaking, scheme or arrangement;

(e) if the disposal of the property by the taxpayer or the acquisition of the property by the person or persons who acquired the property arose out of, or was made in the course of, a transaction, operation, undertaking, scheme or arrangement that the Commissioner is satisfied was by way of dividend stripping or was similar to a transaction, operation, undertaking, scheme or arrangement by way of dividend stripping - that transaction, operation, undertaking, scheme or arrangement; and

(f) any other matters that the Commissioner considers relevant.”

13. (a) In effect, and as to the evidence of Adam, the Tribunal is asked to accept that Hay, referred to as an extremely careful solicitor by Adam would negotiate a lengthy, and in some respects complex agreement without once going through it with his client, would seek amendments without instructions, and would leave the meeting before the matter was concluded. The Tribunal does not think that this is the sort of conduct which one would expect of a careful (and even pedantic) solicitor.



(b) Mr Hamilton, at the submission stage, pointed out that there are times when documents are very rushed and when there is not always time for detailed instructions; he referred by way of example to 30th June deadline tax-driven transactions. Leaving aside the fact that the 18th October 1991 was not a year end date and the fact that there was no evidence as to why the matter was so urgent, one would have thought that a careful solicitor, if pushed into acting hastily such that he was unable to take the care normally required, would write some form of letter to his client seeking to cover himself against possible repercussions.



(c) Nor is it clear to the Tribunal why the Bank and the Receiver had the negotiation strength with which to compel completion on the Completion Date after documents had been sent out only on 14 October 1991. The IM, advertisement and consultation process which commenced in July 1991 and ended in October 1991 appears to have left the Receiver and the Bank with only one possible purchaser. Of all the interested persons the other substantial interested persons were apparently deterred by their inability to obtain the co-operation of the staff and Edward made an offer which was lower than that of the Applicant.



(d) If Hay was the careful solicitor described by Adam it would not be unreasonable to expect Hay to have kept notes. Hay was not called to give evidence before the Tribunal.



14. What emerges from all of this? The Sale Agreement was an arms length transaction negotiated between parties at arms length and legally represented. It may perhaps be that Cross and Adam had, as between themselves privately taken the view that the Book Debts would realise at least $1,050,000 and perhaps more; (see however clause 31 of the Findings referred to previously as to the fact that Cross and Adam did not discuss appropriate values for particular assets). Adam said that if the Book Debts had realised less than $1,050,000 and this had been the figure apportioned to them, the Applicant would have sustained a capital as opposed to an income loss (presumably because acquired debts cannot be written off for income tax purposes). But the solution would surely have been to agree a figure at the lowest possible end of anticipated Book Debt realisations. The Tribunal does not doubt that there was an appropriation discussion at the meeting on the Completion Date and the end result was the insertion of the handwritten figures contained therein. It would seem that the Receiver was concerned as to allocation aspects, having regard to the notations on the offers included in Exhibit R1. It is unnecessary for the Tribunal to speculate on what those concerns may have been.



The effect of clause 3.4 of the Sale Agreement is that any additional amount of Purchase Price over $1.25 million must, in accordance with clause 3.4 of the Sale Agreement, be referable to Book Debts.



15. The figure for Stock does appear to be low when compared with its cost to the Vendors, and having regard to the evidence of Cross, the fact that it realised amounts in excess of that cost when sold by the Applicant in the ordinary course of business. However that figure does accord (in very rough terms) with the offer by Edward which was rejected.



16. (a) It seems reasonable to infer that the Applicant was able to use its very considerable bargaining strength (through its effective control of the staff) to conclude what it may have perceived as an advantageous transaction.



(b) Nor was there any evidence of any kind before the Tribunal, whether by the receiver or anyone else, as to the fact that a sale of the assets by a different method (for example a sale of assets piecemeal) might have produced a more favourable result. Nor was any such suggestion made to the Tribunal.



There is, in any event, some doubt in my mind as to the relevance of any such notional evidence. The fact is that the Receiver sold the assets in accordance with a method of sale which is a recognised method in these circumstances. It may be, and indeed it is quite likely, that it was the only realistic method available to him. A tender method of the type used is similar in some respects (except of course that it takes longer) to a sale by auction and the Bank’s insistence on full payment constituted on the same analogy something similar to a reserve. 21. (a) I turn now to deal with the decision in Commissioner for Inland Revenue NZ v Edge (1956) 11 ATD 91. Henry J in the Supreme Court of New Zealand dealt with a sale by a mortgagee of farm, plant and livestock as a going concern for a total price. Because of its relevance to this Decision, I propose to quote extensively from that judgment. At pages 93 and following Henry J said:

“9.(1) Where any trading stock is sold or otherwise disposed of without consideration in money or money’s worth or for a consideration that is less than the market price or the true value thereof on the day of the sale or other disposition, the following provisions shall apply, namely:

(a) The trading stock shall be deemed for the purposes of the principal Act to have been sold at and to have realised the market price of the day of the sale or other disposition, but, where there is no market price, shall be deemed to have been sold at and to have realised such price as the Commissioner determines:

(b) The price which under this section the trading stock is deemed to have realised shall be taken into account in calculating the assessable income of the person selling or otherwise disposing of the trading stock:

(c) The person acquiring the trading stock shall, for the purpose of calculating his assessable income, be deemed to have purchased the trading stock at the price which under this section the trading stock is deemed to have realised.”

This section appears on its face to be intended to cover the disposal of trading stock other than its sale in the ordinary course of business. This view proceeds particularly from the use of the word “consideration” and the words “money’s worth”. The phrase “without consideration in money or money’s worth or for a consideration that is less than ...” is commonly used to apply to transactions in which the element of gift predominates. If the section dealt with ordinary sales in the course of business, the more restricted term “price” would be used. This construction avoids the absurdity which arises if the section were so construed as to catch up ordinary trading transactions. It would, at a stroke of the pen, eliminate all distinctions between the various types of selling and the various markets in which goods are sold, and confine traders to a uniform basis for all sales each day and probably result in their returns containing fictional and unreal figures for the assessment of tax. It would strike at the different recognised methods of trading wherein sales may be wholesale or retail, by auction or otherwise, in large or small quantities, just to mention a few instances. That I cannot believe was the intention of the legislature.

The sale now under review was not an ordinary trading sale, so I must turn to consider whether the evidence proves that it was a sale for a consideration “less than the market price ... on the day of the sale”. Neither the market price nor the market to which that price refers is defined. The words “fair market price” used in a demise came up for construction in the House of Lords in Charrington & Co Ltd v Wooder. The headnote reads:

“... the term “market” was to be construed with references to the surrounding circumstances, and that upon the true construction of the proviso the lessee was to be charged the fair market price as applying to tenants of tied houses and was not entitled to discount beyond the recognised rates.”

Lord Haldane at p.79 said:

“What is the fair market price must depend on what is meant by the market, and this must be ascertained by reference to the facts proved.”

And also:

“‘Market’ is a word covering a variety of possible forms ...”

All the surrounding facts must be looked at and from those it must be determined first what is the market to which the sale or disposition is referable, and secondly, what is the price in such market. This is largely, if not entirely, a question of fact: Ellesmore (Earl) v Inland Revenue Commissioners.

In the Court below, the Magistrate relied upon authority that the market value must be ascertained without reference to a particular contract and that the terms of a particular contract must not be allowed to fetter the market, and in particular, a forced sale affords no criterion of market value. These authorities were used to dispose of the auction sale of the mortgaged premises as a going concern as being a test of what the market price was, and the learned Magistrate then made two specific findings as follows:

(1) “I consider the words “market price on the day of the sale or other disposition” in the section refers to the best price available or applicable on an unrestricted sale and that the price for the stock would, I consider, be £6,471.”

(2) “I personally feel the appellant purchaser when he bid at the auction based his price primarily on the value of the live-stock he took over and the balance of his bid represented the value he then placed on the land and plant.”

“He could get £6,471 the day after the sale for the stock but he might or might not get the value he placed on the land and plant if he sold same even on good terms and conditions of sale. The land however was not a poor property. It was valued at a considerable sum by the Crown.”

The second appears to me to be pure speculation, and the first fails to make a proper inquiry into what in the circumstances was the market. It would be giving a meaning to this section which was never intended if every taxpayer was required to return in respect of each sale the best price available or applicable on an unrestricted sale, and both sellers and purchasers, if trading in trading stock, would have their books filled with fictitious and unreal figures. They would be put on inquiry in respect of each sale to ascertain what was the best price that could have been realised on an unrestricted sale, and the circumstances of the carrying on of the business would be entirely ignored. It would prevent traders from selecting the market except at the peril of finding that they may have put their goods on the wrong market and then be called upon to pay tax on figures in excess of what their goods actually realised; or in the case of purchasers, enable them to make tax-free profits if they happen to purchase at a price less than the market value so defined. Such a result is to be avoided if the section yields some other reasonable construction.

The proper inquiry here is first to ascertain the market on the day of sale. The legislation does not stipulate any particular market and must be held to have in contemplation all markets upon which the taxpayer in his manifold activities might trade or dispose of his goods, as well as the various markets upon which trading stock is bought and sold. The market upon which this particular sale took place was by a public auction properly conducted, at which the live-stock was sold as part of a farm as a going concern. Such a market for disposing of trading stock, either live-stock or otherwise, is a well recognised market, and is a market which, in my opinion, is within s 9. This live-stock, in the ordinary and by no means unusual course of events, was mortgaged together with all the other items which comprised the farming business of a taxpayer. As the result of default it came on to the market in its then state, namely, as part of mortgaged premises, and was realised on the open market by public auction at the best price obtainable. This was a market which must be held to be within the contemplation of the legislature when dealing with the markets upon which trading stock might be sold. The only evidence by which it is sought to prove that the market price was more than the consideration received is evidence that if this stock had not been subject to the mortgage, or if the mortgagee had otherwise elected, it could have been sold separately for a higher price at the saleyards. That may be so, but, in fact, that market was not chosen. It would be monstrous if every taxpayer had to run the risk of selecting the best market and be penalised if the market chosen turned out not to be the best. On the day of sale this taxpayer’s live-stock could not be sold by him as a separate item. It was then qua him not available for separate sale, and its market was then limited to a market as part of a farm as a going concern. As such, on that market it has not been shown that it did not fetch the market price on the day of the sale. Therefore s 9 does not apply to the live-stock in question and the respondent is not entitled to enter the figure of £6,471 in his return of income.

It should be noted that, if the respondent’s contention were correct, the owner of the stock could be called upon to return this stock as having been sold for £1,760 more than he got for it, and he would be obliged to pay tax on that fictitious sum although there was a bona fide sale by public auction on the open market and although he was powerless to choose any other market. I can find no reason for bringing about such an absurd result or for holding that such a sale as this was not a sale upon a market contemplated by s.9 for live-stock similarly circumstanced. (Emphasis added by the Tribunal)

30. Nothing whatever was said at the hearings about penalties and I therefore assume that I need not be concerned with any such aspect; I note that the amended assessment (at T8 of the T Documents) does not contain any amount in respect of penalties.



31. In all the circumstances the objection decision under review must be affirmed.
