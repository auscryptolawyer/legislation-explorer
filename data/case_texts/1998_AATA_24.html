Thomas Shannon McKnight anf Commissioner for Superannuation [1998] AATA 24 (23 January 1998)

Last Updated: 1 May 1998

Administrative

Appeals

Tribunal

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No W97/56

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re THOMAS SHANNON McKNIGHT

Applicant

And COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

Respondent

Decision No 12556

DECISION

Tribunal Deputy President T E Barnett

Dr J G Billings (Member)

Dr M C Wood (Member)

Date 23 January 1998

Place Perth

Decision The decision under review is set aside and the following decision is substituted:

(a) it is desirable that the late election should be recognised and treated as if it had been made within the period allowed in s.137 of the Act.

...........(Sgd) T E Barnett.........

Deputy President

CATCHWORDS

SUPERANNUATION - late elections - applicant resigned from employment - refund of contributions to superannuation scheme - rejoined scheme - received little information concerning scheme - whether aware of late election to preserve superannuation benefits - whether or not late election should be treated as if it had been made within time period allowed under the Superannuation Act 1976 .

Superannuation Act 1976 ss.157(1), 137(1), (2), (3).

Superannuation Act 1922

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975

Guidelines Applicable to Late Elections for the Preservation of Superannuation Benefits Lodged under s.157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976, Numbers (1), (2) and (3)

Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 14 AAR 456

Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT decision no. 8553; 26 February 1993)

Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd & Ors v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; 7 ALD 315

Secourable and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT decision no. 8609; 19 March 1993)

Foley and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT decision no. 8731; 25 May 1993)

Charles and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT decision no. 8897, 6 August 1993)

REASONS FOR DECISION

23 January 1998 Deputy President T E Barnett

Dr J G Billings (Member)

Dr M C Wood (Member)

1. This is an application for review of a decision of a delegate of the respondent dated 11 May 1994 refusing to direct, under s.157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act 1975 "), that the election made by the applicant on 9 November 1993 be treated as if it were an election made within the period specified under s.137(1) of the Act.

2. The Tribunal heard evidence from the applicant, Mr Andrew Hemus and Mr Roland Morin. It admitted into evidence the documents furnished pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act ("the T documents"), in addition to the following exhibits:

Exhibit A1 Letter from Mr W Mansfield to Mr Pascoe of 30 October 1997

Exhibit A2 A statement from the applicant with four attachments

Exhibit R1 A letter from the applicant to Compuserv of 7 May 1997

Exhibit R2 A copy of a facsimile from Telstra to the Respondent of 19 November 1997

3. The applicant in this matter was represented by Mr Ramsay and Mr Pascoe. Mr Dube and Ms Yu appeared for the respondent,

4. The applicant is employed by Telstra and is a member of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme. He was employed by the former Overseas Telecommunications Commission (OTC) from 11 June 1976 to 29 November 1985. He gave notice of his intention to resign from his position at Carnarvon on or about 29 October 1985. He accepted a refund of his contributions to the scheme of $10530.13. He did not elect to preserve his rights under the CSS which was provided for by s.137 of the Act. On 16 February 1987 the applicant rejoined OTC in Perth and on 18 May 1987 again became an eligible employee for the purposes of superannuation.

5. The relevant sections are as follows:

"137.(1) A person who:

(a) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances to which paragraph (b) does not apply and, upon so ceasing, is not entitled to pension under this Act or invalidity benefit in accordance with section 69, 72 or 73; or

(b) ceases to be an eligible employee in circumstances by virtue of which the person is to be deemed, under subsection 58(3), to have retired involuntarily;

may, not later than 21 days after the person so ceases to be an eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner, that this Division apply in relation to the person.

(2) If a person makes an election for the purposes of this section within 1 month before he ceases to be an eligible employee, the election has effect as if it had been made on the day after he ceased to be an eligible employee.

(3) Subject to subsection (4) an election under this section is of no effect unless the person who made the election gives notice in writing to the Commissioner within 21 days, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, after the expiration of the period that is the prescribed period in relation to him:

(a) stating whether he was employed at the expiration of the prescribed period and, if so, the name and address of his employer; and

(b) stating whether he was at the expiration of the prescribed period a member of the superannuation scheme applicable in relation to that employment and, if so, specifying the scheme concerned."

6. Subsection 157(1) contains a power to recognise an election made after the prescribed time (of 21 days) in certain circumstances. It is in these terms:

"157.(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly".

7. The original decision of the delegate of the respondent determined on 11 May 1994 that, in all the circumstances, it was not desirable that the election made by the applicant under s.157(1) be recognised. This decision was reviewed by a delegate of the respondent and was affirmed by determination dated 30 January 1997. The delegate reached his decision having considered guidelines issued by the Commissioner, decisions of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal and the judgments of the Federal Court.

FINDINGS OF FACT

8. On the evidence and materials before it the Tribunal makes the following findings of fact:

(a) The applicant joined the OTC in 1976 as a technical officer and became a contributor to the CSS on 11 June 1976. He has a diploma in electronic engineering and was employed at the satellite earth station at Carnarvon where his duties required night shift work and listing on a rotational roster.

(b) During 1984 and 1985, the volume of work at the station declined and staff were uncertain about their future. The satellite earth station was in the process of being closed down. The applicant also found his quality of life was declining, his wife was homesick for New Zealand and he was seeing little of their only child.

(c) Staff in Carnarvon were being asked which sites they wished to relocate to. Sydney and Melbourne were available but the applicant believed that the cost of setting up home in either city would be too prohibitive. Perth was not available. His wife was ready to return to New Zealand for a while with or without the applicant.

(d) The staff structure at Carnarvon was headed by a manager, a senior technical officer and a secretary-receptionist. There were about 24 staff and no personnel officer. Telephone calls were allowed to Sydney on personnel matters and were limited in duration. It was rare for personnel information to be circulated. A cluttered notice board or a log book were the main methods of communication with staff.

(e) The applicant resigned on 29 October 1985. After discussions with the manager, he was given Form 2A to complete by the secretary-receptionist. He was given no information about his superannuation entitlements. The form was completed by the applicant himself.

(f) The applicant took a refund of his contributions to the superannuation scheme valued at $10530.13. The refund was deposited in a bank account then in the Commonwealth Bank Finance Corporation where it earned a higher rate of interest.

(g) He moved to New Zealand with his family. Preliminary inquiries indicated that work was available and he was employed by NZ Telecom. He joined its superannuation scheme. After a period in New Zealand, a decision was made to return to Perth and the Applicant rejoined the OTC on 16 February 1987. The money on deposit was used about a month after his return as a deposit on a family home.

(h) The applicant rejoined the superannuation scheme in 1987. On resumption, he received no information about superannuation. Documents tendered in evidence showed that the Applicant was sent a letter of appointment and a brochure on 5 June 1987. The brochure contained information about the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme for new members and included a paragraph on resignation benefits. The paragraph states:

"Resignation Benefits

Employees who resign become entitled to a refund of accumulated contributions. They also have, under the Superannuation Act, the alternative of electing to preserve their superannuation benefits. These could be deferred benefits, or a transfer value to be paid to some other eligible scheme. The preserved benefit includes a Government financed element."

The Applicant told the Tribunal that he just filed the correspondence and does not even recall getting the brochure.

(i) The applicant received annual statements about his superannuation contributions after rejoining the fund in 1987. The statements showed the monetary value of his contributions alone and also contained information about preservation rights. One statement headed "Superannuation Fund Investment Trust" contains the following information:

"Preservation: Certain contributors who resign may elect to preserve their benefits in the Fund until retirement and thereby avoid foregoing the employer contribution of 2.5 times the employee's contributions accumulated with interest. For further details of this option contact your personnel officer."

Another statement dated October 1988 contained the following information under the heading "Resignation Benefits":

"If you resign from Commonwealth employment before age 55 you should consider the value of the preservation option. Briefly, if you become entitled to a deferred benefit and so leave your equity in the fund your equity will continue to grow at the rates of interest prescribed from time to time. In addition, you will have preserved an annual indexed pension financed by your Commonwealth employer."

(j) He attended a seminar in 1991 which was arranged by his employer to advise staff on options for superannuation, which was undergoing change. Documents tendered in evidence showed that information about preservation rights was presented to participants at the seminar.

(k) During the first half of 1993, the applicant became aware of a late election to preserve superannuation benefits which another employee had made. The employee had also worked at Carnarvon. The employee had taken his case to the Tribunal after his application for a late election was not recognised. Following the successful result of the appeal by his colleague, the applicant made inquiries about procedures for a late election and made an application for one on 9 November 1993 in accordance with s137(1) of the Act.

(l) The application was not recognised in a decision of 11 May 1994. The applicant sought a reconsideration on 10 June 1994 .This was handed down on 30 January 1997 and confirmed the initial decision by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation.

(m) The respondent subsequently adopted guidelines for assessing applications for a late election to preserve superannuation entitlements on or about 26 September 1996.

9. The Tribunal accepts the applicant's evidence that his understanding of superannuation in 1985 was that it was compulsory and benefits were provided at retirement. He believed that contributions were handed back on resignation. It also accepts that he sought a refund of his contributions because it was the general belief at the time that on resignation a refund of contributions was paid. The applicant thought he had only one option when he completed form 2A. Evidence presented to the Tribunal by two other witnesses confirmed that the view held by the applicant was a generally held belief.

10. While the seminars held in 1991 dealt with preservation of rights among other matters, the applicant had little interest in the issue then and does not recall reading any material about it at the time. The seminar which he attended included participants close to retirement who asked most questions.

11. He first became aware of the option for a late election when it was discussed in the workplace in the first half of 1993 as Mr Don Charles, who had worked at Carnarvon when the applicant was there, talked about his appeal and sought witnesses to appear at a hearing. The applicant made further investigations and was referred to material provided at a seminar on the new superannuation scheme.

12. Mr Charles was advised that his appeal was successful in August 1993. The Applicant wrote to the Retirement Benefits Office on 7 September 1993 and requested copies of his superannuation records from 1985.

13. The applicant wrote to the respondent on 9 November 1993 requesting preservation of his benefit. A document dated 1 December 1993 was tendered in evidence. It was addressed to the Retirement Benefits Office by the Co-ordinator, Superannuation and Projects at Telstra and noted:

"In conclusion, I would stress that, despite the reference in Part 12 of the Superannuation Manual to the preservation option, it is by no means certain that CSS contributors who resigned in 1985 were advised of this option. If Mr McKnight has stated that this information was not made available to him at that time, I would have no reason to doubt his claim."

14. Though Mr Charles and the applicant work at the same site in Perth, their duties and rosters and the layout of the plant mean that they met infrequently and the Tribunal accepts that the applicant did not know of Mr Charles' application until about mid-1993.

15. In 1996, the CSS Board promulgated Guidelines Applicable to Late Elections for the Preservation of Superannuation Benefits Lodged under s.157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 . This was many months after the applicant had given notice of his desire to make a late election. The Guidelines provide as follows:

1. The Board is of the view that, notwithstanding any current views about the adequacy of forms S2A and S2R, it is not desirable for a late application to be granted solely or largely on the grounds that the applicant now claims not to have properly understood the form at the relevant time;

- in the absence of contrary evidence, completion of form S2A/S2R will be taken to signify an informed decision to select a refund and forgo preservation.

- the expectation is that an applicant who may not have understood the choices available should have made inquiries prior to completing the form S2A/S2R. Failure to have made such inquiries without good reason will be taken as signifying an informed choice unless, had they sought advice on the options available from the appropriate official source relevant to their workplace or circumstances, they would have received materially erroneous or misleading advice that would have caused them to misunderstand their options.

2. The Board is also of the view that it is generally desirable to grant a late election in cases where an application has been lodged promptly after an applicant has learnt that a case for late election might exist, and where the applicant can provide evidence, where appropriate, that:

(i) they were unable to understand the choice they were making at the time by reason of physical or mental incapacity;

- medical evidence needs to be provided to satisfy the Board that the incapacity was of sufficient severity to have prevented the applicant from making a reasoned decision.

(ii) they were materially misinformed at a relevant time by an authoritative source upon which it was reasonable for them to rely concerning either the options available to them in their circumstances, or the substance of any particular relevant option. Corroboration of receipt of incorrect advice would lend support to an application. Such corroboration could be by way of evidence of prevailing practice, supporting statement from others with contemporary evidence, evidence from parties directly involved, or other relevant evidence,

- an authoritative source would include ComSuper (or its predecessors) or the personnel area of the applicant's employer

- the incorrect advice could have been received on an individual basis or provided to all relevant employees, eg by way of a "handout" provided to al employees on termination.

(iii) they sought relevant advice from responsible official sources concerning available options but, for no fault of their own, received no response prior to making the choice at the time;

(iv) they had no practicable access to official sources of advice concerning their options because of the particular circumstances of their location or employment or personal affairs at the relevant time;

(v) they did not select any option on the application form but the form was signed by the applicant, or an option was selected but the form was not signed by the applicant;

(vi) they established an interest, through the use to which the refund was put when received by the applicant, in making provision for his or her retirement, eg:

- where the applicant maintained the money through action such as a deposit in a bank account, purchase of shares or rolled over money to a roll-over facility or another Superannuation Fund or scheme.

- where the funds were invested for long term or gain (including in real estate or a business venture), as opposed to where the refund was applied to immediate consumption items such as holidays, household goods, or consumer debt reduction. Evidence of such action and that the refund monies remain so invested would lend support to an application.

3. The Board would not usually consider it desirable to grant a late election -

(i) where the applicant, in making his or her late election, has allowed more than three months to elapse since he or she first became aware that a late election for preservation could be made; or

(ii) if the applicant had made inquiries and received correct advice at any time prior to resignation/termination.

APPLICATION OF THE LAW

16. Section 157(1) allowed the Commissioner to accept a late election if:

"In all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised."

17. The word "desirable" was considered by Deputy President Forgie in Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 14 AAR 456:

"It could only be desirable to recognise a late election if there were objectively good reasons which justified the person's not being required to comply with the time limits otherwise clearly specified by the Act."

18. The majority in Liddle (Deputy President Forgie dissenting) was influenced by the fact that the applicant was suffering from stress and found that it was desirable to allow the applicant to make a late election as he did not have knowledge of the option and because the Commissioner had not taken adequate steps to make him aware of it.

19. In Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT decision no. 8553; 26 February 1993) in a tightly reasoned decision Deputy President McMahon found that the applicant had an evidentiary burden to establish that there is an acceptable explanation for the delay in making the election but had failed to do so. He applied the principles propounded by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd & Ors v Cohen [1984] FCA 176; 7 ALD 315 at 319-320. In Lander, the applicant was a very well educated librarian at the National Library in Canberra who wrote a letter of resignation requesting refund of her contributions to the superannuation fund because she felt she had been subjected to harassment by a superior officer. She was then sent form T5/26 and signed it to obtain her refund. The appropriate boxes on the form electing a refund rather than preservation of entitlements were marked. When she rejoined the Public Service in 1983 she could not remember signing any documents. She first heard of her right to elect to preserve her benefits years later at a seminar and then applied to make a late election. The Tribunal found that the principle reason she had resigned was because of role conflict between her job and the demands of caring for three young children. Mrs Lander made no inquiries about her superannuation rights at the time of resigning despite the fact that she signed the form in the presence of an experienced personnel officer who swore that she always explained the superannuation entitlements provisions and despite the fact that her husband was a senior executive officer in the Public Service. There was evidence that when Mrs Lander rejoined the Public Service in 1985 she attended an induction course directed at her then current superannuation rights.

20. In Lander each of the principles laid down by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley was considered. The Tribunal was particularly influenced by the fact that Mrs Lander had been located in Canberra, not in a remote location; that her rights had been carefully explained to her by a personnel officer; and that she had the ability and ample opportunity to make inquiries. In the result the Tribunal found that it was not desirable to allow her to make a late election.

21. Lander was followed in Secourable and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT decision no. 8609; 19 March 1993) where the applicant was a Telecom technician who also was unaware of his rights to preserve his superannuation rights at resignation. He was sent a similar memorandum and the same ambiguous S2A form as was given to the applicant in Lander and this present case. Having no knowledge of his rights he did not elect to preserve his superannuation entitlements. (Mr Secourable also had access to a book called "the yellow book", which was a Telecom guide to superannuation contributors, which was quite misleading in that it did not make any mention at all of this right to preserve benefits.) Mr Secourable left Telecom in order to go into a private business venture but resumed employment with Telecom in 1984. The Tribunal found that up until he resumed with Telecom he had no knowledge of his rights to preserve his entitlements and at the time of resumption, had he applied, it would have been desirable to allow him to elect to do so. When Mr Secourable rejoined the scheme, however, he was provided with full details of the rights of contributors, including clear instructions about how a contributor who resigned could elect to retain his superannuation rights. Then in the years 1987 and 1988 he was given, and read, the annual information statements for contributors, which gave him another opportunity to become aware that he had the potential for preserving his superannuation rights on resignation. The Tribunal found that, at that stage, it was reasonable to expect that he should have made inquiries about his position at the time of his earlier resignation. Specific mention was made of the fact that Mr Secourable was approaching retirement age and would have been alert to the question of his superannuation entitlements. The Tribunal in Secourable decided that the continued delay in making inquiries made it not desirable to allow him to make a late election.

22. Lander and Secourable were distinguished in the case of Foley and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT decision no. 8731; 25 May 1993). In the special circumstances of Foley the applicant, who was a Senior Personnel Officer, had very detailed "hands on knowledge" of the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act"), which gave a resignee only the option to obtain a refund of his contributions. Under the 1922 Act there was no possibility of preserving superannuation rights on resignation. Mr Foley was seconded to the United Nations in Montreal in 1967 and at that time he had this knowledge deeply entrenched in his consciousness, because supervising staff and personnel matters under the provisions of the 1922 Act had been the subject of his daily employment. After his secondment to the United Nations Authority he remained in Montreal for many years. During his time in Montreal he was completely out of touch with Australian public service affairs and did not become aware of important changes in the legislation. In 1972 he was obliged to resign and wrote his letter of resignation from his home in Montreal. He unsuccessfully sought information from his department about his rights including his rights to superannuation. Despite his frequent requests the department negligently failed to provide him with this information until his return to Perth about one month after his resignation. A senior personnel officer then gave him a form S2A to sign at his farewell party in Perth. The document was already filled in so that it was limited to the option of claiming a refund and it was handed to him for immediate signature. Having signed the document Mr Foley then returned to Montreal where he continued in his employment with the United Nations Authority and continued to be out of touch with any information about Australian superannuation affairs.

23. When he finished with the United Nations and returned to Australia in 1989 he became aware of his rights which had existed at the date of resignation. He then carried out immediate inquiries and made an early request for a late election to preserve his superannuation rights. The Tribunal held, in those circumstances, that it was desirable to allow him to make a late election.

24. In the case of Charles and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT decision no. 8897, 6 August 1993), decided by this Tribunal, the circumstances are quite similar to Secourable up to the date of his resignation except there is no evidence that Mr Charles had access to the yellow book. Mr Charles was in an isolated area and he was under stress. He was personally given no information about his rights and he did not read the public service bulletin that was placed on the cluttered notice board at Carnarvon station.

25. The only personal communication Mr Charles received regarding his superannuation rights prior to resignation was the memorandum dated 28 December 1978 signed for the Director Administration which was attached to the Form S2A. On the evidence the memorandum was probably received by the local manager just before the applicant departed for Exmouth. It said:

"enclosed is an application for refund of your accumulated contributions. Please complete and return to the Superannuation Clerk"

The expert authority on superannuation, in effect, directed Mr Charles to sign the refund of contributions form and return it to Head Office. No alternative options were mentioned; no relevant information on entitlements was provided. Mr Charles was, therefore, given instruction from his superiors and the recognised authority on pension matters that fitted the common knowledge of long standing -that if you left the service, all you got was a refund of contributions. The memorandum was clearly misleading and directive. The Form S2A which was attached to the memorandum was ambiguous and confusing. The local manager who initialled and handed on the memorandum without comment gave evidence that he also was unaware of the right to elect to preserve benefits on resignation and that he shared Mr Charles' belief, which was common at the time, that the only available option was to claim a refund.

GUIDELINE 1

26. The Guidelines promulgated in 1996 are included at paragraph 15 of these reasons. Dot point (1) of Guideline 1 deals with the interpretation of events relating to discontinued forms S2A and S2R. The Guideline appears to disregard existing case law on this matter. The second dot point seems to be trying to accommodate some case law and, perhaps for this reason, is unduly complex and not easy to apply. In considering this guideline's relevance to Mr McKnight, the Tribunal notes that the applicant signed Form 2A in October 1985 in the belief, held also by others in the service, that his only option was to receive a refund on resignation. Guideline 1 states it is not desirable to grant a late application if the applicant claims to have not properly understood the form at the relevant time. The Tribunal accepts that the applicant held his belief genuinely and that the design of the form would not have alerted him to the need to become informed.

GUIDELINE 2

27. Guideline 2 is expressed to be an inclusive guideline, which does not seek to exclude other circumstances which might also make it desirable to grant a late election. It consists of an opening paragraph stating that it is generally desirable to grant a late election if an application is made promptly after the applicant has learnt a case may exist. This general principle is then qualified by limiting the concession to cases where the applicant can provide evidence, where appropriate, that the circumstances in sub clauses (i)-(iv) exist.

28. Regarding this guideline the Tribunal notes that it is worded in inclusive terms which urge favourable consideration when any of those circumstances exist. It does not purport to exclude the consideration of additional circumstances not mentioned in the Guideline. Applying Guideline 2 to the applicant's circumstance, the Tribunal finds that Mr McKnight learned a case might exist in August 1993 after the result of Charles was known. His correspondence to the respondent of 7 September 1993 was accepted by the respondent as an application. Mr McKnight made a formal application on 9 November 1993, and in either case fits within Guideline 3 which allows for a period of three months to elapse from the time of becoming aware, to the time of making the application.

29. Guideline 2(iv) recommends favourable consideration if an applicant provides evidence that there was no practicable access to official sources of advice about the options available. The Tribunal received evidence about the remote location at Carnarvon, the cluttered nature of the notice board, isolation caused by shift work and the small amount of time available to contact the personnel office in Sydney; all of which were impediments to the applicant having easy access to information.

30. Guideline 2(vi) requires evidence that the applicant might have used the refund to provide for his or her retirement. The Tribunal saw documents and heard evidence that the applicant deposited the funds in a bank account and subsequently used them to purchase real estate (the family home). There has been substantive satisfaction of this guideline.

31. This case differs from that of Secourable in which the applicant received and read information which gave him an opportunity to become aware that he had the potential for preserving rights on resignation. Also, Mr Secourable was approaching retirement age and was thinking about superannuation entitlements. Although the applicant in this case received the annual information returns he gave evidence that he did not read them. He had rejoined Telstra only recently and was not approaching retirement age. The Tribunal accepts that, subjectively, he was not made aware of his rights as a result of receiving this information.

32. The annual information statements sent to scheme members in the years since 1985 have progressively increased the amount of information about preservation rights, generally by providing more detail or using clearer language to alert members to their rights and to encourage them to seek further information. The Tribunal notes that the information statements for 1987 and 1988 contain different information on preservation; that for 1988 does not use the word preservation and indicates that leaving equity in the fund will allow growth and a link to employer contributions.

33. Noting the similarities with Charles, the Tribunal finds that at the date of Mr McKnight's resignation not only did he have no knowledge of his rights to elect for preservation but he was not negligent in failing to inform himself of those rights.

34. Though the applicant was given written information about the CSS when he rejoined in 1987, and subsequently received annual statements, the documents are in general terms and did not alert him to his rights or stimulate him to make further inquiries. Similarly, the seminar he attended in 1991 had a general intent to inform attendees about current changes in the scheme and did not alert him to the particular circumstances relevant to his earlier entitlements.

35. Having found out about these earlier rights in 1993, and having checked appropriate documents, he acted promptly to elect for preservation. The Commissioner for Superannuation, in correspondence dated 13 May 1994 to Mr McKnight, notes that Mr McKnight was taken to have made his application for late election in a letter of 7 September 1993.

DECISION

36. The decision under review is set aside and the following decision is substituted:

(a) it is desirable that the late election should be recognised and treated as if it had been made within the period allowed in s.137 of the Act.
