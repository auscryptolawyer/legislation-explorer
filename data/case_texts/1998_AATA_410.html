CATCHWORDS

REASONS FOR DECISION

10 June 1998 Dr P Gerber (Deputy President)



  1. This is a review of the decision of the respondent, dated 28 October 1996, disallowing the applicant’s objection, dated 20 December 1994 against a decision refusing the applicant’s application of 1 October 1993 for a refund of sales tax.
  2. The applicant’s entitlement to the refund of sales tax referred to above turns on the question whether the addition of trace amounts of ethyl mercaptan (25 ppm) to liquid petroleum gas (“LPG”) constitutes “manufacture” as defined in the relevant sales tax legislation. If it is “manufacture”, the applicant is entitled to succeed and receive a refund of sales tax paid in respect of storage tanks and equipment as “aids to manufacture” or “auxiliaries to aids to manufacture” as defined in the former Sales Tax Regulations.
  3. Turning to the evidence, LPG is a product recovered from oil and gas fields. Its chemical composition varies according to the source, but is mainly composed of the hydrocarbons propane and butane. Propane (the main constituent of LPG) forms a liquid only at less -42ºC. Butane is liquid below -0.5ºC. For most commercial uses, both propane and butane are in the gaseous state even though the gas constitutes a high flammability hazard when mixed with air in certain concentrations. Since the gas is odourless, it is not detectable if it escapes. In order to reduce the risk of explosion, an odorising agent - ethyl mercaptan - is added to the unodorised gas for much the same reason as putting canaries in coal mines, ie to alert persons of the escape of hazardous gas.
  4. Ethyl mercaptan is a liquid compound below 35ºC. Since it is only used in low concentration, its high vapour pressure ensures that it is gaseous when present in LPG. Once added to LPG, it mixes well and does not separate and remains in the same proportions as at the time of mixture.
  5. The process of odorising LPG commences in the pipeline, when the ethyl mercaptan is injected, and is completed within the storage tanks where it disburses.
  6. Odorised LPG is used as a fuel, both by industry and domestically, and a regulatory regime requires that for such use it must contain the odiferous agent.
  7. However, the addition of ethyl mercaptan renders the LPG unusable for some commercial purposes. Unodorised LPG is a raw material for the synthesis of a large range of commercial chemicals, such as acetic acid, olefins, acetaldehyde and alcohol. Again, because of ethyl mercaptan’s physical properties, including smell, odorised LPG cannot be used as an aerosol propellant in spray cans containing perfume, cosmetics, deodorants, hair sprays, etc. (LPG has now largely replaced chloroflurocarbons as a propellant because of the latter’s ozone depleting properties). It is not challenged that odorised and unodorised LPG do not have any common uses.
  8. Turning to the relevant provision in the Sales Tax Assessment Act 1930 (“the Act”) which prevailed up 31 December 1992, “manufacture” is defined in s 3(1) to include “(a) production, (b) the combination of parts or ingredients whereby an article or substance is formed that is commercially distinct from those parts or ingredients” (subject to a qualification not relevant for present purposes).
  9. The first thing to note is that the question of the meaning of an ordinary English word like “manufacture”, when used in an Act of Parliament, is one of fact. Secondly, despite the fact the legislation dealing with sales tax consists of twenty-seven separate Acts, including eleven Assessment Acts, the draftsman, when dealing with the meaning of “manufacture”, resisted the temptation to define the term, contenting himself with merely stating what manufacture includes. This lack of definition has resulted in numerous and diverse decisions of courts and tribunals (all cited by one party or the other in this case), dealing with such refinements as whether cutting big scrap into little scrap, or putting labels on video cassettes involves “manufacture”. In so far as the term is not defined, its ordinary meaning must be determined at the date when the Act came into force. Thus Lockhart J noted in FCT v Jax Tyres Pty Ltd 85 ATC 4001 that:
“When the word ‘includes’ is used in a definition section, it is generally used to enlarge the meaning of the word it defines. So far as its meaning is not defined in the relevant statute, it must be given its ordinary meaning.” (at p 4001)


  1. It seems to me that the issue which I have to decide will ultimately be influenced by the history of the legislation. Thus in the definition section of the original Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930, the draftsman rather unhelpfully restricted himself by merely stating that manufacture includes “production”. “Manufacturer” was defined to mean:
“a person who engages, whether exclusively or not, in the manufacture of goods, and includes a printer, publisher, lithographer or engraver; and, where one person makes goods wholly or in part for another out of materials supplied by that other, the person supplying the materials shall be deemed to be the manufacturer, and the person so making the goods shall not be deemed to be the manufacturer.”


  1. Then came the case of Irving v Munro & Sons Ltd [1931] HCA 57; (1932) 46 CLR 279, decided in November 1931, in which the defendant was the distributor of Ariel motor cycles. The machines arrived from the English manufacturer in crates containing all the parts necessary to assemble them save for the tyres and tubes. On arrival, the parts were removed from their crates and assembled “usually by a lad with the aid of a spanner”. The defendant then fitted tyres and tubes locally purchased, after which the cycles were ready for sale. When the case came before the High Court on appeal from an unsuccessful prosecution by the Commissioner of Taxation, the Court was unanimous in dismissing the appeal, holding that that the defendant was not a “manufacturer” as defined.
  2. In the following year, Parliament enlarged the definition of “manufacture” by including in the amending Act, inter alia, “(b) the combination of parts or ingredients whereby an article or substance is formed that is commercially distinct from those parts or ingredients”. One can speculate that the amendment was aimed at overcoming the loss to the revenue highlighted by the decision in Irving v Munro (supra). Henceforth, including locally manufactured tyres and tubes in the assembly of imported motor cycles for sale would (semble) result in “manufacture”.
  3. The usual starting point whenever the issue of “manufacture” arises in revenue cases is generally taken from the observation of Darling J in McNicol v Pinch [1906] 2 KB 352 at 361:
“I think the essence of making or of manufacturing is that what is made shall be a different thing from that out of which it is made.”


  1. That observation was applied by Dixon J in FCT v Jack Zinader Pty Ltd [1949] HCA 42; (1949) 78 CLR 336, in which a majority of their Honours held that a furrier who remodelled worn fur garments by turning them into coats, capes, collars or stoles, produced goods which were “manufactured” within the meaning of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1), applying the definition of “manufacture” which had remained unaltered from 1932 to 1 January 1993.
  2. The observation of Darling J was quoted and enlarged by Lockhart J in the Jax Tyres case (supra) where his Honour stated:
“To manufacture an article necessarily involves producing a different article from the articles, materials or ingredients from which it was made.” (at p 4004)


  1. Again, in the most recent case in which the term was considered, Commonwealth v Genex Corp Pty Ltd 92 ATC 4764, the Full High Court noted at 4768:
“It has long been accepted that one is required to ask whether what is made is ‘a different thing’ from that out of which it is made” .


The Court added:

“And, equally, it is no novel proposition to say that this question is on occasion difficult to answer”


  1. In that case, the High Court determined that, in the absence of the definition of “manufacture” in s 3(1)(d) of the Act, the process of development of negatives from exposed film did not constitute “manufacture” ( at 4769).
  2. Applying the law to the facts of this case, I find that the definition of “manufacture” as stated by Darling J in McNicol v Pinch (supra) and enlarged by Lockhart J in the Jax Tyres case (supra) “covers the field” in this case. Given the somewhat Delphic statutory pronouncement on “manufacture”, which merely expands the word beyond its ordinary meaning as generally understood, to include the combination of ingredients whereby a substance is formed that is commercially distinct from those ingredients, it seems to me one has merely to determine whether combining the unodorised gas with ethyl mercaptan results in a substance that is commercially distinct from its constituent parts, ie “a different thing”. On that aspect, the words of Windeyer J in M.P. Metals Pty Ltd v FCT [1968] HCA 89; (1967-1968) 117 CLR 631 at 638 are apposite:
“But what is a different thing? Various paraphrases were offered to me, such as a ‘substantially different thing’, not merely an ‘altered thing’; ‘a new entity’; ‘a distinct commodity’. But these are all pregnant with ambiguity. Identity and difference, as concepts, must always be related to some quality of the thing or things in respect of which identity or difference is to be determined. It may be colour, shape, chemical composition or any other quality. To speak of ‘substantial differences’, as distinct from small differences, means little or nothing, unless some quality of the thing is postulated as its essential. And whether a thing is so different a thing from the thing or things out of which it was made as to be properly described as a new commodity may depend not only upon physical characteristics but also on differences in its utility for some purpose.”
  1. I have therefore concluded that the addition of ethyl mercaptan to liquid petroleum gas changes the chemical properties of the gas which gives it distinct properties as a mixture, different from its constituent compounds and resulting in an odorised gas having a different commercial use from the unodorised gas and the two substances from which it is combined. Whilst “the scrap is still scrap”, as Menzies J observed in the M.P. Metals case (supra at p 649), where the issue was whether processing scrap metal for sale by cutting the scrap into smaller length and compressing the cut-offs into bales constituted manufacture, one does not need to be an industrial chemist to conclude that whilst “the gas is still gas”, odorised LPG is “a different thing” from the unodorised LPG - in its unodorised state the gas is used as a chemical for feedstock and as a propellant, in its odorised state it is used as a fuel.
  2. In the circumstances I am spared the necessity of having to go to the Macquarie Dictionary, as I was invited to do by the respondent, to determine the various dictionary meanings of “manufacture”. Nor is it necessary to have recourse to the word’s Latin roots, which take one back to processes made by hand. Alas, since the industrial revolution, the handiwork of craftsmen has become virtually obsolete, and the etymological meaning of the word archaic. Again, I would respectfully echo the observation of Dixon J in FCT v Rochester [1934] HCA 17; (1934) 50 CLR 225, 226-7, where his Honour noted: To attempt some logical analysis of the conceptions of manufacture and production, and to apply the analysis to any process or operation that appears to possess the attributes found to constitute these conceptions, although it would ordinarily be described by the words ‘manufacture’ or ‘production’, must lead to results which do not represent the true interpretation of the Act.”
  3. The respondent submits that the addition of ethyl mercaptan is not done to enhance the properties of the gas, but rather to comply with the local Dangerous Goods Act and Australian Standard AS1596. Again, it is said that the addition of the odoriser does not change the physical characteristics of the liquid petroleum gas. While the ethyl mercaptan does not seem to chemically react with the LPG constituent compounds of butane and propane to produce a different chemical compound, it does change the physical properties of propane/butane mixture to produce a mixture which is different from the original. The LPG mixture comprises other compounds in addition to propane and butane, including oxygen. It is this reaction with oxygen that causes sulphur dioxide to be produced, thus conferring the “smell” properties on the mixture. On a molecular basis, the mixture has changed and is different from the original mixture, which did not include the sulphur dioxide compound.
  4. This case is thus readily distinguishable from such cases as FCT v Hamersley Iron Pty Ltd (1981) 37 ALR 595, which involved the blending of different grades of iron ore to achieve a consistent result for purposes of meeting contractual specifications, merely labelling video cassettes subsequent to manufacture (WEA Records Pty Ltd v FCT [1990] FCA 323; (1990) 96 ALR 365), or winding yarn on to cones (Davies Coop & Co Ltd v FCT [1948] HCA 50; (1948) 77 CLR 299), processes which may legitimately be characterised as “aids to manufacture” as defined in Cl 1(1) of the First Schedule of the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935 which refers to “processing or treatment” for the purpose of bringing manufactured goods “into ... the form or condition in which they are to be marketed or used.”
  5. Again, it is said that the utility of the LPG for the purpose of burning is not altered. That is true, but overlooks the fact that before the gas can be legally burnt, it requires the addition of the odiferous chemical, a very different proposition from putting, say, mustard on steak - steak can be consumed without mustard; LPG cannot legally be used as a fuel without an odiferous additive.
  6. Then it is said that “the ethyl mercaptan represents only a tiny percentage of the odorised product” - de minimus non curat lex. Many chemical changes can be achieved by the addition of tiny amounts of other compounds, I am not persuaded that whether or not a gas is “manufactured” turns on a quantitative analysis.
  7. Finally, it was submitted that odorised gas is properly characterised merely as an aspect of postproduction “storage and handling” of LPG, and involves none of the complex procedures ordinarily associated with manufacture. With respect, that argument is merely a variation of the putting labels on cassettes submission.
  8. For the reasons outlined above, the objection decision is set aside, the Tribunal being satisfied that adding ethyl mercaptan to unodorised LPG constitutes “manufacture” within the meaning of s 3(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930, both because the chemical composition of odorised LPG is “a different thing” as that term is explained by Windeyer J in the MP Metals case (supra), and the utility of the chemically changed product serves a different purpose.
