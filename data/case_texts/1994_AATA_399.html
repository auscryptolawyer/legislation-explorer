ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



INCOME TAX - Foreign currency exchange gains and losses associated with purchase of goods for use as part of production line - whether such gains and losses are to be calculated from date of contract or date of bill of lading.



Income Tax Assessment Act, Part III, Division 3B



Texas Co (Australasia) Ltd v FCT [1940] HCA 9; (1940) 63 CLR 382

Armco (Australia) Pty Ltd v FCT [1948] HCA 49; (1948) 76 CLR 584

Caltex Ltd v FCT [1960] HCA 17; (1959-1960) 106 CLR 205

International Nickel Australia Ltd v FCT [1977] HCA 49; (1976-1977) 137 CLR 347

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No: VT92/5

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re: APPLICANT



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION RESPONDENT



Tribunal: Dr P Gerber, Deputy President

Mr B H Pascoe, Member



Date: 14 January 1994

Place: Melbourne



REASONS FOR DECISION



1. This application is for the review of the decision of the respondent to disallow an objection against an assessment of income tax in respect of the income year ended 30 June 1989. The applicant company was represented by Mr G T Pagone of learned counsel, and the respondent by Dr C Jessup of senior counsel and Mr T Murphy of counsel.



2. The matter came on before a Full Tribunal consisting of Deputy President Gerber, Senior Member Balmford and Mr Pascoe, Member. Before the Tribunal was able to reach a decision, Senior Member Balmford accepted an appointment to the Victorian County Court and is, consequently, no longer a member of this Tribunal. On being informed of this, both parties advised the Tribunal that they have agreed that the determination of this application may be completed by the Tribunal constituted by the two remaining members. It is on this basis, which accords with sec 23(1) of Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (as amended), that the Tribunal constituted by the two remaining members hands down its decision and its reasons for that decision.



3. The applicant is, and was at all relevant times, involved in the manufacture of a certain type of packaging. It accounts on an accrual basis. Between October 1987 and August 1988, the applicant placed orders with six foreign suppliers (four German, one Japanese and one English) for the manufacture and supply of component parts to assemble a second production line. Since the parts did not exist at the time of the order, and indeed, had to be specially designed and manufactured, they were said to constitute "future goods", a characterisation placed on them in reliance on the definition of that term in sub-s 3(1) of the Goods Act 1958 (Vic). When completed, the goods were invariably consigned by sea and assembled at the applicant's premises in Victoria. The second production line came into operation in June 1989.



4. Under each agreement to sell, the applicant was required to make a part- payment of the agreed price at or near the date of the agreement, with subsequent progress payments and a final payment. Those payments were to be made in the currency of the country of the respective foreign supplier. In respect of the bulk of the future payments to be made, extending over an anticipated period of up to some eighteen months, the applicant entered into forward cover contracts ("hedging contracts" as defined in sub-s 82V(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act")) with Australian banks pursuant to which it contracted to acquire the specific amounts of foreign currency at a particular time at a predetermined rate of exchange.



5. The applicant, having contracted to purchase the goods for a specific number of units of the relevant foreign currency, ultimately paid that same number of units of currency. Because of variations in the relationship of the Australian dollar to each of these currencies, the final Australian dollar amount paid was different from the amount anticipated at the time the commitment was entered into. In fact, contrary to the applicant's expectations, the value of the Australian dollar generally increased as against each of the relevant foreign currencies between the dates of the various contracts and the time when the relevant payments became due. It followed, in many instances, that if the hedging contracts had not been taken out, and the foreign currency had, instead, been acquired each time a payment was due, the applicant's total cost would have been considerably less.



6. The movement in exchange rates was such, that with respect to some eight of the hedging contracts, the applicant chose not to take the foreign currency contracted for, but to pay to the banks at the nominated settlement date the difference between the Australian dollar requirement under the contracts, and the Australian dollar equivalent of the relevant foreign currency. The applicant then acquired the required foreign currency at the date when payment was due to the particular supplier.



7. The issue in this case is to identify the first of the two points in time between which the fluctuation in currency exchange rates is to be measured so as to determine whether, in a particular situation, the applicant has made a currency exchange gain, or incurred a currency exchange loss, within the terms of Division 3B of Part III of the Act. The applicant in its notice of objection ("T11") also relied upon the losses being deductible under sub-s 51(1) of the Act, or alternatively, giving rise to capital losses under Part IIIA of the Act. However, at the hearing, Mr Pagone advised the Tribunal that his client was not "pressing" either of those claims before the Tribunal, but simply wanted "to keep them alive", presumably "for another place".



8. In schedule 66 of its income tax return for the 1989 year of income ("T3"), the applicant made a request for the respondent's interpretation of the law, advising that it had claimed a deduction under Division 3B of Part III of the Act "for foreign exchange losses (net) on the purchase of capital assets incurred during the year", and that it wished to know whether the respondent agreed with its approach of using "the date of the `bill of lading' as the `time of purchase' in calculating the exchange gains and losses". The actual claim for the deduction was made in schedule 9 of that return as $3,710,216 and much detailed explanation of the calculations taken to arrive at that figure was set out in schedules 9A/1 to 9A/7. In schedule 9A/4, the applicant explained that, in calculating the exchange gains and losses on the various contracts for the purchase of the goods, it applied the date of the bill of lading relating to the shipment of those goods as the time of their acquisition, because:



[t]his is in accordance with generally accepted commercial practice and represents the date at which the ownership of the assets generally passes to the purchaser and performance of the contract is generally complete.



The exchange gains or losses under the "eligible contracts for the supply of plant items" have therefore been calculated as the difference between:



1. The cost of the goods in relevant foreign currency converted to Australian dollars using the exchange rate at the "bill of lading"; and



2. The amount of the settlement with the supplier in Australian dollars.



9. In his reply, dated 29 April 1990 ("T7"), the respondent stated that "in calculating the exchange gains and losses the `time of purchase' date should be the date the purchase contract or purchase order was made" rather than the various bill of lading dates. The respondent then requested that the applicant re-calculate the amount of the claimed (net) exchange loss deduction based on the respondent's interpretation. This the applicant did, re-calculating the amount of the deduction as $1,720,666, but stipulating that its supply of the re-calculated figures "is in no way intended to infer that [the applicant] accepts that the ATO's basis of calculation used is correct." ("T8")



10. On 14 August 1990, the respondent issued to the applicant a notice of assessment in respect of the 1989 year of income. The adjustment sheet that accompanied that notice of assessment ("T9") indicates that the applicant was assessed on the basis of an allowance of a (net) currency exchange loss deduction of $1,720,666 - the amount based upon the respondent's interpretation - thus increasing the amount of taxable income returned by the applicant by $1,989,550. The respondent took the view that the relevant currency exchange loss was constituted by the sum of the differences between the Australian dollar equivalent of the foreign currency liability as at the exchange rate at the date of the contract with each supplier (rather than at the date of the particular bill of lading as the applicant had done), and the Australian dollar equivalent of the actual amount of foreign currency paid at the exchange rate at the date of payment.



11. The applicant's objection to that assessment ("T11") was extensive and, besides the claims mentioned in paragraph 7 above, included a claim for an additional amount of $22,774 for depreciation, taking into account the exchange loss in the purchase costs of the relevant goods. After the request, and supply of various pieces of further information, the respondent subsequently notified the applicant that its objection had been partially allowed. The respondent issued an amended assessment allowing the applicant's claim for the additional $22,774 depreciation.



12. At the hearing, Mr Pagone advised the Tribunal that, after taking advice, the applicant had modified its claim by recalculating the (net) currency exchange loss deduction, on the assumption that Div 3B of Part III of the Act requires the exchange gains or losses on the contracts for the supply of the goods to be calculated separately from the exchange gains or losses on the forward cover (hedging) contracts. On examining the definition of "eligible contract" in sub-s 82V(1), we consider that such an assumption is, indeed, correct. Several schedules setting out the applicant's revised claim became exhibit 1. Doing the best we can with the voluminous figures, it would appear that the result is that the applicant's claim is reduced from $3,710,216 to $3,354,463.



13. If we understand correctly the applicant's argument in relation to the exchange gains or losses on the contracts for the supply of goods, it runs something like this: (i) the first of the two points in time between which the fluctuation in currency exchange rates is to be measured is the time when the property in the particular goods passed to the applicant; (ii) since the agreements were for the sale of unascertained or future goods, no property in the goods was transferred to the applicant purchaser unless and until the relevant goods were ascertained and unconditionally appropriated to the relevant contract with the applicant's assent, whether express or implied; (iii) as a general rule, the relevant goods were unconditionally appropriated to the particular contract when delivered by the seller to the carrier for shipment to the applicant; and (iv) in the absence of any evidence of knowledge by the applicant of delivery to the carrier and assent to appropriation prior to the bill of lading date, the bill of lading date was the best available evidence of the nearest date as to when property in the particular goods passed. Support for such an approach was said to be found in the decisions of the majority in Caltex Ltd v FCT [1960] HCA 17; (1959-60) 106 CLR 205, particularly the observations of Fullagar J at p 223.



14. In support of the method it employed in calculating its exchange gains or losses, the applicant called three witnesses: one TD, its finance manager; Mr AE Talbot, a partner with the firm of Price Waterhouse, and Professor CG Peirson, an expert in accounting methodology from Monash University. TD gave detailed evidence with respect to the entering into of the supply contracts, the hedging contracts and the utilisation or cancellation of the currency to be provided under these hedging contracts. Both Mr Talbot and Professor Peirson provided evidence relating to the appropriate accounting treatment of contracts for the manufacture and supply of depreciable capital assets and associated forward cover contracts.



15. In essence, the two accounting experts demonstrated that the accounting standards issued by the Australian Accounting Research Foundation (AASB1012 and AASB1015) required the applicant to recognise the purchase of equipment at the date of acquisition, and to include in the calculation of the cost of the equipment any gains or losses as a result of currency exchange rate fluctuations associated with the acquisition of those goods, including hedging contracts. It was sought from this evidence to support the applicant's argument that the dates of the bills of lading were appropriate as the dates of "acquisition" of the goods, hence justifying it's adoption as its first point of reference from which it calculated the exchange gain made or loss incurred.



16. It is interesting to note that the accounting principles, as explained by the experts, do not recognise as such any gain or loss on foreign currency which is required to satisfy a liability for the purchase of capital equipment, it being appropriate to adjust the asset purchase price for any variation in currency exchange rates between the incurring of the liability and the payment. The Tribunal was not invited to regard the accounting principles as determining the income tax result in terms of Division 3B. In the result, we have not been persuaded that this accounting evidence adds any significant weight to the applicant's case. The evidence did lead to the speculation that if Division 3B had not been introduced in 1987, both parties to this application would have regarded the total net final outlay of the applicant as the cost of the goods, subject to depreciation, and would not have sought to identify and deal separately with any foreign currency exchange loss or gain.



17. Turning to the law, Division 3B of Part III of the Act was inserted by Act No. 61 of 1987 and, broadly speaking, applies to losses or gains on an "eligible contract" entered into on or after 19 February 1986. The sections which are relevant to the issue in this case are:-



Sub-s 82U(1) This Division applies in relation to gains and losses only to the extent to which they are of a capital nature.



82U(2) This Division does not apply to a loss incurred by a taxpayer except to the extent to which, if the loss were not of a capital nature, a deduction would be allowable to the taxpayer under section 51 in respect of the loss.



82U(3) This Division does not apply to a gain made by a taxpayer under a contract except to the extent to which, if the taxpayer had incurred a loss under the contract and that loss had not been of a capital nature, a deduction would have been allowable to the taxpayer under section 51 in respect of the loss.



Sub-s 82V(1) In this Division, unless the contrary intention appears--



...



"commencing day" means 19 February 1986;



...



"currency exchange gain" means a gain to the extent to which it is attributable to currency exchange rate fluctuations;



"currency exchange loss" means a loss to the extent to which it is attributable to currency exchange rate fluctuations;



"eligible contract", in relation to a taxpayer, means--



(a) a contract entered into by the taxpayer on or after the commencing day, other than a hedging contract; or



(b) a hedging contract entered into by the taxpayer, on or after the commencing day, in relation to a contract to which paragraph (a) applies;



"hedging contract", in relation to a taxpayer, means a contract that is entered into by the taxpayer for the sole purpose of eliminating or reducing the risk of adverse financial consequences that might result for the taxpayer or an associate of the taxpayer, under another contract, from currency exchange rate fluctuations.



82V(2) For the purposes of this Division--



(a) a currency exchange gain made, or a currency exchange loss incurred, in respect of currency purchased under a contract shall be taken to have been made or incurred under that contract;



(b) a gain shall be taken to have been made, or a loss to have been incurred, at the time when it was realised; and



(c) a reference to a person acquiring rights or obligations arising under a contract is a reference to the person acquiring such rights or obligations otherwise than by reason of having entered into the contract.



82V(3) Subject to sub-section (4), where a taxpayer acquires rights or obligations arising under a contract, this Division applies in relation to the taxpayer as if the contract had been entered into by the taxpayer at the time when, and for the purposes for which, the taxpayer acquired the rights or obligations.



...



Section 82Y The assessable income of a taxpayer of a year of income shall include any currency exchange gain made by the taxpayer in the year of income under an eligible contract.



Sub-s 82Z(1) Subject to this section, a currency exchange loss incurred by a taxpayer in a year of income under an eligible contract is an allowable deduction in respect of the year of income.



18. During argument, the presiding member expressed some surprise why the Tribunal was asked to apply the Goods Act 1958 (Vic) to agreements between a Victorian importer and, for example, German and Japanese suppliers, with respect to contracts which make specific provision that the law of the country of the supplier is to govern the contract. Looking at one of the "Japanese" contracts, for example, it stipulates that "this contract shall be governed by and construed in all respects in accordance with the laws of Japan"; another contract with a German supplier stipulates that "unless otherwise agreed, the Contract shall be governed by the law of the Vendor's country". For good measure, two of the foreign contracts provide that "the time at which the risk shall pass shall be fixed in accordance with the International Rules for the Interpretation of Trade Terms (Incoterms)". It soon became apparent that neither counsel had considered the conflict of laws point. Ultimately, Mr Pagone gratefully accepted the Tribunal's suggestion that, when faced with a question which is governed by the law of a foreign country, courts (and tribunals) will start with the presumption that, in the absence of proof of the foreign law, it is to be presumed that it is the same as the lex fori. However, in his address, Dr Jessup QC rejected the proposition that we should presume that the foreign law was identical with the law of the forum, even though he chose not to call evidence as to the various foreign laws which may be relevant to this case. In the result, the conflict of laws issue was not resolved, although it may well be that Mr Pagone's submission, to the extent that it relies on the Victorian Goods Act, may survive proof of the relevant Japanese or German law, the (former) Hague Rules, or the current United Nations Convention on Contracts for the International Sale of Goods of 1980 (the Vienna Convention), which provides a uniform law for international sales and to which Australia became a signatory on 1 April 1989. In the end, in the view we have taken of the law, we are satisfied that, on the evidence, nothing turns on the conflicts point.



19. It being common ground between the parties that a loss incurred in the factual circumstances of this case is deductible under Division 3B, it is thus necessary to consider the question whether the loss claimed by the applicant properly falls within the provisions of the Division.



20. Although the objection asserts, in the alternative, that "the exchange loss constitutes an allowable deduction pursuant to sec 51 of the Act, offset to the extent of any gain pursuant to sec 25 of the Act", we are satisfied that, in the circumstances, the resultant loss brought about by currency movements is clearly of a capital nature, and thus excluded from deduction under sec 51. This view receives ample support from decisions such as Armco (Australasia) Pty Ltd v FCT [1948] HCA 49; (1948) 76 CLR 584 and Texas Co (Australasia) Ltd v FCT [1940] HCA 9; (1940) 63 CLR 382.



21. We find that the decision in Caltex Ltd v FCT [1960] HCA 17; (1959-1960) 106 CLR 205, depends very much on its own facts. These are succinctly set out in the headnote as follows:



The appellant, a company incorporated in New South Wales and controlled by non-residents, incurred over a period of years a debt for trading stock to a supplier in the USA. During this period there were marked variations in the exchange rate between Australia and the USA for the most part against Australia. These variations were reflected in the appellant's books by monthly adjustments to the supplier's account in its books. Remittances in partial payment of this indebtedness were from time to time made by the appellant which for this purpose used Australian currency to purchase dollars. In 1936, the appellant discharged the debt by two payments made by cheque drawn on its account at a New York bank. A new supplier had lent the appellant the full amount of these payments and had paid this amount in dollars into the appellant's bank account. At the dates of these payments, the Australian equivalent of the US dollars then paid was substantially higher than either the cost at which its supplies had in Australian currency originally been taken into account, or the amount at which by reason of the adjustments in its books that cost then stood in the books. The appellant claimed to have incurred by these two transactions an "exchange loss" deductible as a loss or outgoing under s. 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1940.



The Court in that case held (by majority) that there had been no loss or outgoing within the meaning of sec 51(1) since the company had not discharged its American indebtedness, but had merely substituted one creditor for another, and thus had not suffered any loss of Australian pounds.



22. Reliance was placed on the decision of Dixon CJ, who stated (at pp 218-219):-



.. In Armco (Australia) Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [(1948) [1948] HCA 49; 76 CLR 584] there was a question whether a manufacturer and trader was entitled to a deduction on account of the increase in the outlay involved in purchasing dollars at a more unfavourable rate of exchange in order to discharge a dollar debt incurred in a long past accounting period for stock in trade and raw material. All the members of the Court were of opinion that, apart from considerations not here relevant, the deduction would be allowable and in that we followed the decision of this Court in the case of the now appellant reported as Texas Co (Australasia) Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [(1940) [1940] HCA 9; 63 CLR 382], a decision relating to an earlier period of time. In the case of Armco (Australia) Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation, I attempted to state the basis upon which the allowance of such a deduction was based and I shall take the course of repeating the explanation of why the increased amount required to purchase the exchange formed a trading loss or outgoing which would be an allowable deduction from the assessable income of the accounting period in which it was incurred. For it is just as important in this case as it was in that cited to see how such a result would be reached. "It would arise from the fact that it is a continuing business, depending upon the purchase or manufacture of goods and their resale. The accounts are, of course, made up, not on a basis of receipts and disbursements, but upon the commercial basis of valuation and credit. Nevertheless in such a case actual expenditure in a later accounting period arising from a fortuitous increase in the amount of the liabilities taken into it from the prior period may form a proper debit in the later period, notwithstanding that the item relates to purchases or costs of manufacture included in an earlier accounting period. The comparison made between the beginning and end of an accounting period means that stock in trade and purchases are taken into account not by reference to what is actually paid for them, but according to the value assigned to the one and the liability incurred in acquiring the other. That is done, of course, entirely independently of the period within which the actual disbursement of money is made to discharge the liability. If, as is commonly the case, the amount of the liability is fixed and incapable of subsequent variation, the disbursement itself whenever made could never matter for the purpose of computing profit or loss in that or any subsequent period. But if for any reason the amount is capable of changing, as is the case when the indebtedness is in a foreign currency and the rate of exchange may alter, a further question arises. If the change takes place in a subsequent period and actual payment is then made, is the increase or decrease, as the case may be, to be attributed to the prior period and the net profit or loss reassessed? Obviously not. It is to be taken in as an item belonging to the subsequent period; for the reason that, with continuing trading, when increases beyond the estimates by which assets and liabilities are carried out of one period into the next occur, they must be treated as incidents of the system and they must be regarded as belonging to the period in which they accrue or are realized" [(1948) 76 CLR at p 618]



Fullagar J stated at p.223:-



In the books of the company the invoice price of trading stock (which included f.o.b. cost, freight and insurance) was credited to the old supplier at or about the date of shipment. (This was, of course, in accordance with ordinary commercial practice [(1940) 63 CLR at p 465]). But a considerable period (apparently never less than a year) elapsed between the entry in the books of a particular shipment of trading stock and the making of a remittance to New York in payment for that shipment. If, therefore, the rate of exchange between the Australian pound and the US dollar rose or fell in the period between shipment and payment, the company would have to pay less or more than the invoice price in Australian pounds in order to obtain the necessary dollars for the making of the payment. The way in which the company kept its accounts from time to time with a view to providing for and dealing with fluctuations in the rate of exchange is described in the case stated, and references are made to it in the Texas Co's Case [(1940) [1940] HCA 9; 63 CLR 382]. It is a matter of considerable complexity, and the practice varied from time to time, but I do not think that it affects the substance of the position now in question, and I do not think it necessary to refer further to it.



Later at p.226-7, his Honour noted:-



... If the payment is made in an accounting period later than that in which the purchase was taken into account, the exchange loss or gain will be properly brought into account in the later accounting period. This is because it is only in the later accounting period that the gain has been "derived" or the loss "incurred". The gain derived is assessable income of the later period: the loss incurred is an allowable deduction in the later period.



23. A similar view was expressed by Gibbs J (as he then was) in International Nickel Australia Ltd v FCT [1977] HCA 49; (1976-1977) 137 CLR 347 at 352-3:



Where income is assessed on an accruals basis, a purchase of stock in trade is taken into the books at the time when the purchase is made. The purchase price is an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the taxpayer's assessable income, and is an allowable deduction in the year of the purchase. However if in a subsequent income year, when the time for payment arrives, the taxpayer is compelled, because of a change in the rate of exchange, to expend a larger amount in Australian dollars in discharging his liability for the price than had already been allowed as a deduction, the additional amount which he is required to expend - the exchange loss - is an allowable deduction in that subsequent year.



24. Mr Pagone is so persuasive that we must guard ourselves against his plausibility. It is with no disrespect to him to state that, in the end, we have not been persuaded that his chain of reasoning reaches the correct result. When all is said and done, a bill of lading tells us little more than that the goods have been shipped, that there is a contract of carriage and that it constitutes a document of title to the goods. It seems to us that the effect of the Goods Act is "spent" in circumstances where the contracts in issue evidence a contrary intention. Thus, in one contract (the Brückner contract), the parties provided that:



... if delivery has been made before payment of the whole sum payable under the Contract, the Plant delivered shall, to the extent permitted by the law of the country where the Plant is situated after delivery, remain the property of the vendor until such payment has been effected".



Another contract provides that notwithstanding delivery, the vendor retains property in the goods until payment has been effected in full. Thus, the contracts to which we have been privy, provide their own terms for payment, delivery and the passing of property, hence avoiding the need to have recourse to the Goods Act. Put another way, whatever magical qualities are said to reside in these bills of lading, they cannot apply to calculate the exchange gains/losses for purposes of Division 3B, if only because we are here dealing with contracts in which the parties have agreed that property in the goods shall pass, not at the date of the bill of lading, but at a different point in time, indeed, at a point which may be much later than the date of the bill of lading. It follows that if the bill of lading were to be treated as the point of reference when the goods were purchased, the period over which the calculation of the exchange gain or loss would be effectively eliminated, since ownership does not pass to the purchaser until the last payment has been made. The flaw in adopting the applicant's method of calculating its exchange gains or losses can be illustrated by the fact that whether there is a gain or loss cannot even be demonstrated at the time of making the payment in those cases where the date of the bill of lading is later than the time of payment. It is perhaps noteworthy to record that under our law (unlike the laws of the United States; cf UCC s 1-201(6)) a bill of lading is only used for the shipment of goods by sea. It is therefore tempting to add that it may seem slightly eccentric that, applying the applicant's theory, where an agreement of sale is otherwise silent on the terms for payment, any foreign exchange gain/loss may vary, depending on whether goods are shipped by sea or by air. In any event, we are satisfied that passing of property, whenever occurring, is not a relevant point in time for our determination.



25. It seems to us, when one has regard to the above quoted passages in Caltex Ltd and International Nickel Australia Ltd and the provisions of Division 3B, there can be no dispute that the date of the payment, the amount of which is affected by currency exchange rate fluctuation, is the later of the two points between which that fluctuation is to be measured for the purposes of that Division. Indeed, neither of the parties submitted otherwise. It also seems to us that the provisions of Division 3B clearly illustrate that the Division is concerned with the gain or loss that occurs during the period over which a taxpayer bears the risk of such a fluctuation. That period is not from the time that property passes in the case of fluctuations associated with the purchase of goods, but from the time that the taxpayer enters into the contract to purchase those goods.



26. In the result, we find that the currency exchange gains made, or losses incurred, in respect of the contracts for the supply of goods in this case must be calculated based on the period from the dates of the various contracts to the dates of the various payments in respect of those contracts.



27. So far as the forward cover (hedging) contracts are concerned, needing, as we consider they must, to be considered separately from the goods contracts, the period over which the applicant bears the risk of fluctuation may be different from that period in respect of the goods contract to which it relates. We are therefore satisfied that the currency exchange gains made, or losses incurred, in respect of each of the hedging contracts must be calculated based upon the period from the date when the particular hedging contract was entered into to the date/s when payment is made by the applicant in accordance with that contract.



28. Due to the difficulties in relation to quantum as a result of the mass of figures involved and the changing methodologies adopted in calculation, we have decided that the most appropriate course in the circumstances is to set the decision under review aside and to remit the matter to the respondent to amend the relevant assessment in accordance with the above reasons.
