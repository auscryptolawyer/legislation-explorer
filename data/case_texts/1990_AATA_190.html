VT87/146, VT87/3515 and VT89/487 and Commissioner of Taxation [1990] AATA 190; (1990) 21 ATR 3601; 90 ATC 543 (17 August 1990)

Last Updated: 2 June 2009



Income Tax - assessable income - taxpayer member of accountancy partnership - partnership purchased work-in-progress from previous partnership - work completed and fees charged - whether whole of fees assessable income - objection decision affirmed



Income Tax - Income Tax Ruling - ruling expressed in broad terms - Commissioner's argument inconsistent with applicability of ruling to all circumstances apparently covered by ruling - argument accepted - need for Commissioner to revise ruling



Henderson v Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1969] HCA 14; (1970) 119 CLR 612

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL ) Nos. VT87/146, VT87/3515

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



REASONS FOR DECISION



17 August 1990 Deputy President I. R. Thompson



1. The three taxpayers in the deemed applications in these proceedings (which were heard together by agreement of the parties) were partners in an accounting firm during the 1980 year of income. The assessable income of a partner in a year of income includes his individual interest in the net income of a partnership in that year of income (Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") section 92). The assessable income of each of the taxpayers for the 1980 year of income was assessed by the respondent as including an amount of approximately $31,500 as his individual interest in the net income of the partnership for that year. Each taxpayer objected to the inclusion of that amount as part of his assessable income. The objection was disallowed. Upon request by the taxpayer the Commissioner referred the objection decision to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal pursuant to section 189 of the Act for review.



2. At the hearing of the deemed applications the taxpayers were all represented by Mr B. Jacomb, a solicitor; the respondent was represented by Dr S. Kenny, of Counsel. A number of documents were tendered in evidence. Oral evidence was given by two of the taxpayers, Mr Y and Mr Z. Both are chartered accountants.



3. On the basis of the oral evidence given by Mr Y and Mr Z and the documentary evidence before the Tribunal I find as fact that in the 1979 year of income all three taxpayers were employees of a firm of accountants which carried on practice under the name X. The firm was a partnership of four partners ("the old partnership"). At some time towards the end of the 1979 year of income the three taxpayers were informed that one of the partners wished to retire and that they were invited to join with the other three partners of the old partnership in forming a new partnership to carry on the accountancy practice. There were discussions between the taxpayers and the four partners and agreement in principle was reached. As a result of that agreement in principle steps were taken by the partners of the old partnership to dissolve it on 30 June 1979. Also as a result of the agreement in principle, on 1 July 1979 the remaining three partners and the three taxpayers commenced carrying on business in partnership ("the new partnership") under the same name, X, as the old partnership. On 2 July 1979 heads of agreement were drawn up for the sale of the accountancy practice carried on under the name X by the partners in the old partnership to the partners in the new partnership.



4. Although the old partnership had been dissolved on 30 June 1979, and the new partnership had commenced business on 1 July 1979, it was not until 20 December 1979 that the partnership agreement of the new partnership was formalized. On that same date the partners in the old partnership and the partners in the new partnership signed a sale agreement providing for the sale of "the property and goodwill of the old partnership upon the terms and conditions" set out in the agreement. It provided that the assets and goodwill sold vested in the purchasers on 1 July 1979, that the price for them was $466,523.39 and that 10% of that amount was to be paid on the signing of the agreement and the balance on or before 30 June 1980. Clause (1) provided:-

"(1) THE Vendors shall sell and the Purchasers shall purchase the assets including the goodwill of the old partnership as set out in the Balance Sheet annexed hereto."



5. The balance sheet annexed to the agreement showed the value of the total assets as $617,102.56 and the liabilities as $150,579.17. The assets shown included cash in hand, prepayments, a loan to an associated company, trade debtors, work in progress, deposit on a Xerox copier, furniture and fittings, office machines and goodwill. The value of the work in progress was shown as $195,254.58.



6. The financial statements of the new partnership for the year which ended on 30 June 1980 show the income of that partnership as follows:-

"INCOME

Less Interim Billing Included

in Fees Charged 65550 189842

======



Mr Y gave evidence, which I accept, that during the 1980 year of income the new partnership completed most of the work in progress which it had taken over from the old partnership and received approximately 90% of the fees for it.



7. He also gave evidence that the partnership agreement of the old partnership was in substantially the same terms as the partnership agreement of the new partnership. Dr Kenny objected to the admissibility of that evidence on the ground that the taxpayers should have tendered in evidence the actual old partnership agreement. However, the Tribunal is not bound by the rules of evidence (Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, section 33(1)(c)). The details of the capital accounts of the partners of the old partnership which were annexed to that partnership's income tax return for the 1979 year of income are consistent with the fact of the partnership agreement having contained provision substantially the same as that made by Clause 11(b) of the partnership agreement of the new partnership. That clause provided:-

"11. (b) On the 30th day of June in every year or so soon afterwards as is possible an account shall be taken of the assets (which shall include the fair value of accounts not rendered to clients of the partnership or fees accrued but uncharged at the 30th day of June in respect of work completed or partly performed after proper allowance for amounts charged in advance up to that date) and liabilities of the partnership and of all dealings and transactions of the partnership during the then preceding year, and of all matters and things usually comprehended in accounts of businesses of a similar nature."



I accept that the partnership agreement of the old partnership contained similar provision.



8. At least in the books of the old partnership, therefore, the value of the work in progress on 30 June 1979 had been brought to account and credited to the capital accounts of the individual partners. Upon dissolution of the old partnership each partner became entitled to his share of the net assets of the partnership as and when they might be realized. They were, in fact, realized by the sale of them on 20 December 1979 with an effective backdating to 1 July 1979.



9. The issue in these proceedings is how the work in progress purchased by the members of the new partnership from the members of the old partnership is to be characterized in the hands of the purchasers. There have been several cases in which courts have been required to determine the characterization of payments for work in progress in the hands of the persons to whom the payments were made. In those cases the payments were made to a partner retiring from a partnership in respect of work done by the partnership before the date of his retirement but for which no fees had become due because it had not reached that stage.



10. In Henderson v Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1969] HCA 14; (1970) 119 CLR 612 at page 650 Barwick C.J., with whom the other two members of the Court agreed, held that, until a service for which a fee would eventually be payable was so far performed that the fee had been earned and become recoverable, it was not proper for the Commissioner to estimate the value of that service and include it in the income derived by the person who had performed it. However, his Honour observed that it might be that "a different course can be taken if an estimation of profits is being made for some other purpose than the present". In that case no estimation of profits had been undertaken for the purpose of paying a partner the value of work performed but not completed to the state that a fee was recoverable.



11. In Jamieson v Commissioner of Inland Revenue 74 ATC 6008 the New Zealand Court of Appeal had before it an agreed statement of facts. They included the following:-

(1) the taxpayer and the continuing partners had agreed that the taxpayer was entitled to a proportionate share of fees on uncompleted and current files;



(2) with the agreement of the taxpayer the continuing partners had calculated the value of the work on the uncompleted and current files;



(3) the taxpayer was entitled to his proportionate share of fees earned up to the date of dissolution but not received;



(4) the taxpayer accepted payment in full settlement of his share of the work in progress.



Wild C.J. at page 6011 contrasted the purpose for which the estimation of profits had been undertaken with that in Henderson; it had been made in order to arrive at the partners' respective shares of profit from their practice up to the date of the taxpayer's withdrawal from it. His Honour pointed out that "it is a fundamental principle of income tax law that the Court must look to the true nature of the payment". He, therefore, dismissed the appeal against the decision of the primary judge that the amount paid to the taxpayer for the work in progress was to be included in his assessable income. McCarthy P. expressed similar views at page 6013 and came to the same conclusion. At page 6014 Speight J., agreeing with Wild C.J. and McCarthy P., held that it was immaterial that the payment was made some time after the agreement and the dissolution of the partnership, as the valuation of the files and accounts by way of variation of the previous profit sharing agreement, which was part of the process agreed on for the purpose of the dissolution, would necessarily have taken some time but was part of that process.



12. In Stapleton v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 89 ATC 4818 the Federal Court was concerned with payments made to a taxpayer under the terms of a memorandum which had been handed to him when he became a partner in a firm which did not have a written partnership agreement. The memorandum expressly provided for payment for work in progress on the taxpayer's eventual retirement from the partnership. That occurred seven years later. The agreement evidenced by the memorandum was still in force. The amount of the value of the work was held by Sheppard J. to represent that which in due course of time would become income when the work was complete; when paid to the taxpayer, it was assessable income in his hands. At page 4824 his Honour referred with approval to the following passage from the judgment of Brennan J., as a Judge of the Federal Court, in Federal Coke Co. Pty. Limited v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 77 ATC 4255:-

"When a recipient of moneys provides consideration for the payment, the consideration will ordinarily supply the touchstone for ascertaining whether the receipt is on revenue account or not. The character of an asset which is sold for a price, or the character of a cause of action discharged by a payment will ordinarily determine, unless it be a sham transaction, the character of the receipt of the price or payment. The consideration establishes the matter in respect of which the moneys are received. The character of the receipt may then be determined by the character, in the recipient's hands, of the matter in respect of which the moneys are received. Thus, when moneys are received in consideration of surrendering a benefit to which the recipient is entitled under a contract, it is relevant to enquire whether or not that benefit was a capital asset in his hands. To adapt the words of Lord MacMillan in Van den Berghs Limited v. Clark (1935) A.C. at p. 443 and of Williams J. in Bennett v. F.C. of T. [1947] HCA 25; (1947) 75 C.L.R. 480 at p. 485, the enquiry is whether the congeries of the rights which the recipient enjoyed under the contract and which for a price he surrendered was a capital asset."



That passage is reminiscent of the comment of Wild C.J. in Jamieson that "it is a fundamental principle of income tax law that the Court must look to the true nature of the payment".



13. In Case X20 90 ATC 231 the Administrative Appeals Tribunal held that payment made to partners for work in progress on the dissolution of their partnership was capital. However, in that case there was evidence that, when the partnership was formed, the work in progress contributed by the incoming partners constituted the partnership's major asset and was recorded as such by crediting the partner's capital account. Consequently, when upon the dissolution of the partnership the partners received credit for work in progress, that constituted a repayment of their equity in the partnership. The Tribunal noted that the balance sheet for each year disclosed a valuation of work in progress as a partnership asset, the movement of work in progress being brought to account as income for the purposes of determining each partner's respective share of the profit. The Tribunal distinguished Jamieson and Stapleton on the basis that in those cases work in progress had not previously been brought into the accounts as an asset.



14. Because the work in progress of a practice has not reached the stage at which fees are recoverable for it, the purchaser of the practice does not acquire a chose-in-action. The services of an accountant involve personal skill and competence and are, therefore, of such a nature that a contract to perform them cannot be assigned without the consent of the other party. A client for whom work has been commenced but not completed may decline to permit the purchaser to complete the work. Consequently, when such a practice is purchased, work in progress is in many ways comparable to the goodwill of that practice. It provides the purchaser with the potential for earning fees by his own work but, unlike the goodwill of a business, it relates to particular work which the purchaser may be able to complete. If he does so, he can then charge for the work an amount more than the value, calculated in accordance with usual costing procedures of his profession, of the work which he has himself performed. I have come to the conclusion, therefore, that the Tribunal was correct in Case X20 when it held that in the situation which existed in that case the work in progress was part of the capital of the partnership which acquired it. In the present case I am satisfied that, however the payments received by the partners in the old partnership for the work in progress should be characterized, as to which it is unnecessary for me to express any opinion, the value of the work in progress formed part of the capital of the new partnership from the date of its purchase of the practice of the old partnership.



15. The question which remains to be answered is whether, when the work in progress was completed by the new partnership and it charged fees for that work, the whole amount of those fees was derived as income or only the part of them which exceeded the amount paid to the old partnership for the work in progress. Dr Kenny referred the Tribunal to a decision of the English Court of Appeal in The City of London Contract Corporation, Limited v Styles (Surveyor of Taxes) (1887) 2 TC 239. There the taxpayer had taken over the business of another company and paid to that company an amount of money for the assignment of a number of unexecuted contracts, some of which had been partly performed. It performed, or completed performance of, them. It argued that only the net amount received after deduction of the amount paid for the unexecuted contracts was assessable income. The Court held that the whole of the payments received for performance of the contracts was assessable income. At page 244 Lord Esher M.R. said:-

"Here is a clear statement that the difference between money expended in the year in order to earn income which was to be received in the year, and the income so received, was £128,000. That is the net profit within the year, that is the sum upon which income tax is to be paid; and it is as plain as plain can be that you cannot deduct from those net profits so arrived at any part of the capital which you so invested, whether you paid it or not for the purchase of the business which you were obliged to purchase before you could begin the difference between expenditure and income year by year. If you do not darken it with words, it is as plain as plain can be."



16. Dr Kenny also referred to Case G37 75 ATC 321, where the facts were similar and No. 3 Board of Review followed and applied the decision in Styles. She also drew attention to Case No. F24 6 TBRD 149 in which the taxpayer was a barrister and solicitor. He purchased a practice and a condition of the sale agreement was that he should retain the costs in respect of uncompleted work which had been done by the vendor. He completed the work and was paid fees in respect of it. He argued that only the portion of the fees which represented the value of the work which he himself had performed in completing the work was assessable income. The Board of Review held that the whole of the fees received were assessable income. The facts differed from those in the present case in that the sale agreement did not include any valuation of the work done by the vendor and provided simply for a payment in globo for the purchase of the practice, including the work in progress. There was, however, evidence that during negotiations for the sale of the practice the value of the work had been discussed.



17. The taxpayer in Case No. F24 did not claim that the amount which he regarded as being the value of the work done by the vendor should be deducted from his gross business income but rather that, when he received it, it was a non-revenue or capital receipt at the point of derivation and thus was not to be included as part of the gross income from the business. The Board of Review contrasted the purchase of ascertained book debts with the purchase of unexecuted contracts as in Styles. It considered the situation in the case before it to be more nearly akin to the unexecuted contracts in Styles than to book debts. It concluded:-

". . . It appears to us, therefore, that the taxpayer made lump sum payments of capital, in each instance, for the purchase of a business, a profit yielding subject including all the advantages which the completion of partly executed professional work would yield, but excluding book debts. In due course, the businesses gave forth their yields and the gross revenue or business proceeds thereof actually received by the taxpayer in the relevant period were the full amounts of £980/12/10 and £9,393/8/8 respectively."



18. Mr Jacomb pointed out that in Styles and Case G37 the vendor's interest in the contracts had apparently been assigned to the taxpayers, whereas the work in progress of the old partnership in the present case had been performed under a contract of service. However, a contract of service can be assigned with the consent of the other party to it. There is no evidence before the Tribunal (and the onus rested on the taxpayers to adduce such evidence and thereby show that the assessment was excessive (section 190(b) of the Act)) that the contracts were not assigned. On the contrary, Mr Y gave evidence that the performance of most of them was completed and the fees therefor paid by the clients. I am, therefore, unable to distinguish those cases from the present case in the manner suggested by Mr Jacomb.



19. Mr Jacomb also submitted that Case No. F24 was to be distinguished from the present case by reason of the fact that in that case the work in progress was purchased simply as part of the vendor's practice without being separately valued. However, it appears to me that the distinction is not of any legal consequence. In the present case, although the work in progress was separately valued, it was purchased as an essential part of the ongoing business of the practice. I have come to the conclusion, therefore, that the principles which applied in Styles, Case No. F24 and Case G37 are applicable in the present case also. I find that all the fees received by the new partnership upon completion of the work taken over from the old partnership were assessable income in the hands of the new partnership and that no part of those fees was a non-revenue or capital receipt.



20. Mr Jacomb submitted, in effect, that such a conclusion would be unfair to the taxpayers as they received no benefit from the payment which they made to the partners in the old partnership for the work in progress. Quite clearly such a broad statement is not totally correct. Of the assets acquired on the purchase of the practice the work in progress was the second largest item, less than the amount owed by trade debtors but substantially more than the value shown for the goodwill of the practice. If the work in progress had not been among the assets, it is likely that the value of the goodwill would itself have been considerably less. In other words, it was the essence of the practice. However, what I understood Mr Jacomb to mean was that, to the extent that the taxpayers had paid the partners in the old partnership for the work in progress, they obtained no direct financial gain when the clients paid them for that work on completion.



21. Implicit in that reasoning is the submission that for that reason that part of the fees should not be treated as assessable income. However, that begs the whole question of the characterization of the asset purchased by the payment made and of the fees in the hands of the taxpayers. As I pointed out during the hearing, there was nothing to stop the partners in the new partnership from insisting that the price paid by them for the work in progress should be discounted to take account of their liability to pay tax on the fees charged for that work upon its completion. However, if the decision in Case X20 is correct (I was informed by the parties that it was under appeal) so that, where the method of accounting adopted by a partnership is such as that adopted by the new partnership in this case, work in progress is part of the equity of the partnership, what is lost on the swings in the first year of a partnership will be regained on the roundabout when it is dissolved - provided, of course, that the practice is not allowed to run down and lose its resale value.



22. Mr Jacomb also drew the Tribunal's attention to Taxation Ruling IT 2551 which is entitled "Income Tax: Sale of Interest in a Professional Partnership: Amounts Paid to Retiring Partners on Account of Work in Progress". Taxation rulings do not, of course, have the force of law; indeed, if they are inconsistent with the law, the respondent has an obligation to levy tax in accordance with the law rather than with his ruling. In the circumstances with which the Tribunal was concerned in Case X20, if the law and the characterization of the work in progress immediately before the sale of the practice are as the Tribunal held them to be, the taxation ruling is clearly inapplicable. As the case presented for the respondent by Dr Kenny in these proceedings was inconsistent with the applicability of the ruling to the circumstances of cases such as Case X20 and the present case, it appears that consideration needs to be given to whether it should be amended to make that clear.



23. Mr Jacomb submitted in the alternative that, if the full amount of the fees charged for the work in progress when it was completed was income, it was income only of the partners in the old partnership and not of the partners in the new partnership, or that at least only the partners in the old partnership were entitled to it beneficially, the taxpayers being bare trustees. However, in view of the terms of the agreement for the sale of

the practice, that cannot be so. Whether the fees when charged were capital or income - and I have found that they were income - the partners in the new partnership were entitled to them legally and beneficially.



24. For the reasons which I have stated above the objection decisions under review must be affirmed.
