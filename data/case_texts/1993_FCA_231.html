Re Sonia Szajntop v the Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1993] FCA 231; (1993) 93 Atc 4307 (1993) 114 ALR 396 (1993) 25 Atr 469 (1993) 42 FCR 318 (13 May 1993)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: SONIA SZAJNTOP

And: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

Nos. VG267, VG268 and VG269 of 1992

Income Tax

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

VICTORIA DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - objections and appeals - whether tax agent's letter a valid objection to assessment - whether letter stated fully and in detail grounds of objection - reference to appellant's tax return to determine the scope of the purported objection

ORDER

The Court orders that:

1. The appeal is allowed.

2. The orders made by the Honourable Mr Justice Hill on 1 July 1992

dismissing the appellant's applications by way of appeal from

the Administrative Appeals Tribunal are set aside and in lieu

thereof it is ordered:

(a) that the decisions of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal

dated 13 December 1991 in respect of the appellant's

applications for review in applications numbers VT 91/136,

137 and 138 be set aside;

(b) that the said applications to the Administrative Appeals

Tribunal be remitted to the Tribunal for further

consideration and determination according to law;

(c) that the respondent pay the appellant's costs of the said

applications to this Court.

3. The respondent is to pay the appellant's costs of this appeal.

Note: Settlement and entry of Orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

BLACK CJ AND BURCHETT J The issue in these appeals is whether a letter sent by the appellant's former tax agent to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation was a valid objection to an assessment for the purposes of s. 185(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act") or whether it failed as an objection because it did not state fully and in detail the grounds of the objection.

2. The issue arises in the following way. The appellant was the subject of an income tax audit for the years ending 30 June 1989, 1988 and 1987. At that time she had not lodged tax returns for three years. The Commissioner calculated the appellant's taxable income for the three years in question by the assets betterment method and on 10 July 1987 issued default assessments pursuant to s. 167 of the Act. The assessments were based on an assets betterment statement. Shortly after the default assessments were issued the appellant's then tax agent lodged returns for the years in question on the appellant's behalf.

3. On 11 August 1987, within the 60 days allowed by s. 185(1) of the Act for the lodging of an objection against an assessment, the tax agent sent a letter to the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation in the following terms:-

"We are in possession of your assessment No. 385012/3 issued on

the 10th July, 1987 and hereby wish to object to your assessment

in full.

On the 5th August 1987 we lodged income tax returns for the above

and in each case it indicated that there was no tax payable at

all. The assessments were raised from a betterment statement

produced by one of your Officer (sic) on completely erroneous

information. We are at present getting together absolute proof as

to the above facts.

In the meantime we request that the assessment be set aside and no

penalties charged due to the above events."

4. This is the letter that the appellant contends was a valid objection. It was treated as such by the Commissioner who, on 24 February 1989, disallowed it. The appellant then elected to have the Commissioner's decision referred to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review. The matter was listed for preliminary conference on 19 September 1991 at which the Commissioner raised the issue of the validity of the objection, contending that it disclosed no ground. The Deputy President before whom the preliminary conference was held, Dr Gerber, suggested that particulars might be supplied, but none were supplied. When the matter came on for hearing on 13 December 1991 before Dr Gerber, the solicitor for the appellant applied for an adjournment on the ground that he had mistaken the date of the hearing and was unprepared. He said he had attempted unsuccessfully to arrange for counsel to represent his client. The Deputy President refused the application and in the absence of any evidence that the Commissioner had acted on erroneous information in making the assessments he upheld the decisions disallowing the objection in respect of each assessment.

5. The appellant then made an application to this Court by way of appeal under s. 44 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act (Cth) 1975 from the Deputy President's decision. As Dr Gerber had made three separate decisions in respect of the three objection decisions before him, the primary judge gave leave to file additional applications under s. 44. It was conceded by both parties before the primary judge that the case turned on one issue only, namely whether the letter of 11 August 1987 was a valid objection. If it was not, then the Tribunal had no jurisdiction to review any decision with respect to it and the appellant's application to the Tribunal for review would necessarily fail. If the letter was a valid objection then, counsel for the Commissioner conceded, the Tribunal had erred in law in refusing to grant the adjournment.

6. The primary judge concluded that the letter of 11 August 1987 was not a valid objection and dismissed the applications. These appeals are against the order dismissing the applications. The appellant had also sought judicial review under the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act (Cth) 1977 of the Deputy President's decision to refuse to grant an adjournment. The judge dismissed that application and there has been no appeal from that decision.

7. On the hearing of these appeals it was again conceded by Mr Davies of counsel for the Commissioner that if the letter was a valid objection the Tribunal erred in law in refusing to grant the adjournment.

8. Section 185(1) of the Act provides:

"A taxpayer dissatisfied with any assessment under this Act may,

within 60 days after the service of the notice of assessment,

lodge with the Commissioner an objection in writing against the

assessment stating fully and in detail the grounds on which he

relies."

9. The learned primary judge reviewed the authorities and concluded that although non-technical language may be employed, and although the Court should not give a narrow or rigid construction to the grounds stated and in the event of doubt should give the taxpayer the benefit of that doubt, no amount of generosity of construction enabled him to conclude that the letter of 11 August 1987 in any way set out fully and in detail the appellant's grounds of objection. His Honour considered that the letter could be construed as involving no more than the general complaint that the asset betterment statement was wrong. Where it was wrong and the extent to which it was wrong remained, in his Honour's view, undefined.

10. We turn first to the construction of the letter. The starting point is that the letter was, as appears from its terms, intended to be an objection. It stated "We ... hereby wish to object to the assessment in full" (emphasis added) and it concluded with a request that the assessments be set aside.

11. The reference in the second sentence of the letter to tax returns lodged on behalf of the appellant should be read having regard to the stated object of the letter, which was to object to the assessments for the years ending 30 June 1985, 1986 and 1987. It should also be read having regard to the nature and circumstances of the assessments to which it purported to be an objection; they were default assessments based, as both parties obviously then knew, upon an assets betterment statement prepared by the Commissioner's officers.

12. It is clear that the returns for the years 1985, 1986 and 1987 were readily available to the Commissioner when considering the objection and they were before the Administrative Appeals Tribunal and before the primary judge, who referred to them. They showed a modest income from various sources: a pension paid by the Department of Social Security, a small income from tuition, and interest. The appellant also claimed a "pensioner rebate". The returns carried the usual taxpayer's declaration that the particulars in the return and in the accompanying documents were true in every detail and disclosed a full and complete statement of the total income derived by the taxpayer in and out of Australia in the year of income.

13. The income disclosed by the returns was very much less than the amounts assessed by the Commissioner under s. 167 of the Act and, as the primary judge pointed out, if the returns be accepted as disclosing the applicant's income from all sources, no tax or only nominal tax would have been payable.

14. We see no difficulty in this case in reference being made to the appellant's taxation returns to determine the scope of the purported objection. They were specifically identified, and they related to the very issue of the appellant's income for the years in question, for they set out what the appellant declared her income from all sources to have been. In Poole v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1970] HCA 25; (1970) 122 CLR 427 at 441, Walsh J, in deciding that an argument sought to be raised by the taxpayer was within the grounds of objection, took into account the fact that information which bore upon the scope of the objection had been supplied to the Commissioner by attachment to the taxpayer's return. In that case there was no express reference in the objection to the document containing the information, as there was here in the letter of 11 August 1987. The Court was referred on this question to the judgment of Brooking J in Executor of Estate of Weinberg v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1983] HCA 39; (1983) 83 ATC 4,747, but that case concerned very different circumstances. There, it was sought to rely on a letter sent by the Commissioner some 20 years before the objection was lodged to determine the scope of the objection.

15. In these circumstances we consider that what the tax agent's letter would have conveyed to the Commissioner was more than the mere generality that the assessments were excessive. The letter was expressed to be an objection to the assessments "in full" and the reference in the second sentence to the appellant's tax returns for the years in question and to their indicating that there was no tax payable at all, would have conveyed to the Commissioner the taxpayer's claim that her income from all sources for the years in question was as set out in her returns, and that she had received no other income. Her case was that the returns truly disclosed, as the law required and as the appellant declared, all her income, and that the particulars in the returns were true in every detail. By necessary inference the letter conveyed the claim that at least the conclusions drawn from the assets betterment statement were wrong, and the letter emphasised that this was the nature of the case being put by contending that the assessments were raised from a betterment statement "produced ... on completely erroneous information".

16. This was in substance the view in fact taken by the Commissioner when he considered the objection. In the statement of findings on material questions of fact, evidence and reasons for decision, prepared by the Commissioner for the application to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review of the decision to disallow the objection, the dispute is identified as follows:

"- Whether assessments which issued on the 10th July 1987 (which

show taxable incomes based on calculations using an asset

betterment statement) are correct.

- Whether subsequent returns lodged by the taxpayer on the 5th

August 1987 are correct."

17. We would therefore conclude that the letter should not be construed as involving no more than a general complaint that the asset betterment statement was wrong. As we have indicated, we would interpret the letter as conveying considerably more than this.

18. The question that must now be asked is whether the letter, as so interpreted, contained any ground at all and, if it did, whether it stated any such ground fully and in detail as s. 185 requires.

19. In our opinion the letter did contain an intelligible ground of objection. The ground was not merely that the assessments were excessive, but that each of them was wholly excessive in that it was contended that the only income the taxpayer derived from any source in the relevant years of income was the income disclosed in her tax returns for those years, which income was of such an amount that no tax was payable at all.

20. Although such a ground inferentially denies the correctness either of the betterment statement upon which the assessments were based or of the conclusions drawn from the betterment statement, it offers no detail at all as to how the statement is said to be incorrect. The assertion in the letter that the betterment statement was produced "on completely erroneous information" conveys no idea of the particular respects in which it is contended that the statement was incorrect. There are many ways in which a betterment statement may be incorrect: the methodology may be inappropriate, particular facts may be wrong, values attributed to assets may be incorrect or there may be the omission of relevant facts of which the Commissioner was unaware or mistaken. The letter does not come to grips with any of the possibilities; the best it does, apart from saying that the statement is based on "completely erroneous information", is to say that "absolute proof as to the above facts" is being got together.

21. But s. 185 does not require a taxpayer to state grounds of objection to a betterment statement. What the section requires is a statement of grounds of objection to the assessment. And to succeed in demonstrating that the assessments were excessive the taxpayer did not, as a matter of logic, have to show that the betterment statement was wrong in any particular respect. It was, in our view, open to her to take the position that all her income was set out in the tax returns to which the letter referred and that, whatever might appear from the betterment statements, that was the only income she derived in the relevant years. As a matter of forensic reality the taxpayer might be thought unlikely to get very far by adopting such an approach if the betterment statement was at all accurate, but it was an approach that was open to her.

22. In Ma v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1992) 92 ATC 4,373, Burchett J considered a case in which assessments had been based on asset betterment statements that were founded on an analysis of the taxpayer's bank accounts. It was said that those accounts indicated that more deposits had been made than could be accounted for by the taxpayer's known income and capital receipts. The taxpayer claimed, however, that the betterment statement was the product of double-counting because it ignored that fact that he would withdraw money from the accounts for betting activities and would later re-deposit the total after addition or subtraction of the net of his gains or losses. The Administrative Appeals Tribunal took the view that it was not necessary to consider the taxpayer's credit as a witness. In relation to the relevance of the taxpayer's credit as a witness Burchett J said (at 4,377):

"Whether, ultimately, Mr. Ma should be believed is not, of course,

a matter for me. Nor if it were, could it be decided upon the

written record alone. The decision must take account of the onus

under s.190(b). But if the taxpayer denies any undisclosed source

of income, provides acceptable evidence of how he spends his time,

and demonstrates a reasonable explanation for any appearance of

the possession of assets, he will generally discharge his burden

of proof unless some positive reason is shown why he is to be

disbelieved. Any other view would introduce a degree of

arbitrariness into liability for tax."

23. Federal Commission of Taxation v. Dalco [1990] HCA 3; (1990) 168 CLR 614, whilst showing that the demonstration of an error by the Commissioner in the process of assessment does not prove that an assessment is excessive, does not restrict a taxpayer in the ways in which he or she may discharge the burden of showing that an assessment is in fact excessive: see per Burchett J in F C of T v. Ma at 4,378-4,379.

24. In Dalco, Toohey J said (at 631):

"I agree with Wilcox J in the Federal Court that "the task for

the taxpayer, upon an appeal or a review under Pt V of the Act, is

to show that the amount of money for which tax is levied by a

particular notice of assessment exceeds the actual substantive

liability of the taxpayer". As his Honour points out, a taxpayer

will generally discharge that onus by satisfying the court or

tribunal that his or her true taxable income is less than that

appearing in the assessment."

25. The question in this case is not whether the taxpayer had a ground of objection that might succeed but whether she had any valid ground of objection at all, that is, any intelligible ground of objection that was stated fully and in detail. For the reasons we have given, we consider that the letter sent by the tax agent on behalf of the taxpayer did state a ground of objection. The question then becomes whether it can be said that the ground of objection was stated fully and in detail as that requirement has been interpreted in the cases to which the primary judge referred: H.R. Lancey Shipping Co. Pty Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1951) 9 ATD 267 at 273 per Williams J; A.L. Campbell and Co. Pty Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1951] HCA 36; (1951) 82 CLR 452 at 461 per Dixon J; Archer Bros. Pty Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1953] HCA 23; (1953) 90 CLR 140 at 149; Poole v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1970] HCA 25; (1970) 122 CLR 427 at 441 per Walsh J; Estate of Weinberg v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1983] HCA 39; (1983) 83 ATC 4,747 at 4,749 per Brooking J, and the cases there cited.

26. The requirement that the grounds be stated fully and in detail has not been taken to mean that the grounds have to be lengthy or complicated. In Lighthouse Philatelics Pty Ltd v. Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 32 FCR 148, the Full Court of this Court, in outlining the history of the amendment to s. 190(a) of the Act to allow the Tribunal or court to permit a taxpayer to rely upon grounds not stated in his objection said (at 151-152):

"...objections which were supposed to be able to be written in

layman's language with a minimum of formality, provided they

conveyed to the Commissioner the substance of what the taxpayer

desired to argue, often became a proliferation of technical

pleadings, seemingly commencing at s. 1 of the Act and proceeding

to at least s. 260, contained in hundreds of pages and signifying

nothing to the Commissioner of the real issue in dispute."

27. Grotesque objections of this nature were unnecessary. In H.R. Lancey Shipping Co. Pty Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation, the grounds that were considered sufficient by Williams J were as follows:

"(5) That the amount of compensation received by the company from

the Department of the Navy which has been included, is not

assessable income of the taxpayer.

(6) That such compensation is of a capital nature and is not

made assessable by any of the provisions of the Income Tax

Assessment Act."

28. In the circumstances of that case they were regarded as "sufficiently explicit to direct the attention of the (Commissioner) to the particular respects in which the taxpayer contends the assessment is erroneous and his reasons for that contention" (see at 268 and 273). See also, Poole v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation at 441.

29. If the letter is read in the way we have discussed and it is accepted that the tax returns to which it refers are to be read as incorporated in the objection by reference, the ground is in our view stated "fully and in detail" as that expression has come to be understood in accordance with the authorities.

30. It tells the Commissioner the extent to which the taxpayer contends the assessments are excessive, why she says they are excessive and precisely what the taxpayer says her true income from all sources was for the years in question. By incorporating the tax returns, the objection also tells the Commissioner what the taxpayer says are the sources of what she contends was her only income for the years in question.

31. What the letter does not do is to give the Commissioner any useful information at all, let alone any detail, about the betterment statement that provided the basis for the assessment, but in our view the failure to do so does not mean that there is no valid ground of objection. As we would interpret the letter, the ground the taxpayer puts forward must be taken to ignore the betterment statement and to put her whole case on the simple basis that her income in each year was of a particular amount and that the assessment is excessive because it assesses tax on the erroneous basis that she derived income of greater amounts in relevant years. That is a case she is entitled to seek to make out. The fact that it might be thought to be a hopeless case unless the taxpayer positively and directly challenges the betterment statement, or parts of it, does not mean that the ground she has chosen is not a ground that the Act recognises or that it is not stated fully or in detail.

32. Without an amendment to her ground (which, however, could be allowed under s. 190(a): see Lighthouse Philatelics Pty Ltd v. Commissioner of Taxation) the taxpayer would be severely limited in the way in which she could present her case before the Tribunal. She would be restricted to proving what her income actually was for the years in question and could not, as part of her own case otherwise introduce evidence that would attack the betterment statement.

33. But a betterment statement is only a species of circumstantial evidence, and one against whom a case is mounted upon circumstantial evidence is never compelled to dispute the circumstances; he or she is entitled to go straight to the inference, and disprove it by disproof of the ultimate fact sought to be inferred. After all, human experience is not necessarily contradicted by the debater who says to an opponent: I cannot find the answer to your argument, but I will prove by quite separate considerations that your conclusion is wrong, so your argument must be. Galileo, required to acknowledge an infallible proposition, is reputed to have muttered: "Nevertheless, it does move". Cf., L'Estrange v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1978) 78 ATC 4,744 at 4,772 per McGarvie J.

34. Once the appellant's entitlement to dispute the assessments (not the betterment statement) in this way, by showing what was her income, is acknowledged, the difficulty about whether the ground of the objection is stated fully and in detail vanishes. For the returns incorporated by reference in the objection are full and detailed by any standard. Certainly it complies with the formulation by Kitto J in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Western Suburbs Cinemas Ltd [1952] HCA 28; (1952) 86 CLR 102 at 106; from it the Commissioner could be expected to gather the appellant's contention, to which he was asked to apply his mind, that her income was stated fully in the returns, so that the additional amounts of income upon which she had been assessed had not been received by her, or represented capital. Accepting that where an assessment is based on a return, though rejecting some contentions in it, a mere reiteration that the return was correct may well be insufficient, this is not such a case; these returns were lodged after the assessments, are inconsistent with them, and provide the very sort of details which might have been set out in the objection itself.

35. In these circumstances we would allow the appeals with costs. The order of the primary judge dismissing the applications by way of appeal from the Administrative Appeals Tribunal should be set aside and instead there should be an order allowing those appeals and remitting the matter to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for further consideration and determination according to law.

GRAY J The circumstances in which these appeals come to be determined by this Full Court are unusual and unfortunate. They began with the failure of the taxpayer to lodge returns of income for taxation purposes in respect of the years ended 30th June 1985, 30th June 1986 and 30th June 1987 by the due dates. Pursuant to s.167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"), the Commissioner made an assessment of the taxpayer's income for each of those years by the assets betterment method. In consequence, the Commissioner assessed the taxpayer as liable to pay income tax in respect of each of those years. The notices of assessment were issued on 10th July 1987.

2. By letter dated 11th August 1987, the taxpayer's accountants and tax agents wrote to the Commissioner in the following terms:-

"We are in possession of your assessment No. 385012/3 issued

on the 10th July, 1987 and hereby wish to object to your

assessment in full.

On the 5th August, 1987 we lodged income tax returns for the

above and in each case it indicated that there was no tax

payable at all. The assessments were raised from a

betterment statement produced by one of your Officer (sic.)

on completely erroneous information. We are at present

getting together absolute proof as to the above facts.

In the meantime we request that the assessment be set aside

and no penalties charged due to the above events."

The issue to which these appeals are confined, in the manner which I shall describe, is whether that letter amounted to an "objection", within the meaning of s.185(1) of the Act.

3. Although the Commissioner now contends that the letter did not amount to an objection, he treated it as if it were one. On 24th February 1989 he notified the taxpayer of a "decision on objection", informing her that her objection dated 11th August 1987 had been considered and disallowed.

4. The taxpayer then elected to have this decision referred to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. The proceeding in the Tribunal was instituted on 28th August 1991 and was set down for hearing on 13th December 1991. On that day, immediately after announcing his appearance, the solicitor for the taxpayer requested an adjournment of the hearing, on the ground that he had mistakenly believed that the matter was to be listed for the following Monday, 16th December 1991. Counsel for the Commissioner opposed any application for an adjournment.

5. The learned Deputy President who constituted the Tribunal refused an adjournment. In his reasons for decision, after reciting a more detailed history of the matter than is necessary for present purposes, the learned Deputy President posed for himself the question whether the application for review could be dealt with "without amendment to the objection; if not, what is the likelihood of an application to amend the objection succeeding?". The learned Deputy President referred to some authorities and applied them, to reach a conclusion to the effect that the letter dated 11th August 1987 did not satisfy the requirements of the Act. He then said, "in other words, before this taxpayer can get her foot in the door of the Tribunal, her objection clearly requires amendment". He stated that the Tribunal would be unlikely to exercise its discretion in favour of the taxpayer, because her advisers had failed to provide information, or to heed warnings that failure to do so could seriously compromise the taxpayer's claim, had disregarded "common professional courtesies" and had failed to note the correct date of the hearing.

6. The learned Deputy President then refused an adjournment. He went on to hold that, "in the absence of any evidence, the only "ground" pleaded - that the Commissioner acted on erroneous information in arriving at the assessments has not been made out." He upheld the decision of the Commissioner on the objection.

7. The taxpayer then instituted two proceedings in the Federal Court of Australia. The first was an application under the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977, seeking to review the decision of the learned Deputy President refusing to grant an adjournment of the applications to review made to the Tribunal. The second was an appeal to the Federal Court of Australia under s.44 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. Such an appeal lies on a question of law only. Although a single appeal was lodged, Hill J gave leave to file two more appeals out of time, taking the view that each assessment involved a separate decision.

8. Those proceedings were heard and determined by Hill J, who delivered judgment on 1st July 1992, dismissing them. His Honour held that the Court had no jurisdiction to deal with the application under the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977. He determined the appeals on the basis that both parties conceded that only one issue needed to be determined, namely "whether the letter of 11th August 1987 was a valid objection". As his Honour said:-

"If it was not, then the Tribunal had no jurisdiction to

review any decision with respect to it and the applicant's

application to the Tribunal for review necessarily would

fail. If, on the other hand, it was a valid objection, then

counsel for the Commissioner conceded that the Tribunal

erred in law in refusing to grant the adjournment."

9. His Honour held that:-

"While non technical language may be employed, and while the

court should not give a narrow or rigid construction to the

grounds employed and in the event of doubt should give the

taxpayer the benefit of that doubt, no amount of generosity

of construction enables me to conclude that the applicant's

letter in any way set out fully and in detail her grounds of

objection. The letter can be construed as involving no more

than the general complaint that the asset betterment

statement is wrong. Where it was wrong and the extent to

which it was wrong remain undefined. Such a general

statement in my opinion does not satisfy the language of

s185."

10. Section 185(1) of the Act provides:

"A taxpayer dissatisfied with any assessment under this Act

may, within 60 days after service of the notice of

assessment, lodge with the Commissioner an objection in

writing against the assessment stating fully and in detail

the grounds on which he relies."

11. Over the years, the courts have tended to substitute for the words "fully and in detail" formulations which appear less stringent than the words of the statute. These attempts go back at least to R. v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation for Western Australia; Ex parte Copley (1923) 30 ALR 86, at p 87, where Knox CJ said:-

"I think it is effective notice of objection under the Act

if the written communication is expressed in words that are

reasonably calculated to convey to the understanding of the

person to whom it is addressed (1) that the taxpayer

contends that the assessment is not in accordance with law;

and (2) the grounds on which that contention is based."

12. In the same case, at p 88, Starke J pointed out that an objection need not be in formal language, or in language that lawyers would adopt. In A.L. Campbell and Co. Pty. Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1951] HCA 36; (1951) 82 CLR 452, at p 461, Dixon J (as he then was) said that grounds of objection should not be interpreted technically, narrowly or with rigidity. In H.R. Lancey Shipping Co. Pty. Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1951) 9 ATD 267, at p 273, Williams J said:-

"The grounds of objection need not be stated in legal form,

they can be expressed in ordinary language, but they should

be sufficiently explicit to direct the attention of the

respondent to the particular respects in which the taxpayer

contends that the assessment is erroneous and his reasons

for this contention. In each case the sufficiency of the

grounds is a matter for the Court. Vague grounds such as

that the assessment is excessive are not, in my opinion, a

compliance with the Act."

13. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Western Suburbs Cinemas Ltd. [1952] HCA 28; (1952) 86 CLR 102, at p 106, Kitto J applied the test of asking whether the Commissioner, when considering whether an objection should be allowed, could reasonably be expected to gather from the written objection that he was being asked to apply his mind to a particular contention. In Archer Bros. Pty. Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1953] HCA 23; (1953) 90 CLR 140, at p 149, the Full High Court referred to several of these authorities and declined to allow a particular contention to be raised in reliance on a ground which was said to be expressed too vaguely. In Poole v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1970] HCA 25; (1970) 122 CLR 427, at pp 441-442, Walsh J pointed out that the sufficiency of what is stated must be determined according to the circumstances of each case.

14. In Executor of Estate of Weinberg v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1983) 83 ATC 4747, at p 4750, Brooking J set out various formulations derived from the authorities as to how an objection might comply with the requirement that its grounds be stated fully and in detail:-

"So it is said that the objection must fairly cover the

point; that it must convey to the Commissioner that the

objector would rely upon the point; that it must be

sufficiently explicit to direct the Commissioner's attention

to the particular respects in which the taxpayer contends

that the assessment is erroneous and his reasons for this

contention; that the grounds must be intended to cover the

point and that they must convey it to the Commissioner; and

that the objection should be such that the Commissioner

could reasonably be expected to gather from it that he was

being asked to apply his mind to the contention."

15. Like Hill J, I am of the view that, taking into account the extreme laxity of these authorities, no amount of generosity of construction permits the conclusion that the taxpayer's letter of 11th August 1987 in any way set out fully and in detail her grounds of objection. Far from fairly covering a point, or conveying to the Commissioner that the taxpayer would rely upon a point, it does not state any point at all. It is not at all explicit to direct the Commissioner's attention to the particular respects in which the taxpayer contends that the assessments were erroneous, let alone any reasons for that contention. No grounds are specified at all. The Commissioner could not reasonably be expected to gather from the letter that he was being asked to apply his mind to any particular contention, except perhaps that the taxpayer desired to object to the assessments in their entirety. Such a statement is too vague to comply with any view which has been taken of the meaning of s. 185(1).

16. The suggestion was made in argument that the requirements of s.185(1) might now have to be construed with even more generosity to the taxpayer, as a result of an amendment to s.190(a). That provision requires that, in proceedings on a review before the Tribunal or on appeal to a court, the taxpayer shall, unless the Tribunal or court otherwise orders, be limited to the grounds stated in the objection. The words, "unless the Tribunal or court otherwise orders" were inserted by s.30 of Act No. 112 of 1986 and came into operation on 4th November 1986. Previously, the rule was strict; the taxpayer was limited to the grounds stated in the objection, which could not be amended. The grounds can now be amended, even to the extent of substitution of new grounds, not contemplated at all in the original objection. See Lighthouse Philatelics Pty. Ltd. v Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 32 FCR 148.

17. In my view, this argument cannot be accepted. S.185(1) still requires that an objection state grounds "fully and in detail". No change has been made to that wording. The possibility of the addition or substitution of new grounds does not absolve the taxpayer from stating in a notice of objection some ground complying with s.185(1). In the present case, there is no such ground stated in what has been treated, for the purposes of the judgment appealed from, as the only document which might constitute a notice of objection. In my view, the learned Deputy President fell into error by supposing that he would have had the power to permit the letter dated 11th August 1987 to be amended. If that letter did not constitute a notice of objection, because it did not comply with s.185(1), it was a nullity. There was no power to allow it to be amended by the insertion of grounds complying with s.185(1), when no such grounds are to be found in the letter.

18. It was also suggested in argument that the letter of 11th August 1987 should not be considered in isolation. That letter refers to returns alleged to have been lodged by the taxpayer. It was suggested that those returns should be incorporated by reference into the letter and that, if this were done, the grounds of objection would be disclosed "fully and in detail". There was even a suggestion that another letter had been forwarded to the Commissioner by or on behalf of the taxpayer within the sixty day time limit laid down by s.185(1). The factual basis for these arguments has never been laid. This Court can deal only with a question of law. In the present case, it can deal only with the narrow question which was agreed by the parties to be the only question on which Hill J was required to pronounce. That question did not involve any issue as to whether any document other than the letter of 11th August 1987 was capable of constituting, or forming part of, a valid objection. It may be that there remains an undischarged statutory duty on the Commissioner to deal with an objection constituted wholly or partly by documents other than the one which has been the focus of this proceeding. This Court cannot deal with such a supposition.

19. For the reasons which I have given, I am of the view that Hill J reached the correct conclusion, namely that the letter dated 11th August 1987 did not constitute a valid objection. It was not capable of founding any jurisdiction of the Commissioner to give the decision he purported to give, or any jurisdiction of the Tribunal to review that purported decision. The appeal must be dismissed and the taxpayer must pay the Commissioner's costs of the appeal.
