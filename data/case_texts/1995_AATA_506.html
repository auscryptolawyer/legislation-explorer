Re: GREGORY ANDERSON- CLIFT



Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

REASONS FOR DECISION

Tribunal: Dr D J Grimes, Senior Member

Mr N J Attwood, Member

Mr H G Julian, Member

  1. This is an application for review of a decision of the delegate of the respondent dated 2 November 1993 made under sub-section 157(1) of the Superannuation Act 1976 (“the Act”) which affirmed a determination of 19 September 1990 that an election made by the applicant on or about 9 August 1990 under sub-section 137(1) should not be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed by that sub-section of the Act.
  2. At the hearing before the Administrative Appeals Tribunal the applicant represented himself. The respondent was represented by Mr S Adams. The Tribunal had before it documents lodged pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1976 and oral evidence was given by the applicant.
  3. The benefits the applicant is seeking are “deferred benefits” provided for by s.136 of the Act but often referred to as preservation of superannuation rights. Sub-section 137(1) of the Act provided for the time in which to elect for the preservation of superannuation rights at the time the applicant elected to take a refund of his accumulated contributions as follows:

“s137(1) Where a person ceases to be an eligible employee and is not entitled to pension under this Act, he may, not later than 21 days after he ceases to be an eligible employee, elect, by notice in writing to the Commissioner, that this Division apply in relation to him.

  1. Section 157(1) of the Act provides a discretionary power for the Commissioner of Superannuation to recognise late elections as follows:

"s157(1) Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an election under this Act is made by a person after the expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for the making of the election, and the Commissioner is satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised, the Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if it had been made within the period allowed and the election shall have effect accordingly."

  1. The applicant was born on 25 May 1956 and is married with three children. He graduated with a Bachelor of Education from the Canberra College of Advanced Education in 1977 and became a teacher with the ACT Schools Authority. He became a member of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme (CSS) on 30 May 1980. He ceased to be a contributor on 27 January 1986 when he resigned from the ACT Schools Authority.
  2. The applicant is a mathematics teacher but has a particular interest in music and in 1985 sought to investigate career possibilities in music. The applicant applied for and had approved leave without pay from his employer for the period 29 January 1985 to 27 January 1986 during which period he moved to Sydney to further his studies. After one years’ leave without pay the applicant sought to extend this period for another year during which time he was to work part-time with the NSW Department of Education. Leave without pay was denied so the applicant resigned from the ACT Schools Authority on 27 January 1986.
  3. In his letter of resignation dated 18 December 1985 (Document T28/106) the applicant said that “...my business and family interests compel me to remain in Sydney...” and “...(I) hope to return in the not too distant future.” In the same letter he stated “I have already been given a statement of service and the only matter left unfinished is my superannuation. I don’t know what the normal procedure is, but I would like the amount forwarded to me as soon as possible.”
  4. In evidence the applicant said he filled out form S2A which had been posted to him in Sydney. This form contained three options namely:

“ (i) apply for a refund of accumulated contributions

(ii) elect for a payment of a lump sum benefit

(iii) elect for the preservation of superannuation rights” (See Document T9/74)

  1. The applicant chose the first option, scoring out the other two. He said that he believed that this was the only option applicable to him and claimed this was confirmed in a phone call to an officer in the personnel section of his employer in Canberra. He noted the option of preservation of benefits but thought this was not meant for someone in his situation. He thought it implied that he would have to keep paying superannuation contributions and financially he was not in a position to do this. He had hoped to transfer his superannuation to the NSW Department of Education where he was teaching part-time but this was not possible.
  2. The applicant received his contribution and interest totalling approximately $6500. His evidence was that he put $5000 in a unit trust in his wife’s name and used the remaining $1500 to purchase furniture. He is uncertain how long the money stayed in the unit trust but remembers that after his family’s return to Canberra in 1987 the money was used for maintenance on the family home. In 1987 he returned to the ACT and rejoined the teaching service again becoming an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act on 1 May 1987.
  3. The applicant gave evidence that there was an induction day when he re-joined the service but that he can remember no details of how he rejoined the superannuation scheme. He did not discuss superannuation with his contemporaries. He conceded he would have received information documents about superannuation on rejoining the service but that he did not read them. In 1990 he became aware through the union of the existence of preservation of benefits and the possibility of late election and as a result made an application to the Commissioner for Superannuation, the rejection of which is the subject of this appeal.
  4. The applicant’s case is that in moving to Sydney his intention was to improve his training but there was also the possibility that a career as a song-writer/musician may have resulted. Having a wife and one child at the time, he said that security was important to him and for this reason he took leave without pay and sought an extension of leave without pay so that he could later return to the ACT teaching service.
  5. He believed he would certainly have taken the option of preservation of his superannuation if he had been aware of the possibility of this choice. He claimed he had no need for the money, as he was staying with relatives in Sydney and not paying rent, but the Tribunal notes that one of the reasons he gave in evidence for rejection of the preservation option was that he thought it involved continuing contributions which he could not afford.
  6. The applicant claimed that he was confused at the time he filled out the S2A form. In his letter to the Commissioner for Superannuation he referred to his “ignorance” about the options available to him and in evidence to the Tribunal he stated that “I didn’t feel I had a role to play in it”. However the applicant conceded that he had most probably not read the information statements sent to him when he rejoined the superannuation scheme and he “had a history of not reading them.”
  7. The applicant actively sought out and obtained information to enable him to successfully claim recognition for his previous years of service. This enabled him to retain entitlements to long service leave and incremental salary increases. He made no such efforts to obtain similar information in relation to his superannuation until 1990.
  8. It is the respondent’s case that the applicant in these proceedings does not satisfy the basic test of desirability. The applicant has not given an acceptable explanation for his delay. He accepted that he received “Information Statements” which expressly mention preservation. The respondent also relied on the applicant’s use of his refund, arguing that even though the circumstances show that there was no desperate need for the money it may have been useful.
  9. The relevant principles to be used as a guide by the Tribunal were summarised in Re Ward and Commissioner for Superannuation (Unreported Tribunal Decision No 9987) as follows:
“17. In recent times, the application of sub-section 157(1) has been considered by this Tribunal frequently. There have been 2 decisions in which a presidential member has presided, namely Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation [1991] AATA 191; (1991) 25 ALD 307 and Re Lander and Commissioner for Superannuation [1993] AATA 65; (1993) 29 ALD 870. The former concentrated upon a discussion of the meaning of “desirable” in sub-section 157(1). The latter concentrated upon the application in cases of this nature of principles gathered together and expressed by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Development Pty Limited v Minister for Home Affairs and Environment (1984-1985) 58 ALR 305. Broadly, both of these decisions have been followed or departed from depending upon the particular facts of the case in the many other decisions which this Tribunal has made. Neither decision has been disapproved as such in later Tribunal or Federal Court decisions. The emphasis in both cases however has been modified by observations made in 2 judgments of the Federal Court. To the extent that they have not been disapproved, we consider that they are still of assistance and ought to be followed wherever possible.

18. A Full Court judgment given by Black CJ, Davies and Cooper JJ in Chalk v Commissioner for Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 19 AAR 450 was followed by a later judgment of Von Doussa J in Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman [1994] FCA 1159; (1994) 123 ALR 239. From a consideration of extracts from both judgments, assistance can be derived in relation to various aspects which recur in each of the cases coming before the Tribunal. We would summarise them as follows —

A General considerations setting the context within which the discretion is to be exercised

(1) The discretion given by s 157 should be exercised by reference to the words of the statute and the contents within which the discretion is conferred. Section 157 uses simple words and the issues which arise under it are invariably issues of fact, the ultimate issue being whether it is desirable that a late election should be recognised (Chalk at 455).

(2) On the one hand an election recognised under s 137(1) will extend to a person who has periods of broken service, benefits which fully reflect the total period of service as an eligible employee. That would be in accordance with the general policy of the Act. On the other hand the orderly administration of the superannuation scheme requires that time limits be applied, generally speaking, so that decisions which impinge on investment and accounting procedures are made as soon as possible after an event such as resignation occurs. Matters of this kind set the context within which the discretion under s 157(1) is conferred and the context in which the discretion must be exercised (Boardman at 246 and 247).

(3) In s 157(1) “desirable” is not referable to a state of mind - whether of the elector or of the Commissioner. Whether a late election is “desirable” is to be judged objectively having regard to all the circumstances of the case...whether the recognition of a late election is “something worthy of achievement” must depend on what is fair in order to do justice between the person who seeks to make the late election and the requirements of the administration of the fund (Boardman at 247).

B Observations on whether extension of time principles apply —

(1) Wilcox J referred to the general principles on which time for the performance of an act may be extended but His Honour did not limit the factors which might arise from the context in which a particular statutory discretion to extend time arises (Chalk at 455).

(2) It is understandable, indeed inevitable, that a decision-maker considering an application under s 157(1) will have close regard to the assistance which decisions such as Hunter Valley Developments and A’hern offer (Boardman at 247 and 248).

(3) There is a public interest in enforcing procedural time limits which extends beyond the immediate parties (Boardman at 248).

(4) The time limit specified in the statute gives a prima facie rule as to when the application should have been made. Before an exercise of discretion in the applicant’s favour can occur, it must be made to appear that it is fair and just in all the circumstances to extend time. One of the relevant considerations will be whether there was a reasonable explanation for the delay, although the absence of a reasonable explanation is not necessarily fatal : Comcare v A’hern at 88. There is therefore an evidentiary burden on the applicant to place information before the decision-maker sufficient to satisfy the decision-maker that in all the circumstances of the case, it is desirable that the late election should be recognised (Boardman at 249).

(5) The time limit has its purpose, but the Act recognises that there will be deserving cases where fairness to the applicant requires that the time limit be relaxed. In such cases it is the policy of the Act, and the legal right of the applicant, to a favourable exercise of discretion under s 157(1). If the proper application of s 157(1) to individual cases, results in many people obtaining recognition of late election, so be it, that is the law (Boardman at 252).

C Application of extension of time principles may not always give the correct answer —

(i) Most provisions which authorise an extension of time are instances of beneficial legislation which, accordingly should be applied beneficially (Chalk at 455).

(2) In the exercise of the discretion under s 157 it may be more important to consider the consequences of extending or refusing to extend time, than to debate the reasons why the act was not done in time (Chalk at 456).

(3) Extension of a procedural time limit gives a party an opportunity of establishing rights but to recognise a belated election immediately confers rights. However in each case the question is whether it is fair and just that the party who is out of time should by the exercise of the discretion be extended the rights which would have been enjoyed had the act in question been done within time (Boardman at 248).

D The existence or non existence of fault on the part of the respondent or its agent will have little bearing on desirability —

(1) The operation of s 157 is not dependent upon the existence of fault on the part of officers of the employer or of the scheme, although matters such as lack of advice may well be relevant (Chalk at 455).

(2) Whether or not there is a fiduciary duty by the Commissioner, is not relevant. The conduct of the Commissioner is only relevant to the exercise of the discretion to treat the election as within time under s 157 if it helps to explain why the appellant did or did not make a timely decision in a particular way if the conduct of the appellant is itself a relevant consideration (per Cooper J in Chalk at 458).

(3) Section 157 does not require that the Commissioner determine whether there exists attributable fault on the part of the appellant or any other person giving rise to the need to extend time; nor that the discretion be exercised on the basis of the existence or absence of attributable fault...the section merely requires that the Commissioner “be satisfied that in all the circumstances it is desirable that the election should be recognised” (per Cooper J in Chalk at 458).

(4) It is unnecessary to decide whether the Commissioner was under a legal or equitable duty to provide to contributors to the scheme information as to choices available to them under it. The relevant fact may be that the Commissioner did not provide the information (per Cooper J in Chalk at 459).

E What obligation is there on an employee to make enquiries?

(1) In the application of s 157 it may be of little consequence that the employee did not enquire as to his or her right to elect under s 137 or even that the employee positively decided not to elect. Section 157 confers a discretion which operates in either case (Chalk at 456).

(2) Wherever it is necessary to have regard to factual circumstances as they existed at a date in the past, delay in making the application which initiates that consideration is likely to add to the difficulties of ascertaining those circumstances. This difficulty is likely to be a consideration in almost every application to extend time no matter what the context (Boardman at 249).

(3) Depending on the circumstances, the decision-maker might proceed on the footing that there is no reasonable explanation for delay beyond the point when lines of enquiry so suggested would have revealed to the applicant the right to apply for an extension. But where the evidence establishes that the suggested lines of enquiry would have produced no useful information, so as to leave the applicant in the same state of ignorance or misinformation, the absence of enquiry becomes irrelevant (Boardman at 251).

F Does delay of the applicant create prejudice to the respondent?

(1) Frequently the discretion is exercised favourably because the facts which the employee took into account when he or she decided not to elect, subsequently turned out differently. Often times the discretion is favourably exercised when the employee has encountered health problems of which he or she was unaware at the time of retirement. One of the objects of s 157 is to alleviate the consequences of such a change in circumstances (Chalk at 456).

(2) It is difficult to see prejudice to the fund in a late election. A favourable exercise of the discretion would extend to the respondent the benefit of additional years of service, but this liability would have arisen in any event had the election been exercised in time. To grant the respondent the benefit of years of service actually performed is consistent with the policy and objects of the Act (Boardman at 252).

(3) In saying that the issue before the Tribunal involved a “determination as to what is fair in the circumstances of this case, when the need of the applicant for the extension of time to be granted is weighed against the prejudice to the respondent if that extension is granted” the Tribunal did not apply a wrong test (Boardman at 247).

G Should the decision-maker have regard to the totality of service?

(1) A decision-maker exercising the s 157 discretion could think it fair to the employer, to the employee, and to the scheme that the benefit should be calculated by reference to the totality of the employee’s service and to the totality of his contributions, including any sums which must be refunded (Chalk at 456).

H What significance should attach to the form of investment of the lump sum?

(1) Where the accumulated contributions were immediately invested in a business enterprise such a use of the money would in itself often indicate that a deliberate decision had been made to reject the option of preservation in favour of receiving the immediate benefit of the accumulated contributions (Boardman at 250).

19. By and large the Federal Court has taken a broad brush approach to the exercise of the s 157(1) discretion. Ultimately the justice of the case will determine the outcome. The Federal Court has shown a reluctance to limit the elements to be considered in deciding what is just. Some of those elements, however, have been established. They include a consideration against the background that the sub-section is beneficial legislation. They include the fact that excusable ignorance of rights is a relevant factor to be taken into account. They include an assertion of the applicant’s evidentiary burden to explain the delay, although failure to offer an acceptable explanation is not necessarily fatal. Other elements have been considered in individual contexts in the many decisions of this Tribunal. It may well be that in the absence of the adoption of detailed criteria of universal application by the Federal Court, other steps should be taken.”
  1. In these proceedings the applicant’s explanation for his delay was that at the time of his resignation he was unaware of the preservation of benefits option. He also submitted that he was given wrong information regarding his options when he enquired by telephone of the personnel section about the absence of an employers contribution in his lump sum. He conceded that at the time he filled out the S2A form he found it ambiguous and thought the preservation option may mean he would have to keep contributing to the scheme but made no further enquiries to ascertain whether this was true.
  2. On the evidence before it the Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant received ‘Information Statements’, which included information on the preservation option, after he rejoined the ACT Schools Authority in 1987, but that the applicant chose not to read them. The Tribunal is of the view that the applicant had sufficient opportunity to avail himself of information regarding his superannuation options at the time he resigned from his employment in 1986 and when he re-joined in 1987.
  3. The applicant in these proceedings was not limited in his ability to make further enquiries by his health, level of education or the location of his employment (see Re Liddle and Commissioner for Superannuation (1991-92)14 AAR 456). He has a Bachelor of Education and can therefore be considered well educated and no evidence was put forward of any health problems. When he resigned from his employment the applicant was in Sydney and his employer in Canberra. However the applicant gave evidence that he contacted a personnel officer regarding his options and that he contacted the respondent when he received his contribution as he had been unaware that he would not receive his employer’s contribution. In these instances the applicant was not hindered by the distance between himself and his employer.
  4. The evidence showed that the applicant initially invested the majority of his superannuation contributions in a unit trust in the name of his wife. However the money was withdrawn sometime after the family returned to Canberra and was spent on the family home.
  5. The applicant in evidence said he had been diligent in seeking continuity of service for the purposes of his long-service leave and for his incremental salary increases but that he had not been so diligent in relation to his superannuation. The applicant conceded that he had relied heavily upon his employer to avail him of information on superannuation at the time he resigned and readily admitted that superannuation was a mystery to him.
  6. The Tribunal finds that the applicant intended to take a refund of his contribution to his superannuation scheme when he resigned from his position in 1986. In his letter of resignation dated 18 December 1985 he expressly requested that the amount be forwarded to him “as soon as possible” admitting that he did not know at the time what the normal procedure was. He relied heavily upon the information provided to him by a personnel officer of his employer and made no enquiries of his own even when he found the form S2A ambiguous. The applicant had further opportunities on re-joining the Public Service to utilise the information provided to him in superannuation documents but on his own admission chose not to read them. In this case evidence has not been produced that the applicant received wrong information or that he received information at all.
  7. The Tribunal finds that there is no evidence of a reasonable explanation for the delay by omission on the part of the respondent or by acts or omissions on the part of the applicant. For the reasons given the Tribunal finds that it is not satisfied in all the circumstances that it is desirable that the late election of the applicant should be recognised. The decision under review is therefore affirmed.
