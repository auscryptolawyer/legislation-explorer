VT84/34 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 97; (1987) 18 ATR 3295; 87 ATC 392 (17 March 1987)

Last Updated: 16 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - deductions - travelling expenses - managing director of family company - activities as company's representative in trade association, as president of association and as member of a government advisory council - whether within scope of his employment as managing director - whether his own expenditure or expenditure of company

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss 51(1), 190(b)

Handley v The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1981] HCA 16; (1981) 148 CLR 182

Commissioner of Taxation v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60

Ronpibon Tin N L v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47

REASONS FOR DECISION

Deputy President I. R. Thompson

  1. The matter at issue in this application and the relevant facts are set out in Mr Brady's statement of his reasons for the Tribunal's decision and Mr Trowse's statement of his reasons for dissenting from it. I accept that they are correctly stated by them, except that I find that the expenditure by the applicant in respect of visits to Canberra to attend meetings of the trade association was incurred as agent of the one of his companies on behalf of which he was attending them. Moreover, there is one fact on which I consider that emphasis should be laid; that is that neither in his tax return nor in his evidence at the hearing of this proceeding did the applicant attempt to allocate any separate parts of outgoings of $1,210 to any specific visits to Canberra for particular purposes. It was apparent that he had not maintained sufficient records of them for him to be able to do so.
  2. I also agree generally with the reasons stated by Mr Brady. However, in view of the reasons stated by Mr Trowse for his dissent, I wish to state the following further reasons why I have come to the conclusion that the Commissioner's decision is correct and must be affirmed.

3. Section 51(1), so far as relevant, provides as follows:-

"51. (1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income . . . shall he allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature ..."

4. In Handley v The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1981] HCA 16; (1981) 148 CLR 182 at 189-190 Stephen J. said:-

"The text of s51(1) itself provides the only criterion of deductibility. Disregarding for the moment the exclusion of particular sub-classes, it is that there should exist a particular relationship between a loss or outgoing and the gaining or producing of the taxpayer's assessable income (I need here refer only to the first limb of the description of the general class of deductibility). That relationship must be such that the loss or outgoing is of a nature or character which is incidental and relevant to the gaining of assessable income and must have been incurred 'in the course of gaining or producing' it. Whether or not on each occasion the loss or outgoing is sufficiently incidental or relevant will depend upon a variety of factors. In some circumstances the purpose for which the advantage occasioning the loss or outgoing is sought may evidence a sufficient relationship with the income-earning process, as for instance where interest is payable on borrowed money. On other occasions, the purpose may be of little assistance; it will be necessary to look to other factors to see what role, if any, the loss or outgoing plays in the conduct of the income-earning activities. As Dixon J. said in Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay's) Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295 at p 309: 'The expression 'in gaining or producing' has the force of ' in the course of gaining or producing' and looks rather to the scope of the operations or activities and the relevance thereto of the expenditure than to purpose itself.' See also the judgment of the Court in Charles Moore & Co (WA) Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1956] HCA 77; (1956) 95 CLR 344 at p 351."

Although Stephen J was in the minority in that case, that statement is one to which, with respect, I consider that no exception can be taken.

5, What we are concerned with in the present proceeding is whether the outgoings which are the subject of it were sufficiently incidental or relevant to the gaining of assessable income by the applicant. The character of the outgoing has to be' ascertained with reference to the income of the applicant himself and not with reference to the income of any of his companies. He was managing director of all of them; one of them was member of the trade association. In the year of income with which we are concerned he was paid a salary of $8,890 by that company. He and his wife were the only shareholders in that company. It is apparent that, because as he put it, he "owned" the company and he controlled its day-to-day operations, he decided for himself what his duties as managing director were (apart from those imposed on directors by statute).

6. In Commissioner of Taxation v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60 the High Court of Australia was concerned with activities of an employed architect while on leave in South America. At page 69 Kitto J. said:-

"The respondent incurred the expenditure during a period of leave, and in carrying out activities beyond any which had been or could lawfully have been specifically required of him by [the employer]. But it was nevertheless in my opinion incidentally to the proper execution of his office and not otherwise that he engaged in those activities. For the office was of a kind which by its nature made incumbent upon the occupant much more than the performance of set duties at set times."

At page 70 he said further:-

"In my judgment the respondent, in making the investigations and studies which he pursued during his period of leave, was acting within the scope of his office, and therefore in the gaining of his salary."

7. The expenditure with which we are concerned in these proceedings was incurred:-

(1) partly to enable the applicant to attend meetings of the trade association;

(2) partly to enable him to undertake work on behalf of the association in his capacity as its president; and

(3) partly to enable him to attend meetings of the Advisory Council on Secondary Industry.

8. As the trade association existed to further the trade interests of its members, including the applicant's company, and each of its full members was either required or expected to send a representative to all its meetings, I consider that, when the applicant was attending the meetings of the trade association as representative of the one of his companies which was a member of it, he was acting within the scope of his office as managing director of that company. If the expenditure relating to his attendance at those meetings had been incurred by him on his own behalf, I should have been willing to accept that it was an outgoing incurred in gaining part of his assessable income, namely his salary from that company. However, the expenditure was incurred on behalf of his company and not on his own behalf; so while it might have been possible to treat it as deductible in relation to the company, it is not deductible in relation to him.

9. The expenditure incurred to enable the applicant to undertake work on behalf of the trade association in his capacity as its president (e.g. visits to Ministers and government offices to discuss the forging industry) and to enable him to attend meetings of the Council I consider to be outside the scope of his office as managing director of his company, and insufficiently incidental or relevant to his gaining assessable income by way of salary paid to him by that company. No doubt he accepted office in the association and nomination as a member of the Council with the object of furthering the interests of the forging industry and thereby of his own companies. They paid his fares to travel to Canberra for the visits; I accept that that indicates that they wished him to make them. However, the range of possible activities of a senior officer of a company which may benefit that organisation and which it consequently approves and supports financially is broad. It extends from active soliciting of business to personal activities of a public-spirited nature which it may be hoped will create, maintain or enhance a favourable image of the company in the public's mind. But not every such activity can be regarded as being within the scope of his office so that expenditure on it is sufficiently incidental or relevant to the gaining of his assessable income by way of salary to be deductible under section 51. In respect of the expenditure other than that incurred to enable the applicant to attend meetings of the trade association as a representative of his company, my view coincides with that of Mr Brady.

10. Even if I had not concluded that the expenditure on attending meetings of the trade association was incurred by the applicant on the company's behalf and I had been satisfied that it was incurred on his own behalf, my decision on his claim in respect of the whole of the amount of $1,210 would have been the same. That is because there is no evidence how much of that amount was expenditure incurred on visits to Canberra made for the purpose of attending meetings of the association.

11. Because of the facts which I have found, it is unnecessary to consider whether the expenditure was an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the applicant's assessable income by way of dividends paid to him by his companies. However, I note that, although there is no requirement that a taxpayer should show that in the year of income there was assessable income, or that there was an expectation of assessable income, with which the outgoing could be matched (Ronpibon Tin N L v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47), the outgoing must have been incurred "in the course of the income-producing activity" (Re Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Limited v The Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295). The income-producing activity in relation to the applicant's dividends is his holding of shares in his companies, not the activities of those companies. The expenditure with which we are concerned in this proceeding was not incurred in the course of that activity.

Mr. K. P. Brady, Senior Member

1. I have had the advantage of reading the findings of fact and reasons in law for the conclusions reached by ray colleague, Mr. D. J. Trowse. However, while I am in substantial agreement with his findings of fact and his conclusions concerning the general principles to be observed in interpreting the provisions of section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, I am unable to accept his conclusions in law based upon its application to the facts in the instant case.

  1. From the evidence it is quite clear, in my opinion, that> the expenditure in issue was incurred by the applicant, first, as a representative of his own group of companies, secondly, as the president of the association concerned with the interests of all companies engaged in the forging industry, and thirdly, because of his appointment to the Advisory Council on Secondary Industry, as a representative of the association at the various meetings which he attended. All three positions, which concerned separate legal entities, were honorary from which no income was derived and from which, it seems, none would be derived.
  2. Whilst it may well be that the activities of the applicant in the various roles undertaken by him gave rise to commercial advantages for his employer (as well as for the other separate legal entities represented), I am nevertheless of the opinion that the nexus between the expenditure and the applicant's salaried income of the year in issue, or of subsequent years, was too tenuous or remote to allow it to be said that the expenditure was "incurred in gaining or producing the (applicant's) assessable income" of any year. In the words of Helsham J in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v White 75 ATC 4018 at p 4022, in a somewhat different but nonetheless relevant context, the expenditure could not "be seen to have a direct effect on income", with the consequence that it does not, in my opinion, qualify as a deduction in terms of section 51(1) of the Assessment Act.

4. I refer to three Board of Review cases which I consider to he supportive of my conclusions in law which I have reached in the instant case. In the first of these cases, reported as J46 77 ATC 420, the applicant was a high school science teacher employed by the Department of Education. With the encouragement of his employer, the applicant became the president of an association of science teachers. The position was an honorary one. In support of his claims for a deduction in respect of expenditure on telephone, postage, travel, etc. incurred in respect of his position as president, the applicant contended that the position had contributed to more rapid promotion in the Education Department, and hence to more income, and that it had enabled him to earn extra income from writing, examiner fees, etc. The expenditure was held by the Board of Review, in an unanimous decision, not to he deductible under section 51(1). 5. In the second of these cases, reported as J47 77 ATC 423, the applicant was also a high school science teacher employed by a State Education Department. He was Secretary Treasurer of a science teachers' association. The position was also an honorary one. In support of his contention that various expenses incurred on association business were deductible, the applicant claimed that his duties as Secretary/Treasurer were an integral part of his professional activities from which he generated income and that his promotion within the teaching service, with a consequent increase in income, could in part be attributed to his work as Secretary/Treasurer and to various contacts which this position had enabled him to make. He also claimed that this position enabled him to earn additional income from writing, lecturing and from his activities as an examiner. In an unanimous decision, the Board of Review held that there was not the necessary connection between the expenditure incurred and» the applicant's assessable income to support a claim under section 51(1).

  1. Finally, in the last of the three cases to which I refer, reported as S32 85 ATC 290, the applicant was a lecturer in physics who specialised in cloud physics. As part of his duties in a research project to investigate the feasibility of seeding clouds to produce extra rainfall, the applicant was required to fly through extremely hazardous cloud conditions. In the interests of safety, to ensure that someone other than the pilot would be able to fly the plane in an emergency, the applicant undertook flying lessons at his own expense. By majority, the Board of Review found that the cost of the flying lessons was not deductible under section 51(1) because the nexus between that cost and the earning of the applicant's assessable income was too tenuous. In the opinion of the majority of the Board, the flying lessons bore no relationship to the derivation of the applicant's assessable income.
  2. However, should - I be wrong in my conclusion concerning the application of section 51(1) in the circumstances of the instant case, I am of the opinion that, in any event, the applicant has failed to discharge the onus, in terms of section 190(1)) of the Assessment Act, of proving that the assessment is excessive. As indicated earlier, the expenditure in issue arose out of the applicant's activities in his several roles and it is not possible on the evidence to determine what part of the amount of $1,210 should be attributed to his activities as a represent ative of his company employer

8. For the above reasons, I would disallow the applicant's claims and affirm the respondent's decision under review.

REASONS FOR DISSENT

Mr D. J. Trowse, Member

  1. The matter at issue in this reference is whether certain items of expenditure incurred by a managing director of a group of family companies in travelling between a capital city and Canberra are deductible in terms of section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. The outgoings in question total $1,210 and relate to 12 separate journeys, all of which were undertaken in the 1979 year of income.
  2. At the hearing, the applicant appeared on his own behalf and gave evidence under oath. I regard him as a witness of truth and have no hesitation in accepting his testimony. The Commissioner of Taxation (the respondent) was represented by one of his officers.
  3. It appears that the applicant was instrumental in the establishment of a ferreous forging business in about 1950, and that several family companies were incorporated to carry on that and associated activities. The evidence indicates that the group had expanded and prospered since inception and that much of that success was due, in no small way, to the technical skills and enthusiasm of the applicant. All of the companies were under his control and, throughout the year under review, he occupied the position of managing director and received from those entities salaries totalling $23,733. It seems that the applicant was also the recipient of dividends and interest from what appears to be the holding company of the group. The amounts of dividends and interest received in the 1979 year were $4,701 and $7,161 respectively.
  4. As part of the background, it is mentioned that the applicant played a major role in the formation of a trade association for companies engaged in the forging industry in Australia, the prime function of which was the advancement of both the industry and its members. Furthermore, it seems that one of the companies within the family group was a financial member of the association and that the applicant was its nominated representative to attend all meetings of that organisation. It appears that the applicant was the president of that association for several years, including the one under review, and that the holding of such a position was one of the factors leading to his invitation and ultimate appointment to the Advisory Council on Secondary Industry. It was in those capacities that he was required to journey to Canberra on 12 separate occasions during the 1979 financial year, and it is the expenses related to that travel which are the subject of the dispute.
  5. According to the applicant's testimony, the visits to Canberra were undertaken for the express purpose of attending meetings, discussing industry matters with secretaries and officers of several Government Departments and the making of, representations to various Ministers of the Government. It seems that on some of those visits the applicant remained in Canberra overnight and that the expenditure in issue refers to taxi fares to and from airports, together with the costs of accommodation and meals. It seems also that the applicant did not seek nor receive reimbursement of those outgoings, notwithstanding that the cost of the air travel, as distinct from the expenditures outlaid by the applicant, was met by the companies within the group. Further, it seems that the applicant did not seek reimbursement, not because he was not entitled to it, but rather because of some personal inconvenience plus the fact that he was unable to recognise the need to differentiate between his resources and those of the companies which he owned and controlled.
  6. It is convenient to pause here to consider the quantum of the claim as well as the burden of proof imposed on the applicant by the operation of section 190(b). In so doing, I make the observation that the amount in question appears reasonable when taken on a cost per individual trip basis. Whilst it is possible to conclude that the description afforded the claim in the return was misleading (inadvertently in my opinion), and that the evidence pertaining to the amount actually expended lacked precision (which perhaps is not surprising after a long waiting period for a hearing of his reference) the applicant nevertheless deposed explicitly to the fact that a sum of money of at least $1,210 was so expended. On the basis of the evidence, the amount claimed appears not to be unreasonable. In the circumstances I take the view, as did Derrington J. in Shieli v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 86 ATC 4731 (see p. 4739), that the evidence should be accepted notwithstanding the quality of the supporting material.
  7. Reference is also made to the wording of the applicant's objection, and in particular to the claim that the expenditure under review was incurred in the derivation of assessable income in the form of salary, dividends and interest. I am of the opinion that the relationship between the outgoings and the acts required of the applicant in the derivation of his salary income is such that the amounts claimed to qualify as a deduction must be considered having regard to his employee status only. In the circumstances, I find it unnecessary to comment on any connection, which at best could only have been remote that may have existed between the outgoings and the investment income received by him.
  8. The provisions of section 51(1), as they relate to this reference, are as follows;-

"All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income . . . shall he allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature ..." he words "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income" mean incurred "in the course of gaining or producing such income" Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay's) Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295 at p. 303) and require the outgoings to be "incidental and relevant to that end" (Ronpibon Tin N L v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at p 56).

  1. In issues involving an interpretation of this section, "the question is how far was it (the outgoing) incurred in the course of, how far was it incidental or relevant to, gaining or producing the assessable income" (Ronpibon Tin N. L. (supra) at p 58). From this it is obvious that the extent or degree of the relationship between the outgoing and the assessable income will be determinative of the question now under consideration. The nexus so necessary for the operation of the section will, in my opinion, exist if it is established that the outgoings totalling $1,210 were incurred by the applicant in the discharge of the duties of his employment.
  2. On this fundamental point, it will be recalled that the employer of the applicant had nominated him as its representative with the trade association and that to some extent the presidency of that organisation had precipitated his appointment to the Advisory Council of Secondary Industry. As a consequence of those appointments, the applicant was required to travel from time to time to Canberra in order to attend to matters directly related to the industry engaged in by his employer, and thus it appears reasonable to conclude that the activities of those two bodies were to the commercial advantage of the employer and that that was the object of such an involvement. It seems clear to me that, when the applicant was travelling on those 12 occasions, he was performing the duties required of his employment and which were productive of his salaried income. On that basis, the degree of nexus required in terms of section 51(1) has been met and, accordingly, the claim for expenditure of $1,210 is allowable under that section.

11. For the reasons stated, I allow the applicant's objection.
