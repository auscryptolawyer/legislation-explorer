Statement of Reasons

  1. Is an oral health therapist (OHT) entitled to receive superannuation contributions paid by the dental practice which engages them? That is the question the Tribunal has been asked to decide in this case. The outcome is important to Balmain Dental Clinic Pty Ltd (the Applicant) which has been assessed for around $70,000 in unpaid superannuation guarantee charge (SGC). It is also important to Kathryn Ganora, the OHT who contends she was entitled to superannuation, even though she worked under a contract.

PRELIMINARY

The background facts

  1. Dr Saade[1] is a dentist practising in Balmain Sydney. He is the sole director, shareholder and controlling mind of the Applicant which is the trustee for the trust via which the dental clinic operates in Balmain, Sydney. During the relevant period, the clinic comprised between 3 and 6 treatment rooms, equipped for the treatment of dental patients, as well as other facilities available for the use of dental practitioners and clinic staff.
  2. Dental practitioners were engaged during the relevant period to work at the clinic, including other dentists, a dental hygienist and OHTs. Dr Saade told the Tribunal that the dental practitioners were all engaged by written contracts which allowed them to use the facilities in return for a percentage of the paid fees they derived from the patients they treated.[2]
  3. The clinic was managed by Dr Saade with assistance from his wife, Jamie Saade, and a small group of employees in consultation with a small management group. Dr Saade told the Tribunal and it accepts, that he and Jamie Saadie had “the ultimate say” over decisions relating to the clinic.[3]
  4. Ms Ganora[4] commenced working as an OHT at the clinic in early January 2014 and on the understanding that she would be paid on a fixed 40% commission rate, on invoices paid by patients each month.[5]
  5. Dr Saade told the Tribunal Ms Ganora commenced work at the clinic on 10 January 2014 and signed a contract with the Applicant on the same day.[6] Ms Ganora did not recall her start date. She also did not recall signing a contract when she commenced working at the clinic.
  6. Ms Ganora left the clinic in around June 2020 to work for another local practice. She said she was asked in 2020 to sign a Facilities and Services Agreement but did not do so.[7] After leaving the clinic, Ms Ganora subsequently found herself in dispute with the Applicant about a “non-compete” restraint in the original contract and denied she had signed it. Ms Ganora instructed solicitors to advise her about the contractual dispute. In the course of assisting her, both Ms Ganora’s solicitors and her accountant advised her that the Applicant may be under an obligation to pay superannuation contributions on her behalf.[8]
  7. Dr Saade confirmed that in or around June 2020 (after the relevant period), the Applicant changed the contracts it entered into with dental practitioners and thereafter they more closely resembled facilities and services agreements, similar to the one Ms Ganora had refused to sign in 2020.
  8. Dr Lauren Braunston is a dentist who worked at the clinic from around January 2016 to November 2018. She gave evidence to the Tribunal about the working arrangements at the clinic.[9] Dr Braunston had signed a contract with the Applicant, but it is different to the contract which Dr Saade contends Ms Ganora signed on 10 January in 2014.
  9. Nafeena Feroz is an OHT who gave evidence to the Tribunal about a service agreement she entered into with the Applicant around June 2020 – after the relevant period[10] as well as some practice issues. Ms Feroz’ agreement is also different to the contract which the Applicant contends was signed by Ms Ganora in 2014.[11] Some of Ms Feroz’ evidence was directly contradicted by prior records tendered by the Respondent. For this reason, the Tribunal has treated her evidence with caution where it was not corroborated by contemporaneous records and/or other witnesses’ evidence.

The decision which is the subject of this application

  1. The decision which is the subject of the application is the Respondent’s objection decision dated 7 June 2023[12] to assess the Applicant for payment of SCG for the tax quarters ending 1 January 2014 to 30 June 2020,[13] on the basis that the terms of Ms Ganora’s engagement by the Applicant as an OHT fell within the extended definition of “employee” by virtue of s 12(3) Superannuation Guarantee (Administration) Act 1992 (Cth) (the Superannuation Act).

The steps which led to the application to the Tribunal may be summarised as follows:

  • - on 31 May 2022, the Respondent notified the Applicant that he was checking if Ms Ganora was eligible for superannuation guarantee for the period 1 January 2014 to 31 March 2020;[14]
  • - on 14 October 2022, the Respondent issued a Position Paper explaining the reasons for his view that Ms Ganora was an “employee” of the Applicant for the relevant period;[15]
  • - on 8 December 2022, the Applicant issued his audit decision and reasons;[16]
  • - on 3 February 2023, the Applicant lodged notice of his objection to the audit decision;[17]
  • - on 7 June 2023, the Applicant issued his objection decision and reasons and determined the amounts set out at Attachment A were payable;[18] and
  • - on 3 August 2023, the Applicant lodged an application with the Tribunal seeking review of the Respondent’s objection decision.[19]

  1. On 14 October 2024, the Administrative Appeals Tribunal (AAT) became the Administrative Review Tribunal (the Tribunal). Under the transitional provisions in the Administrative Review Tribunal (Consequential and Transitional Provisions No. 1) Act 2024 (Cth), proceedings in the AAT not finalised before 14 October 2024 are to be continued and finalised by the Tribunal. Anything done in relation to these proceedings before 14 October 2024 is taken to have been done by the Tribunal.
  2. These are tax proceedings brought by the Applicant under Division 4, Part IVC Tax Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (TAA 53). Accordingly, the Applicant bears the onus of proving that the Respondent’s assessments of SGC were excessive and what they should have been.[20]
  3. The practical issue for determination, is whether the Applicant has demonstrated on the balance of probabilities[21] that Ms Ganora was engaged by the Applicant on terms which mean she was not entitled to superannuation under s 12(3) Superannuation Act.
  4. Section 12 Superannuation Act relevantly states:

12 Interpretation: employee, employer

(1) Subject to this section, in this Act, employee and employer have their ordinary meaning. However, for the purposes of this Act, subsections (2) to (11):

(a) expand the meaning of those terms; and

(b) make particular provision to avoid doubt as to the status of certain persons.

......

(3) If a person works under a contract that is wholly or principally for the labour of the person, the person is an employee of the other party to the contract.

The authorities

  1. Whether a person is engaged to work on terms which satisfy s 12(3) Superannuation Act requires close assessment of the facts of their case. The Courts have established legal tests to assist decision makers to evaluate those facts and determine whether a particular person’s arrangements satisfy the extended definition of “employee” in s 12(3) Superannuation Act. The key legal authorities which discuss and apply those tests are briefly discussed below, and will be referred to throughout these reasons.

Older authorities which specifically address the right to delegate/subcontract

  1. In Neale v Atlas Products (Vic) Proprietary Limited,[22] the High Court of Australia considered the status of workers engaged by a tiling company to fix roofing tiles. The company offered jobs to one of a number of available tilers (which they were free to refuse) and paid them regularly but on the basis of each tiling job they completed. The tilers would sign contracts with the company at the completion of each job, for their labour and materials provided. Relevantly, the contracts did not require the tiling jobs to be completed by the particular tiler (i.e. the tiler/s were free to engage another tiler to do their work).[23]
  2. The High Court held that the relationship between the company and the tilers in Neale was not one of master and servant[24] and the tilers were independent contractors who did not earn salary or wages as they “were not required, by the terms of their contracts to perform personally the contractual work”.[25]
  3. In Australian Mutual Provident Society v Chaplin, the Privy Council considered entitlement to long service leave under the Long Service Leave Act 1967 (SA) in the context of a contract for service. The Privy Council distinguished between the unlimited right to delegate the whole of a person’s performance of work and considered a person’s power to control the manner of doing their work as “a very important (perhaps the most important) indicator of a contract for services as opposed to a contract for service”.[26] The Privy Council found that Mr Chaplin had not been engaged under a contract of service because he had the right to incorporate himself[27] and the right to wholly and freely delegate his work to others to perform. It observed:[28]

The unlimited extent of the power of delegation is one consequence of the striking absence of any express obligation upon the respondent to perform any particular duties or to work any particular hours, or indeed to do any work at all on behalf of the Society.

  1. The Privacy Council also considered whether the written contract between the parties had been materially varied by their subsequent conduct but was not convinced there was evidence of significance in Mr Chaplin’s case to suggest an intention to depart from the terms of the written agreement.[29]

Authorities which address the requirements of s 12(3) Superannuation Act

  1. In On Call Interpreters and Translators Agency Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (No 2),[30] the Federal Court was asked to determine whether interpreters providing interpreting services under contract were “employees” for the purposes of s 12(3) Superannuation Act. Bromberg J observed that:[31]

Simply expressed, the question of whether a person is an independent contractor in relation to the performance of particular work, may be posed and answered as follows:

Viewed as a “practical matter”:

(i) is the person performing the work an entrepreneur who owns and operates a business; and

(ii) in performing the work, is that person working in and for that person’s business as a representative of that business and not of the business receiving the work?

If the answer to that question is yes, in the performance of that particular work, the person is likely to be an independent contractor. If no, then the person is likely to be an employee.

  1. The case of Dental Corporation Pty Ltd v Moffet, concerned a dentist who had sold his practice but then entered into a contract to provide dental services. There, the Full Court of the Federal Court of Australia[32] found as a matter of construction that:[33]

In our opinion, what s 12(3) requires is that: (a) there should be a ‘contract’; (b) which is wholly or principally ‘for’ the labour of a person; and (c) that the person must ‘work’ under that contract. There is no doubt that Dr Moffet provided his work under the Services Agreement so the requirements of (a) and (c) are met.

So far as (b) is concerned, the word ‘for’ is purposive but even the simplest employment relationship has two purposes depending on the perspective from which it is viewed. From the employer’s perspective an employment contract is ‘for’ the provision of labour (in return for wages); from the employee’s perspective it is ‘for’ the receipt of wages (in return for labour).

  1. In Moffet, the Full Court rejected the approach adopted in On Call Interpreters which it said focussed on the concept of whether the person worked in an employment-like setting which was not required by the statutory test in s 12(3).[34] It also cautioned against the application of the “indicia, especially control, which govern the general issue of whether one person is employed by another” to assess s 12(3) Superannuation Act as this was not its purpose.[35]
  2. The Full Court also rejected the suggestion that provision of premises from which Dr Moffet could conduct his dental practice was evidence of the absence of an employment-like relationship.[36] Nor was it persuaded that provision of other health care professionals to assist him to run his dental practice suggested an absence of an employment-like relationship.[37] It was satisfied that the contract in question was “principally” for Dr Moffet’s labour, highlighting the importance of the words “wholly or principally” in s 12(3) Superannuation Act.
  3. In considering whether a person is an employee for the purposes of s 12(3) Superannuation Act, the authorities emphasise the need for objective assessment of the contractual relationship between the person engaged and the person or entity which has engaged them.[38] In Construction, Forestry, Maritime, Mining and Energy Union v Personnel Contracting Pty Ltd, the High Court observed:[39]

The foregoing should not be taken to suggest that it is not appropriate, in the characterisation of a relationship as one of employment or of principal and independent contractor, to consider "the totality of the relationship between the parties" by reference to the various indicia of employment that have been identified in the authorities. What must be appreciated, however, is that in a case such as the present, for a matter to bear upon the ultimate characterisation of a relationship, it must be concerned with the rights and duties established by the parties' contract, and not simply an aspect of how the parties' relationship has come to play out in practice but bearing no necessary connection to the contractual obligations of the parties.

[emphasis added]

  1. In JMC Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation,[40] the Full Court considered a series of short term contracts between a lecturer and an education provider and found that the lecturer was an independent contractor because the contracts into which he entered did not establish controls over how, when or where he was to deliver the lectures.[41] The Full Court referred[42] with approval to Personnel Contracting Pty Ltd as well as Stevens v Brodribb[43] and Chaplin in support of the proposition that the power to delegate was an important factor in deciding whether a worker was a servant or an independent contractor:[44]

Whatever the precise language deployed in a contract, it is plain enough that, if a person engaged to perform work has a contractual right to have someone else perform that work, that is a matter which at the very least tends against a conclusion that the person is an employee. The existence of the right is inherently inconsistent with an employee relationship. In the absence of significant countervailing considerations, how can you be an employee if, within the scope of the contract, you can lawfully get someone else to perform the entirety of your contractual obligations, whether for a short period, or for a longer period?

  1. The Court in JMC Pty Ltd, also warned that the right to control a person’s work is the relevant indicator of an employer-employee relationship, as opposed to what actually transpired by the parties’ conduct.[45]
  2. In Jamsek v ZG Operations Australia Pty Ltd (No 3),[46] the Full Court[47] made various observations about the three elements of the test identified in Moffet before applying them to the facts of that case. There, the applicant truck drivers were engaged by contract to deliver goods and the Court found they had not established that they fell within s 12(3) Superannuation Act both because the contracting party was a partnership, and because they had not demonstrated that the contract was principally for their labour. The Court’s key observations in Jamsek may be summarised as follows:
    (a) section 12(3) Superannuation Act contemplates there will be evidence of a bilateral exchange of promises between the contracting parties, on one side for the provision of labour in return for payment and on the other, the promise of payment in return for that labour;[48]

    (b) the question of what the contract was for from the perspective of the imputed employer is to be determined by reference to the terms of the contract;[49]

    (c) the “person” must personally be a party to the “contract” in question;[50]

    (d) the “labour” in question must be provided by the person under the contract in their individual capacity;[51]

    (e) the relevant contract must establish a relationship of master and servant between the two parties;[52]

    (f) the labour component of the relevant contract must be the whole or principal benefit;[53]

    (g) a person who is free to employ others to perform the “labour” under the contract will not satisfy the test in s 12(3). The percentage of work which was actually delegated in practice is not important;[54] and

    (h) a promise which effectively amounts to provision of a service or a given result rather than the provision of personal labour, will not satisfy the test in s 12(3).[55]

  3. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Hatfield Plumbing Pty Ltd (as trustee for the Peter Hatfield Trust)[56] [2025] FCA 182, Logan J upheld a decision by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal that a plumber engaged to perform work for the Applicant on a “do and charge” basis when it was unable to do the work, charging on a fixed hourly rate for work performed, was not an employee for the purposes of s 12(3) Superannuation Act. His Honour referred by way of comparison, to the lecturer’s arrangement in JMC Pty Ltd but also noted that the mode of remuneration may not always be determinative of whether a contract is for labour or a result or product.[57]

THE EVIDENCE

What was the “contract” between the Applicant and Ms Ganora?

  1. The parties were in dispute about whether there was a written contract between the Applicant and Ms Ganora during the relevant period, whether she was remunerated for her labour/service or for services performed, and whether she was permitted to freely delegate/sub-contract her work.
  2. Both Dr Saade and Ms Ganora had lodged written evidence with the Tribunal about both the contract and Ms Ganora’s work history at the clinic. Both were also cross-examined at the hearing. The Tribunal found both to be forthright, but highly motivated and self-interested witnesses. It also accepted both were giving evidence about historical events by referring to aged and disparate documents. In the circumstances, the Tribunal assessed there to be sizeable risk that their various recollections or “interpretations” of actions or records were tainted by the passage of time and/or coloured by impermissible hindsight reasoning.
  3. The Tribunal particularly observed that Dr Saade’s observations about the clinic and the way it operated during the relevant period were sometimes contradicted by the contemporaneous records.
  4. Ms Ganora on the other hand, suffered from poor recollection about historical events. She corrected a number of facts when prompted. She also conceded under cross-examination that she may have forgotten things given the passage of time and her lack of access to the clinic’s records.
  5. In the circumstances, the Tribunal has approached the uncorroborated evidence of both Dr Saade and Ms Ganora with some caution. It has relied where it can, on contemporaneous records and/or the corroborating evidence of witnesses who do not have a direct interest in the outcome of this application. In this regard, the Tribunal was particularly assisted by the evidence of Dr Braunston, who the Tribunal found to be a compelling witness.
  6. Returning to the contract, Dr Saade exhibited a signed version to his first affidavit.[58] The contract contained what appeared to be Ms Ganora’s signature as well as the signature of a witness.[59] The Applicant submitted the contract governed the relationship between the Applicant and Ms Ganora during the relevant period, subject to a possible amendment arising from the conduct of the parties in respect of the right to delegate.
  7. Ms Ganora conceded her signature was on the contract at an unusual angle. She also conceded she could not recall her start date at the clinic.[60] Despite this, she maintained she had not signed any contract in January 2014[61] and produced a letter signed by Dr Saade and dated 3 December 2019 (the letter) which she said she had requested to obtain a home loan. It was reproduced under cover of an email from the clinic, was signed by Dr Saade and stated that as at that date there “is no written contract, contractor agreement or service agreement between Dentist & Co and Kathryn Ganora” but that “for the past 7 years Kathryn Ganora has been working at Dentist & Co as an independent contractor”.[6] Dr Saade told the Tribunal he had not authorised any such letter.
  8. Ms Ganora then conceded under cross-examination that her memory had somewhat faded and that she may have forgotten signing the contract on 10 January 2014.[62]
  9. Both the contract and the letter appeared to be genuine. The Tribunal does not accept that the Applicant needed to produce copies of all other contracts in existence at the time to establish that Ms Ganora’s contract was authentic.[63] In any event, there was one other contract in evidence from Dr Braunston, albeit with different terms.[64]
  10. In the circumstances, the Tribunal is satisfied that the Applicant has established on the balance of probabilities that the contract is genuine and was signed by Dr Saade and Ms Ganora and operated to bind them from 10 January 2014.
  11. The Tribunal also accepts the letter to be genuine. Ms Ganora’s explanation that she requested the letter in 2019 to prove her earning circumstances for a home loan was plausible. No compelling evidence was led to suggest the letter was a fabrication.[65] Both documents could be genuine because they were created five years apart, for different reasons and possibly by different people within the Applicant. While the Tribunal finds Ms Ganora’s evidence about the letter to be credible, it is not persuaded this proves the contract is not genuine.

The terms of the written contract

  1. Having established there was a written contract executed on 10 January 2014, the Tribunal is next required to consider its relevant features to determine the basis upon which Ms Ganora was engaged by the Applicant.
  2. There was no dispute that the contract named Ms Ganora as a contracting party rather than any business entity associated with her[66] and she was referred to throughout the contract, as “the contractor”.[67] There was also no suggestion throughout the contract that anyone other than Ms Ganora was intended to fulfill the terms and conditions imposed upon the “contractor”.
  3. Clause 4 of the contract was entitled “Duty to the Company” and required Ms Ganora as the contractor to:
    • - faithfully serve the Applicant and use her best endeavours to promote its interests and welfare;
    • - devote the whole of her time and attention during her working hours to her responsibilities and duties as an OHT and dental hygienist;
    • - observe all policies and procedures of the Applicant.

[emphasis added]

  1. Clause 4 was strongly worded. It imposed an obligation upon Ms Ganora to represent and promote the Applicant’s business and interests ahead of her own.[68] The words “faithfully serve”, “devote” and “observe” also suggest an asymmetrical relationship was established between the Applicant and Ms Ganora by the clause – more like that of master and servant, rather than the relationship which would exist between a commercial tenant and landlord/service provider.[69]
  2. Additionally, the words “devote the whole of her time and attention during her working hours” conveyed to the Tribunal a sense that Ms Ganora would be labouring for the Applicant rather than providing services.
  3. The following emails issued on behalf of the Applicant/Dr Saade to Ms Ganora and other dental practitioners during the relevant period support this interpretation:
    • - emails reminding the dental practitioners that they needed to apply for, and be personally approved by, Dr Saade to take leave;[70]
    • - emails requesting that dental practitioners be present at the clinic for a full day’s work, even if they did not have patient bookings;[71]
    • - emails confirming Ms Ganora’s request to take leave had been approved;[72] and
    • - emails directing dental practitioners to be present at the practice on workdays in time to attend the “morning huddle” at 8.15am.[73]
  4. Clause 4 also required Ms Ganora to observe all policies and procedures of the Applicant.[74] The Tribunal was told of a discount protocol issued by or on behalf of the Applicant to all dental practitioners which provided directions as to the instances when discounts could be applied and by whom.[75] The protocol is discussed further below.
  5. Clause 5 of the contract provided for Ms Ganora’s days and hours of work to be as agreed between the parties. There was no dispute that Ms Ganora nominated the number of days she worked at the clinic[76] but as noted above, the records showed that in practice, the Applicant sought to dictate the hours Ms Ganora attended the clinic on her workdays.
  6. Clause 7 of the contract was entitled “Remuneration”. Dr Saade told the Tribunal that the Applicant charged a service fee to Ms Ganora under the contract for use of the clinic in the amount of 60% of the amounts she billed to patients. Clause 7 made no mention of a service fee. Instead it provided that “your total remuneration is 40% of your gross payments, less consumable costs associated with labratory (sic) and whitening procedures. No other amounts are payable”. Ms Ganora told the Tribunal she was not paying for use of the premises[77] but conceded she was aware that the cost of the premises, fixtures and equipment was built into the 60% of net revenue kept by the Applicant each month.[78]
  7. The Macquarie Dictionary defines “remuneration” as “that which remunerates; reward; pay”. The use the term “remuneration” in clause 7 does not suggest a service fee was being imposed. Instead it suggests rewards or payments would be made by the Applicant to Ms Ganora in return for her service as an OHT. The Tribunal is satisfied clause 7 establishes a mechanism by which Ms Ganora’s commission payments would be calculated as “remuneration” or “reward” for her work.
  8. It is telling that clause 7 made no provision for Ms Ganora to pay any fee to the Applicant in the situation where no patients were seen by her in a month. If clause 7 was intended to establish a service fee component (which the Tribunal is not satisfied it did), it certainly did not place any business/commercial risk upon Ms Ganora or guarantee the Applicant would always be paid one.
  9. In evidence, Ms Ganora appeared to have no knowledge or expectation that she was to pay monthly “rent” or fees for use of the rooms in the clinic or the equipment.[79] The Applicant did not attempt to quantify the costs associated with the 60% component but generally referred to the building and equipment as being provided.
  10. Under clause 8, Ms Ganora was obliged to attend “any training program as directed” (emphasis added) – a further indication that she was submitting to the Applicant’s oversight and direction.
  11. Clause 10 of the contract provided among other things for “Termination of this Agreement” in the case of Ms Ganora’s “serious misconduct” including for “neglect of duties”, “breach of trust” and “failure to hold or maintain any required licences, insurances, or qualifications”. Once again, the Tribunal finds this wording to be suggestive of a master and servant relationship than a commercial relationship being established. However it also acknowledges that clause also provided mechanism for Ms Ganora to give notice and end the engagement.
  12. Ms Ganora’s obligations to the Applicant under clauses 8 and 10 of the contract to hold and maintain professional qualifications also seemed at odds with Dr Saade’s evidence that each clinician operated “independently of him and the facility.”[80] While the Tribunal is prepared to accept that Ms Ganora was not “supervised” by Dr Saade or any dentist in the clinic as an employee would be,[81] clauses 8 and 10 suggest that the Applicant had an interest in her qualifications.
  13. Clauses 10 and 12 of the contract established that Ms Ganora had no right to possession or control of her clinical files. The clinic centrally managed patient treatment records, including for Ms Ganora. That is significant because the professional Code of Conduct operating as at March 2014 otherwise placed obligations on dental practitioners to maintain their own adequate practice records for patient care.[82]
  14. Finally, the contract contained a 3 year restraint of trade clause (the restraint clause). The restraint clause[83] relevantly referred to the Company’s clients, employees and business associates. The Applicant submits the inclusion of the restraint of trade clause supports its contention that Ms Ganora was engaged as an independent contractor, while the Respondent submits it suggests Ms Ganora was engaged to work under the contract, as part of the Applicant’s business/practice as opposed to a commercial tenant who was managing her own independent dental practice.
  15. Both parties’ arguments have merit. However, the use of the words “the Company’s clients” implies the restraint was not intended to be limited to patients who were personally attached to Ms Ganora. It tends to suggest the Applicant wanted to guard against Ms Ganora soliciting the “Applicant’s clients” to another practice. This gives weight to the Respondent’s contention that Ms Ganora was being engaged to work at the client as part of an integrated dental team rather than on her own.
  16. Separately, the Tribunal notes that Ms Ganora was permitted to and did undertake some work at other clinics while working for the Applicant.[84] This work was not subject to the restraint clause.

The relevance of the national dental standard, scope of practice and the code of conduct

  1. Both Dr Saade, Ms Ganora and the other dental practitioners operating at the clinic were required to comply during the relevant period with the Dental Board of Australia, Scope of Practice Registration Standard (the Standard). The relevant Standard was in evidence.[85] The Dental Board’s relevant Guidelines for the Scope of Practice for all registered dental practitioners, including OHTs were also in evidence (the Guidelines),[86] together with the Dental Board’s relevant Code of Conduct.[87]
  2. In evaluating the contract, the Tribunal has assumed that both parties intended at all times to operate in a manner which complied with their professional obligations under the Standard, the Guidelines and the Code of Conduct.[88]
  3. Dr Saade contended that the obligations established under the Code of Conduct established goals for professionals to follow but did not impose obligations upon him to supervise or control Ms Ganora. He contended that,

accordingly, any suggestion that I supervised or controlled Ms Ganora is inconsistent with both the Code of Conduct and the fully independent manner in which she practised. My interactions with Ms Ganora were collegial and professional, consistent with the obligations set out in the Code of Conduct.[89]

  1. The Tribunal does not accept this as wholly correct. Ms Ganora was not permitted under registration Guidelines to practise as an “independent practitioner”.[90] The Standard repeated this requirement and expressly stated that an OHT was required to work in a “structured professional relationship” with a dentist and must not practise independently.[91] The Standard further provided for the dentist to be “the clinical team leader”.
  2. The Standard, the Guidelines and the Code of Conduct, provide a detailed and comprehensive picture of how the dental practitioners should work together in a clinical setting, with the dentist/s performing the role of “clinical team leader”. The Tribunal is satisfied those requirements are incompatible with the claim that Ms Ganora operated independently at the clinic.
  3. They also made implausible the suggestion that OHTs were engaged “to utilise the Facility in the further of their businesses” (emphasis added)[92] and that Ms Ganora’s contract was intended to establish a leasing or services arrangement between her and the Applicant to allow her to run her own practice. While Ms Ganora had an ABN she was not permitted to run an independent business.

Fees charged vs remuneration under clause 7 of the contract

  1. Dr Saade, Dr Braunston, and Ms Ganora each explained how dental practitioners entered codes for treatment items they performed on a program operated by the clinic. The front desk staff then generated invoices for payment by patients at the end of their treatments.[93] Ms Ganora told the Tribunal, “my responsibility was to put the item codes in, not produce the invoice.”[94]
  2. Dr Braunston and Ms Ganora both told the Tribunal and it accepts there was some scope for adjustment of the pre-populated prices assigned to each code for fees they entered on the invoicing system when treating a patient.[95] Dr Saade told the Tribunal that he did not oversee the fees charged by the dental practitioners[96] and that Ms Ganora generated her own invoices. This evidence was contradicted by the contemporaneous records and other evidence which suggested the invoices issued in the name of the clinic and were produced by the front desk staff.
  3. The Applicant contended that by entering codes for tasks she performed, Ms Ganora was running her own practice, treating patients and subsequently was remunerated by the Applicant services she provided to patients or “results” as opposed to being remunerated for her labour. The following suggests that cannot be correct:
    (a) the Tribunal can accept there was a practical benefit in using standard codes for tasks performed by dental practitioners on a patient and assigning charging codes to them. However, the fact that Ms Ganora recorded such codes for tasks performed does not automatically mean she was engaged under the contract to provide a contract for services or a result. It also does not necessarily mean she was compensated under the contract per task performed;

    (b) in fact, under the contract, Ms Ganora was not directly remunerated for fees charged for tasks or per job completed, but instead was paid a percentage commission calculated on the basis of 40% of “fees paid” for treatments coded to her practitioner number;

    (c) Ms Ganora and Dr Braunston told the Tribunal they submitted monthly invoices to the Applicant for their fees based on the payment mechanism but understood that:

    • - Dr Saade had the ultimate authority to apply add backs for a claimed payments for a dental practitioner where discounts had been applied;[97] and
    • - Dr Saade or Jamie Saade could amend dental practitioner invoices based on their discretion, if they otherwise decided certain amounts should not be paid (e.g. where there were customer complaints);[98]
  4. Dr Saade contended that any adjustments he directed to be made to the invoices presented were made by way of agreement between him and the affected dental practitioner. Ms Ganora and Dr Braunston’s evidence contradicted that contention. Dr Braunston’s records in particular showed that Dr Saade had directed adjustments be made to an invoice before paying.[99]
  5. Dr Saade told the Tribunal that the dental practitioners “bore the risk” if substandard services were applied.[100] The evidence for this appeared to be policies drafted by Dr Saade and issued to dental practitioners to follow on 15 May 2018 and 24 September 2018. The Tribunal cannot that is wholly correct because Dr Saade held the discretion whether to include certain fees charged and paid in the Applicant’s calculation for remuneration to a practitioner[101] and also because the contract provided for the Applicant and Ms Ganora to share that risk via the payment mechanism.
  6. Dr Saade also told the Tribunal that the dental practitioners “bore the risk” if patients cancelled their appointments. Once again, this is not correct because the fee mechanism meant both Ms Ganora and the Applicant would bear the cost of a patient cancelling their appointment without paying a missed appointment fee in the ratio of 40/60.
  7. The Applicant submitted that Ms Ganora was not paid for her physical time spent at the clinic and this was a significant factor in determining whether she was engaged for her labour or to produce a result. In JMC Pty Ltd, the Court observed mode of payment may not always be determinative of the question of whether a person is engaged for their labour. For example, in Hatfield Plumbing Pty Ltd, Mr Hatfield was paid on a set hourly basis and for an estimated number of hours per day but was not found to be an employee under s 12(3) Superannuation Act.
  8. The Tribunal is satisfied there were practical reasons why a charging mechanism derived from fees paid might be used to calculate a dental practitioner’s remuneration. The profession generally billed for fees coded on a task by task basis so it would not be surprising to see commission payments used as a mechanism to calculate fees. However, the Tribunal is not persuaded this fact alone should determine Ms Ganora’s status.
  9. The Tribunal does not accept as correct, the suggestion that the Applicant only needed to review the billings supporting Ms Ganora’s invoices to check its 60% service fees. The contemporaneous records revealed that Dr Braunston’s invoices were subjected to detailed scrutiny by Dr Saade and the Tribunal accepts the outcome directly impacted monthly payments to Dr Braunston.
  10. Ultimately, what the evidence showed was that Ms Ganora could control the fees she entered on the system when treating a patient, but the amount of commission ultimately paid to her at the end of each month was a percentage calculated from those fees and the size of the payment she received was subject to the Applicant’s discretion.[102]

The discount protocol

  1. Dr Saade, Ms Ganora and Dr Braunston all gave evidence about the Applicant’s discount protocol with which Ms Ganora was also required to comply.[103] A copy of a discount protocol was in evidence.[104] The Tribunal does not accept as correct, Dr Saade’s evidence that the Applicant never had any discount protocols.[105] It does accept some of the discounts applied by the clinic were imposed by reception e.g. for new patients and children but others (e.g. family discounts) were applied at the discretion of the practitioner. Dr Saade told the Tribunal and it accepts that he could apply “his own” discounts and could and did approve add backs for the dental practitioners where discounts had been applied.[106]
  2. Ms Ganora told the Tribunal, and it also accepts, that she occasionally performed treatments at Dr Saade’s request for a discount or no cost.[107] She also told the Tribunal that friends and family receiving discounts under the discount protocol would receive an invoice and pay for the discounted treatment direct to the clinic.[108]
  3. The contemporaneous records reveal that discounts were closely reviewed and queried by Jamie Saade each month.[109] The Applicant’s own evidence discloses that whether discounts were the subject of approved add/buy backs would also directly impact the monthly remuneration paid to a dental practitioner.[110]

Clients and public profile

  1. While these issues were not directly addressed under the contract, they indirectly relate to Ms Ganora’s obligations under clauses 4 and 12 of the contract. Ms Ganora told the Tribunal she was not permitted to advertise or promote her services and instead, the clinic advertised her services as a member of the clinic.[111] Dr Braunston also told the Tribunal that she did not advertise her services.[112]
  2. Dr Saade told the Tribunal that Ms Ganora was never directed to wear a uniform (scrubs) with the clinic’s name on them.[113] Ms Ganora disagreed and told the Tribunal that a few years into her engagement with the Applicant, she was directed to purchase scrubs from the Applicant which were branded with the name of the clinic.[114] She said these were added as charge backs by the clinic to her monthly remuneration.[115] Dr Braunston said she was asked, but declined to wear scrubs with the clinic logo on them.[116]
  3. The Tribunal is not satisfied on the evidence that dental practitioners were directed to wear scrubs but is satisfied the Applicant offered them to dental practitioners.
  4. Dr Braunston told the Tribunal that patients were assigned to her by the clinic.[117] She said: “patients would usually be booked in by the reception staff at Dentist & Co to see a dentist or other clinician (depending on the work to be performed), based on the availability of the relevant clinician in their appointment book.”[118] The Tribunal is satisfied that while patients tended to be assigned to dental practitioners, there was flexibility and the option for repeat treatment with one practitioner and patients were ultimately allowed to express a preference.[119] However, Dr Saade’s evidence that he did not know how patients “who elected to be treated by Ms Ganora were introduced to her”[120] was not corroborated by Dr Braunston or the Applicant’s own records[121] and does not seem plausible. In particular, the evidence in the form of extracts from the clinic’s appointment book suggested that the dentists and OHTs would often be scheduled see patients in tandem (in a way which might be familiar to many readers who have six monthly check-ups – dental clean with a check-up by the dentist either before/after or during the dental clean).[122] This supported the view that patients may be assigned to an OHT matched by work days to work in tandem with the dentist.
  5. The parties disagreed about whether the invoices which issued to patients during the relevant period were under the name of the dental practitioner/s or the clinic. This dispute was compounded by the fact that two different management systems had been used by the clinic for invoicing between 2014 and the date of the hearing, resulting in different versions of invoices being produced by the parties.[123] The Tribunal prefers and relies upon the sample invoices exhibited by Ms Ganora to those produced by Dr Saade because Ms Ganora’s samples were contemporaneous and had been collected by her from former patients. These samples included the name of the clinic and its logo and did not identify the dental practitioners by name.[124] Dr Braunston and some emails from Ms Feroz to the Respondent also corroborated this evidence.[125]

Was the contract wholly or principally for Ms Ganora’s labour?

Delegation/subcontracting[126]

  1. The Standard expressly required Ms Ganora to refer management of a patient to another dental practitioner (for example, a dentist) when the care required by that patient fell outside her scope of practice.[127] The Code of Conduct[128] then defined the terms “delegation”, “referral” and “handover” in a professional context:

Delegation involves one practitioner asking another person or member of staff to provide care of behalf of the delegating practitioner while that practitioner retains overall responsibility for the care of the patient or client.[129]

Referral involves one practitioner sending a patient or client to obtain an opinion or treatment from another practitioner. Referral usually involves the transfer (in part) of responsibility for the care of the patient or client

Handover is the process of transferring all responsibility to another practitioner.

  1. The Applicant contended Ms Ganora’s right to “delegate” her work to others to perform counted against “the existence of an employment relationship” such that the contract was not wholly or principally for Ms Ganora’s labour.[130] It used the terms “delegation” and “sub-contracting” interchangeably, suggesting both meant an arrangement contemplating that the work would be performed by someone else.[131]
  2. The parties were at odds on the subject of delegation.[132] Dr Saade told the Tribunal that “each Clinician had the sole responsibility for determining the fee structure for the services they provided to patients”. He said the Applicant did not dictate who undertook Ms Ganora’s work for her[133] and she could decide to have another dental practitioner perform her work on her behalf.
  3. To illustrate, Dr Saade gave evidence more than 30 examples of alleged “shared treatment” by Ms Ganora and other dental practitioners (the scenarios). He illustrated these by referring to the clinic’s records.[134] These, he contended, showed Ms Ganora was free to delegate work to others, including him.
  4. Ms Ganora did not accept that she was free to delegate or sub-contract her work for receipt of payment. She contended there was no evidence that she ever attempted to engage another outside practitioner (e.g. a locum) to perform her work.[135] She told the Tribunal whenever she could not personally attend the clinic or was on leave, she was required to report her absence and another practitioner from the clinic would cover her work or her patients would be rescheduled.[136] She was also sometimes listed to work on a patient in tandem with a dentist – providing the OHT services as part of broader treatment.[137]
  5. Ms Ganora conceded she sometimes sought assistance from or referred a task to a dentist or dental practitioner when professionally required, for example, when a patient was difficult, the work was complex, or time was short.[138] She attempted to explain each of the scenarios raised by Dr Saade in that context with the reservation that she was responding to detailed these examples after many years and without complete access to the clinic’s records.[139]
  6. As previously mentioned, the invoices Dr Saade produced to the Tribunal were inconsistent with the contemporaneous samples Ms Ganora exhibited to her evidence.[140] The Respondent also pointed to a number of inconsistencies between the invoices produced and the reports produced to the Tribunal of Ms Ganora’s examinations and consultations.[141] The Tribunal has treated these records cautiously but notes that Ms Ganora tended not to dispute that she received remuneration based on at least some of the alleged payments described in the invoices produced by Dr Saade. It has had regard to them for that purpose.
  7. The contract was silent on the question of delegation/sub-contracting. Nevertheless, the Applicant contended it did not expressly prohibit it and argued the Tribunal could and should have regard to the conduct of the parties in the scenarios, and in this case should find that their conduct amounted to a variation of the contract which permitted Ms Ganora to freely delegate/sub-contract her work.[142] It even went so far as to suggest that the absence of any express prohibition on delegation in the contract could be significant.[143]
  8. There was no evidence to suggest any variation to the contract was ever discussed and/or agreed between the parties. Nor was there evidence of any mutual understanding that delegation/sub-contracting would be permitted. Against this, the wording of the contract suggested Ms Ganora was to personally perform each of the obligations it set down and clause 9.2 of the contract expressly provided for amendments to its terms and conditions to be made in writing and signed by both parties. In the circumstances, the Tribunal finds it difficult to accept that any variation occurred.
  9. Despite this, it turned its mind to the scenarios – to determine whether they showed the parties intended to permit delegation by their conduct.
  10. It is not necessary in these reasons to address and make findings on each of the scenarios presented. Collectively, the Tribunal is not persuaded they support the Applicant’s contention that Ms Ganora was delegating/sub-contracting her work in the way contemplated by the authorities. In fact, they had the opposite effect, leaving the Tribunal with the strong impression that Ms Ganora was simply working as a member of an integrated dental practice and acting in each case, consistent with her professional obligations under the Standard, the Guidelines and the Code of Conduct.
  11. Many of the scenarios disclosed nothing more than Ms Ganora (consistent with her written evidence)[144] referring a task which was beyond her skills or qualifications to a dentist or seeking assistance to manage a patient.
  12. To illustrate, the first patient example addressed by Dr Saade, was an invoice issued to a patient LR. Dr Saade said this was an example of “delegation” by Ms Ganora to him. The invoice recorded both Dr Saade (practitioner 1) and Ms Ganora (practitioner 11) as having performed treatments on LR in the same treatment session but only recorded Ms Ganora’s code on the invoice for extracting two teeth. Dr Saade suggested the clinical notes showed that “Ms Ganora gave instructions to me to perform the task” (of extracting the second tooth)[145] (emphasis added). Ms Ganora disagreed and told the Tribunal in evidence that she requested that Dr Saade assist because the second tooth extraction was difficult and the patient was distressed. She pointed out that only a dentist could administer gas so she called on Dr Saade and once he administered the gas, he was able to extract the second tooth.[146]
  13. The clinical notes corroborate Ms Ganora’s account of this treatment rather than Dr Saade’s evidence that she “instructed him” to undertake the work on LR.[147] The Tribunal prefers and relies on these records. In particular, it is satisfied that the notes show Ms Ganora seeking assistance from Dr Saade rather than delegating or sub-contracting her work to him to perform on her behalf for payment.[148]
  14. It was an undisputed fact that the whole fee for both tooth extractions for LR was charged to Ms Ganora’s practitioner code. Dr Saade told the Tribunal that Ms Ganora was responsible for entering billing codes for the tasks she performed for a patient on the billing system to generate an invoice for the patient to pay at the end of the treatment session.[149] The suggestion was that she was receiving a fee for services he had provided on her behalf.
  15. Ms Ganora acknowledged the invoice recorded both extractions under her practitioner code.[150] However, she noted that it was not possible for her to create split invoice entries (recording both practitioners’ names for a treatment).[151] She could not explain why Dr Saade did not charge for the extraction in the case of LR.
  16. Dr Braunston separately told the Tribunal that where two dental practitioners worked on the same patient during a session, each would enter treatment codes for the tasks they performed for charging purposes.[152] She explained her approaches to billing where one practitioner needed to call upon another to assist as follows:[153]

There were often occasions where an OHT or another dentist would request my help. If I considered at the time that I was only assisting them with their treatment then I would not input a separate treatment code for the work performed by me. However, if I needed to take over the treatment and the patient also came to my treatment room, then I would separately input the relevant treatment codes for the portion of the work undertaken by me.

[emphasis added]

  1. Dr Braunston’s observation was plausible. As the senior practitioner and clinical leader, she could choose whether to input a code for work she undertook when assisting an OHT and she might choose not to charge unless she had to take over the patient for the other practitioner. This was a very different and explanation to Dr Saade’s and could have explained the absence of a charge on the invoice for LR. Dr Braunston’s explanation was supported by the evidence that treatment codes could not be split between dental practitioners. In the circumstances, the Tribunal accepts and relies on Dr Braunston’s evidence and prefers it to Dr Saade’s explanation for this and the other scenarios which involved him.
  2. At this point, two things should be noted about Ms Feroz’s evidence. She spoke about being able to delegate her work in the following ways. First, she gave evidence about referring work because it was beyond her scope of practice.[154] Second, and relatedly, she gave examples or referring a patient to a specialist outside the clinic.[155] There is no suggestion in Ms Feroz’s evidence that she was paid for the resulting work undertaken by the outside specialist. The Tribunal is not persuaded either of these were examples of delegation or sub-contracting for payment.
  3. Each of the 33 scenarios presented by Dr Saade were based on different facts. Some examples concerned instances where for example, Ms Ganora had decided to call upon another OHT or hygienist (e.g. to calm or reassure a patient),[156] while others (such as the example of LR), were examples where she had made the decision to call upon a dentist. A number appeared to be cases where the patients were children and were distressed or were proving difficult to manage.[157] Ms Ganora acknowledged where the invoices showed entries against her practitioner code.
  4. The Tribunal having heard and considered the evidence, is not satisfied they show that Ms Ganora delegated her work.

Findings on delegation

  1. For the following reasons, the Tribunal is not persuaded the evidence demonstrates that Ms Ganora was permitted under the contract or by her conduct to operate of her own free will to delegate/sub-contract her work to others to perform on her behalf:
    (a) the Tribunal is satisfied the wording of the contract contemplated personal performance of the contract by Ms Ganora;

    (b) the Tribunal is also satisfied, the contract established a master and servant arrangement between Ms Ganora and the Applicant;

    (c) the Tribunal accepts the contract contemplated “delegation” in the professional sense imposed by the Standard, Scope and the Code of Conduct, but is not persuaded these professional obligations meant Ms Ganora had the freedom to choose to delegate/subcontract her obligations to another person of her choice in the way contemplated by the legal authorities;

    (d) there was no evidence that the parties ever mutually agreed to amend the contract to permit delegation/sub-contracting of the type contended for by the Applicant and in any event, clause 9.2 of the contract required written agreement by the parties to amend;[158]

    (e) the evidence does not establish conduct by the parties which would support a finding that they effectively agreed to vary the contract by their conduct to permit delegation/sub-contacting but even if it did, the Tribunal does not accept that an amendment of this significance would not operate to affect the mode of performance of the contract;[159]

    (f) there are plausible alternate explanations for the assistance colleagues provided to Ms Ganora in the scenarios presented to the Tribunal, including that the dental practitioners were simply assisting each other consistent with their professional and ethical obligations. The Tribunal prefers and relies upon these to Dr Saade’s evidence and interpretation of events; and

    (g) even though Ms Ganora may have charged for the whole of particular treatments, there was no guarantee under the contract that she would ultimately be compensated for 40% of the items she entered on an invoice. First, the patient had to agree to pay the invoice as rendered and second, any invoice and was subject to review by Dr Saade who could amend the items claimed.

  2. In summary, the Tribunal is satisfied Ms Ganora was at all relevant times operating as part of an integrated professional team, making clinical decisions with other the dental practitioners in terms of treatment of the clinic’s patients. It is not persuaded Ms Ganora had the right under the contract or by her conduct to delegate/sub-contract her work to other parties while still collecting remuneration under the contract.[160]
    Was the contract wholly or principally for Ms Ganora’s labour?
  3. For the following reasons, the Tribunal is satisfied and finds that the contract between Ms Ganora and the Applicant was wholly or principally for Ms Ganora’s labour:
    (a) reading the contract as a whole, its purpose was not to provide use of the clinic and its facilities to Ms Ganora for a fee but instead was to engage her to work personally as a member of an integrated dental health team at the clinic. In return for her work as an OHT, Ms Ganora was remunerated under the contract;

    (b) the contract by its terms established a master and servant relationship between the Applicant and Ms Ganora and obliged her to personally serve the Applicant and its interests as opposed to her advancing her own business interests. It also permitted the Applicant to terminate Ms Ganora’s engagement, based on her conduct, irrespective of any treatment she provided, creating an asymmetrical relationship between the parties;[161]

    (c) in any event, Ms Ganora was part of a regulated profession and was not permitted at the time to practise independently of a structured dental practice;

    (d) whether Ms Ganora could freely delegate/sub-contract her services to others is an important factor in determining whether the contract was for her labour. The Tribunal has found that it is not persuaded on the evidence that she could delegate her work in the way contended for by the Applicant; [162]

    (e) Ms Ganora was remunerated under the contract on a commission basis and paid a monthly amount calculated using a mechanism derived fees paid on items she charged for tasks she completed while treating patients subject to add backs, discounts applied as part of the Applicant’s review of her invoice. She was not directly rewarded by fees resulting from those services. Instead, she was rewarded by commission paid monthly for the service she provided, as an OHT;

    (f) Ms Ganora did not make any capital contribution as part of the contract;[163]

    (g) while the Tribunal accepts that Ms Ganora only received 40% of the calculation derived from fees attributable to her practitioner code, the Applicant has not established that part/the whole of the remaining 60% was a service fee payment. The contract did not impose a service fee and did not provide for a service fee payment. It also did not guarantee a service fee payment by Ms Ganora for use of the premises, regardless of her earnings each month.

DISPOSITION

  1. In this application, the Applicant bore the onus of satisfying the Tribunal that the Respondent’s assessment of SGC for the relevant years (the assessment) was excessive and what the correct assessment should have been.[164]
  2. To be successful, the Applicant needed to demonstrate that Ms Ganora was not an “employee” of the Applicant by virtue of s 12(3) Superannuation Act.
  3. For the reasons stated, the Tribunal is satisfied on the evidence that Ms Ganora was an “employee” of the Applicant by virtue of s 12(3) Superannuation Act during the relevant years.
  4. In light of that finding, the Tribunal is not satisfied that the assessment was excessive. It follows that the Applicant has not discharged its statutory onus under s 14ZZK TAA 53.
  5. For these reasons, the Respondent’s objection decision is affirmed.
