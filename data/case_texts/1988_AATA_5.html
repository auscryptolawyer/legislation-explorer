Re Taxation Appeals [1988] AATA 5 (6 January 1988)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

Nos. AT86/341-344, 346 and 350

AAT No. 4056

Taxation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

R.K. Todd (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Taxation - whether carrying on a business - primary production - quarter-horses - cattle

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936

Tweddle v. F.C. of T. [1942] HCA 40; (1942) 2 AITR 360

Case P14 82 ATC 62

F.C. of T. v. Walker 85 ATC 4179

Case U179 87 ATC 1031

HEARING

CANBERRA

6:1:1988

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the objection decisions under review.

DECISION

In this review the question for the Tribunal to decide is whether in the years of income the subject of the review (fiscal 1976 to fiscal 1981 inclusive) the applicant company M Pty Ltd was carrying on a business of primary production. In those years the income of the applicant in respect of the activities in question was as follows:

Income Claimed Expenses

1976 $250 $ 609

1977 $250 $1603

1978 $ 72 $1163

1979 $303 $1195

1980 $750 $ 993

1981 Nil $ 631

2. The Commissioner assessed the applicant on the basis that the figures set out above under the column "Income" were not assessable income, and that the amounts set out under the column "Claimed Expenses" were not allowable deductions. In fiscal 1974 and fiscal 1975 small amounts of income had been accepted as assessable income. The case does not therefore involve a large claim, but as always in such cases that does not necessarily make it any easier to resolve.

3. The representative of, and the witness for, the applicant, is a practising accountant. I shall call him X. He lives on the 10.81 ha property upon which the activities in question are carried out, and has his accountancy practice in a rural city some 5 km away. M Pty Ltd became a family company in about 1973, and at that stage it acted as a trustee of a land development for which it received a small fee. In subsequent years M Pty Ltd made loans to friends of X and the net income derived was returned as income. In 1975 X and his wife purchased the property where they now live.

4. In 1975, X said , his children, none of whom are interested in riding or, specifically, in pony clubs, put to him the idea that money could be made by rearing quarter-horses for the Sydney market, selling off lesser quality progeny to local pony club members. The children were shareholders in M Pty Ltd. Like them, X was not interested in horse-riding as such, but he interested himself in the idea and consulted a person who was knowledgeable in the matter. This person advised (see Ex.A) that the idea was feasible, and proposed a buying strategy. He attended a quarter-horse sale with A and on behalf of M Pty Ltd purchased a foundation mare that was in foal to a proven quarter-horse stallion, and also a "first cross filly unclassified but of superior conformation". The applicant said that the idea was that if the pregnant mare produced a filly, the company would have two first-cross fillies from which they could breed. If that did not happen, the purchased filly, when classified, could be got in foal and they could eventually breed up to full blood quarter-horses.

5. X had clearly taken good advice to this extent, that prices on the day were paid for the mare and for the quarter-horse filly. The mare cost $1,000, and the filly $850, but associated costs took the total outlay to around $2,000. The mare had the foal, which was sold in fiscal 1980 for $1,000, but the mare died in fiscal 1978, $1,000 insurance being received in respect thereof. The filly's problem was more complicated. It grew handsomely lengthways but not upwards. In the result she was not tall enough to be classified and as a result is useless for breeding quarter-horses. She has later produced offspring from a stock horse stallion.

6. Some cattle have also been run on the property. X said that he had been advised by experienced farmers in the district that a property of that size should not have only horses or cattle run on it. The running of only one type of animal would "tend to turn the land sour". Principally however the cattle were considered to be "pretty well immediate income" which would produce a return before the horses had been reared, broken in and handled. The first cattle, a Jersey cow, was purchased in fiscal 1979, in calf to a Black Angus. The calf was born in that year and was later sold for a good price. In the 1981 year 2 cows and 5 calves were acquired. X said that the plan was "only to try and breed up". He did not think that the property would carry more than six or seven cattle, but that it might carry eight or nine cows if some horses were disposed of: "But we are very lucky", he said, "we have a very friendly bull next door which is a very good quality Hereford and ... I would like to get up to some good quality Herefords".

8. In his evidence X made the point that it was only depreciation of equipment on the property, which had been allowed as a deduction, and mistaken readings of the water meter, that had led the activities in question into recording a loss. The water supply is on the town's supply, and for some time the meter reader had adopted a system of driving past properties and making his own assessment of water use rather than reading meters. After he was discovered and dismissed, M Pty Ltd had not had to pay the excess water content of the account which it had always paid. But for depreciation and excess water there would have been a cash surplus.

9. The applicant has never had to purchase fodder for the animals run on the property. No animal has been killed for rations, nor have any of the horses been used for recreational purposes.

10. On the other side of the coin, Miss Field, who appeared for the Commissioner, elicited in cross-examination of X, and in examination-in-chief of Mr. Armstrong, who is a qualified rural valuer whom she had called to give evidence on behalf of the Commissioner, certain features of the applicant's conduct of its activities which were suggested by her to be inconsistent with a finding that a business was being carried on during the relevant years. These were that there was no organised plan of breeding or stock management; and that the following features were absent:

(i) Stables and Stock Control Yards

On the question of stabling X said that the

equable climate of the area meant that stables

were unnecessary, and he pointed to the fact that

a high quality trotting establishment in the area

managed without stabling.

(ii) Grain Handling Stores

As already pointed out, X said that the animals

had never had to be fed and thus such stores were

unnecessary.

(iii) Further Purchases of Stock

To this X said that capital had been lacking.

(iv) Subdivisional Fencing

11. Cases such as these are not easy to determine. It is easy enough to say that this or that should have been done by a person carrying on a business of primary production or indeed any business. Yet Tweddle v. F.C. of T. (1942) 2 AITR 360 tells us effectively that misplaced faith in a business does not make it cease to be a business, and it is trite law that it is not for the Commissioner to tell someone how to run a business. In Case P14 82 ATC 62 Dr. P. Gerber (now a Senior Member of this Tribunal) at p 63A emphasised again that "whether a man is or is not carrying on a business is, ultimately, a question of fact ...". He went on to set out the factors to be considered as follows:

"9. Doing the best I can with such tried old

warhorses as Thomas 72 ATC 4094, Martin (1952-53)

[1953] HCA 100; 90 CLR 470 and Ferguson 79 ATC 4261, the

following indicia seem to emerge:

(i) 'A man may carry on business although he

does so in a small way' (Thomas)

(ii) 'The test is both subjective and

objective: it is made by regarding the nature

and extent of the activities under review, as

well as the purpose of the individual engaging

in them ... the determination is eventually

based on the large or general impression

gained' (Martin).

(iii) 'If the transactions which go to make up

the activity or operations of the taxpayer have

an element of regularity or repetitiveness,

this factor assists in concluding that the

taxpayer is carrying on a business rather than

indulging in a recreational or hobby activity'

(Ferguson)."

12. I have come to the conclusion that on the whole and on the footing of "the large or general impression gained" M Pty Ltd's activities should not, notwithstanding the attractive manner in which X presented the case for the applicant, be seen as constituting the carrying on of a business during the relevant years. I have not overlooked the decision in F.C. of T. v. Walker 85 ATC 4179, the case of the single Angora goat, but the evidence in the present case offers major obstacles to a decision in favour of the applicant. The very small scale of the applicant's operations is the first such obstacle. In the case of the cattle the operation was minute, but the number of horses carried was also very small. But the greater, and fatal, obstacle is that in my opinion the most that can be said is that the applicant was in the relevant years merely preparing to carry on a business. It was "an operation which has not yet become a business and is still in a preparatory stage ...": see Case U179 87 ATC 1031. The horse-breeding business was based on a plan which, having failed, was not replaced. Instead it was ultimately decided to move the business towards the raising of cattle, but in the relevant years this had not reached a point at which it could be said that the business had passed beyond a preparatory stage having regard to the scale of the cattle-raising operations.

13. In all the circumstances the Tribunal must affirm the objection decisions under review.
