DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION [1999] AATA 820

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No VT1998/285

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re PAUL WILLIAM BOTT and DIANE MARGARET BOTT Trading as HOUSE OF KRAFTWERKZ

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



DECISION

Tribunal Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member



Date 29 October 1999

Place Melbourne

Decision The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review and in its stead finds that the relevant goods are exempt from Sales Tax under Item 115 of Schedule 1 of the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1992 and Item 68 of the First Schedule to the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935. The Tribunal certifies that proceedings have terminated in a manner favourable to the applicant.



...... (Sgd) B. H. Pascoe........

Senior Member

CATCHWORDS

SALES TAX – jewellery and artefacts in silver, gold and/or alloys of precious metals – whether works of art – whether exempt

Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1992

Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Murray 90 ATC 4182

Case W25 89 ATC 265

Harrington v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 74 ATC 4143

Cuisenaire v Reed (1963) VLR 719

George Hensher Ltd v Restawile Upholstery (Lancs) Ltd (1976) AC 64

Thomson Australian Holdings Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 88 ATC 4916

REASONS FOR DECISION



29 October 1999 Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member

1. This is an application to review a decision of the respondent to disallow an objection against a decision to refuse a claim for refund of sales tax. The claim for refund was made on 17 October 1995 in relation to sales tax paid on sales of goods in the period from 8 June 1992 to 30 May 1995. The goods were described as "art pieces made with gold, silver, semi precious and precious gems". It was claimed that the goods were exempt from sales tax under Item 115 of Schedule 1 of the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1992 ("the 1992 Act") and Item 68 of the First Schedule to the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935 ("the 1935 Act").

2. At the hearing the applicants, Paul and Diane Bott (trading as the House of Kraftwerkz) were represented by Mr P. Senserrick, an accountant, and the respondent by Mrs J. Batrouney of counsel. Evidence was given on behalf of the applicants by Mr Paul Bott, Mr M. Levy, an art director and designer and Mr T. Kean, an art and craft teacher and a gold and silversmith. The respondent called Ms T, a jeweller and University lecturer in metal/jewellery, Ms J, a gold and silversmith and lecturer, and Mr H, a curator of contemporary art at a State Gallery. At the request of the respondent and with no objection by the applicant, the Tribunal directed that the identity of the three witnesses for the respondent be confidential.

3. Mr Paul Bott is the designer and manufacturer of individually handcrafted fine art jewellery pieces in silver, gold and/or alloys of precious metals. These pieces frequently have precious and/or semi-precious gems set therein and some use enamel colouring. The goods in question were mainly pendants, bracelets, earrings or rings but included other artefacts for display as distinct from pieces to be worn. Mr Bott is a qualified goldsmith and silversmith and a member of the Gold and Silversmiths Guild of Australia. Mr and Mrs Bott have operated as a partnership from premises at Sorrento, Victoria, for some eight years. Most of the goods have been on display at the premises at which goods from other Guild members are also displayed. Over many years, pieces created by Mr Bott have been exhibited at other galleries and exhibitions. The income of the partnership is derived primarily from the sale of goods manufactured by Mr Bott and from commissions from the sale of pieces held on consignment from other Guild members.

4. On 3 March 1994, Mr Senserrick, on behalf of the partnership requested confirmation from the respondent that the goods produced and sold by the partnership were always-exempt goods under Item 115, Schedule 1 of the 1992 Act. This Item exempts "works of art". After further correspondence and discussions, the respondent advised by letter of 2 August 1994 that the goods were not considered works of art and, therefore, not exempt under Item 155 but taxable under Item 3, Schedule 5. After a deal of further correspondence, the applicants lodged a claim for refund of sales tax paid on 8 June 1995. This claim was disallowed on 6 November 1995. An objection was lodged on 11 December 1995 and substantively disallowed on 7 September 1998. It is somewhat difficult to understand why it required nearly three years to determine the objection.

5. At the hearing photographs of 34 pieces of jewellery and 4 artefacts were produced and accepted by both parties as properly representative of the goods in dispute. Some actual pieces were produced for inspection but not retained by the Tribunal as exhibits, given their intrinsic value. The three expert witnesses called by the respondent had based their evidence on viewing photographs of pieces of jewellery.

6. Schedule 5 of the 1992 Act includes as taxable goods such things as luxury motor cars, fur skins, watches, cameras, video equipment etc. Items 3 and 5 of Schedule 5 state (as far as they are relevant to this matter):

"ITEM 3:

(1) Jewellery, imitation jewellery and personal adornments.

(2) Precious stones and semi-precious stones, pearls (including cultured pearls, reconstructed pearls and synthetic pearls), imitation, reconstructed or synthetic stones.

(3) This Item does not cover goods of a kind ordinarily used as parts for clothes for human wear.

...

ITEM 5:

(1) Goods consisting principally of a precious metal or precious metals...

(2) Plated ware plated with precious metal (other than silver)

(3) Goods made of rolled gold.

(4) Gold-filled goods.

(5) ..."

Schedule 1 of the 1992 Act lists goods which are exempt. Item 115 in Chapter 12 of this Schedule states simply:

"ITEM 115:

Works of art. "

Other Items in Chapter 12 are museum exhibits and imported paintings, collectors pieces and antiques. Equivalent Items in the 1935 Act are Items 1 and 2 of the Second Schedule and Item 68(2) of the First Schedule. The only significant difference is that the exemption in the 1935 Act was limited to works of art "produced in Australia or produced abroad by Australian artists". Nothing in this case turns on the difference of wording between the two Acts.

7. Mr Bott gave evidence that, after an arts based education and a period of painting and sculpture, he resolved to work with metals as he considered that this medium more appropriately satisfied his creative need. He studied gold and silversmithing, predominantly with jewellery. Some of the pieces produced are commissioned by a buyer but many are produced for his own reasons. He maintained that all pieces are original designs and, where a buyer commissions a piece similar to some other piece of jewellery, he, at best, will produce his own version of the suggested style. He stated that all pieces created by him have some meaning, frequently a political or social comment, although he does not always explain that meaning. His intention was said to be to create something beyond the common realm. Mr Bott said that he opened his own gallery as his work was not suited to wholesaling or mainstream jewellery outlets. Whilst he accepted the need to sell his pieces in order to continue his work, he maintained that his primary aim was to fulfil a need to create and to prove something. He said that he was able to alloy metals to create different colours, strengths and varying carats which are unavailable for purchase otherwise. In relation to one piece (Photograph No. 23) which was a gold fob chain, Mr Bott accepted that some would see it as a reproduction but said that he had made it to prove to himself that he could, that it provided a link to the past and was an experiment in a different use of links such as the used of a "Birmingham Knot".

8. Mr Levy gave evidence that he was an art director for a major commercial organisation responsible for creative and graphic art. He has known Mr Bott for some 20 years and regarded him as an artist. He expressed the view that an artist who creates his own work without support, irrespective of the medium used and in non-commercial quantities produces works of art.

9. Mr Kean, a gold and silversmith and an art and craft teacher, gave evidence that he had known Mr Bott for some 10 years and displays and sells some of his own work at the House of Kraftwerkz gallery. In his view any piece created individually by a person using artistic talent is a work of art. He used the analogy of music. The question, in his view, was not whether a person judges the music as good or bad but whether it is music. He believed that Mr Bott was an artist rather than a jeweller, with the articles he produces reflecting a creative approach and artistic intent being no different, in essence, from painting or drawing.

10. Ms T was of the opinion that a work of art is something which contributes to, or comments on, the society in which it is produced. She believed that jewellery may be classified as art if it does this or if it challenges the parameters of what jewellery is. She said that it is not enough to say something is beautifully or skilfully made, it must contribute more than that and to be art more than craft. She was of the view that only relatively few jewellers could properly call themselves artists. Two examples she gave of works of art were Otto Kurzli's work "Chain" and a brooch designed by Robert Baine as part of a project concerning brooches worn by U.S. Secretary of State, Madeleine Albright. The first was a necklace made from 48 old wedding rings collected by the artist. The second was said to be a personal statement about the makers attitude to Canberra and demonstrated his exceptional skill as a goldsmith, particularly in filigree and granulation techniques, covered by a powder coating of red lacquer which conceals the fine gold work from the viewer. In her opinion, the jewellery of Mr Bott ought not to be classified as works of art but as jewellery in a very known context using metal and stones in a very conventional sense and in known combinations.

11. Ms J was of the opinion that, for a piece of jewellery to be a work of art, it had to explore new ideas, practice new techniques or expose the inner thoughts of the artist. She believed that only relatively few of the pieces made by her are works of art in the sense that they come from within rather than in response to consumer demand. Ms J did not express an opinion on whether any of Mr Bott's work could be described as a work of art. She agreed with Ms T that the Kurzli "Chain" and the Baine brooch were works of art. She added to the list a Jewish ceremonial ring which she had made and which is now in the Jewish museum and a brooch in her possession made by a West Australian artist using welded dental stainless steel.

12. Mr H gave evidence that he was responsible at his Gallery for the acquisition of contemporary metal work and jewellery. He was of the view that the work of Mr Bott, while containing a degree of skill in their execution, did not represent the conceptual depth and innovation that he looks for when considering works of contemporary art under the term of jewellery. He said that, in assessing whether an item is a work of art, he would take into account matters such as whether any innovative concept was evident in the piece, the use of materials in a completely new and unexpected manner, the way an object could be worn or relate to the body and whether the design might be interesting or new. He considered that it was the conceptual concept of a piece of jewellery, its aesthetic merit, the use of materials and the skill and craft that had gone into the production of the piece that might make it so conceptually rich and new that it ought to be classified as a work of art. He was of the opinion that the pieces made by Mr Bott, although skilfully crafted, were not works of art. He considered them each a piece of mainstream jewellery. He considered much, but not all, of the work of Robert Baine to be works of art including the brooch referred to by Ms T and Ms J. He felt that his innovative use of contemporary materials such as pieces of Coca Cola cans and bicycle reflectors in relation to gold and silver as a comment on the history of jewellery and how it relates to the contemporary world.

13. The three experts called by the respondent took a very similar view of what constitutes a work of art. Each required a work, to satisfy that description, to make a comment on contemporary life or to be innovative in the use of materials. When asked their views in relation to another art medium, painting, each was of the view that a painter of the realism school who produces landscapes or portraits does not produce a work of art unless there is some innovative methods or use of materials. Mr H did not consider paintings by the artist Pro Hart as works of art because he has pursued the same subject matter throughout the course of his career and the paintings do not tell Mr H anything about a genre, a particular style of painting or a particular way of treating the landscape and the outback as a subject. He believed that the conceptual depth and contemporary relevance makes a work of art, not skill.

14. For the applicants it was not submitted that the goods made by Mr Bott were works of art and exempt from sales tax. It was said that he had a long experience, a passion for creativity particularly in gold and silver and had an intention in relation to each piece to produce something to gratify the aesthetic emotions. It was argued that it was the intention of the artist not the judgement of others which separated a work of art from commercial jewellery.

15. For the respondent it was submitted that the goods in issue do not constitute works of art. The evidence of the three witnesses by the respondent was relied upon in the argument that a work of art embodies a comment on society, the exploration of a concept or the extension of the borderlines of jewellery beyond the traditional expectations of the genre. It was said that the goods in issue are clearly and with particularity included in a taxing Schedule whereas the Item said to confer exemption, if it refers to them at all, refers to them by broad and inappropriate (rather than particular and apt) description. As such, it was said to be inappropriate to decide that the goods were covered by the exemption Item. The respondent did not argue that jewellery which can be worn as adornment cannot be a work of art. However, it was said that there is a clear distinction between mainstream jewellery created for use as conventional adornment and jewellery or artefacts created as works of art.

16. The major difficulty with this case is that sales tax is a self-assessing tax. Consequently, a taxpayer when selling goods needs to know whether the goods are subject to sales tax and, if so, at what rate. The Courts and Tribunal over many years have been faced with determining whether particular goods fall within a classification so as to be taxable at a particular rate or exempt from sales tax. Some which immediately come to mind include the meaning of "pastry", "adaptor", "wall-paper", "goods of a kind ordinarily used for household purposes" and "catalogues or price lists". This case raises an even more difficult issue. Here, the taxpayer has to be in a position to know at the time of sale of a piece of jewellery or artefact whether it is a "work of art". In addition, an officer of the respondent who may audit the taxpayer's sales tax returns or may be required to determine a request for an assessment under section 102 of the Sales Tax Assessment Act 1992 could be required to determine whether a specified dealing related to a work of art. As Hill J said in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Murray 90 ATC 4182 (at page 4196):

"The idea that the question whether a particular item is a work of art is one primarily to be determined by an assessor of the taxation office fits ill with such a legislative scheme. The taxpayer must be able to determine his own liability. Considerable penalties are imposed if a taxpayer fails to lodge returns of all taxable transactions and to pay the tax appropriate to those transactions."

The respondent and each of the respondent's witnesses accepted that jewellery can be a work of art and included in the exemption. Each of the respondent's witnesses believed that some pieces created by some well-known gold and silversmiths were works of art but not necessarily all pieces . Ms J said that she would classify some of the pieces she had made as works of art. Each of the witnesses accepted that Mr Bott may create a work of art but that the pieces which they had reviewed were not. How then does this taxpayer determine whether and which pieces are taxable or exempt and when? Is it the time that the concept came to mind, when it is completed, or when it is sold?

17. This case raises issues similar to those considered by the Full Court of the Federal Court in Murray's case (supra). There the Court was concerned with whether individual pieces of hand-made and hand-glazed pottery platters and vases were works of art and exempt from sales tax under Item 68(2) of the First Schedule of the 1935 Act. The matter was determined initially in favour of the taxpayer by Deputy President Todd in this Tribunal in a decision reported as Case W25 89 ATC 265. The decision was appealed and heard by a Full Court consisting of Sheppard, Weaver and Hill JJ who dismissed the appeal and confirmed that the pieces in question were works of art.

18. In considering the issue the Court considered dicta in such cases as Harrington v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation 74 ATC 4143, Cuisenaire v Reed (1963) VLR 719 and George Hensher Ltd v Restawile Upholstery (Lancs) Ltd (1976) AC 64. In the decision of Deputy President Todd the test of whether an item is a work of art was formulated as follows:

"(i) The true test is whether the author:

(a) had the main object of creating an article which, while utilitarian, would have aesthetic appeal; or

(b) was, in creating the article, cultivating one of the fine arts with the main object of appealing to aesthetic tastes.

(ii) The expression 'the fine arts' is to be given the wide meaning of any application of skill and taste to the production of articles which are beautiful in themselves or which have an appeal to aesthetic taste.

(iii) The emphasis is on the object of the author in creating the work, rather than on the reaction of the viewer.

(iv) But that is not the sole test. A court will apply an objective test by examination.

(v) If the article appears to fail that objective test, the object of the creator of the work will play a dominant part in resolving the question."

The members of the Court on appeal found no fault with this formulation although Sheppard J made the following comments (at page 4187):

"I am in agreement with propositions (i), (ii) and (iii) but I have some reservation in relation to the way in which propositions (iv) and (v) are phrased. I think that the intention of the creator must be all-important. I think this is established by the circumstance that, whether one is considering the matter in the context of copyright law or revenue law, the article will either be an artistic work or a work of art from the moment it comes into existence. It will not become a work of art at a later point of time if it is not a work of art from the moment it comes into existence. At that time many works of art will not have been seen by anyone except their creator. In any event, one would not attribute to an article the quality of a work of art if its creator did not intend that it should have that quality. Nevertheless I do not think that objective considerations are without substantial importance and significance. It is by making a judgement of the thing itself, of what it purports to be, that one usually determines whether it is a work of art or not. This is a matter, notwithstanding the wide range of objects which are displayed in the art galleries of the world, which occasionally vexes the minds of distinguished connoisseurs of art. But it is the way in which one is struck by a particular object which will very often determine whether or not an object is properly regarded as a work of art. In borderline cases it may be appropriate to enquire of the creator what his or her intention was. In those cases one's own objective reaction to the article itself may provide a check against accepting, for example, the gimmick referred to (pp. 92, 94) by Lord Simon in his speech in the Restawile case as a work of art. More than this I do not think one can say because the more one says the more one substitutes other and different words for what is a plain English expression. If this happens, one is likely to be led away from giving to that expression its true meaning."

Hill J, who delivered the leading judgement said (at page 4196):

"The proper test to determine whether an item is a work of art is both objective and subjective. The item in question must be one which is capable of being a work of art in itself. It will not, merely because it is also utilitarian, for that reason fail to fall within item 68(2), nor did the Commissioner so submit. In considering the matter objectively it will be relevant to have regard to the person who produced it (was he or did he purport to be an artist?), and the circumstances in which the work was produced (was it a case of mass-production, or did the work have originality?). Production of a number of items in a series such as a set of original lithographs in a series would not necessarily be excluded from item 68(2). Relevant will be whether it is got up and sold as an item of artistic endeavour or whether it is got up and sold for a utilitarian function. Pottery made up for sale as vases per se would not, without more, qualify. In this respect price or value might play a part, although again these matters will not be determinative. The item must also be such as is capable of being seen as produced to gratify the aesthetic emotions.

To define 'art', 'artistic' or aesthetic' (which may be little more than a synonym for artistic in modern usage) and to state the essential quality which distinguishes art from other human activity is a task more appropriate to the philosopher than the jurist."

In relation to this latter comment Sheppard J expressed his respectful agreement with what Lord Simon of Glaisdale said in the Restawile (supra) decision where his Lordship concluded that artistic merit of a particular work was irrelevant and continued (at pages 94-95):

"Not only is artistic merit irrelevant as a matter of statutory construction, evaluation of artistic merit is not a task for which judges have any training or general aptitude. Words are the tools and subject matter of lawyers; but even in matters of literary copyright the court will not concern itself with literary merit."

Later (at page 4187) Sheppard J said:

"I wish to make it abundantly clear that one does not reach a conclusion by judging the artistic merit of the work. All the authorities are to that effect and it would be quite wrong to include artistic merit as a criterion in the exercise."

At page 4197 of his judgement, Hill J said:

"In most cases the application of an objective test will suffice to determine whether a particular work is a work of art. The present is such a case and indeed in applying an objective test the Tribunal found that the items the subject of the assessment were works of art. That question is one of fact for the Tribunal and there was no attack upon it. It clearly was open to the Tribunal to so find.

In some cases however the application of the objective test may leave a Tribunal in doubt. In part this could come about because the assessment of whether an item is capable of being a work of art might involve the fact finder, no doubt unconsciously, applying his own aesthetic values. Aesthetic judgements are not matters for a court (or indeed for a revenue collector) cf. George Hensher Ltd. V. Restawile Upholstery (Lancs.) Ltd (supra). Such doubts may be resolved by an application of the subjective test referred to by Pape J. in Cuisenaire v. Reed (supra). But the significance of the subjective test goes beyond the resolution of doubt. An item created without the intention of making an artistic statement would not be a work of art no matter how artistic it might appear to be. The history of art hoaxes is replete with examples of items admired as works of art but which turned out to be other than what they were said to be. For a work to be a work of art therefore it will be necessary that the 'artist' bringing it into existence intends thereby to make with it an artistic statement.

Where, as in the present case, an object is both utilitarian and artistically pleasing, reference to the purpose of the maker in bringing it into existence will, if the objective test still leaves room for doubt, assist in the resolution of that doubt. The subjective test, however, could not be the sole criterion. A schoolchild might draw figures in the belief that he was creating a work of art whereas the application of the objective test would ordinarily lead to a conclusion that what was produced was the mere scribble of a child and not a work of art."

19. In this case, I was urged by the respondent to accept the view of the three witnesses called by the respondent that the pieces reviewed by them were not works of art but mainstream jewellery. Putting aside for the moment what is meant by "mainstream" jewellery, I have some difficulty in accepting their remarkably similar definitions. The primary difficulty is that the definition involves a subjective judgement by the viewer of an object and excludes many well-recognised artists from producing "works of art" unless each production makes some social comment discernible to the viewer or explores new methods, again in the eye of the beholder. While Ms J believed some of her work constituted works of art, the distinction between those that were and those that were not was very blurred to an observer. In the Restawile case, Lord Kilbrandon said (at page 97):

"Whether a given object is a work of artistic craftsmanship can be posed as a question of fact, but only after the meaning of the word 'artistic' has been determined; what that meaning is, is a question of law, since it involves a decision of what Parliament meant by the word Parliament used. I do not believe that it is possible, as matter of law or of exegesis, to arrive at a comprehensive definitive interpretation of such a familiar English word, so as to be armed with a test which will enable one, by the application of it, at a glance, to exclude all that does not properly fall within the scope of the simple word itself. It is, indeed, seldom that a simple word can, by translation into some easier or more difficult phrase, be rendered the more capable of furnishing such a test. But it is quite plain, in my opinion, that you cannot get on without exercising, in any case in which this kind of dispute arises, the judicial function of holding whether the facts bring the object within the meaning of the statutory definition. You will get no assistance, until you have exercised that judicial function, by asking the opinion of an expert; if he says 'I regard that object as artistic', the next question which must be asked in order to make his last answer intelligible is 'What do you mean by artistic?'. That question is incompetent, because the answer would be irrelevant. Since the word is a word of common speech, it requires, and permits of, no interpretation by experts. It is for the judge to determine whether the object falls within the scope of the common meaning of the word."

Experts can provide an opinion as to whether, in their view, a particular piece is a work of art but that opinion is inevitably based on their subjective view of what is "art" or what is "artistic". Mr H was reasonably and, perhaps, inevitably influenced in his view of what works are appropriate for inclusion in a Gallery collection. Both Ms T and Ms J worked in the same medium as Mr Bott with the difficulty of passing judgement on the pieces created by a colleague. Whilst not suggesting that their evidence was not useful to the Tribunal, I am unable to accept it as determinative of the issue. The two witnesses called by the applicants were friends and colleagues of Mr Bott and, again, while providing useful evidence it was limited in assisting to provide an answer.

20. In my view it is clear, in the words of Hill J in Murray's case, that each of the items in dispute "...is capable of being a work of art in itself". There was no dispute that a piece of jewellery or an artefact such as the items in question is capable of being a work of art. If the tests set out by Deputy President Todd and reiterated by the members of the Full Court in Murray's case are considered in relation to the items here, the evidence of Mr Bott produces the following answers:

  1. Did the author have the main object of creating an article which, while utilitarian, would have aesthetic appeal? Yes. Mr Bott maintained that his primary aim in relation to each piece was to fulfil a need to create items of aesthetic appeal not necessarily to meet a consumer need.
  2. Was the author, in creating the article, cultivating one of the fine arts with the object of appealing to aesthetic tastes? It was accepted by all witnesses that the objects in question were inclusive in the fine arts within the meaning adopted in Murray's case and I was left in no doubt that Mr Bott was genuine in his belief that each piece was an original design appealing to his aesthetic tastes and, hopefully, of others.

Sheppard J in Murray's case thought that the intention of the creator must be all important. The objective test referred by their Honours in that case would appear to be satisfied here. I take this test to include that the pieces are capable of being a work of art in themselves, each is capable of being seen as produced to gratify the aesthetic emotions, the person who produced the pieces was an artist, or at a minimum purported to be an artist, each piece was an original piece designed by the creator and it was created, on the evidence of Mr Bott, as an item of artistic endeavour. It is not for the Tribunal to judge whether Mr Bott succeeded fully in that latter requirement.

21. The Tribunal was urged by the respondent to regard the fact that the applicants claimed that every piece made was a work of art and exempt from sales tax as casting doubt on the validity of the claim. It may be said also, that acceptance of the claim by the applicants could lead to the view that any craftsman who considers himself an artist and creates original pieces which are aesthetically pleasing (at least in his or her own view) could claim exemption from sales tax which might be applicable otherwise. I accept that as a possibility but would reasonably assume that that unless the piece is accepted as such by potential buyers the impact on collection of sales tax would be minimal. If the piece is so accepted by potential buyers, it would very likely satisfy the objective test of whether the piece is a work of art. Unfortunately (or, perhaps, just as well for the Tribunal) every piece made by Mr Bott was not referred to at the hearing. Only a number of such pieces were referred to. One of those (Photograph No. 23 of Exhibit R4) may be judged by some as simply a gold fob chain with no particular artistic or aesthetic merit over many mainstream or commercial fob chains. If this had been the only piece in dispute, it may well have been seen as not satisfying the test of a work of art. However, with this piece, there is considerable doubt with the explanation of its genesis and structure from Mr Bott leading more to satisfying the relevant tests. While bearing in mind the concern expressed previously on the requirement of the applicants to determine at the time of self assessment whether a piece is taxable, it is not possible to say at this stage that all pieces manufactured by Mr Bott are or are not works of art. It is possible only to find that the pieces referred to in a hearing of this application satisfy the description of works of art on the tests set out in Murray's case.

22. The respondent submitted that the Tribunal should find that the essential character of the majority of pieces in question was jewellery (Thomson Australian Holdings Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 88 ATC 4916 at 4917). It was said that a taxing Schedule which clearly and with particularity includes goods in issue should be preferred to an Item in a Schedule which refers to them (if at all) by a broad and inappropriate description. It was said, also, that it is relevant to consider the context within which Items are found. "Work of art" is included in Chapter 12 and co-located with museum exhibits, paintings, engravings, sculptures and antiques and it was submitted that jewellery sits uneasily in the context of this range of goods. However, the respondent also accepts, in my view, quite properly that a piece of jewellery can be a work of art within any definition and in line with the evidence of the respondent's own witnesses. I am unable to accept that the exemption should not apply because it is a "broad and inappropriate description". It may well be broad covering a wide range of goods but it cannot be described as inappropriate. Jewellery and artefacts can be works of art and as difficult as it is to determine which pieces are and which pieces are not, it is equally appropriate to such goods as to paintings or sculpture. Consequently, I am satisfied that two particular Items, one a taxing Item and the other an exempting Item, cover the goods in question. The result is that jewellery is taxable under Item 3 of Schedule 5 unless it satisfies the description of "work of art" under Item 115 of Schedule 1. Having found that the goods in question in this case so satisfy that description, they are exempt from sales tax.
