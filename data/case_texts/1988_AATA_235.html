Last Updated: 30 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



Income Tax - sole parent rebate - whether "sole care" - whether "special circumstances"

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, SS.159K(1), 159K(3), 159J(2)

Case M78, 80 ATC 549

Sharma v F.C. of T. 84 ATC 4260

Case T55 86 ATC 423

Case U140 87 ATC 307

Case T56 86 ATC 428

AAT Decision No 4511

Deputy President R.K. Todd

(sgd.) R.K. Todd

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

28 July 1988 Deputy President R.K. Todd

  1. This application sought review of a decision by the Commissioner of Taxation to disallow a claim by the applicant for the sole parent rebate in the year of income ended 30 June 1986 ("fiscal 1986").
  2. The applicant was first married in 1969. She had one child by this marriage and it is for this child that she sought the rebate in fiscal 1986. Her marriage to the father of this child was very traumatic, and it ended in divorce in the mid 1970s with the applicant retaining custody of the child.
  3. In 1979 the applicant remarried. She subsequently had twins and another child with her new husband. The child of her first marriage continued to live with her and her new family. Before the second marriage the applicant discussed with her husband his role in the upbringing of her first child, and in the words of the husband she made it clear that "she wanted to make the decisions regarding [the child]". It was clear from the applicant's evidence that she was a very independent person. She had had a rough first marriage which had left her somewhat scarred and very keen that she should have sole responsibility for the child of that marriage. To that end, she did not consider allowing her second husband to adopt the child. She gave evidence that in the relevant year she made most of the decisions, in respect of the child, that are generally made by a parent of a sixteen year old. She did say however that, as she and her husband and children all lived in the same house, certain matters pertaining to the child were discussed with the husband, that is to say he had some input. The applicant's husband gave evidence that he occasionally took the child to sporting events when the applicant was not able to do so. He also said that, if the child misbehaved at home he would admonish the child just as he would one of his own children. In fact, he said "I do not treat [the child] differently to any other child in the ... family".
  4. The situation concerning finances was somewhat confused. The applicant stated that in fiscal 1986 she did not work full time but rather worked part-time for her husband's company doing minor secretarial work. For her efforts she earned $4,023. In addition to this she also had "independent means" totalling in excess of $19,000. The applicant was asked how the household was financed, and she said that in an earlier year, when a new house was purchased, the applicant and her husband contributed equally to the cost of its purchase as they had done with the purchase of all significant household items. The applicant in her evidence stated that she contributed the amount that she earned from working for her husband's company to the payment of general expenses, such as, inter alia, food and electricity on behalf of her child. Her husband, on the other hand, said that this amount was contributed for the benefit of the family in general. He stated that this sum was a small component of the total household expenses, the rest of which were met by him. As to the rest of the child's expenses such as clothing, pocket money, holidays, outings and the considerable cost of his private school education, the applicant and her husband stated that the applicant paid for these out of her private funds.
  5. The relevant provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 in relation to the sole parent rebate are S.159K(1) and s.159K(3) (and the table contained in S.159J(2)). As was stated by the applicant's representative, the applicant must overcome two hurdles to be eligible for the rebate. First, she must have had "sole care" of the child in the relevant year, and secondly, since she was married in that year, it was necessary for her to establish "special circumstances" making it just for the Commissioner to allow a rebate.

6. The applicant's representative submitted that "sole care" had the meaning attributed to it in Case M78 80 ATC 549. In this case at 552 the following was said:

"In other words, we consider that 'sole care' vests in that person who has the full and unshared responsibility for making decisions on a day-to-day basis as to the child's upbringing and welfare, whether it be the food that he eats, the clothes that he wears or the school that he attends."

  1. It was submitted that this interpretation was to be preferred to that proffered in Sharma v. F.C. of T. 84 ATC 4260. In that case Rogers J. stated that "sole care" meant sole financial responsibility for a child. The applicant's representative argued that there was sufficient evidence to conclude that the applicant did have sole care of the child, as that expression was explained in Case M78.
  2. On the question of "special circumstances" the applicant's representative sought to rely on several factors pertaining to the applicant's situation. The first of these was the trauma that she went through in her first marriage. This marriage had left her with a preoccupation with the independence of spouses in a marriage. Furthermore, the applicant believed that there was an understanding before her second marriage that she would be responsible for the child after marriage to her second husband. Lastly, it was stated that the applicant had been allowed a sole parent rebate in previous years. The applicant sought to rely on Case T55 86 ATC 423, because in that case the requirement of "special circumstances" was met. The applicant in Case T55 had entered into a premarital agreement as to parental responsibilities. The couple had then carried out the agreement so that the applicant financially supported her child totally and took on all other responsibilities involved in his upbringing.

9. The Commissioner contended that any determination of whether a taxpayer has "sole care" requires a finding of fact that the applicant alone has the actual responsibility for a dependant. This, it was said, was to ensure that the rebate is not obtained by a multiplicity of taxpayers. Authority for this proposition was found in Sharma's Case at 4264. In that case there was a finding of fact at 4261 that the taxpayer was "solely responsible for the financial upkeep of his youngest sister". In the present case, the Commissioner submitted that the applicant was not alone responsible for the welfare of her then dependent son. Rather, the evidence here was said to support a similar finding to that made in Case U140 87 ATC 307 by Mr. P.M. Roach, Senior Member. In that case the applicant lived in a de facto situation with a man, whom she later married, and her child of another marriage. The applicant contributed substantially more than one half of the total costs of the household. She was left to be responsible for the "well-being and personal development" of the child. Her de facto husband however did occasionally transport the child to sporting events and went on holidays together with the applicant and the child. In addition he also discussed with the child day-to-day happenings in the child's life. In Case U140 Mr. Roach decided that the applicant did not have "sole care". At 308 he said the following:

"She was not 'alone' in providing for her son. She had the support of a 'spouse' with whom she shared her home and he was not indifferent to the well-being of the boy or indifferent to the applicant's responsibilities and concerns as mother of the boy, although I do accept that he left the burden of responsibility to her; something not at all uncommon in family life. Further, even if 'sole care' only took into account financial responsibility, I am not satisfied that the contributions of the deceased to the household did not ease the financial burden for the applicant of providing for her son and herself."

  1. The Commissioner submitted that here the evidence is stronger still because it does not show clearly that the burden of responsibility on the applicant is anything but extremely light. Case T56 86 ATC 428 was also relied upon. In that case Dr. P. Gerber, Member, as he then was, stated at 428 that where "parties have intermingled - however occasionally - their respective contributions in the running of the common household, the benefit of the section is lost". Here, it was stated, there has also been no precise accounting since the parties have identified themselves as a family unit.
  2. The question of "special circumstances" was then addressed by the Commissioner's representative. The dissenting decision by Dr. G.W. Beck, Member, as he then was, in Case T55 86 ATC 423 was relied upon. In this case Dr. Beck found that there were no "special circumstances" and at 427 said:

"The test I put on the words used in sec.159K(3)(a) is to decide whether the allowance of a rebate is appropriate because the taxpayer is bearing costs of caring for a dependant that are caused, not by a decision that the taxpayer has made of his or her own volition, but because some onerous outside event or 'special circumstance' has made that situation unavoidable. ... The rationale behind the subsection seems to me to be to assist a parent more or less left in the lurch by intentional (e.g. desertion) or unintentional (e.g. accident or illness) action by the spouse."

12. The Commissioner also sought to rely on Case M78 where the No.2 Board of Review stated at 553 that in its opinion -

"...the intent of the Legislature in enacting sec.159K was essentially to provide a rebate to the sole parent taxpayer to assist in maintaining a child where there is an absence of the human supportive role and financial assistance normally forthcoming from the other partner, either because the taxpayer is widowed or divorced, or because the taxpayer has not married."

It was also submitted that if the applicant did not receive the sole parent rebate she would not suffer any hardship. While the applicant gave psychologically based reasons as to why she should be entitled to the rebate, it was submitted by the respondent that Rogers J. in Sharma said that it is financial responsibility upon which attention should be concentrated.

  1. In my opinion the applicant's claim must fail. She did not have the "sole care" of her child in fiscal 1986. As discussed above, Sharma's Case and Case M78 have each interpreted "sole care" in somewhat different ways. In the former case "sole care" was held to mean sole financial responsibility, while in the latter case it was thought to mean "full and unshared responsibility for making decisions on a day-to-day basis" for the child (see at 552). Each decision has received support in subsequent cases. I think it unnecessary however to consider which is the more appropriate interpretation, because I believe that either way the applicant has not proved that she had "sole care" of her child. Applying the Sharma test, which of course, unlike Case M78, has the authority of a decision of a superior court, on the evidence the applicant and her husband intermingled their finances. Both the husband and the applicant made contributions to the household which would have been enjoyed by her son. The applicant therefore did not have sole financial responsibility for the child. She was helped by her spouse. Even if I were to apply the test laid down in Case M78, while the applicant was the main decision-maker in respect of issues concerning the child she did receive input from her husband. They lived as a family unit, and there was therefore bound to be some discussion between all parties living under the same roof in harmonious circumstances. Simply because the applicant had more of a say than her husband does not mean that the applicant had the "full and unshared responsibility" vis-a-vis the child. Often in a family one parent/spouse will have more to say about family matters than the other, especially where one has a stronger will or is more dominant than the other, as may have been the case here.
  2. Section 159K(3) of the Act states that where the taxpayer has a spouse during the year of income then there will be no entitlement to the rebate unless the Commissioner thinks that special circumstances exist which make it just to allow it. Once again, the applicant's evidence failed to convince me that "special circumstances" existed. There was no "absence of the human supportive role and financial assistance normally forthcoming from the other partner" (Case M78 at 553). On the contrary, my impression of the applicant's husband when he gave evidence was that of a caring husband who was interested in the concerns of the applicant and her child. Furthermore, when the husband spoke of the child it was not without affection. The applicant claimed that before the marriage she and her husband agreed that she should be responsible for the child. As discussed above however I do not think that she did have sole responsibility. In any case I would agree with Dr. Beck in Case T56 at 431 that an "agreement by a taxpayer and his or her spouse to separately support one or more dependants cannot of itself create special circumstances". I believe that the facts in this case are very similar to those in Case U140 and, like Mr. Roach, I find that there are no "special circumstances" in this case. I therefore must affirm the Commissioner's decision on the objection.
  3. I would only note finally that it is of no consequence that the rebate had been allowed in the two previous years to the one in question here. It should be observed yet again that where the Commissioner misconstrues the law in the assessment of a particular year he is in no way bound by such a misconstruction in a subsequent year.
  4. For the above reasons the objection decision under review is affirmed.
