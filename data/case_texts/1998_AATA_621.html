CATCHWORDS

TAXATION - private ruling - whether decision should not have been made or should have been made differently

Taxation Administration Act 1953 ss 14ZAN(g), 14ZAN(j), 14ZAS(3), 14ZAZA, 14ZZK(b)(iii)

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss 160L(2)

REASONS FOR DECISION

                   

11 August 1998 Deputy President BJ McMahon

  1. This is an application to review a decision of the respondent to disallow an objection dated 11 July 1995 against a Notice of Private Ruling (‘NPR’) issued on 19 April 1995 in respect of the year ended 30 June 1995.
  2. The application to the Commissioner was made on 15 December 1994. It was in the prescribed form and was accompanied by a good deal of information prepared and presented by the applicant’s tax agent. Essentially, the applicant sought a ruling whether there was a liability to capital gains tax under subsection 160L(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 or whether that section did not apply because the subject property was acquired prior to 20 September 1985. A document headed “Full Description of the Facts” accompanied the application. Considering that further information was required, the Commissioner then entered into a course of correspondence with the applicant’s representatives seeking further details. These were supplied on 4 occasions. In addition to material supplied by the applicant, the respondent himself obtained information and, in particular, copies of a transfer and a mortgage from the Land Titles Office.
  3. Prior to the substantive hearing of this application by the Tribunal, a directions hearing was held at the request of the respondent to deal (inter alia) with the way in which the hearing was to be conducted and, in particular, whether the above material and any other evidentiary material could be considered. I directed that the evidence to be tendered on the hearing was not to be limited to evidence of matters considered by the decision maker.
  4. The respondent appealed against this direction to the Federal Court where I was held to be in error. The judgement of the Full Court is reported under the name of Federal Commissioner of Taxation v McMahon 97 ATC 4986. The court ordered as follows:
‘1. The Tribunal’s decision that it had power when conducting its review to consider facts other than those identified by the Commissioner in his ruling be set aside;

2. The matter be remitted to the Tribunal for the purpose of enabling it to determine the review on the basis that the Tribunal does not have power to consider facts other than those identified by the Commissioner in the private ruling dated 19 April 1995 as constituting the relevant arrangement.

3. There be no order as to costs.’
  1. It is clear from the 3 separate but concurring judgements of the court that, not only does the Tribunal have no power to consider evidence beyond that considered by the decision maker, it has no power to consider any facts other than those identified by the Commissioner in the NPR as constituting the arrangement. This document, and this document only, must therefore be the subject of the present review. It is in the following terms:
‘NOTICE OF PRIVATE RULING

This Ruling is a “Private Ruling” for the purposes of Part IVAA of the Taxation Administration Act 1953

THIS RULING APPLIES TO:

Name: [Applicant]

Tax File Number: [set out]

YEAR(S) OF INCOME TO WHICH THIS RULING APPLIES:

The year ended 30 June 1995

TAX LAW:

The following provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act (the Act)

Section 160A

Section 160L

Section 160T

Section 160U

Section 160Z

Section 160ZC

Section 160ZD

Subsubparagraph 160ZH(9)(c)(i)(A)

Subparagraph 160ZH(9)(c)(ii)

Section 160ZJ

Section 160ZO

Subsection 160ZZQ(1)

Subsection 160ZZQ(16)

WHAT THIS RULING IS ABOUT:

This ruling answers the question “Did [the applicant] acquire a beneficial interest in the property [described], New South Wales, in 1984 so that no capital gains tax liability arises on the disposal of the property in 1994?

THE SUBJECT OF THE RULING:

On or about 31 August 1984 and not later than 20 September 1984 [the applicant], . . . his mother, and . . . his then wife, entered an agreement with [his uncle] to purchase the property [described], New South Wales for $85,000

A deposit of $22,000 was paid being $5,000 each from [his mother] and [his then wife] and $12,000 from [the applicant]. It was a condition of the agreement that monthly payments of $400.00 be made by [the applicant] and [his then wife] and that “in the event of anyone of the three parties desiring to withdraw from said agreement the property will be sold and all monies paid by the three buyers will be repaid on completion of sale of the property.”

The deposit of $5,000 given by [his then wife] and her contributions to the monthly payments of $400.00 were loans to [the applicant]. These loans were repaid following the divorce of [the applicant] and [his then wife].

[His then wife] commenced divorce proceedings in 1986. The decree nisi of dissolution of marriage dated 25 May 1988 became absolute on 26 June 1988. On 25 June 1987 [the applicant] mortgaged the property [described], New South Wales to [his uncle] for $48,960. [His uncle] died on 10 August 1989 and in April 1990 the mortgage was discharged in accordance with the terms of his will. On or about 1 May 1990 the legal title in the property was registered in the name of [the applicant].

[The applicant] worked overseas from 1979 to 1990. He used the property from 1984 as his permanent address in Australia and as his residence when visiting Australia. He visited Australia from 21 December 1985 to 11 January 1986 (22 days), 2 September 1986 to 28 September 1986 (27 days), 20 December 1987 to 15 January 1988 (27 days), 27 July 1988 to 9 August 1988 (16 days) and 23 December 1989 to 13 January 1990 (22 days). His mother occupied the home unit from 9 September 1984 to 26 July 1993.

In October 1990 [the applicant] returned to Australia with his [new wife] and they occupied the home unit from 20 October 1990 to 20 December 1990. From December 1990 to July 1991 [the applicant] and his [new] wife resided in rented premises in Sydney, from July 1991 to May 1993 the couple worked and reside[d] temporarily in Brisbane. They returned to Sydney and resided in the property from May 1993 until 9 September 1994. Contracts for sale of the home unit were exchanged in or about August 1994.

COMMENCEMENT OF ARRANGEMENT:

The year ended 30 June 1995

ASSUMPTIONS:

None made.

RULING:

[The applicant] did not acquire a beneficial interest in the property being [described], New South Wales in 1984.

[The applicant] alone acquired the beneficial interest in the home unit property [described], New South Wales from [uncle] on 25 June 1987 for its market value at that date in terms of subsubparagraph 160ZH(9)(c)(i)(A) and subparagraph 160ZH(9)(c)(ii) of the Act [sic].

The home unit was the sole or principal residence of [the applicant] during the period 9 May 1993 to the date of disposal of the property only. [The applicant’s] occupancy after 25 June 1987 for the periods of 27 days from 20 December 1987 to 15 January 1988, 16 days from 25 July 1988 to 9 August 1988, 21 days from 23 December 1989 to 13 January 1990, and 62 days from 20 October 1990 to 20 December 1990 did not establish the dwelling as his sole or principal residence. Accordingly in terms of subsection 160ZZQ(16) of the Act there is only a partial exemption of the capital gain realised on disposal.


D J Cortese

DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

19 April 1995

EXPLANATION: (This does not form part of the Notice of Private Ruling):

The 1984 agreement was subject to two conditions. One was that monthly payments of $400.00 be made by [the applicant] and [his then wife] and the other was that none of the parties [his mother, the applicant or his then wife] withdraw from the agreement.

When [his then wife] commenced divorce proceedings against [the applicant] she withdrew from the 1984 agreement and that put an end to that agreement.

A new agreement between [the applicant] and [his uncle] on 25 June 1987 replaced the 1984 agreement. As a consequence of that agreement [the applicant] alone acquired the property [described], New South Wales.’
  1. After the matter was remitted from the Federal Court a second directions hearing was held. It was contended by the applicant that the terms of the NPR did not enable him to isolate the facts identified by the respondent as constituting the arrangement, nor was he able to identify any documents which might form part of an arrangement pursuant to section 14ZAS(3) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (‘the Administration Act’). The applicant had objected only to part of the ruling, namely whether or not the applicant had acquired a beneficial interest in the property in 1984. No objection had been taken to that part of the NPR dealing with the question of whether the property constituted the applicant’s sole or principal residence giving rise to an entitlement to a partial exemption. Nonetheless the applicant was left unclear as the way in which his claims would be resisted by the respondent at the substantive hearing. Accordingly, I directed that the relevant facts constituting the arrangement be identified, together with any relevant documents and that the respondent articulate the grounds on which the application would be resisted at the hearing.
  2. On 26 February 1998, the respondent filed and served a document in the following terms, omitting formal parts:
‘1. Facts identified by the Respondent in Notice of Private Ruling as constituting “the arrangement” the subject of the private ruling

(a) That [the applicant] alone acquired the beneficial interest in the home unit property [described], NSW from [his uncle] on 25 June 1987 for its market value at that date (See T documents pages 158 to 160 being Real Property Act Transfer dated 27 January 1987 and Mortgage dated 25 June 1987).

(b) The home unit was the sole or principal residence of [the applicant] during the period 9 May 1993 to the date of disposal of the property only.

(c) In the period after 25 June 1987 up until [the applicant’s] disposal of the property, he occupied the unit for the following periods:-

(i) 27 days from 20 December 1987 to 15 January 1988

(ii) 16 days from 25 July 1988 to 9 August 1988

(iii) 21 days from 23 December 1989 to 13 January 1990

(iv) 62 days from 20 October 1990 to 20 December 1990
  1. Documents (if any) identified in the ruling and by reference to the matters set out in which documents ‘the arrangement’ mentioned above is identified in terms of s 14ZAS(3) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953.
There are no documents identified in the notice of private ruling in terms of subsection 14ZAS(3) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953.
  1. Grounds on which this application will be resisted at the hearing by Respondent.
(a) The arrangement involved an acquisition of the relevant property after 20 September 1985 and accordingly was not exempt from Capital Gains Tax as a pre CGT property.

(b) The applicant is entitled to partial exemption from CGT on the basis that the property was used by the applicant as his sole or principal residence during part of the relevant period, the relevant period being between the date of acquisition of legal ownership of the property and the date of sale, and the part of the relevant period being between 9 May 1993 and 9 September 1994.

(c) Even though the Commissioner had other material before him and took it into account, the arrangement that he identified and which was open to him to identify was that evidenced by the Memorandum of Transfer and Mortgage contained at pages 158 to 160 inclusive of the T documents.’
  1. The judgements of their Honours in McMahon make it quite clear that the Tribunal is restricted to a consideration of the terms of the NPR. At page 4990 Lockhart J said:
‘If a taxpayer seeks a review of the private ruling before the Tribunal, the subject matter of that review is the arrangement as identified by the Commissioner in his private ruling. That arrangement is constant throughout the process of the private ruling and any review or appellate process that ensues. The Tribunal may form its opinion as to how the tax law operated or would operate on the facts that constitute the arrangement; and it may disagree with [the] Commissioner and alter the objection decision. But the review is not a review in the usual sense that applies to the processes of administrative review when it is dealing with actual facts. These are hypothetical facts. They may turn out to be the real facts; but the whole notion of a private ruling is that the facts are not necessarily the facts that will underlie the making of any ultimate assessment. If the factual matrix as explained to the Commissioner in aid of a request for a private ruling are suspicious, the Commissioner has ample powers to decline to make a private ruling. Once the ruling is made, it is made with respect to the facts that are identified for the purposes of the private ruling itself.

In my opinion on a process of review the Tribunal cannot redefine the arrangement. The Tribunal is limited to the facts that constitute the arrangement as identified by the Commissioner in his own ruling. I agree with the submission of counsel for the taxpayer that the arrangement is a “constant” and a ruling is about how a tax law applies to that arrangement. The question for the Tribunal is whether the Commissioner’s opinion as to the application of the law concerning the arrangement is correct. In considering the correctness or otherwise of the objection decision the Tribunal must be limited to the facts as identified by the Commissioner in his ruling as constituting the arrangement.’
  1. At page 4996 Beaumont J said:
‘It must follow, in my opinion, that the subject of any review of that taxation decision and of any review or appeal relating to the ruling will be that question, and no other or different question. In that sense, ‘the stream can rise no higher than its source’ and that source is the construct constituted by the “arrangement”, as nominated and identified by the applicant for the ruling. The Tribunal’s jurisdiction is thus limited to a review of the Commissioner’s opinion on that question, so that the Tribunal has no power to address any other question. In particular, neither the Commissioner nor the Tribunal on review, has jurisdiction or power to “redefine” the “arrangement” the subject of the Commissioner’s ruling.

As has been seen, the “arrangement” will consist of an identified “scheme” etc. There is a conceptual element involved here, consisting of the proper attribution to be given to the relevant primary facts said to amount to an “arrangement”. This process of characterisation calls for findings or conclusions to be made by way of “secondary” facts in the definition of the subject arrangement. Any attempt to redefine the arrangement would likewise involve a conceptual aspect, that is, something more than findings of primary facts. It is by travelling beyond the primary facts into the realm of the secondary facts that the process of defining the “arrangement” occurs; that is, by way of a conclusion from the primary facts.’
  1. On pages 5003 and 5004 Emmett J said:
‘It follows that the Tribunal cannot, on the hearing of an application for review of a decision on an objection against a private ruling, redefine the arrangement. The Tribunal is limited to making a decision upon the basis of the arrangement, including assumptions, identified by the Commissioner in the ruling. The only question for the Tribunal is whether the Commissioner’s view as to the application of the law to the arrangement so identified was correct: that is to say, should the decision on the objection by the Taxpayer to the ruling not have been made or should it have been made differently. The only material which need be before the Tribunal would be the ruling and particulars of the person, the tax law, the year of income and the arrangement identified in the ruling pursuant to section 14ZAS(1). In the present case, that is the notice sent to the Taxpayer’s agent under cover of the Commissioner’s letter of 19 April 1995. Accordingly, the only question for the Tribunal is whether, on the basis of the material identified in that Notice of Private Ruling and of no other material, the taxation decision constituted by that Notice should not have been made or should have been made differently.’
  1. Since McMahon, these views have been followed by a differently constituted Full Court in a unanimous judgement in Bellinz Pty Ltd & Ors v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 98 ATC 4634. At page 4639 their Honours said:
‘The statutory provisions relating to private rulings have been the subject of a number of decisions in this Court: CTC Resources NL v FC of T 94 ATC 4072; [1994] FCA 947; (1994) 48 FCR 397; First Provincial Building Society Limited v FC of T 95 ATC 4145; (1995) 128 ALR 118; United Energy Limited v FC of T 97 ATC 4796 and FC of T v McMahon & Anor 97 ATC 4986; [1997] FCA 1087; (1997) 149 ALR 159. It is unnecessary to canvass these cases. It suffices to say that, where a private ruling is sought in respect of an arrangement, it is imperative that an applicant give full details to the Commissioner either in the initial application or in response to requests by the Commissioner for additional facts: s 14ZAM. The ruling itself must, inter alia, identify the arrangement to which the ruling relates: s 14ZAS(1), although it may do so by reference to matters set out in a document identified in the ruling and which, or a copy of which, is available to “the rulee”: s 14ZAS(3).

Both parties to the appeal proceeded on the basis that the arrangement the subject of the ruling, identified both in the application and the ruling itself, was described in a document which the parties prepared for the purposes of the appeal. Such an approach should not be encouraged. The Court can have regard only to the arrangement as described in the ruling itself, supplemented by any documentation referred to in it.’
  1. In conducting a review such as this, the Tribunal’s functions are defined by a combination of sections 14ZAZA and 14ZZK(b)(iii) of the Administration Act. The function of the Tribunal is to decide whether the NPR (‘the taxation decision’) should not have been made or should have been made differently. Relying upon the terms of the NPR, the applicant submitted firstly that the ruling should not have been made. Written submissions of the applicant set out the appropriate arguments as follows:
‘18.8 - The Commissioner’s 26.2.98 Document states as follows (the Applicant now believes correctly) -

‘There are no documents identified in the notice of private ruling in terms of subsection 14ZAS(3) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953.”.

Despite that statement, the Commissioner’s 26.2.98 Document seeks in paragraphs 1(a) and 3(c) to identify “the arrangement” ruled upon as an arrangement [unspecified in the NPR itself except as mentioned at 18.12 below], being an arrangement “evidenced by” the Transfer and Mortgage of which copies are at T-file pp.158 to 160 [but which are not mentioned in the NPR itself].

18.9 - McMahon’s Case, United Energy and Bellinz require the Respondent (when making the NPR and throughout the process following) and the Tribunal (in this review) not to consider the Transfer or Mortgage either as facts or as evidence of facts for the purpose of deciding whether the subject ruling “should not have been made” or “should have been made differently”.

18.10 - That prohibition against the Tribunal’s considerations of facts (or evidence of facts) “other than those identified by the Commissioner in the [NPR]”, extends also to the fundamental document in relation to the subject assessment of the Applicant: namely, the written agreement of 1984 (at T-file p 79). The NPR does not mention that the 1984 “agreement” was in writing; nor does it mention that [the property] was the subject to the Real Property Act 1900 or the (now former) Strata Titles Act 1973; nor that [the property] was the subject of a “Transfer” under the Real Property Act 1900 nor that [the property] was the subject of a “Mortgage” under that Act.

18.11 - In the face of the Federal Court’s Order, the Respondent does not have authority to introduce into the “arrangement” ruled upon either the Transfer or the Mortgage referred to at T-file pages 158 to 160 (in turn referred to at paragraphs 1(a) and 3(c) of the Commissioner’s 26.2.98 Document). Nor may the Tribunal consider either.

18.12 - The Respondent says that his opinion as to the way in which the relevant tax law(s) would apply to the Applicant was an opinion formed in relation to an arrangement “evidenced by the Memorandum of [sic] Transfer and Mortgage contained at pages 158 to 160 inclusive of the T documents”: para. 3(c) of the Commissioner’s 26.2.98 Document. The NPR itself says nothing about any such arrangement except what it says in this solitary sentence -

“On 25 June 1987 [the applicant] mortgaged the property [described], New South Wales to [his uncle] for $48,960.” [T-file, p137, 4th para., 3rd sentence].

That is the only fact identified in the NPR which occurred on 25 June 1987. The fact so stated is not capable of supporting the Respondent’s opinions (ruling) that -

“[The applicant] did not acquire a beneficial interest in the property ... in 1984.” and

“[The applicant] alone acquired the beneficial interest in the home unit property ... from [his uncle] on 25 June 1987...”

[T-file, p136, 1st and 2nd sentences under “RULING”].

18.13 - Accordingly, those opinions of the Respondent “should not have been made”: “on the basis of the material identified in [the NPR] and of no other material” (as Emmett J. expressed the question for the Tribunal, cited at 18.3 above).

18.14 - In particular support of the Applicant’s contention that the subject ruling (the 2 opinions expressed as quoted in 18.12 above) should not have been made, the Applicant draws attention to -

18.14.1 - the fact that if the Applicant on 25 June 1987 “mortgaged” the Property to his Uncle for $48,960 it tells us nothing about -

a) whether the mortgage was legal or equitable;

b) what interest in the Property was mortgaged by the Applicant to his Uncle - for example, whether that interest was the Applicant’s legal title to the Property (if any) subject to equitable interests of the Applicant’s mother and/or then-wife and/or the Applicant himself; or

c) whether the Applicant on 25 June 1987 owned, acquired or ceased to own any (let alone, the sole) “beneficial interest” in the Property;

18.14.2 - the absence of any other facts identified in the NPR which enable any inference to be drawn to assist to answer the questions raised at 18.14.1(a), (b) & (c) above in the way in which the Respondent has answered them;

18.14.3 - the impermissible attempt by the Respondent in the Commissioner’s 26.2.98 Document (at paragraph 1(a)) to utilise his own opinion/ruling as “the facts identified by the Respondent in Notice of Private Ruling as constituting ‘the arrangement’ the subject of the private ruling”. That is an impermissible attempt to utilise the Respondent’s opinion to provide the “factual” basis of the opinion itself. It is not enhanced by the Respondent’s reference (also in paragraph 1(a) of the 26.2.98 Document) to “the Real Property Act Transfer dated 27 January 1987 and Mortgage dated 25 June 1987”. If the Commissioner had based his opinion/ruling on the Transfer and/or the Mortgage, he should have relied on s.14ZAS(3) and identified them (or incorporated their material contents) in the Commissioner’s statement in the NPR of the facts “constituting the relevant arrangement”; and

18.14.4 - the Respondent’s third ground for resisting this Application at the hearing (paragraph 3(c) of the Commissioner’s 26.2.98 Document) is of no assistance to the Respondent. It is based on the following 2 errors flowing from the Respondent’s failure to apply the reasons for judgment given by the members of the Court, and the Court’s Order No. 2, in McMahon’s Case. The Respondent’s 2 relevant errors are -

firstly - the admission that “the Commissioner had other material before him and took it into account”. That is contrary to the rationale of the private ruling system: for example as articulated by Lockhart J. in McMahon’s Case in these 2 extracts from p4, 990 -

“When making a private ruling the Commissioner does not make findings of fact. He simply identifies facts and then states his opinion about the way in which the relevant tax laws apply to the applicant in relation to those identified facts.”

“In making his decision about the private ruling the Commissioner is bound by the facts said by him to constitute the arrangement as identified in the ruling. Nor can the Tribunal travel beyond those facts as identified in the ruling.”; and

secondly - the admission that the Commissioner identified the arrangement “evidenced by the Memorandum of [sic] Transfer and Mortgage contained at pages 158 to 160 inclusive of the T documents”: yet the Commissioner failed to identify in the NPR either that “arrangement” or either of the documents now said to evidence the “arrangement” on the basis of which the Respondent now says he formed his opinions expressed in the NPR.’
  1. I agree with these submissions. The Commissioner has stated in his own February document that he has ventured beyond permissible limits in arriving at his ruling. Although the written 1984 contract and copies of the Transfer and Mortgage were included in the section 37 documents and were before the decision maker, they were not identified in a distillation of the primary facts as the basis for the identified arrangement. The Commissioner, in effect, has misconceived his obligations as stated by Lockhart J. In effect, the Commissioner has made a finding of fact and has adjudicated between the applicant’s submission that he acquired a beneficial interest under the 1984 contract, and the Commissioner’s conclusion that he acquired the beneficial interest under the 1987 transfer. In the absence of either of these documents as identified documents within the ruling, the Commissioner was not entitled to find as he did.
  2. The Commissioner, oddly enough, also submitted that the ruling should not have been made. However, he relied upon different grounds to support this submission. The first ground is based upon paragraphs (g) and (j) of section 14ZAN. The Commissioner is not required to comply with an application for private ruling if the application is frivolous or vexatious or, if in the opinion of the Commissioner, it would be unreasonable to comply, having regard to any matters which the Commissioner considered relevant.
  3. At the conclusion of the correspondence with the applicant’s representatives, the applicant’s position was finally put. The taxation agent withdrew the original questions and substituted the following:
‘The question now addressed to the Commissioner is to confirm that since all beneficial interests were acquired prior to 20 September 1985, no capital gains tax liabilities arise on the sale of the property in 1994.’
  1. It was submitted that this was a vexatious application since it postulated as a fact an obvious conclusion. In effect, the applicant was seeking a ruling on a proposition as clear as a statement that night follows day. If pre-1985 acquisition is asserted as a hypothetical fact to be assumed, there is no need for any ruling. This argument was previously put by the Commissioner as a basis for asserting that the NPR was not a valid ruling and that accordingly, this Tribunal had no jurisdiction to review it. That argument was not pursued before the Federal Court in McMahon. Put in the alternative way as a discretionary argument, I agree with it. The Commissioner was not required to comply with the application in this form and should not have done so.
  2. The second basis put by the respondent to support a submission under paragraph 14ZAN(j) was that the material put before the Commissioner lacked crucial factual elements and, ultimately, was inadequate in order to enable the Commissioner to identify an arrangement. Relying upon the terms of the NPR alone, I agree that this ground has also been made out. Notwithstanding all the enquiries made by the Commissioner both of the applicant and externally, there was still an inadequate basis upon which the Commissioner could have proceeded. For this reason also the ruling should not have been made.
  3. A coalescing of these factors supports a finding setting aside the ruling and substituting a decision that the ruling should not have been made. I will formalise that finding.
  4. However, the applicant went further. He submitted that the taxation decision should not only be set aside, but that the private ruling should be altered to a ruling that the applicant’s disposal in August 1994 of such beneficial ownership as he then had in respect of the property, did not give rise to any assessable income under section 160ZO(1) because the beneficial ownership so disposed of was acquired by the applicant before 20 September 1985.
  5. The applicant submitted that the ruling should confirm this exempt status for two reasons. The first reason relied upon the terms of section 160M(3) and the claim that the applicant had the use and enjoyment of the asset for a period at the end of which he took title. The written submissions in support of this argument were as follows:
‘19. Contention 2 - S.160M(3)(d) & S. 160U(7). - The Applicant contends not only that the subject private ruling should not have been made, but that on the basis of the facts identified in the NPR the Private Ruling should have been made differently. In support of that contention the Applicant submits as follows -

19.1 - The facts identified by the Respondent under the heading “THE SUBJECT OF THE RULING:” properly found the application of s. 160M(3)(d) and s. 160U(7) to the Applicant’s acquisition of his ownership interest in the Property. Those provisions at all relevant times have been in these terms -

“s. 160M(3) Without limiting the generality of subsection (2), a change shall be taken to have occurred in the ownership of an asset by:

...

(d) subject to subsection (4), a transaction in relation to the asset under which the use and enjoyment of the asset was or is obtained by a person for a period at the end of which the title to the asset will or may pass to that person.”

“(4) A change shall not be taken to have occurred in the ownership of an asset by a transaction referred to in paragraph (3)(d) if the period for which the person referred to in that paragraph has the use and enjoyment of the asset terminates without the title to the asset passing to that person and ...”.

“S.16OU(7) Where the acquisition or disposal of the asset occurred as a result of a transaction referred to in paragraph 160M(3)(d), the time of acquisition or disposal shall be taken to have been the time when the use and enjoyment of the asset was first obtained by the person mentioned in that paragraph.”

Note that, under s.160U(2) the timing rule under a subsequent subsection of s. 160U takes precedence over an earlier subsection: so that, for example, if s. 160U(7) applies to a transaction to which s.160U(3) also applies, the s.160U(7) rule has precedence.

19.2 - The Explanatory Memorandum for the Income Tax Assessment Amendment (Capital Gains) Act 1986, which introduced s.160M(3)(d) [since unchanged] states -

“An example of a transaction to which paragraph 160M(3)(d) applies would be the entry into a hire purchase contract.”.

The Applicant contends that an equally clear example would be the entry into a terms purchase agreement for the purchase of an item of real property, with “use and enjoyment” of the real property being obtained by the terms purchaser before the transfer of title: effectively the “hire purchase” of real estate.

19.3 - The following facts identified in the NPR (referring to the page thereof copied at T-file, p. 137) support the application of s.160M(3)(d)/s.160U(7) to the Applicant’s acquisition of such interest in the Property as the Applicant disposed of in August 1994 -

19.3.1 - the “transaction” in relation to the Property: the agreement to purchase the Property for $85,000 - identified in the first paragraph on p. 137;

19.3.2 - “the use and enjoyment” of the Property was obtained by the Applicant: both directly personally and through his mother’s occupation - identified in the fifth paragraph on p. 137 in these terms -

“[The applicant] worked overseas from 1979 to 1990. He used the property from 1984 as his permanent address in Australia. He visited Australia from 21 December 1985 to 11 January 1986, 2 September 1986 to 28 September 1986 (27 days) ... His mother ... occupied the home unit from 9 September 1984 to 2 July 1993.”;

19.3.3 - such use and enjoyment was obtained “under” the transaction comprising the agreement to purchase: that being the only possible source of the Applicant’s and his mother’s use and enjoyment of the Property, owned by the Uncle (as unpaid vendor). The transaction involving the purchase of the Property was the transaction “under which” use and enjoyment was obtained by the applicant. That can be concluded from these facts identified in the NPR -

first - the agreement to purchase is expressed to have been entered into “On or about 31 August 1984 and not later than 20 September 1984”: and the Applicant is said to have “used the property from 1984” and his mother as having “occupied the home unit from 9 September 1984”;

second - no other source of right to occupy, use and enjoy the Property is identified in the NPR. For example, there is no reference to any tenancy agreement or licence agreement independent of the terms purchase agreement. The only relevant relationship mentioned in the NPR between the Applicant and his mother (on the one hand) and his Uncle, her brother (on the other hand) is that of deposit-paid, monthly-instalments-paying purchasers and unpaid vendor: leaving aside the not-here-relevant relationship of mortgagor-mortgagee on and from 25 June 1987;

third - the “deposit of $22,000” paid by the purchasers was a comparatively large proportion (nearly 26%) of the purchase price for the Property and the NPR states that “It was a condition of the agreement [i.e. the agreement to purchase the Property] that monthly payments of $400.00 be made by [the applicant] and [his then wife]”. [The NPR also identifies that “The deposit of $5,000 given by [his then wife] and her contributions to the monthly payments of $400.00 were loans to [the applicant]. These loans were repaid ...”.] The existence of actual use and enjoyment by the 2 (beneficial) purchasers after paying such a large proportion of the purchase price and agreeing to go on paying instalments of the price strongly suggests that the use and enjoyment was referable to the terms purchase transaction and, thus, “obtained under” that transaction;

19.3.4 - the Applicant’s own (and his mother’s) use and enjoyment of the Property was obtained by him (and on his behalf via his mother’s occupation) “for a period” - the period which it would take for the Applicant (including with contributions by way of loan from his then-wife) to pay in full the $63,000 balance of $85,000 purchase price, at the rate of $400.00 per month. That was the period of 158 months (13 years 2 months) from September 1984. That the Applicant (including with loans from his then-wife) did substantially perform his bargain under the 1984 Agreement can be inferred from this information in or derived from the NPR -

- 34 payments of $400.00 for the 34 months from September 1984 to June 1987 (both inclusive)

- an aggregate of $ 13,600

- amount of mortgage debt secured to the vendor of the Property on 25 June 1987

- 3rd sentence in 4th paragraph on p.137 $ 48, 960 $ 62, 560

That $62,560 compares with the $63,000 balance of price.

19.3.5 - Finally, to establish the application of s.160M(3)(d) it seems self-evident that “at the end” of the 158-month period, when all 158 monthly payments had been made (157 @ $400 + 1 @ $200), such title as the Applicant had purchased, and such title as his mother had purchased, would be transferred to them. In the words of s.160M(3)(d), as of not later than 20 September 1984 it could have been said that, if all monthly payments were made in accordance with the 1984 Agreement, at the end of the period of 13 years 2 months ending in October 1997 the title to the Property “will or may pass to” the Applicant and his mother, as beneficial purchasers, for their respective interests in the Property. The Applicant’s such interest is all the beneficial interest in the Property of which he disposed in Aug-Sept 1994;

19.3.6 - The NPR identifies no fact which would have triggered the application of s.160M(4) to the Applicant’s acquisition of beneficial interest in the Property. The reverse is the case. Even if the NPR identified some arrangement in June 1987 which supplanted (and, thus, rescinded as distinct from merely varied) the 1984 Agreement, s.160M(3)(d) would still operate according to its terms. Even a new purchase agreement in June 1987 would not have attracted s.160M(4), provided that the Applicant’s use and enjoyment of the Property was not terminated without the title to the Property passing to the Applicant. There is not a fact identified in the NPR which suggests that there was any such termination of use and enjoyment of the Property - until 9 September 1994 (long after title had passed to the Applicant);

19.3.7 - In terms of s.160U(7) the time of acquisition of the Applicant’s beneficially owned interest in the Property is “taken to have been the time when the use and enjoyment of the [Property] was first obtained by the [Applicant]”. According to the NPR’s 5th paragraph on p.137, that was in 1984.

19.4 - That ought to suffice to lead the Tribunal to decide that the Private Ruling should have been made differently, namely along the lines contended for at paragraph 21.3 below.’
  1. The second and alternative claim for exemption from capital gains tax, the applicant submitted, could be deduced from the terms of the arrangement as identified by the Commissioner and which should have been reflected in the substantive ruling, is based upon an analysis of those facts recited in the NPR to support a finding of appropriate trusts urged by the applicant as a preferable basis for an amended ruling. The relevant written submissions were as follows:
‘20.2 - The 1984 agreement and actions taken under it gave rise to these separate beneficial interests in the Property -

20.2.1 - The Uncle’s absolute legal and beneficial interest in fee simple became a legal interest in fee simple coupled with a beneficial interest as unpaid (or partly paid) vendor of the fee simple. In equity, the Uncle was no longer free to deal with the beneficial interest, except in accordance with the 1984 Agreement;

20.2.2 The Applicant’s mother had some sort of beneficial interest in the Property - by virtue of being a buyer under the 1984 Agreement, paying $5,000 of the $22,000 deposit, and moving into occupation of the Property on 9 September 1984.

Whatever the precise nature and extent of [his mother’s] beneficial interest, there is no suggestion in the facts (actual or hypothetical) that she divested herself of that beneficial interest before disposing of it for CGT purposes when the Property was sold in August 1994. Nor is there any suggestion in the facts that the Applicant acquired the legal fee simple in the Property in July 1987 other than subject to his mother’s beneficial interest, of which the Uncle and the Applicant certainly had notice binding them in equity. Most importantly, there is not any fact recorded in the NPR on which the Respondent or the Tribunal could conclude that the Applicant acquired at any time his mother’s beneficial interest in the Property. We do know that at all relevant times the NSW Real Property Act 1900, s 82 has forbidden the recording in the Register of “any notice of trusts whether express, implied, or constructive”. Nor would it have been lawful to refer (in a Transfer from the Uncle) to [his mother’s] beneficial interest if the title to the Property was under the NSW Strata Titles Act 1973 and the Real Property Act 1900. That was in fact the case, but McMahon’s Case prevents the Tribunal from having regard to that (actual) fact. The Applicant must have become a “constructive trustee” for his mother if and when he accepted the transfer of the legal estate in June 1987: prior to mortgaging the Property to his Uncle on 25 June 1987. Until registration of the transfer to the Applicant the Uncle would have been the trustee for his sister, the Applicant’s mother. Refer to Jacobs’ Law of Trusts in Australia, 6th Ed. (1997), Meagher & Gummow, pp 308-311 at paragraphs [1304], [1306] & [1307] and pp 285-6 at [1201];

20.2.3 - The beneficial interest referable to the Applicant’s then-wife’s having been a “buyer” under the 1984 Agreement and having agreed to contribute $5,000 of the $22,000 deposit plus, with the Applicant, to pay monthly $400 instalments of purchase price. Whatever the precise nature of that beneficial interest, it was not acquired beneficially by the Applicant’s then-wife. She was merely the “apparent purchaser”, as between herself and the Uncle, as vendor. The Applicant was the “real purchaser” as between himself and his then-wife. She was the purchaser at law, but she held the beneficial interest for the Applicant under a “resulting trust”. That is the inescapable conclusion from the facts which are recorded in the NPR including the facts recorded as follows -

“The deposit of $5,000 given by [his then wife] and her contributions to the monthly payments of $400.00 were loans to [the applicant]. These loans were repaid ...”

Thus, the Applicant was a borrower from his then-wife and used the borrowings (from the initial part deposit onwards) to pay purchase price to the Uncle. The Applicant was the “real purchaser” in respect of his then-wife’s interest as purchaser: in relation to all payments of purchase price from September 1984 onwards. Refer to Jacob’s Law of Trusts in Australia, 6th Ed. (1997) at page 291, under the heading “2. Purchase in Another’s Name”, paragraphs [1210] and [1211];

20.2.4 - The Applicant’s beneficial interests as a purchaser (real and apparent) flowing from his payment of $12,000 part deposit in September 1984 and liability, with his then-wife to pay all the $63,000 balance of the $85,000 price, by monthly instalments of $400.00. It is certain that, with the passage of time (and the payment of more monthly instalments of $400.00 + more outgoings in relation to the Property + the enjoyment by the Purchasers of the right to possession of the Property), specific performance would have been ordered in favour of the 3 Purchasers against the Uncle - specific performance of the 1984 Agreement. In that context, the Uncle held, as constructive trustee, the Taxpayer’s beneficial interest(s) in the Property under the 1984 Agreement. Jacobs’ Law of Trusts in Australia, 6th Ed. (1997) at pp 309-311, paras [1306] and [1307], discusses the probable relationship in equity as between each of and all the 3 Purchasers (on the one hand) and the Uncle (on the other hand) between August/September 1984 and the time when the transaction was completed or rescinded/terminated [depending on facts which are not available to be considered by the Tribunal in this review. The Applicant submits that the facts in the NPR require the inference that the transaction was completed before the Applicant “mortgaged” the Property to his Uncle on 25 June 1987].

20.3 - The interests of the 3 Purchasers (including of the Applicant’s then-wife as apparent purchaser for the Applicant) arose from the 1984 Agreement. The Federal Court’s decision (applied to the incomplete statement of the facts in the NPR) seems to preclude the Tribunal having regard to the fact that (had it ever become necessary to resort to legal proceedings to enforce the buyers’ rights) the Agreement most certainly complied with the NSW Conveyancing Act requirements for writing: for example, s.23C(1) and s.54A(1). However, for s.160U(3) purposes it is not necessary to establish enforceability of the contract in question: for example, by proving that the 1984 Agreement was in writing complying with s.54A(1). That is established by Kiwi Brands and McDonald - see 18.14.5(k) above. In any event, s.54A(1) “does not affect the law relating to part performance” - s. 54A(2). Nor does s.23B, 23C or 23D - see s.23E(d). The facts stated in the NPR would establish past performance. In addition, the NPR states no fact to suggest that the 1984 Agreement might not have been legally effective having regard to NSW Conveyancing Act, ss. 23C, 23D and 23E. Further, s.23C(2) dispenses with the requirement of writing in cases like the apparent purchaser-real purchaser relationship between the Applicant and his then-wife and the constructive trust relationship between the Applicant and his mother (after the Applicant acquired legal title to the Property). Those 2 situations are encompassed by the s.23C(2) dispensation that - “This section does not affect the creation or operation of resulting, implied or constructive trusts.”

Accordingly, the Respondent’s several references to the need for writing were misconceived: see paragraphs 10., 12. and 14. above. The Respondent’s assertions as to the law were inaccurate and, applied to the facts, false. In fact, if anything, the complete lack of writing or other evidence of the transfer back to the Uncle of the 3 Purchasers’ interests in the Property under the 1984 Agreement removes any foundation for the Respondent’s suggestions that the Uncle disposed of the entire beneficial ownership of the Property to the Applicant on 25 June 1987. Unless, on or before 25 June 1987, the Uncle reacquired such beneficial interests from each of the Applicant, his then-wife and his mother, then such beneficial interests were not the Uncle’s to dispose of to the Applicant under some supposed “new agreement” made by the Uncle with the Applicant alone on 25 June 1987 (or at any time after September 1984).

20.4 - There is no fact recorded in the NPR which properly supports any suggestion that the Applicant or his mother disposed of their respective beneficial interests in the Property at any time from August/September 1984 until August 1994: or that the Applicant’s then-wife disposed of her legal entitlement as 1 of 3 buyers under the 1984 Agreement. In equity, she was obliged to the Applicant, for whom she was a constructive trustee, to maintain her legal contractual rights against the Uncle under the 1984 Agreement. Otherwise she would have breached her trust for the benefit of the Applicant. In particular, there are no facts recorded in the NPR which support the conclusions asserted by the Respondent, referred to at 10., 12. or 14. above.

20.5 On the contrary, the facts recorded in the NPR require the conclusion (by inference) that the parties chose a transfer of legal title to the Applicant and mortgage of the legal title back to the Uncle as the means of completing the 1984 Agreement. To that extent, the 1984 Agreement was varied: not rescinded or terminated and replaced with a new agreement. The distinction between variation (on the one hand) and rescission/new contract (on the other hand) is discussed in many judicial decisions: but conveniently summarised by the Full Federal Court in FCT v Mercantile Credits Ltd (1986) ATC 4,119 at 4,121 & 4,122 and by the NSW Supreme Court in Zoverton Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Stamp Duties (NSW) 1989 ATC 4, 339 at 4,342 to 4,345 [in which a deed increasing the price by $100,000 was characterised as a variation of the earlier purchase agreement, not as a rescission and fresh agreement]. In this case, the NPR states no fact which suggest that the $85,000.00 purchase price under the 1984 Agreement was varied. Furthermore, the NPR facts produce the figures at 19.3.4 above and found the inference that the $85,000.00 purchase price under the 1984 Agreement was partly discharged with the loan by the Uncle to the Applicant of the $48,960 secured by the mortgage of 25 June 1987. As between the 3 Purchasers, there is no fact identified in the NPR which suggests that in (or before) June 1987 there was any change in their respective interests: except such enhancement of those interests as resulted from completion of the 1984 Agreement.

20.6 - Such beneficial interest(s) (if any) as the Applicant did acquire in the Property after 19 September 1985 was (were) acquired under the 1984 Agreement (including as a result of his “real purchaser-apparent purchaser-loan agreement” with his then-wife). Under s.160U(3) such beneficial interest(s) (if any) are deemed to have been acquired by the Applicant not later than 20 September 1984.’
  1. In my view, the applicant cannot succeed in these proceedings in either of the contentions put forward for amendment of the terms of the ruling. In effect, what the applicant is seeking to do is to re-agitate the primary facts. This is not a course which is open to the Tribunal. The arguments put forward by the applicant necessarily involve findings of fact, whether or not those findings can be based exclusively upon the terms of the NPR as the applicant submits. As soon as the Tribunal embarks upon an enquiry as to what the arrangement really was, it is embarking upon a task from which it is clearly precluded by judicial authority.
  2. In order to deal with the second contention, it would be necessary to consider events, conversations, and conduct which are not available for consideration. To conclude that there was a constructive trust would involve an examination of events upon which equity would fasten in good conscience. The applicant invites me, in effect, to make assumptions and to make findings of fact contrary to those identified in the arrangement. This is an impermissible course of conduct.
  3. The applicant is not without his remedies, however. The facts as identified in the NPR are so hypothetical that, in my view, the existence of an arrangement as identified by the Commissioner would not preclude an examination of the real facts if an assessment for capital gains tax should be issued and if an unsuccessful objection is to be reviewed by this Tribunal. However, within the facultative procedure of private rulings, the approach which the applicant seeks to take is not available. The Tribunal is not permitted to investigate primary facts again and to substitute its own conclusory facts.
  4. The decision of this Tribunal will therefore be that the decision under review be set aside and in substitution, therefor, the Tribunal decides that the taxation decision concerned should not have been made.
