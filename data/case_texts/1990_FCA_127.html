Re Robert Hamilton Ex Parte: Deputy Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1990] FCA 127 (24 April 1990)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: ROBERT HAMILTON

Ex Parte: DEPUTY COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

No. P107 of 1989

FED No. 202

Bankruptcy

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

EXERCISING FEDERAL JURISDICTION IN BANKRUPTCY

Northrop J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Bankruptcy - review of an order of a Deputy District Registrar of the Court - whether petition signed in accordance with the Rules - Form 5 - irregularity or formal defect - whether petition lapses after order of dismissal.

Bankruptcy Act 1966 ss. 31A(1), 43, 44, 47, 52, 306, 308, Rule 12, Form 5

HEARING

MELBOURNE

24:4:1990

Counsel for the Petitioning: Mr G. McEwen Creditor

Counsel for the Debtor: Mr N. Franzi

ORDER

1. The order of the Deputy District Registrar made on 27 February 1990 be set aside.

2. A Sequestration Order be made against the estate of the debtor.

3. The Petitioning Creditor's costs be taxed and paid according to the Act.

4. A draft of this order be delivered to the Registrar within 7 days in
accordance with Rule 124(2).

Date of Commission of the Act of Bankruptcy: 23 August 1988 This order is to be settled and filed in accordance with rule 124 of the Bankruptcy Rules.

DECISION

By order made on 27 February 1990, a Deputy District Registrar of the Court, in the exercise of the powers conferred by s.31A of the Bankruptcy Act 1966, dismissed the petition by the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia for a sequestration order against the estate of Robert Hamilton. In particular, the Deputy District Registrar exercised the power conferred by paragraph 31A(1)(n), the parts relevant to this proceeding being:-

"... the following powers of the Court under this

Act may ... be exercised by a Registrar of the

Court in relation to a proceeding;

(a) ...

(n) the power to make a sequestration order

against the estate of a debtor under

sub-section 52(1) and the power under

sub-section 52(2) to dismiss a creditor's

petition:

(p) ..."

2. By reason of sub-section 31A(10), in s.31A "the Court" means the Federal Court of Australia exercising jurisdiction under the Bankruptcy Act and "the Registrar" includes a Deputy District Registrar."

3. By application dated 20 March 1990 the petitioning creditor applied to the Court to review the exercise of the power by the Deputy District Registrar; see sub-sections 31A(6) and (7) of the Bankruptcy Act. I discussed the nature of a review under those two sub-sections in Re: Kwiatek; ex parte Big J. Ltd, 8 September 1989 (unreported) and do not repeat what was said in that case, but in summary, the review is a rehearing of the proceeding upon the evidence before the Registrar supplemented by any evidence the parties desire to produce. On review the Court is exercising its original jurisdiction and in the present case is exercising the jurisdiction conferred by sections 43 and 52 of the Bankruptcy Act. The parts of s.52 relevant to this proceeding are set out:-

"52(1) At the hearing of a creditor's petition,

the Court shall require proof of-

(a) the matters stated in the petition (for

which purpose the Court may accept the

affidavit verifying the petition as

sufficient);

(b) service of the petition; and

(c) the fact that the debt or debts on which

the petitioning creditor relies is or are

still owing.

(2) If the Court is not satisfied with the

proof of any of those matters, or is satisfied by

the debtor-

(a) that he is able to pay his debts; or

(b) that for other sufficient cause a

sequestration order ought not to be made,

it may dismiss the petition."

4. The debtor had not given any notice under Rule 20 of the Bankruptcy Rules of his intention to oppose the petition with the result that notice of the grounds of opposition had not been given to the Court or to the petitioning creditor. At the hearing, the Court consented to the debtor relying upon the two grounds sought to be relied upon namely:-

"1. That the petition had not been signed by the

petitioning creditor or his agent in conformity

with Rule 12 and Form 5 of the Rules or s.308 of

the Act; and

2. That the petition had lapsed on 27 February

1990."

5. Before referring to the relevant provisions of the Bankruptcy Act, the facts should be stated. The petition was presented to the Court on 22 February 1989. The petition was founded upon an act of bankruptcy which had occurred on 23 August 1988 and thus was presented within the six months period referred to in paragraph 44(1)(c) of the Bankruptcy Act. The opening paragraph of the petition is as follows:-

"I, Leslie John Hutchinson, a Deputy Commissioner

of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia, whose

offices are at 280 King Street, Melbourne in the

state of Victoria petition the Court for a

sequestration order against the estate of Robert

Hamilton, Transport Driver, Lot 16 Kilmore Road,

Lancefield (hereinafter referred to as 'the debtor')."

6. The petition is dated the 20th day of February 1989 and after that date is set out, the petition is as follows:-

"SIGNED by Kenneth Raymond Laurence )

the duly authorised agent )

of the petitioner ) H.R. Laurence

in my presence ) (Signed)

) Deputy Commissioner of

C. Depasquale ) Taxation of the

(signed) ) Commonwealth of

) Australia

CHRIS DEPASQUALE )

A Commissioner for taking ) P. P.:

Affidavits )

280 King Street

Melbourne"

7. An affidavit sworn by Kenneth Raymond Laurence on 20 February 1989 and filed in support of the petition contained the following paragraph:-

"I am an Australian Government Officer

employed in the office of the Petitioner named in

the Petition hereunto annexed and marked with the

letter 'A'. I am duly authorised by the Petitioner

to make this affidavit on his behalf and to sign

the said Petition on his behalf and in his name."

8. The petition contained an endorsement that the petition would be heard on 1 May 1989. That date was extended on a number of occasions the latest of which specified that the petition would be heard on 27 November 1989. The petition was duly served on the debtor on 26 October 1989. On 27 November a Deputy District Registrar at the request of the parties by order adjourned the hearing of the petition to 12 February 1990. On 12 February, a Deputy District Registrar, by order, adjourned the further hearing of the petition to 27 February and ordered that "The life of the Petition be extended to the 27 February 1990". On 27 February 1990, a Deputy District Registrar dismissed the petition on the ground that the petition had not been signed by the petitioning creditor in conformity with the Bankruptcy Act.

9. Among other things, sub-section 47(1) of the Bankruptcy Act provides that a creditor's petition shall be in accordance with the prescribed form. Under Rule 12(2) of the Bankruptcy Rules, a petition shall be in accordance with Form 5. In the present case, the petition is in accordance with Form 5 until the final part, being the part set out earlier in these reasons. The corresponding part of Form 5 is as follows:-

"Signed by the petitioner)

in my presence: (a) )

(a) If the petitioner is a corporation, the petition

may be under the seal of the corporation and the

sealing need not be in the presence of a person

other than a person required to witness the

sealing of a document by the corporation.

(Signature, address and

occupation of witness)"

10. On its face, Form 5 requires a petition to be signed by the petitioner. In the present case, the petitioning creditor is "Deputy Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth." The petition is founded upon a bankruptcy notice in which "Deputy Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia" is stated to be a judgment creditor under a judgment obtained in the Magistrates' Court in which "Deputy Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth" was the complainant. The judgment was for unpaid tax which, under s.209 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, could be "sued for and recovered in any Court of competent jurisdiction by the Commissioner or a Deputy Commissioner suing in his official name". Even though the tax is a debt due to the Commonwealth; see s.208 of the Income Tax Assessment Act, it has been held by the High Court that the Commissioner or a Deputy Commissioner is empowered to take proceedings in bankruptcy for the recovery of tax as a Crown debt; see James v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1957] HCA 36; (1957) 97 CLR 23 at p 35.

11. Leslie John Hutchinson is a Deputy Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth. In his official capacity, he has presented the petition in the present case. Counsel for the debtor has submitted that since Mr Hutchinson has not signed the petition in conformity with the requirements of the prescribed form, the petition does not comply with the requirements of s.47 of the Bankruptcy Act.

12. In Deputy Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v Boxshall, Full Court of the Federal Court of Australia (Lockhart, Burchett and Gummow JJ.) 12 October 1988, unreported, the Court said:

"At common law, where a person authorises another

to sign for him, the signature of the person so

signing is the signature of the person authorising

it: The Queen v The Justices of Kent (1873) 8

LRQB 305 per Blackbaum J. at p 307. There are

cases in which a statute may require personal

signature. Whether a particular case is in that

category is a question of construction of the terms

of the particular statute."

13. In Boxshall, the Court considered a number of authorities relating to this issue and concluded:-

"The common law principle has been applied to

the signing of a creditor's petition in bankruptcy

by the creditor's duly authorised agent: Ex parte

Wallace in Re Wallace (1884) 14 QBD 22; In re

Anderson [1909] VicLawRp 82; (1909) VLR 465; Re Williams; Ex parte

Trustees of Assigned State of Vass (1911) 28

WN (NSW) 119."

14. The Court then made reference to s.308 of the Bankruptcy Act as follows:-

"In our opinion s.308(d) is the statutory

source of authority for a petition to sequestrate a

debtor's estate to be signed by a Deputy

Commissioner of Taxation either himself or by an

agent duly authorised by him for that purpose. We

discern no contrary intention to be found in the

Bankruptcy Act. Nor do we discern any contrary

intention in the concluding words of Form No. 5

providing for the signature and attestation of the

petition."

15. The facts in Boxshall were very different to the facts of the present case. There the attestation clause appeared as follows:-

"Signed by the petitioner )

in my presence )

)

(There follows the )

signature of 'C. McElwain', ) R. Conwell

an officer of the )

Australian Government ) (signature)

Solicitor's Office ) Deputy Commissioner of

Melbourne) ) Taxation for the State

) of Victoria"

16. The petition was not signed by Mr Conwell, who was a Deputy Commissioner of Taxation and being named in the petition as the petitioner, but his name was written in as his signature by another officer duly authorised by Mr Conwell to sign the petition. There was nothing on the petition to show that the petition had not been signed by Mr Conwell in person. Nevertheless, the Court held that the petition did in fact conform to the prescribed form. The Court said:-

"It is permissible for a petition to be signed

in the name of a Deputy Commissioner by his duly

authorised agent who signs the Deputy

Commissioner's name in the manner which occurred in

the present case. Alternatively, a petition may be

signed by a duly authorised agent of the Deputy

Commissioner in the Deputy Commissioner's name but

in terms which make it clear that the signature is

by a person acting as agent. The latter is the

form of signature which commended itself to Denning

L.J. in Long County Council v Agricultural Food

Products Limited (1955) 2 QB 218 at 222."

17. In that passage, Lord Denning said:-

"... where a man is allowed to sign by the hand of

another who writes his name for him ... the one who

does the writing should add the letters p.p.' to

show that it is done by proxy, followed by his

initials to indicate who he is."

18. It is interesting to note that in Boxshall the Court said that the attestation clause should have made clear the fact that the petition had been signed by the duly authorised agent of the petitioner but that that defect constituted a formal defect or an irregularity and that in the absence of evidence that any prejudice or injustice had been caused by that defect, the proceedings were not invalidated by reason of sub-section 306(1) of the Act which provides:-

"306(1) Proceedings under this Act are not

invalidated by a formal defect or an irregularity,

unless the Court before which the objection on that

ground is made is of opinion that substantial

injustice has been caused by the defect or

irregularity and that the injustice cannot be

remedied by an order of that court."

19. In the present case, the attestation clause states that the petition was signed by Mr Laurence, the duly authorised agent of the petitioner. Mr Laurence signed the petition. Having regard to the common law rules, strengthened by s.306(d) of the Bankruptcy, that would have been in conformity with the Bankruptcy Act, Rule 12 and Form 5. There is nothing said in Boxshall which prevents this conclusion being formed. For reasons similar to those expressed in Boxshall the words "Deputy Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia P.P:" immediately under the signature of Mr Laurence constitute at most a formal defect or an irregularity within sub-section 306(1) of the Bankruptcy Act. There is no evidence that any prejudice or injustice has been caused and thus s.306 operates automatically with the result that the petition is not invalidated.

20. A further submission was put by counsel for the debtor based upon the form of authority given by the petitioner. The authority is stated as follows:-

"I am duly authorised by the Petitioner ... to

sign the said Petition on his behalf and in his

name."

21. Attention was directed to the words "and in his name" and it was contended that this meant that the agent was authorised to write the name of the petitioner and not to sign his own name. Mr Laurence had not written the name of Mr Hutchinson and thus, it was contended, he had not exercised properly the authority conferred upon him and that this defect was not a formal defect or irregularity within s.308 of the Bankruptcy Act. This was the view taken by the Deputy District Registrar.

22. It is a pity that the actual authority of Mr Laurence is not before the Court. The petition is brought in the name of Mr Hutchinson. It will be recalled that the opening words of the petition are "I, Leslie John Hutchinson, a Deputy Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia, ... petition the Court for a sequestration order". The authority is to sign the petition on behalf of the petitioner and in the name of the petitioner. It is not in the form "to sign the name of the petitioner".

23. This issue turns upon the proper construction of the authority given to Mr Laurence. In my opinion the words to "sign ... on his behalf and in his name", on their proper construction, are to be read as authorising Mr Laurence to sign the petition as if he were the petitioner. Thus, if relying on that authority Mr Laurence had written in the name "L. J. Hutchinson" as if it were the signature of Mr Hutchinson in the way in which the name of Mr Conwell had been written in the case of Boxshall and the attestation clause had made no reference to agency, the petition would not have been invalidated. This case would have been identical with Boxshall. Having formed the view I have of the proper construction of the authority, it follows that Mr Laurence had authority to sign the petition in his own name. Having done that, the attestation clause was in proper form.

24. In any event, if I am wrong in that construction, in my opinion the irregularity in the form of the signature is a formal defect or irregularity within s.306 of the Bankruptcy Act. In Boxshall, the Court said:-

"In our opinion the incorrect attestation

clause constitutes a formal defect or an

irregularity. Analogous defects or irregularities

have been held to constitute formal defects or

irregularities: see Re Marsden, Ex parte EH.

Sellers and Sons Ltd. (1921) Hansell's Report of

Cases in Bankruptcy 188 where the Court held that

the form of signature was not defective, but if it

were so the defect would have been merely formal;

Russell v Australia and New Zealand Banking Group

Limited (1987) 14 FCR 72 and Re Manias, Ex parte

Edsill Pty. Limited (1986) 15 FCR 1."

25. The cases of Russell and Manias are of particular relevance to the present case. In Russell, the debtor did not comply with the command contained in the Registrar's notice endorsed on the petition relating to giving notice of opposition. As a result, no grounds of opposition were given. If notice had been given, ratification of the authority could have been given, or an amendment made to the petition; compare Manias.

26. In the present case the defect or irregularity, in the context of form, is one that could not reasonably mislead the debtor and there is no evidence that any prejudice or injustice has been caused by that defect or irregularity. Thus the petition is not invalidated and s.306 of the Bankruptcy Act operates to protect the petition.

27. I turn to consider the second ground of opposition namely that the petition had lapsed on 27 February 1990. This was a ground that could not have succeeded at the time the petition was heard and determined by the Deputy District Registrar. This ground depends upon the application of sub-sections 52(4) and (5) of the Bankruptcy Act. Those sub-sections are set out:-

"(4) A creditor's petition lapses at the

expiration of-

(a) subject to paragraph (b), the period of 12

months commencing on the date of

presentation of the petition; or

(b) if the Court makes an order under subsection

(5) in relation to the petition - the

period fixed by the order.

unless, before the expiration of whichever of those

periods is applicable, a sequestration order is

made on the petition or the petition is dismissed

or withdrawn.

(5) The Court may, at any time before the

expiration of the period of 12 months commencing on

the date of presentation of a creditor's petition,

if it considers it just and equitable to do so,

upon such terms and conditions as it thinks fit,

order that the period at the expiration of which

the petition will lapse be such period, being a

period exceeding 12 months and not exceeding 24

months, commencing on the date of presentation of

the petition as is specified in the order."

28. In the present case, the petition was presented on 22 February 1989. Before the expiration of the period of 12 months commencing on that date, a Deputy District Registrar, in the exercise of the power conferred by paragraph 31A(i)(m) of the Bankruptcy Act, on 12 February 1990 ordered that "The life of the Petition be extended to 27 February 1990". At the same time, the hearing of the petition was adjourned to the same date. Two comments are made relating to that order. If it is considered just and equitable to do so, an order under sub-section 52(5) should be in the form of that sub-section namely "that the period at the expiration of which the petition will lapse" be a specific period. In specifying that period, care must be taken to ensure that the order is made at a time before the expiration of the period of 12 months commencing on the date of the presentation of the petition and that the new period does not exceed 24 months commencing on the date of the presentation of the petition. Secondly, as a matter of discretion, it should be noted that it may be unwise, where other options are available, to extend the period so that it expires on the day that the petition is to be heard. The uncertainty of litigation illustrates dangers that can arise if this is done. Thus, if the hearing is not completed on the adjourned day, the petition lapses. After the expiration of the period of 12 months commencing on the date of the presentation of the petition, the period cannot be further extended. If the hearing is within the first 12 months period but the hearing is adjourned to a date beyond that period but, by an oversight, the period is not extended under sub-section 52(5), the petition lapses. On this aspect, it should be noted that Re Draper; Ex parte Brosalco Pty. Ltd. (1983) 72 FLR 179 has been overruled by Re Young; Ex parte Smith (1985) 5 FCR 204. Allowance should be made for human fallibility.

29. Before considering Young in more detail, it should be noted that in the present case an order was made on 27 February dismissing the petition. Under sub-section 52(4) of the Bankruptcy Act, a petition lapses at the time specified, "unless, before the expiration of whichever of those periods is applicable, a sequestration order is made on the petition or the petition is dismissed or withdrawn". It should be noted that under sub-section 47(2) a creditor's petition cannot be withdrawn after presentation "except with the leave of the Court", but that under paragraph 31A(i)(j), a Registrar of the Court is empowered to exercise that power. In the present case, the petition was dismissed before it had lapsed. Applying sub-section 52(4), therefore, it would appear that the petition herein cannot lapse, at least for as long as the order for dismissal remains operative.

30. This question was considered by a Full Court of the Federal Court in Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Clyne [1984] FCA 345; (1984) 4 FCR 156. In that case, a petition had been presented on 4 January 1983 and a sequestration order had been made on that petition on 7 October 1983. On 21 July 1984 the High Court set aside the sequestration order that had been made on 7 October 1983 and remitted the matter to the Federal Court to be dealt with in accordance with its reasons for judgment; see Clyne v Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1984] HCA 44; (1984) 154 CLR 589. The hearing of the petition came on for hearing before the Federal Court on 25 October 1984 being beyond the period of 12 months commencing on the date of the presentation of the petition and no order made under sub-section 52(5) having been made. At the hearing, the debtor contended that the petition had lapsed and therefore no sequestration order could be made. The trial Judge reserved a number of questions for the consideration of a Full Court. The Full Court held that a sequestration order had been made on 7 October 1983 even though it had been set aside by the High Court and that as a result, the petition had not lapsed within the meaning of sub-sectin 52(4) of the Act. In a joint judgment, Toohey and Wilcox JJ. rejected a submission that that conclusion was wrong since, it was contended, it would mean that "the life of the petition would be extended indefinitely". In rejecting that contention, their Honours said at p158:-

"This is the result of the view we take but it need

not occasion concern; the court maintains control

over the petition and may make such orders,

including an order substituting a new creditor or

dismissing the petition, as the circumstances

require. The alternative position, which may

accord to a debtor immunity in respect of a

particular petition in relation to which an

irregular order had been made, possibly with

important consequences in respect of the

commencement of the bankruptcy and the assets

available for distribution to his creditors, is

much more difficult to reconcile with the

principles underlying this Act."

31. In that case, a sequestration order had been made. Of necessity the same result must apply where the petition was dismissed even though the reasoning expressed could not apply. It is noted that the effect of this conclusion is that appeals from orders of sequestration or dismissal are freed from the constraints resulting from lapse by effluxion of time under sub-section 52(4).

32. Counsel for the debtor in the present case contended that Clyne was overruled by the later decision of Young, even though Clyne was not referred to in Young. In Young the question that had been reserved for the consideration of a Full Court was whether the Court has power under sub-section 52(5) of the Bankruptcy Act to extend the period at the expiration of which a creditor's petition will lapse if the period of twelve months commencing on the date of presentation of the petition has expired before the Court is asked to make an order extending the period; see p 204. In essence, the question was referred to a Full Court to determine whether Draper was corectly decided. It should be noted that in Young, no order had been made on the petition. The Full Court held that the period could not be extended if the initial period of 12 months had expired before an order had been made under sub-section 52(5).

33. Counsel for the debtor in the present case contended that Young had overruled Clyne. He referred to two passages from the judgment of the Court in Young at pages 207 and 208 respectively where Bowen C.J., Sweeney and Lockhart JJ. said:-

"Section 52(4) and (5) cannot be considered

independently of each other. They are designed to

ensure that a petition has no life beyond a maximum

of twenty-four months from the date of its

presentation. It has an automatic span of twelve

months from the date of its presentation, but it

cannot survive beyond the initial twelve month

period without an order of the court. The life of

a petition may, however, at any time during its

life, be brought to an end by the making of a

sequestration order on the petition or by the

dismissal or withdrawal of the petition."; and

"No problems of uncertainty arise during the

first twelve months because the events that bring

an end to the petition are clearly ascertainable.

Indeed they are matters of public record: the

making of a sequestration order or the dismissal or

withdrawal of a petition. Also, if the duration of

the petition is extended by order of the court made

during the first twelve months then certainty will

necessarily attend its extended life."

34. The contention is rejected. Clyne is not referred to. No reference is made to what happens to the petition when a sequestration order is made or the petition is dismissed. With great respect, it is doubtful if it is correct to say that a petition may be brought to an end by the making of a sequestration order on the petition or by the dismissal or withdrawal of the petition. Sub-section 52(4) provides that a petition lapses in the circumstances specified "unless, before the expiration of whichever of those periods is applicable, a sequestration order is made on the petition or the petition is dismissed or withdrawn". In one sense it might be said that on the happening of any of those events, the life of the petition is brought to an end, but Clyne shows that the petition, though said to be dead, may be resurrected and that as a result of that resurrection the petition never lapsed. The making of the order prevents the petition lapsing.

35. On the facts of the present case, I am bound to follow Clyne.

36. In the result, on the whole of the evidence I am satisfied that the debtor, on 23 August 1988, committed the act of bankruptcy alleged in the petition and I am satisfied with the proof of the other matters of which sub-section 52(1) of the Bankrutpcy Act requires proof. Accordingly, the order of the Deputy District Registrar made on 27 February 1990 should be set aside and a sequestration order should be made against the estate of the debtor.
