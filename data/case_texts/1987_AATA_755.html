NT86/7645-47 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 755; (1987) 18 ATR 3837; 87 ATC 967 (24 August 1987)

Last Updated: 24 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Allowable deductions - Pharmacist employed as pharmacy manager by his wife - remuneration package - joint borrowing to finance purchase by wife - interest paid jointly -whether interest deductible by husband - terms of engagement.

Income Tax Assessment Act (1936) Cth S51

Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay's) Ltd v FCT [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295

Ronpibon Tin NL v FCT

Tongkah Compound NL v FCT [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47

Ure v. FCT 81 ATC 4100

And : COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

AAT Decision No 3731

Peter M Roach (Senior Member)

The Tribunal varies the decisions under review by reducing the taxable income of the Applicant by $3,429; $4,759; $5,190 in the years of income ended 30th June 1981, 1983 and 1984 respectively.

P M Roach

Senior Member

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No NT36/7645-7

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

REASONS FOR DECISION

Mr P M Roach (Senior Member)

  1. These three references relate to assessments for income tax and arise out of the affairs of a registered pharmacist and his family. They relate to the years of income ending 30th June, 1981, 1983 and 1984. (I was advised by the Commissioner's representative at the hearing that, in relation to the 1982 year of income, the claim made by the Applicant was inadvertently allowed.)
  2. At the time of hearing the Applicant was a successfully married man of nearly 30 years standing and the father of three children who are now adult. In. 1963, as a young married man conducting his own suburban pharmacy business in Sydney, he decided to sell his pharmacy business to his wife and from that date forward it is agreed that the business was owned by the wife. The sale was intended (inter alia) to free the Applicant from restrictions proposed by the then pending Pharmacy Act (No 43 of 1964 NSW) as to ownership of multiple pharmacies (cf 325 (2)). Upon his evidence he enjoyed the confidence of his wife - a confidence manifested by the fact that he exercised complete control over the management of the pharmacy business, being "employed" by his wife to do so.

3. For some years leading up to 1979 the business was conducted from premises owned by the husband. As befits a successful marriage , and as is common place in many commercial situations where there is complete mutuality of confidence, the relationship between husband and wife as landlord and tenant and the employment relationship between them were not formalised by documentation or precisely defined by detailed terms. (During the hearing the employment relationship was referred to as a "master and servant" relationship. I find that confusing because the "master" in these circumstances was the "servant". Further, to have referred to "husband and wife" as "servant and mistress" would be even less appropriate.) As "Manager" of the business, the husband was able to determine, and did determine, what rent would be paid to him by his wife for the use of the premises which he wholly owned; and what "salary" or remuneration would be paid to him for the services he rendered. That extended to power to determine how the net income generated by the business would be divided between them. That power was exercised by deciding what outgoings would be borne by the husband and what by the wife; and by deciding to what extent the wife would remunerate the husband for all his services.

4. For the year of income ended 30th June, 1978 his wife paid to him a "salary" of $8,000.00 and rent of $2,600.00. Of necessity those payments had a direct bearing on the profitability of the business for the wife. In that year her net profit was $8.473.00: $127.00 in excess of the rent and salary he derived from the business. In the following year rent and salary were $3,600.00 and $8,000.00 respectively and the wife's net profit from the business was $10,505.00; $1,095.00 less than the amounts derived by the husband from the business.

5. At the time one of the difficulties experienced by the business was competition from another Pharmacist trading nearby. When the proprietor of that business indicated his willingness to sell, the husband caused the prospect to be investigated and the accountants to the family carried out a feasibility study in which they contemplated borrowings of $80,000.00. In carrying out that feasibility in May 1979 they assessed assets and liabilities on a joint basis between husband and wife; and approached Profit and Loss projections on the same basis. They compared 1978 experiences with 1980 projections, assuming amalgamation of the two pharmacies in the premises of the latter pharmacy. They correctly projected that turnover would nearly double that of the first pharmacy; and that the gross profit would nearly double. They also projected that, excluding from account wages and rent to the Applicant and interest on borrowings, "profit" would increase from over $19,000 by just over 525,000 to more than $44,000. How much of that "profit" would accrue to each spouse would depend on the remuneration of the Applicant.

6. In the end result the wife of the Applicant purchased the goodwill, fixtures and fittings and stock in trade of the Vendor's business. The Vendor entered into an appropriate restrictive covenant and it became his obligation to ensure to the purchaser occupation of the Vendor's premises under a term lease from the lessors to the Vendor. The agreement for sale and assignment of lease (both in favour of the wife) were duly completed, following which the original business was transferred to the site of the newly acquired premises. Thereafter the two businesses were conducted as one. Having lost his wife as the rent-paying occupier of the freehold he owned, the husband was able to let these premises to third parties at a commercial rate arranged on an arm's length basis. In doing so he took care to ensure that, provided both leases ran to full term, his wife's tenancy of the new premises and his letting of the old would run for periods which were co-terminous. By doing so he ensured that for the balance of the initial term of the new premises no new pharmacy could be set up in the old premises and that, should the tenancy of the new premises not be extended, the wife's business could be re-established in its former premises.

7. To make all these things possible monies had to be borrowed. This was arranged on the basis that the husband and wife -jointly borrowed 570,000, securing payment of principal and interest against the title to their jointly-owned family residence. When the monies were advanced $15,130 was applied in discharging the subsisting first mortgage on the family residence; and, it was alleged, the balance was used in paying the purchase price and the wife's costs of and incidental to the purchase and thereafter any remaining was applied in due course in the business. (As to the latter, the monies were first invested by the wife at interest.) This was made possible because the husband allowed her the use of the monies. There was no formal agreement between them. No interest was sought by the husband and no interest was paid by the wife.

8. In July, 1982 the original mortgage was paid out and the couple borrowed $75,000 from another mortgagee. The additional $5,000 was applied by the wife in the conduct of the business.

9. In his returns of income the Applicant claimed as a deduction a proportion of all mortgage interest paid, treating the outgoing as a matter of equal obligation for his wife and himself, but in doing so acknowledging as "private" and not allowable only so much of the principal as was paid to the prior mortgagee. The soundness of the approach was not challenged. Nor was there any challenge to the calculations which followed. Accordingly, I approach the resolution of the matter on the basis that, if the arguments presented for the Commissioner fail, the Applicant is entitled in full to the deductions he claimed ($3,429, $4,759 and 35,190 respectively).

  1. I now refer to a matter not referred to by either side during the hearing - a circumstance confirmed when I called on the parties to appear again before me after I had analysed the returns of income presented in evidence. In the course of preparing these Reasons for Decision I discovered that, although the wife was tenant of the new premises and the Applicant was liable only as her guarantor, according to those returns he paid the rent for her in the first two years in issue. He claimed deductions for the rent so paid and the claims were allowed. The amounts were $6,539 and $9,359.
  2. That discovery caused a difficulty in that the evidence presented before me and the arguments advanced in the hearing were directed to the issues being as represented by the following table:

  1. It is unfortunate that the question only arose after the hearing was completed and the decision reserved. It is also unfortunate that the Commissioner had not included in the referred documents the tax returns of the Applicant, which were relevant to the definition and presentation of his claims to the deductions he sought. If those returns had been presented, even if only presented 43 hours, or even 24 hours, prior to the hearing the questions which arose could have been raised at the hearing and it would then have been open to both sides to call such evidence and address such arguments as they pleased. As that was not done I propose as a matter of fairness to exclude from consideration the additional factors so arising and to confine ray deliberations to the situation as represented by Table A.
  2. In determining to follow that course it is appropriate for me to observe that I express no view as to whether the allowance to the Applicant of a deduction for rent paid by the Applicant on behalf of his wife is deductible; or whether, assuming the deduction to have been incorrectly allowed, it was allowed in consequence of a mistake of fact or a mistake of law.
  3. As the evidence was presented I find that the husband did seek financial advantage out of the arrangements with his wife. He expected, as she did, that the re-constituted business would be more profitable and that he would participate in that increased profit in that he would receive increased remuneration at a rate to be determined by him. That expectation was fulfilled. In addition, he expected that he would be able to obtain a higher rental for his own premises from a family-related Trust which on-let to strangers than he had drawn from his wife in the past. That expectation was also fulfilled. The latter factor was significant because the rapid and substantial increases in rental income caused that source of income in some years to be more productive of income for the husband than ownership of the business was for the wife, even though major expenses, such as interest and rent, had been borne by the husband.

15. The Commissioner's representative commenced his argument by pointing to the decision of the High Court of Australia in Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay's) Ltd v FCT ((1935) [1935] HCA 81; 54 CLR 295): authority (inter alia) for the view that expenditure "completely disassociated from the gaining or production of the assessable the assessable income" is not deductible. As Dixon J. (as he then was) said (at p 331) in denying the claim to a deduction:-

"What is important is the entire lack of connection between the assessable income and the expenditure. None of the assessable income arose out of business in the course of which the taxpayer became liable to the charge. The sources from which the assessable income did arise included no operations in the course of which the payment was made. It was a payment independent of the production of the income, not an expenditure incurred in the course of its production"

16. He then cited the joint judgment of the High Court of Australia in Ronpibon Tin N.L. & Tongkah Compound NL v FCT [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 and the requirement expressed by the Court that:

"For expenditure to form an allowable deduction as an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income it must be incidental and relevant to that end." (p 56)

17. From there the argument moved from the general to the particular and reliance was placed on the decision of the High Court of Australia in FCT v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153. In that case the taxpayer had borrowed monies on the security of rent reducing properties. He applied the monies so borrowed in purchasing shares. Of the 20,000 shares purchased, 18,000 were gifted to his two sons and 2,000 held by the Taxpayer. He claimed a deduction pursuant to the provisions of the 1922 Income Tax Assessment Act which provided that no deduction would be allowed in respect of "money not wholly and exclusively laid out or expended for the production of assessable income." As Knox CJ said (at p 170):

"It is quite clear that the money borrowed from the bank was not so laid out or expended, for nine-tenths of the amount was applied directly or indirectly for the benefit of his two sons - directly as to the £18,000 paid for the shares given to them, and indirectly as to the advances made to the Company, in which they held nine-tenths of the shares."

For the moment I merely note that Section 51(1) of the 1986 Income Tax Assessment Act would have at least have allowed that taxpayer a deduction for so much of the interest paid as related to the shares held by that taxpayer for income producing purposes (c.f. Ronpibon (ante) as to apportionment - "to- the extent to which").

18 The Commissioner's representative then pointed to the authority of the decision of the Full Bench of the Federal Court in Ure v FCT 31 ATC 4,100. In that case the taxpayer, who had borrowed monies at an interest rate of 12.5% per annum, lent those monies to a third party at 1% per annum. The Commissioner allowed a deduction of interest but not exceeding the amount of interest derived. The Full Court upheld the assessment. In his reasons for decision, Brennan J. said:

"Section 51 requires that deductible expenditure be incurred "in" gaining assessable income, that is to say, it must be incidental and relevant to the gaining of that income (Ronpibon Tin NL & Tongkah Compound DLL v FC of T [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at p56). An outgoing interest may be incidental and relevant to the gaining of assessable income where the borrowed money is laid out for the purpose of gaining that income (FC of T v Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153 at pp 170, 171, 197; Texas Co (Australasia) Ltd v FC of T [1940] HCA 9; (1940) 63 CLR 382 at p 468). The laying out of the borrowed money for the purpose of gaining assessable income furnishes the required connection between the interest paid upon it by the taxpayer and the income derived by hime for its use.

"In the present case a question arises as to whether it can truly be said that the incurring of interest charges at rates of 7.5%, 8.5%, 10% and 12.5% per annum is

incidental to the gaining of income by way of interest at the rate of 1% per annum. The answer to that question does not turn directly upon the disparity in interest rates, but upon an examination of the purposes for which the money was laid out. The disparity in interest rates is itself eloquent to suggest the existence of purposes ulterior to the earning of interest at the rate of 1% per annum, and the evidence confirms the existence of further purposes........

.............where a question arises as to the purposes for which money is laid out by a taxpayer, it is erroneous to exclude as irrelevant evidence of the use of that money, albeit by others, in conformity with arrangements made by the taxpayer. That is not to deny the separate identity of those who use the borrowed money, nor to attribute vicariously to the taxpayer the activities of others. It is simply to take some relevant factors into account in determining the purposes for which money is laid out. Those purposes do not depend upon the state of the taxpayer's mind but upon what the taxpayer in the circumstances of the case is ascertained to have done in using and arranging for the use of the borrowed moneys.

The purposes for which money is laid out is an issue of fact, turning upon the objective circumstances which human experience would judge to be relevant to the issue (cf Magna Alloys and Research Pty Ltd v FC of T 80 ATC 4542 at p 4549). In the present case, there is an air of unreality about the proposition that the borrowed moneys were laid out wholly for the purpose of earning a return of 1% per annum. Rather is it right to say that the purpose for which the borrowed moneys were laid out included all the purposes earlier mentioned, only one of which was to earn a return of 1% per annum.

If the borrowed moneys had been laid out solely for the purpose of gaining assessable income, the interest would be wholly deductible; but as they were laid out in part for that purpose, and in part for other purposes "

19. On the basis of those authorities it is contended for the Commissioner that the "purpose" of the Applicant was nothing more than to place his wife in a situation whereby she could purchase the business of the Vendor pharmacist; and that the making of the loan was quite unrelated to the income derived by the Applicant, whether derived as a lessor of the original pharmacy premises or whether derived from his spouse as his employer in rewarding him for services rendered by him as an employee. I accept the authority of those decisions, and with it the obligation to apply in appropriate cases the principles they express. In my view the correct answer is to be determined by a analysis of the facts of this case.

  1. As to that, subject to what I have said in par. 10-13, one must take the factual situation as it was established by the conduct of those concerned. The Applicant and his wife might have been in partnership, but they were not. The Applicant and his wife might have bargained that, as between themselves and the mortgagee, she alone would be the borrower, and his remuneration reduced accordingly, but they did not. They might have arranged that she would pay him a sufficient sum by way of interest on the moneys borrowed to indemnify him against liability to the mortgagee, but they did not. They might have entered into a formal, detailed and binding written agreement drafted by Solicitors independently instructed which would have obliged the wife to continue employing the Applicant so long as he made available to her on loan interest-free the principal sum in question here and that in consideration of so doing in addition to a fixed, or determinable, salary she would pay him a bonus based upon increased turnover or increased gross profit or, subject to calculation of the bonus, increased net profit from the business, but they did not.
  2. Had they followed the latter course I am of opinion that the Applicant would have been entitled to the reduction he seeks. The entitlement to a deduction for interest expended is not conditioned upon deriving assessable income by way of interest. So are as is material here, the Act only requires that the loss or outgoing sought to be deducted be incurred in the course of deriving assessable income, whether it be interest or any other form of income, Entitlement to a deduction does not arise by reason of the degree of formality and precision adopted by the parties in establishing the relationship between them.
  3. I accept the evidence of the Applicant that the arrangements given effect to between the joint mortgagors permitting one to have the use of the moneys borrowed while each accepted liability was an arrangement which took effect in the context of the totality of the arrangements between them. I accept that it was arranged with a degree of informality appropriate to the matrimonial relationship which subsisted between them. The arrangement was that, in their mutual interest and with a view to the well-being of the family, the wife would own the business and the husband would manage it; that they would jointly borrow moneys to be wholly used by the wife in order to expand the business she owned; that the husband would exercise the widest powers in the management of the business in its original and in its expanded form; that it was to be expected that the expanded business would be more profitable; and that the rewards to be paid to the husband for his services in the management of that expanded business would be increased in a manner to be determined.
  4. In my view this case is not like either Munro (ante) or Ure (ante). In Munro, in so far as shares were gifted to the sons, there was no financial benefit in prospect for the father, let alone the prospect of the derivation of assessable income. In Ure the only prospect of financial benefit by way of assessable income to the lender was the prospect of receiving interest at the rate of 1% per annum. For this Applicant there was no such limitation or, subject to what I have said in pas. 10 to 13 (ante), any other which would make the outlay disproportionate to any return there might be. Nor, in my view, does it matter that (inter alia) the Applicant was motivated to assist his wife to increase her income or even to reduce the level of his income tax liability below what it might have been had he purchased the new business and operated it himself. Unlike Munro (ante) he was not making a "gift" of the moneys borrowed, and unlike Ure (ante) he was not making a "gift" without reward or income return to himself - of the interest on the moneys borrowed.
  5. In reaching that conclusion I have not taken into account the increased rental incomes from the premises owned by the Applicant. Without deciding the matter, I express doubts as to whether the payment of interest in relation to the operation and management of the re-structured business could be said to have any, or any sufficient nexus, to the derivation of income from the letting of other premises.
  6. In my view as the issues were presented before me the Applicant is entitled to succeed and I will order that the determinations of the Commissioner upon the objections be varied accordingly.

POSTSCRIPT

NT86/7645-47

After the foregoing reasons had been finalised and arrangements made for the decision to be formally handed down, the Applicant's adviser forwarded a submission to me and, as I was to learn later, to the Commissioner. At my request the parties appeared to receive the decision and, without having looked at the Applicant's submission, I made it available to the Commissioner's representative for perusal. At that time he had not received the copy forwarded to the Commissioner by the Applicant.

Bearing in mind the opportunity to seek a re-opening of the hearing already afforded to both parties by reason of the matters referred to in paragraphs 10 to 13 and being conscious of the decision I had reached, I decided that in fairness I should not consider the Applicant's further submission without affording the Commissioner opportunity to seek to re-open the case. I did so. When the Commissioner's representative elected to rest his case on the materials which had been presented earlier, I proceeded to formally hand down my decision without reading the Applicant's submission.
