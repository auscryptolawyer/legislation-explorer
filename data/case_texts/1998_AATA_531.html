REASONS FOR DECISION

15 July 1998 Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Mrs H M Pavlin (Member)

Dr K P Kennedy (Member)

1. The applicant sought review of a decision of the respondent dated 26 August 1997 in which a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation confirmed a decision implicit in correspondence of 13 March 1995 not to allow the applicant a period further to the thirty day period prescribed in s 154(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") in which to request reconsideration of the respondent's decision arising from action taken on 22 June 1984. Apparently by a self executing decision, the respondent accepted that the applicant had resigned and refunded his interest in the Superannuation Fund. As a result, it was said, he was not eligible for invalidity benefits as prescribed under s 66 of the Act. The applicant ceased duties with Telecom in March 1984 and later sought to be eligible for invalidity benefits under the Act on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties.

2. At the hearing, the applicant appeared in person and gave oral evidence. He also tendered a number of documents accepted into evidence as exhibits. The documents lodged in the Tribunal pursuant to s 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") were put into evidence as the T documents. The respondent was represented by Mr Dubé who relied upon the documents accepted into evidence and a further document that he tendered.

3. At the commencement of the hearing there was some debate as to the jurisdiction of the Tribunal and the necessity to grant an extension of time to review this matter. The respondent explained that an extension of time was granted on 7 February 1997 but not with regard to the cessation of duties in 1984. On 16 February 1995 the applicant in a letter addressed to the Commissioner requested a decision under either ss 7(1) or 7(2) of the Act (and an extension of time to review the decision of 22 June 1984 in which a benefit was paid under s 80 of the Act following his cessation of duties). In March 1995 the applicant was informed that further information was required for the consideration of the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme Board of Trustees No 2 ("the CSS Board") and in December 1995 the Board made its decision. No decision was made by the Commissioner in relation to an extension of time to review the initial decision of March 1984 as found by Senior Member Beddoe in Grice and Commissioner for Superannuation (AAT 11200; 10 September 1997).

4. The s 37 documents contained detailed correspondence from the applicant to the respondent in relation to this matter. In his evidence to the Tribunal, the applicant also referred to the s 37 documents in the earlier matter of Q97/318 which was marked as Exhibit 1 in this matter. The respondent provided a chronology of proceedings which was marked as Exhibit 2.

BACKGROUND

5. There was no dispute that on 21 April 1955 the applicant became a contributor under the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act") by virtue of his employment with the then PMG. On 1 July 1976 the applicant became an eligible employee within the meaning of the Act. On 13 March 1984 the applicant tendered a "letter of resignation" to his employer Telecom and on 22 June 1984 he received a payment of $9,375.49 under s 80 of the Act being a lump sum payment of his accumulated contributions. Section 80(1) provides:

80(1) Subject to subsections (2) and (3), where:

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee otherwise than by reason of

death; and

(b) the person is not entitled to benefit under Division 1, 2 or 4 of this

Part or under Division 3 of Part IX;

the person is entitled to a lump sum benefit, payable out of the Fund, of an amount equal to the person's accumulated contributions.

Other relevant provisions of the Act include s 66 within Division 4 which provides for payment of an "invalidity pension":

66. (1) Subject to subsections (3), (3A) and (4) of this section and to section 79, where a person ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining his or her maximum retiring age then, except in a case where subsection (2) of this section applies, the person is entitled:

(a) if the person does not make an election under section 68 or 69 - to invalidity pension in accordance with section 67 and, where the person has paid supplementary contributions, a lump sum benefit in accordance with that section;

(b) if the person makes an election under section 68 - to invalidity pension, and a lump sum benefit, in accordance with that section; or

(c) if the person is entitled to make an election under section 69 and makes such an election - to a lump sum benefit in accordance with that section.

(2) Subject to subsections (3), (3A) and (4) of this section and section 79, where:

(a) a person ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining his or her maximum retiring age;

(b) there was in force in respect of the person, immediately before the person's retirement, a benefit classification certificate; and

(c) the Board is of the opinion that the incapacity which was the ground for his or her retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or such conditions; the person is entitled:

(d) where the period of contributory service of the person is not less than 8 years:

(i) if the person does not make an election under section 71 or 72 - to invalidity pension in accordance with section 70 and, where the person has paid supplementary contributions, a lump sum benefit in accordance with that section;

(ii) if the person makes an election under section 71 - to invalidity pension, and a lump sum benefit, in accordance with that section; or

(iii) if the person is entitled to make an election under section 72 and makes such an election - to a lump sum benefit in accordance with that section; or

(e) where the period of contributory service of the person is less than 8 years - to a lump sum benefit in accordance with section 73.

(2A) Where the Board is of the opinion that:

(a) a person will, in the near future, cease to be an eligible employee by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity before attaining the person's maximum retiring age;

(b) there will be in force in respect of the person, immediately before the person's retirement, a benefit classification certificate; and

(c) the incapacity which will be the ground for the person's retirement was caused, or was substantially contributed to, by a physical or mental condition or conditions specified in the certificate or by a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or such conditions;

then:

(d) the Board shall inform the person in writing that the Board had formed the opinions referred to in paragraphs (a), (b) and (c); and

(e) where the person becomes a person referred to in paragraph (2) (a) and to whom paragraph (2) (b) applies, the Board shall be taken to have formed the opinion referred to in paragraph (2) (c) in relation to that person immediately on the person becoming a person referred to in paragraph (2) (a) unless, whether before or after the person became a person so referred to, the Board informed the person in writing that this subsection is not to apply to the person.

(2B) Where the Board forms the opinion referred to in paragraph (2) (c) in relation to a person on a date after the date on which the person retired, any payment to that person before the first-mentioned date of benefit or of pension purported to be made under subsection (2) shall be deemed to have been as valid as it would have been if the Board had formed that opinion immediately on the person's retirement.

6. >From the documents before the Tribunal it appears that the applicant had been employed in a personnel role as a welfare officer and had also undertaken social work studies. In this regard he had sought a position as a social worker which he did not obtain. He did however over the years obtain promotions and served in positions on acting higher duties prior to his cessation of employment with Telecom.

7. On 17 December 1991 the applicant forwarded correspondence addressed to the "Australian Government Retirement Benefits Office" outlining the circumstances surrounding his resignation from Telecom and its requirement for him to undergo a medical examination on 30 March 1984 (Exhibit 1 T6/69). As a result of that examination the applicant was found fit for continued duty (Exhibit 1 T7/70). However it appears that the applicant formed the view that he could not return to work with his employer because of the alleged discrimination in the workplace which gave rise to an anxiety state. The applicant now contends that the advice that he was fit for duty was incorrect because the examining doctor had actually recommended redeployment, of which he was not advised at the time.

8. The applicant in his correspondence of 17 December 1991 requested the respondent's opinion as to his "entitlements following termination of his employment in 1984". The Commissioner responded on 7 January 1992 (Exhibit 1 T30/130) in relation to issues raised and the applicant's query as to the application of s 7(2) of the Act:

"I confirm my advice that it is very unlikely that an application, pursuant to that subsection, to the Superannuation Board of Trustees No 2 would be successful for the following reasons:

* the CMO found you fit for work just prior to the date of your resignation;

* subsequent to your resignation you were again found fit by the CMO for employment by the Commonwealth; and

* you were subsequently employed by the Department of Veterans' Affairs."

9. On 9 January 1992 the applicant requested consideration under s 7(2) of the Act being assessment on the grounds of invalidity, s 7 then provided:

"7(1) A reference in this Act to the retirement of an eligible employee on the ground of invalidity shall be read as a reference to his having been retired, or his services having been otherwise terminated, on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties.

7(2) Where a person has, before attaining his or her maximum retiring age ceased to be an eligible employee otherwise than by reason of death or by reason of his or her having been retired, or his services having been otherwise terminated on the ground of invalidity because of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties, but the Board is satisfied that, at the time he ceased to be an eligible employee, he was, by reason of physical or mental incapacity unfit to perform his duties, the Board may direct that the person shall, for the purposes of this Act, be deemed to have been retired on the ground of invalidity because of that physical or mental incapacity."

At the time of the applicant ceasing duty in 1984 the exercise of the powers and functions in s 7(2) vested in the respondent Commissioner.

10. In response to that correspondence the Commissioner requested further details from the applicant in order to pursue investigation of the matter and also sought medical records from Telecom (Exhibit 1 T9/75 and T31/131). On 5 May 1992 the applicant withdrew his application reserving his rights to reapply at a later stage if needed (Exhibit 1 T10/77). On 16 February 1995 (Exhibit 1 T11/78-79) the applicant forwarded further correspondence to the Commissioner again outlining his allegations of discrimination and the circumstances surrounding his cessation of employment with Telecom. He stated:

"I believe I am entitled to an invalidity pension, and accordingly make application. I understand that process first requires consideration under either ss 7(1) or ss 7(2) of the Act".

11. The Case Manager of the Invalidity Assessment Section in a letter dated 11 December 1995 (after presumably being provided with the documentation as requested under an earlier letter of 13 March 1995 (Exhibit 1 T12/80-81)) advised that the CSS Board had decided on 7 December 1995 to exercise its discretion to not direct that the applicant be deemed to have retired on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform duties under s 7(2) of the Act. Reasons for that decision were provided to the applicant on 13 February 1996 (Exhibit 1 T16/89).

12. The applicant then sought review of the Board decision by the Commissioner for Superannuation under s 154 of the Act on the basis that he "retired on 13 March 1984 on account of invalidity within the meaning of s 7(1) of the Act". Included in reasons for that request was "...My application was not an application pursuant to ss 7(2), unless that provision can be read as conferring a right in my specific case." (29 December 1995; Exhibit 1 T14/83).

13. The respondent replied on 4 January 1996 advising that a decision of the CSS Board can only be reviewed under s 153AM of the Act and not by the Commissioner as provided under s 154. The applicant was advised that s 153AN(1) required the payment of a then current fee of $150 as prescribed under the Superannuation (CSS) Fees Regulations which the applicant was requested to pay and to also provide further evidence not taken into account previously if he wished to pursue the reconsideration (Exhibit 1 T15/85). The relevant provisions relating to review of a Board decision and a decision by the Commissioner are as follows:

"153AM. (1) A person affected by a decision made by the Board may apply to

the Board for a reconsideration of that decision.

(2) An application must:

(a) be in writing addressed to the Board; and

(b) set out the particulars of the decision that the person wishes to be reconsidered; and

(c) specify the grounds for reconsideration of those particulars.

(3) An application made to the Board before the commencement of this section for the reconsideration of a decision made by the Board under subsection 7(2) is taken to have been duly made under this section.

153AN. (1) Such fees as are prescribed are payable to the Board by a person

who makes an application under section 153AM.

(2) Any fees received by the Board under subsection (1) are to be paid into the Consolidated Revenue Fund.

(3) The regulations may make provision in relation to the refund of any fees paid under subsection (1).

153AP. (1) A decision is to be reconsidered only if there is evidence relevant to the decision that was not previously taken into account by the Board in making the decision.

(2) If an application is not supported by evidence in accordance with subsection (1), the Board must dismiss the application.

(3) The dismissal of an application in respect of a decision does not preclude the applicant from subsequently submitting another application in respect of the decision.

`Superseded Act' means the Superannuation Act 1922.

154. (1) In this section:

`decision' has the same meaning as in the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975;

`reviewable decision' means a decision of the Commissioner, or a delegate of the Commissioner, under this Act, under the superseded Act or under the regulations made under either of those Acts, and includes a decision of the Superannuation Board, or a delegate of the Superannuation Board (other than a decision under section 141 of the superseded Act);

`Superannuation Board' means the Superannuation Board established by the superseded Act.

(2) A person affected by a reviewable decision who is dissatisfied with the decision may, by notice in writing given to the Commissioner within the period of 30 days after the day on which the decision first comes to the notice of the person, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, request the Commissioner to reconsider the decision.

...

(4) Upon receipt of the request, the Commissioner shall reconsider the decision and may confirm or revoke the decision or vary the decision in such manner as he thinks fit.

(5) Where the Commissioner confirms, revokes or varies a decision, he shall, by notice in writing served either personally or by post on the person who made the request, inform the person of the result of his reconsideration of the decision and his reasons for confirming, revoking or varying the decision, as the case may be.

(6) Applications may be made to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review of reviewable decisions that have been confirmed or varied under subsection 4 and for review of decisions of the Superannuation Board, or delegates of the Superannuation Board, made under section 141 of the superseded Act.

Where s 3(1) provides:

`Board' means the CSS Board established under section 27A;"

14. It appears that the applicant failed to comply with the requirements as notified for review by the Board as the applicant advised the Commissioner on 19 February 1996 that he would take the matter to this Tribunal. There followed a further series of correspondence between the applicant and Commissioner and a Member of Parliament acting on behalf of the applicant and the Commissioner.

15. On 14 May 1997 the CSS Board again wrote to the applicant advising that a payment of $150 was required in order to allow reconsideration of the Board decision of 7 December 1995.

16. On 7 February 1997 the applicant made a further request to the Commissioner under s 154(2) for reconsideration review of the payment of benefit on 22 June 1984 pursuant to s 80 of the Act and the decision of 5 February 1992 to refuse to pay a benefit under s 66 of the Act (Exhibit 1 T19/93). On 13 February 1997 the applicant's letter was acknowledged by the Assistant Director, Civilian and Military Reconsiderations (Exhibit 1 T88/271). The applicant again wrote to the Commissioner on 6 May 1997 (Exhibit 1 T10/29) requesting a review under s 154 of the Act of the decision of 11 February 1994 in response to his letter of 4 December 1993 not to pay a benefit pursuant to s 66 of the Act. That application was stated as being in addition to the application of 7 February 1997 and made reference to the fact that the applicant considered he met the requirements of s 7(1) of the Act. On 14 May 1997 the Assistant Director (Exhibit 1 T12/37) advised of the Commissioner's position raising the following points:

* the letter of 11 February 1994 was not a decision but a general explanation of the steps for an employer to initiate the process for assessing whether an eligible employee should be retired on the grounds of invalidity and also gave an explanation of s 7(2) of the Act;

* the letter of 16 February 1996 may be considered as a request for reconsideration of the 1984 decision and the letters of 7 February 1997 and 6 May 1997 may be considered as in continuation of that request;

* the letter of 7 February 1997 is being treated as a request for an extension of time to make a request for reconsideration of the decision of 22 June 1984.

17. A delegate of the Commissioner on 26 August 1997 under s 154(4) confirmed the decision as follows

"... CONFIRM the decision, implicit in correspondence of 13 March 1995, not to allow Mr Grice a period further to the 30 day period prescribed in subsection 154(2) in which to request reconsideration of the decision, implicit in action taken on 22 June 1984 to pay him a refund of his accumulated contributions in accordance with section 80 of the Act, that for the purposes of subsection 7(1) of the Act, when he ceased to be an eligible employee on 13 March 1984 he was not retired, or his services were not otherwise terminated, on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties and hence could not be regarded as having been retired on the ground of invalidity and as such entitled to an invalidity benefit pursuant to section 66."

18. By way of application lodged on 18 September 1997 in this Tribunal the applicant sought review of the decision of the delegate refusing to grant the extension of time for review of the decision of June 1984. An application had also been lodged on 7 April 1997 in the matter of Q97/318 seeking review of the decision of "around 12 March 1997" which was not formally given but being noted as confirming a decision not to pay a benefit pursuant to s 66 of the Act. In that matter Senior Member Beddoe decided on 7 July 1997 for reasons given orally that the Tribunal did not have jurisdiction to hear the matter (AAT 11200; 10 September 1997) however it was agreed that the respondent would thereafter undertake a reconsideration of the deemed refusal. That reconsideration decision was made on 26 August 1997 (T2) following the hearing of Q97/318 in the Tribunal.

19. This present matter therefore concerns only a review of the "refusal" of 13 March 1995 in not exercising a discretion to grant a further period to request reconsideration of the decision under s 154(2).

APPLICANT'S EVIDENCE AND SUBMISSIONS

20. The applicant provided detailed and copious evidence as to the merits of the matter and the circumstances surrounding his cessation of employment with Telecom which essentially he considered to have arisen from alleged discrimination and practices of management in relation to promotions and other matters. The applicant contended that these circumstances gave rise to a health condition for which he was forced to take leave and which he considered would be aggravated upon return to the same workplace the subject of the allegations. At the time it appears that he felt he had no option but to leave the workplace and as he was unable to obtain long service leave he therefore considered the only course open to maintain his health was to tender a resignation. The applicant stated he tendered the resignation as a conditional resignation being a resignation on health grounds and not a voluntary resignation. He was requested to undergo a medical examination in March 1984 and was advised that he was considered fit to continue work. The applicant did not return to his former workplace and subsequently commenced employment with the Department of Veterans Affairs.

21. The applicant's major point of grievance was the fact that he was not aware of the actual outcome of his medical examination prior to his resignation being accepted. He considered he had been misled by Telecom as to the outcome of the examination and was not made aware of the recommendation of redeployment by the Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO") until some considerable time later. It is on this basis that he submitted that he should be eligible to be deemed to have retired on the grounds of invalidity. The applicant discussed in detail the possible interpretation and application of ss 7(1) and 7(2) to his circumstances and made submissions in this regard relying upon the Tribunal decisions in Brown v Commissioner for Superannuation (1991) 23 ALD 655, Scott v Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 8 AAR 20, and Hastings and Commissioner for Superannuation (1986) 4 AAR 376.

RESPONDENT'S SUBMISSIONS

22. The respondent's submissions outlined the legislative history of s 7 of the Act and the effect of the amendments introduced in 1990. As to the review under s 154(2) of the Act it was submitted on the basis of Hall v Nominal Defendant [1966] HCA 36; (1965) 117 CLR 423; ALR 705; [1966] HCA 36; 40 ALJR 102 that there was a wide discretion offered to consider review outside the 30 day period provided. As to the principles to be applied, the judgment of Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd and Others v Cohen (1992) 58 ALR 305 was cited as the leading authority for a consideration for extension of time, albeit that judgment dealt with s 11 of the Administrative Decisions (Judicial Review) Act 1977 ("the ADJR Act").

23. As regards s 29(7) of the AAT Act which deals with extensions of time for applications before the Tribunal, Justice O'Connor in Mulheron v Australian Telecommunications Authority (1991) 23 ALD 309 at 314 stated:

"The principles to be applied in considering an application for extension of time under s 29 (7) were considered by Deputy President Todd in Re Johnson and Commonwealth of Australia (unreported, AAT 5619, 5 January 1990). Briefly, these are:

(a) Prima facie proceedings commenced outside the prescribed period will not be entertained. An extension of time will be granted, however, if it is proper to do so.

(b) It is relevant whether the applicant rested on his rights or took action to make the decision-maker aware that the decision was being contested.

(c) Any prejudice to the respondent that would be caused by granting the extension of time is relevant.

(d) Any wider prejudice to the general public in terms of disruption to established practices is relevant.

(e) The merits of the substantial application are relevant.

(f) Fairness of granting the extension of time as between the applicant and other persons in a like position is relevant."

24. It is these factors as stated in Mulheron which the respondent submitted should be applied in reviewing the discretion to extend time to review the decision in this matter. Essentially the respondent submitted that the applicant was fully aware of his circumstances and rights with respect to superannuation and that he had failed to take action until December 1991 when he requested the respondent's opinion as to his entitlements. As there has now been a 14 year passage of time since the date of cessation of employment, it was submitted that the respondent would be prejudiced as would the general interests of the public. Further, the merits of the matter were not considered to be sufficient to allow an extension of time. In this regard the applicant had placed great emphasis on the operation of s 7(1) of the Act which he contended applied to his circumstances by the use of the words "otherwise terminated on the grounds of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties". The respondent submitted that s 7(1) had no operation in these circumstances as the words "otherwise terminated" do not apply to resignations under s 39 of the Act or under the Telecommunications Act 1975 . In relation to the operation of s 7(1) both the applicant and respondent relied upon the full Tribunal decision in Brown and Commissioner for Superannuation (supra).

FINDINGS

25. As is evident, this matter has had a somewhat convoluted history in part attributable to the applicant's penchant for detailed discourse relating to his employment circumstances arising from more than 14 years ago and his grievances in relation to that employment. It appears that this has resulted in perhaps some misinterpretation and frustration on the part of the respondent in dealing with and replying to the voluminous correspondence from the applicant. Those circumstances in themselves do not influence us in the review of this matter but we note them by way of comment in addressing what may be perceived as the unorthodox factual path this matter has traversed. It was the applicant's right to seek review of the decision he is primarily aggrieved with - that is the decision of the respondent in June 1984 to accept his "resignation" although the applicant had indicated that the resignation was offered as a result of the condition he claimed he suffered and which he contended arose as a result of the duties and circumstances of his employment with Telecom.

26. In his evidence the applicant discussed in detail those circumstances and also provided supporting documents which were marked as exhibits. The applicant was quite obviously unhappy with what can be described as the sometimes subjective approach taken in granting promotions and other entitlements such as leave to officers of Telecom. We accept that he may have reason to perceive inequities in the processes of his employer particularly when applied in relation to a person such as the applicant who was clearly ambitious. On our observations of the applicant, it may have been that those perceptions may well have arisen because of the applicant's demeanour and intensity in approach with his superiors and co-workers (for example see Exhibit G). However this is not to derogate from earlier favourable reports received by the applicant in relation to his work performance (Exhibit A). We will deal with the relevance of this material further in considering the merits of the matter under review. We also make these observations to assist in explaining in part our findings of fact concerning this matter.

27. The crux of the applicant's discontent and claim seems to relate to the workplace difficulties adverted to above. It is on the basis of these difficulties and their effect that the applicant tendered his "conditional" resignation. In considering this resignation, the applicant's employer took the appropriate action in referring the applicant for medical examination. This action appears to accord with the employer's resignation policy guidelines in order to determine whether separation should be on the grounds of invalidity (Exhibit J - Clause F1/2/5). The employer informed the applicant in writing on 10 April 1984 that on the basis of the examination he was found fit for continued duty. As a result his resignation was processed as effective from 13 March 1984 and a superannuation refund form forwarded for completion.

28. A copy of the applicant's sick leave records for the period October 1980 to November 1983 (Exhibit H) shows that the applicant took sick leave on a number of occasions and for 8 September 1983 to 13 October 1983 the nature of the illness was recorded as "anxiety state". That is the only entry for "anxiety state" on the sick leave record. Following that no further sick leave was recorded until late November 1983 when another three days was taken for an unspecified condition. In early 1984 it appears that the applicant sought re-crediting of sick leave and also sought to take long service leave which was denied (Exhibits E and G). No evidence was provided as to the continuation of the anxiety condition nor any medical reports disputing the findings of the CMO that the applicant was fit to return to duty. The applicant took issue with that finding as he contended he was later informed that the CMO had in addition to making a finding of being fit for continued duty also recommended a deployment to a different workplace. The applicant says this was not initially communicated to him and there is no documentary material before us to suggest otherwise. It is the evidence of the applicant and the applicant's correspondence which adverts to this recommendation of deployment in addition to the finding of being fit for continued duty. This issue goes to the merits of the matter however it is quite clear that the merits are not sufficiently compelling in view of the CMO finding that the applicant was nevertheless fit for continued duty. The recommendation of deployment does not diminish this finding - it was in addition to it and it appears that the decision of Telecom in accepting the resignation was sound.

Jurisdiction of Tribunal

29. Such a finding on the merits is required under the principles to be applied in this matter and as established by the case law which principles will be considered further. Before proceeding in that direction it is prudent at this point to address certain procedural issues in establishing the jurisdiction of this Tribunal to review the decision. As indicated above the applicant's prolixity in correspondence to all and sundry has tended to cloud the factual and procedural matrix surrounding this matter. The applicant's letter of 16 February 1995 to the Commissioner essentially gives rise to the present application to the Tribunal in which the applicant sought review under ss 7(1) or 7(2) of the Act. That request was dealt with by the CSS Board and only s 7(2) was considered. This Tribunal has no power to review the decision of the Board (except in relation to a decision under the 1922 Act defined in s 3(1) of the Act as the "Superseded Act") which in considering his application took cognisance of the applicant's continued service with the Commonwealth in two government Departments for periods from August 1986 to February 1988 and from April 1990 to August 1990 respectively. On obtaining the appointment for the first period with the Department of Veterans Affairs, the applicant was found fit for employment. However on his second appointment to another Department he was found fit for employment but did not meet the medical standard. It was noted that he resigned from both of those positions without seeking invalidity retirement. The CSS Board in its correspondence excluded the application of s 7(1) in its determination.

30. There was no response by the Commissioner (as opposed to the CSS Board) to the request of February 1995 - it must therefore be considered whether the Commissioner has made a decision and if so whether the Commissioner has power to make a decision under s 7(1). The Board's power is confirmed only in relation to decisions arising under s 7(2) for which the Tribunal has no jurisdiction to review. Such decisions under s 7(2) are not "reviewable decisions" in accordance with s 154(1). A decision of the Commissioner under the Act or Superseded Act is however subject to review by the Tribunal as a "reviewable decision". Section 154(2) provides that a person affected by a reviewable decision may apply for review of the decision by the Commissioner within 30 days of notice or such further period as allowed. A discretion is therefore provided to the Commissioner in order to grant an extension of time for review of the original decision of 1984.

31. In this matter the request for review to the Commissioner was made several years after the 1984 decision. The Commissioner did not respond to the applicant's request of February 1995 so that the matter proceeded upon a deemed "refusal" to grant an extension of time although there was no such consideration by the Commissioner and no formal notice of the refusal. By agreement between the parties in previous proceedings, the Commissioner undertook to make a reconsideration decision of the deemed "refusal". In hindsight, it may have been more appropriate for the Commissioner to make an initial review decision in writing in response to the request of February 1995 prior to the reconsideration decision of 26 August 1997. As there is no time limit as to when the Commissioner must make a decision under s 154 of the Act following the receipt of a request it may well be considered that such a decision has not in effect been made in order to allow the operation of s 154 and the jurisdiction of this Tribunal. If this is the case then the Tribunal would have no jurisdiction to hear the matter.

32. In order to clarify this issue the following summary sets out the factual situation:

Decision 1

Primary decision - March 1984 - applicant not retired on grounds of invalidity (Decision 1)

Request for reconsideration of decision of March 1984 - 16/2/95 (initial request of 17/12/91 withdrawn on 5/5/92 and followed by request of 16/2/95 for consideration under either s 7(1) or 7(2)). Section 154(2) applies providing application within 30 days of decision. Commissioner may exercise discretion to reconsider the decision beyond the 30 day period.

No notification by Commissioner as to (i) whether will reconsider and allow extension beyond 30 days and if so (ii) whether the Commissioner confirms revokes or varies the decision in accordance with s 154(4). No notice given under s 154(5) as to whether confirms revokes or varies.

Decision 2

Implied that letter of 13/3/95 was a "refusal" to extend time beyond 30 days for reconsideration in accordance with s 154(2) above and thus a "decision" of the Commissioner in refusing to exercise a discretion. This now prima facie becoming a "reviewable decision" (Decision 2).

Request of 29/12/95 for reconsideration of Board decision of 7/12/95 (and further requests of 7 February 1997 and May 1997 for review of decision under s 154(2) to review decision of March 1984) construed by respondent as request for reconsideration of Decision 2.

Reconsideration of Decision 2 - 26 August 1997 (Implied extension of time granted by exercising discretion of Commissioner under s 154(2)) - Decision under review before Tribunal in this matter.

33. In reference to the facts, whether the letter of 13 March 1995 constitutes a "decision" by the Commissioner under s 154(1) must be considered. "Decision" has the same meaning as in s 3(3) of the AAT Act which definition accords with the meaning under s 3(2) of the ADJR Act. In Australian Broadcasting Tribunal v Bond [1990] HCA 33; (1990) 94 ALR 11; 64 ALJR 462; 170 CLR 321; 21 ALD 1 Mason CJ observed, it was:

"... clear that a 'decision to which this Act applies' must be a decision of an administrative character, that it may be made in the exercise of a discretion, and that it must be made under an enactment. But these characteristics provide little guidance as to the meaning of the word 'decision' upon which the definition in s34(1) is based."

Mason CJ went on to say:

"...Another essential quality of a reviewable decision is that it be a substantive determination. With the exception of s3(2)(g), the instances of decision mentioned in s3(2) are all substantive in character. Moreover, the provisions in sub-ss(1),(2),(3) and (5) of s 3 point to a substantive determination. In this context the reference in s3(2)(g) to `doing or refusing to do any other act or thing' (emphasis added) should be read as referring to the exercise or refusal to exercise a substantive power. I do not perceive in s16(1)(b) or in para (e) of Sch 1 or para (1) of Sch 2 to the ADJR Act any contrary implication. These exclusions from the ADJR Act or from s13 appear to have been introduced for more abundant caution and it would be unwise to take too much from them."

34. Section 3(3) of the AAT Act provides:

(3) A reference in this Act to a decision includes a reference to:

(a) making, suspending, revoking or refusing to make an order or determination;

(b) giving, suspending, revoking or refusing to give a certificate, direction, approval, consent or permission;

(c) issuing, suspending, revoking or refusing to issue a licence, authority or other instrument;

(d) imposing a condition or restriction;

(e) making a declaration, demand or requirement;

(f) retaining, or refusing to deliver up, an article; or

(g) doing or refusing to do any other act or thing.

35. In this instance the Commissioner has not made a decision to reconsider nor has he refused to do so as provided under subparagraphs (a) and (g). A "refusal" connotes a consideration of the matter or some positive action which conveys non-acceptance such as discussed in QT94/289-291 and Commissioner of Taxation 96 ATC 413; [1996] AATA 694; 33 ATR 1005. In this instance there does not seem to be such positive action as to convey non-acceptance so as to constitute the inaction of the Commissioner as a "decision" or a "reviewable decision" under s 154. Nor does it appear to be a substantive determination with respect to either subparagraph as the Commissioner did not make a determination. Therefore the deemed "refusal" may not be properly considered as a "decision" so as to satisfy the requirement of being a "reviewable decision". The result would then be that this Tribunal does not have jurisdiction to take the matter further in accordance with s 154(6) even though a reconsideration has been made of the "decision" which is better referred to as an "implied decision" rather than an "implied refusal".

Discretion to grant further period of time

36. The respondent proceeded on the basis that the implied decision was properly made and as a consequence a reconsideration decision also properly made. If, contrary to what we have stated above, this is the case then we must address the arguments raised in relation to the implied exercise of the discretion of the Commissioner refusing to grant a further period in excess of the 30 days prescribed for lodging an application for reconsideration of the first decision.

37. To do so, the Tribunal stands in the shoes of the Commissioner in considering whether the discretion should be granted under s 154(2) in accordance with the phrase "or within such further period as the Commissioner allows" beyond the 30 days for making such a request. It is accepted that the principles to be applied in addressing whether a discretion should be exercised so as to grant an extension of time to allow reconsideration are the principles enunciated by Justice O'Connor in Mulheron v Australian Telecommunications Authority (supra) approving Deputy President Todd in Re Johnson and Commonwealth of Australia (supra) (as relates to s 29(7) of the AAT Act) as stated above. For these purposes we must accept that the Commissioner made a decision refusing to allow a further period of time for application to review.

38. In considering the factors as enunciated by O'Connor J in relation to the facts of this case, we find as follows.

Proceedings commenced outside the prescribed period will not be entertained, except if it is proper to do so

39. In Lucic v Nolan (1982) 45 ALR 411 at 416 the prima facie rule was stated by Fitzgerald J:

"...time limits are not absolute...None the less, it must, in my opinion, be accepted that it has deliberately been made the prima facie rule that proceedings not commenced in time should not be entertained."

40. So that proceedings should not be entertained out of time as a matter of policy and while exceptional or special circumstances need not be shown to displace such a presumption, each individual case should be considered on its facts. The applicant needs however to show an acceptable explanation for the delay that it is fair and equitable in the circumstances: Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Minister for Home Affairs and Environment (1992) 58 ALR 305 at 311 per Wilcox J.

41. On the facts as found the applicant delayed at least seven years until December 1991 but then later withdrew and reapplied for reconsideration of the decision some further four years later. While there was some confusion and queries as to the operation of the relevant provisions of the Act, the applicant sought advice from the Commissioner some number of years later and after ceasing his further periods of employment with other Commonwealth Departments. From the evidence we do not consider that this is an acceptable explanation for the quite considerable delay. In that period of delay the applicant had proceeded with certain review action and then withdrew that action however reserving his rights. The legislation provides a time limit for review and a discretion with respect to that time limit however the circumstances are not such as to reasonably account for the delay and furthermore should not, in our view, displace the prima facie rule as to time limits as expressed by Fitzgerald J.

Whether the applicant rested on his rights or took action to make the decision-maker

aware that the decision was being contested.

42. Following the decision of March 1984 the applicant apparently did not make contact with the respondent concerning his superannuation entitlements until 1991. It can reasonably be inferred that after such an effluxion of time the respondent would consider the decision of March 1984 to be a final decision not to be contested. The applicant's evidence was that he had become aware in 1985 of the actual content of the CMO's report however he failed to take action at that stage. The 1991 request for review followed the striking out of an action for damages in the claimed amount of some $8 million against the respondent in the Supreme Court of Queensland arising from the same circumstances of employment. There is no evidence as to the date of the commencement of that action in the Supreme Court of Queensland (c.f. Petrovska and Telstra Corp Ltd (1993) 31 ALD 767). We infer from the facts that some considerable delay occurred between date of resignation and institution of the Supreme Court of Queensland proceedings and consider that this delay is sufficient to find that the applicant rested on his rights.

Prejudice to the respondent

43. The respondent administers legislation intended to benefit persons employed by the Commonwealth and in that sense represents the Commonwealth. We do not perceive that there is any prejudice to the Commonwealth other than prejudice caused by delayed decision making and diminishing memory.

Wider prejudice to the general public

44. As stated by von Doussa J in Commissioner for Superannuation v Boardman (1994) 50 FCR 236 at 245:

"Where a time limit is one arising under procedural rules governing dispute resolution there is the overshadowing object of all litigation, to bring disputation between citizens to an end for the public good, and to do so sooner rather than later. There is a public interest in enforcing procedural time limits which extends beyond the immediate parties."

45. The public interest in enforcing time limits and bringing about the timely resolution of litigious matters is one which has received increasing importance over the years. For the reasons as stated above there has been a considerable delay on the part of the applicant in seeking review. To exercise a discretion to extend time for review at this stage after such a delay does not promote such timely resolution of matters nor serve the public interest in that regard.

46. Further, the applicant has engaged in a considerable amount of correspondence with all concerned and as well has taken action in the Supreme Court in relation to his dissatisfaction with his former employer. Particularly in the matter before Lee J in the Supreme Court the applicant appears to have utilised considerable amounts of the Court's time and resources in addressing this issue to the detriment of dealing with other pending matters which his Honour alludes to in his judgment. Likewise in this Tribunal the applicant again availed himself of the Tribunal's limited time and resources in conducting his matter by engaging in a reiteration of all work place issues of some 14 years ago with which he was still dissatisfied. This Tribunal is charged with the duty to review decisions of certain Commonwealth decision makers de novo. In these proceedings it is not the role of the Tribunal to review the decision and actions of Telecom (which is not a party to these proceedings) nor to make any findings as to possible maladministration by that organisation. Much of the applicant's evidence concerned his allegations in this regard which obviously has caused him a great deal of distress. However it is not in the public interest nor within the jurisdiction of this Tribunal to allow the review to be concerned by the grievances of the applicant particularly where the issues about which he complains have previously been considered by the Supreme Court of Queensland.

Merits of the substantial application

47. The applicant argued the application of s 7(1) of the Act as assisting his case. He sought to attribute a wide meaning to the words of that subsection so that his circumstances may come within that provision. We need not make a finding as to whether this argument may be successful nor indeed may we make a conclusive finding on the merits. By way of comment, we do however consider the applicant's argument as to the application of the Full Tribunal's decision in Brown v Commissioner for Superannuation (supra). In that case it was accepted that the annulled appointment of a probationary officer of the public service in accordance with the Public Service Act 1922 following an unsatisfactory medical report was not outside the operation of s 7(1). The Tribunal stated that the perceived intention was not to construe the language of the Act narrowly but to allow it to apply to a variety of authorities and bodies and to diverse factual situations. The effect of s 7(1) is that the Act is to be construed where mention is made of retirement on the ground of invalidity as a person having been retired or otherwise terminated on grounds of physical and mental incapacity for the purposes of s 7(2) thus qualifying for invalidity entitlements.

48. On the facts here, there would appear to be a difference between retirement and resignation albeit a medical examination was conducted. The applicant in this instance took action to resign, he was not in our view terminated from his employment with Telecom by the employer. The applicant in his evidence agreed that his "resignation" was a conditional resignation. The conditional aspect was removed upon medical examination arranged by the respondent and the resignation later accepted. This in our view does not constitute retirement for the purposes of s 7(1). In any event the applicant went on to undertake other employment with Commonwealth Departments following that resignation, this fact pointing against a finding that the applicant retired from the workforce on invalidity grounds. The Act does not define "retire". Further whether "otherwise terminated" in s 7(1) relates to a resignation is arguable. This phrase in the context of that provision would seem to connote action taken by the employer to cause the employee to cease employment. In this instance the opposite seems to have prevailed. The applicant as employee took action to cease his employment by tendering a resignation which, although not accepted immediately, was later accepted by the employer. This was action initiated by the applicant as an employee who chose to terminate his own services rather than the employer terminating his services. It would appear to us that this phrase cannot have the wide scope of application as contended by the applicant and in the context of the provision should be taken as referring to services being "otherwise terminated by the employer".

49. Next, whether the Commissioner can make a "decision" under s 7(1) must be considered. Section 7(1) does not establish a substantive decision making power but merely serves as a form of definition concerning the retirement of an eligible employee "on the ground of invalidity". Section 7(1) expands or defines what a ground of invalidity shall be read as when appearing in the Act. If this is applied to the term as used in s 7(2), it is now the CSS Board (previously the Commissioner) who can direct whether a person shall be deemed to have retired on the ground of invalidity. As stated previously the Tribunal has no power to review a CSS Board decision. Accordingly, we do not consider that the Commissioner is now capable of making a "decision" under s 7 as assumed by the applicant.

50. On the basis of the applicant's arguments as to the merits of the legal issues, the factual findings as relate to the outcome of the medical examination and the general circumstances surrounding the applicant's employment conditions we do not consider that the merits are sufficiently compelling in order to allow a further period beyond the 30 days prescribed. It is difficult to see how the applicant could succeed, particularly in view of the factual findings as relate to the medical examination in which he was found fit for continued duty thereby being excluded from "invalidity" retirement.

Fairness as between the applicant and other persons in a like position.

51. For much the same reasons as expressed above, to allow the applicant to proceed at this time after such considerable delay would be unfair to other persons who may have been in similar positions.

CONCLUSION

52. For these reasons, we consider that the Tribunal does not have jurisdiction to review this matter as there is not a reviewable decision. If this is not correct, on consideration of the factors as to the grant of an extension of time beyond 30 days for reconsideration, we have decided that the extension of time for review as allowed under the discretionary provision of s 154(2) should not be granted. This is because of the considerable delays, prejudice to the respondent and the public and the unfairness if the application was granted. Furthermore and assuming jurisdiction, the merits of the case do not indicate that the applicant has a reasonably arguable case. However this may depend on whether a wide view can be taken of s 7(1) and particularly the term "otherwise terminated" as contended for by the applicant. We do not consider that this is so as "otherwise terminated" does not refer to a resignation as occurred in this matter. Even if the merits may be sufficient on this factor alone to suggest an extension under an exercise of the discretion, the weight of the findings as to the other factors outweighs this consideration.

53. We therefore find that the Tribunal does not have jurisdiction and in the alternative if jurisdiction exists the decision under review should be affirmed.
