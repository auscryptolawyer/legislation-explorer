TT84/314 and Commissioner of Taxation [1986] AATA 431; 87 ATC 165 (19 December 1986)

Last Updated: 5 August 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Deductions - Commercial flying business - Cost of qualifying licenced commercial pilot and partner to fly helicopters in addition to some fixed-wing aircraft – whether deduction allowable.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) S 51.

Sun Newspapers Ltd and Associated Newspapers Ltd v FC of T [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337

John Fairfax & Sons Pty Ltd v FC of T [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30

Nos. TT84/314

AAT Decision No 3117

19 December 1986

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) Nos.

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicants

And:

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

1. In these proceedings two businessmen, J and M, sought the review of determinations of the Commissioner of Taxation whereby he disallowed objections made by each of them to assessments of income tax. The applications were heard together by consent. J and M were qualified pilots, both licenced to fly some conventional fixed-wing aircraft. J had a commercial pilot's licence and M a private licence. Across the range of aircraft for which they were respectively qualified J could provide his services for reward. M could only provide his services for reward on a more limited basis which restricted his commercial flying to such activities as towing gliders and flying parachutists. J and M decided to go into business in the aircraft industry and to that end formed a partnership to carry on business on a part-time basis. At a cost of $16,000 they purchased a fixed-wing aircraft which each was licensed to fly and thereafter set about earning assessable income. They towed gliders and flew parachutists and serviced bushwalking and similar clubs.

2. The business continued into and throughout the year of income ended 30 June 1983. In that year they derived approximately $12,600 by way of assessable income of which $4,800 was derived from parachutists. They claimed deductions for the year against that income of the order of $21,000 (including depreciation of $2,000.)

3. Faced with declining revenues from parachutists ($4,800 year ended 30 June 1983; cf. $7,900 the previous year) occasioned by the fact that the parachutists had acquired their own aircraft, the partners looked for other opportunities to improve their assessable income. They decided that one of them should become qualified to fly helicopters. J was chosen for this assignment because of his more advanced qualifications. He attended a helicopter training school at Mt. Isa and there in a period of some six weeks qualified as a helicopter pilot. His endeavours cost the partnership $7,426 for flying instruction, $597 for accommodation; and $779 in travelling expenses - $8,802 in all. The partnership claimed to deduct the expenses so incurred and the claim was disallowed. The adjustment sheet described the disallowance as "training costs, disallowance of capital expenditure". The returns of income from J and M were adjusted accordingly and they objected to the assessments so made. The objections having been disallowed the Applicants requested a review of the disallowance and in due course the objections came before this Tribunal for review.

4. In addition to the foregoing the evidence established that:

(a) During the relevant period nearly 300 helicopters were in service in Australia owned by approximately 150 operators. Some 250 machines were licenced for commercial operations: nearly 50 of the operator's of commercial helicopters operated both fixed wing aircraft and helicopters; and that approximately half the helicopters in service were operated by such persons;

(b) Helicopters, like fixed-wing aircraft are heavier-than-air machines, with air being their natural operational environment and both are used for the transport of persons and goods;

(c) The partners considered the possibility of acquiring a helicopter but the idea was not pursued in any depth;

(d) The only income derived by the partnership from operations of any helicopter came in a later year of income when J's services were made available on stand-by and as a pilot. The partnership derived income of the order of $200 to $300.

(e) The partnership was dissolved in about October 1985. Since that date J has derived significantly greater income flying the helicopters of others.

(f) By reason of J's earlier qualifications as a commercial pilot his helicopter training was less extensive than it would have been had he had no such other qualifications;

(g) It was J's opinion that as a result of his helicopter training he had become a more skilled and a safer pilot of fixed-wing aircraft.

5. The basic question is one of characterization which is always a question of fact and degree. Were the expenses incurred "in the course of carrying on business"? or were they - as the Commissioner contends expenses incurred in preparing to undertake a new business or incurred in so expanding an existing business as to make it appropriate that they should be characterized as expenses of a capital nature. In support of his contentions the Commissioner argues that a new "profit-yielding subject" was being introduced by gaining helicopter flying qualifications (cf. Dixon J. in Sun Newspapers Ltd and Associated Newspapers v FC of T [1938] HCA 72; (1938) 61 CLR 337 at p.359). He also argues that the expense was incurred "at a stage too remote from the receipt of income to be regarded" as incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income of the partnership (cf. Menzies J. in John Fairfax & Sons Pty Ltd v FC of T [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30 at p.48).

6. The Commissioner in support of those views points to the fact that no helicopter was ever owned by or even within the possession of the partnership; that only one partner was trained; that the expenses were expenses of the training of a partner rather than of an employee; that virtually no income was earned even after training was complete; and that helicopters and fixed-wing aircraft are markedly different in their flying characteristics and capacities.

7. On the other hand the Applicants point to the fact that whether the aircraft be fixed-wing, flexible-wing or wingless; whether designed for conventional take-off or vertical take-off; whether requiring a spacious aerodrome or a landing-pad only slightly larger than the spread of a helicopter's rotors; whether ultra-light, not requiring licensing, or a heavy military type transport; whether powered by jet-turbine or (as here) by piston engine; whether limited to changes of direction or elevation by forward movement or not; whether capable of hovering or not, all have in common the character of being heavier-than-air machines functioning in air space and overcoming the natural force of gravity. Further the Applicants contend that all the activities undertaken - flying the fixed-wing aircraft as they were licenced to do; and being available to fly and flying the helicopters of others - were within the "general aviation industry", defined by the Applicants as the industry providing for as "the carriage of persons and goods by aircraft other than by scheduled airlines".

8. In weighing up those competing contentions and forming a decision in this matter I set aside as irrelevant the circumstance that only one of the two partners gained the further qualifications - after all a person "grounded" by physical disability could have been a partner; and the circumstance that the expenditure was incurred by a partnership - the claim would have been no more or less allowable if J had been a sole trader. Further, I do not attribute great significance to the circumstance that the partnership did not own or have any long-term possessory interest in a helicopter - ownership of or having a long-term possessory interest in the fixed-wing aircraft was not essential to the business. However, I do place considerable significance on the circumstance that the expenditure related to extending the qualifications of J to enable him to operate helicopters rather than other fixed-wing aircraft. Gaining the latter qualifications would have only-extended existing skills, whereas in the former case new skills of different character were gained, even though in both cases they related to aircraft.

9. It is, as I have said, a question of fact and degree. Having weighed all of the foregoing considerations I am not satisfied that the expenses in question are to be considered as "losses and outgoings....necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing (assessable) income". In my view helicopter operations were to be a new "profit-yielding subject" (cf. Sun Newspapers, ante).

10. I affirm the decision of the Commissioner upon each objection.
