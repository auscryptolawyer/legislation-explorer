DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION NO 12060

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No VT97/37

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re      

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member

Date 25 July 1997

Place Melbourne

Decision The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.



........(Sgd) B. H. Pascoe.....

Senior Member



CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - deduction for losses of prior year - bankruptcy after losses incurred - whether deduction in year after date of bankruptcy - reliance on publications of respondent - whether respondent estopped from applying legislation - whether losses deductible - decision affirmed.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss. 79E(8) and 80(4)

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Wade [1951] HCA 66; (1951) 9 ATD 337

REASONS FOR DECISION

25 July 1997 Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member



  1. This is an application to review a decision of the respondent to disallow an objection against an assessment of income tax based on income for the year ended 30 June 1995. The primary issue raised by the objection was that the applicant was entitled to a deduction of losses carried forward from a prior year amounting to $1,300,000.
  2. At the hearing the applicant was not represented and the respondent was represented by an officer of the respondent. The Tribunal had the documents provided by the respondent pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 which included several letters from the applicant setting out his reasons for disputing the assessment. At the hearing the applicant provided further oral evidence. Pursuant to section 14ZZE of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 the applicant requested that the hearing be in private.
  3. The applicant had carried on business as an insurance agent. It is not clear when that business ceased but it would appear that it was some time in the late 1980’s. He became bankrupt on 29 July 1993 and the statement of affairs filed by the applicant on 21 September 1993 showed an excess of liabilities over assets of $1,387,277. The return of income for the year ended 30 June 1995 disclosed assessable income of $45,204 from employment and a pension and claimed deductions of $3,715 as work related expenses, $30 donation and $1,300,000 for prior year losses. Against the prior year loss claim the applicant had written “Bankruptcy 29/7/93”. On 27 July 1995 a refund notice was issued by the respondent showing a nil taxable income and a full refund of tax instalment deductions. On 7 August 1996 the respondent wrote to the applicant requesting details of his bankruptcy and details of the loss of $1,300,000. On 22 August 1996 the respondent again wrote to the applicant advising that a review of his returns for the years ended 30 June 1990 to 30 June 1995 had been completed and that no carry forward loss was allowable in the 1995 year as a result of the bankruptcy and the provisions of subsection 79E(8) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”).
  4. A notice of assessment based on taxable income of $41,459 for the year ended 30 June 1995 was issued on 19 September 1996. This showed an amount payable of $10,909.89 including interest of $1,111.10. From this amount $4,750.12 was deducted being a credit arising from an amended assessment for the year ended 30 June 1994 issued to allow a deduction for losses of prior years. In addition the respondent adjusted each of the years ended 30 June 1990 to 30 June 1993 inclusive to allow a deduction for losses commencing at $1,300,000 in the year ended 30 June 1990. Inclusive of the 1994 year the respondent ultimately allowed $159,680 in total for losses of prior years.
  5. During this process the applicant wrote several long letters to the respondent setting out a history of his financial misfortunes and arguing vehemently that the respondent should not disallow the deduction for prior year losses in the year ended 30 June 1995 because of his bankruptcy. His basic argument put in those letters and to the Tribunal at the hearing was that, on the advice of an officer of the respondent, he had completed his return for that year based on the information contained in Tax Pack and a booklet published and provided by the respondent, relating to professional and business returns. These publications provided information that items such as bad debts, interest, etc were deductible and that losses in one year could be carried forward as a deduction in subsequent years. The applicant said that nowhere within these publications, on which he was advised he could rely, was there any mention of the effect of bankruptcy on deductions for losses. He also maintained that the respondent had clearly stated that, if a taxpayer properly followed the information in these publications “he could not go wrong” or be penalised. Consequently, the applicant argued, the respondent was estopped from disallowing the losses under some provision not contained or referred to in the publications on which he was told to rely. The applicant submitted that he had been penalised both by not being entitled to the refund due to him and being charged interest on that amount. He submitted that he had completed the data required in the return form, disclosed his bankruptcy and had left the respondent to make the appropriate calculations. He had assumed that these proper calculations had been done on receiving a full refund of tax instalment deductions in July 1995 and it was both improper and negligent of the respondent to issue the debit assessment over one year later and seek interest on the amount to be payable.
  6. For the respondent it was simply argued that the relevant legislation is clear and the provisions of subsection 79E(8) and/or subsection 80(4) preclude the allowance of a deduction for losses incurred prior to bankruptcy in any year of income after the year of income in which a taxpayer became bankrupt. Whilst accepting that the publications relied on by the applicant may not have referred to this preclusion, it was said that the respondent and the Tribunal, have no discretion to ignore the clear requirement of the legislation.
  7. Section 79E applies to losses incurred in a year of income commencing on 1 July, 1989 or any later year of income. Section 80 applies to losses incurred in any year up to the year ended 30 June 1989. The evidence of the applicant was not sufficient to identify the actual year or years in which the alleged losses were incurred. However the relevant provision of each section are identical in substance. Section 79E states:
“79E(3) Subject to his section, so much of a taxpayer’s losses incurred in any of the post-1989 years of income before a particular year of income as has not been allowed as a deduction from the taxpayer’s income of any of those years is allowable as a deduction in accordance with the following provisions:

(a) where the taxpayer has not derived exempt income in the particular year of income, the deduction is to be made from the taxpayer’s assessable income of that year;

(b) where the taxpayer has derived exempt income in that year, the deduction is to be made successively from the taxpayer’s net exempt income and from the taxpayer’s assessable income of that year;

(c) where a deduction is allowable under this section in respect of 2 or more losses, the losses are to be taken into account in the order in which they were incurred.

...

79E(8) In spite of any other provision of this section, if, before a year of income, a taxpayer:

(a) has become a bankrupt; or

(b) not having become a bankrupt, has been released from any debts by the operation of an Act relating to bankruptcy;

then no loss incurred by the taxpayer before the day on which the taxpayer became a bankrupt or was so released is an allowable deduction in respect of the year of income under subsection (3).

79E(9) Where:

(a) in a year of income (in this section called the “payment year”), a taxpayer pays an amount in respect of a debt incurred by the taxpayer in a preceding year of income; and

(b) that preceding year of income (in this section called the “loss year”) is a year in which the taxpayer incurred a loss to which subsection (8) applies;

then, subject to subsection (10), the amount paid by the taxpayer is an allowable deduction for the payment year, but only to the extent (if any) that it does not exceed so much of the debt as the Commissioner is satisfied was taken into account in calculating the amount of the loss.”

The provisions of section 80 as they relate to the effect of bankruptcy are in similar terms, as follows:

“80(4) Notwithstanding any other provision of this section, where, prior to the year of income, a taxpayer has become a bankrupt, or, not having become a bankrupt, has been released from any debts by the operation of an Act relating to bankruptcy, no loss incurred by him prior to the date on which he became a bankrupt or the date on which he was so released, as the case may be, shall be an allowable deduction.

80(4A) Where, in the year of income, a taxpayer pays an amount in respect of a debt incurred by him in one of the 7 years next preceding the year of income, being a year in which the taxpayer incurred a loss to which subsection (4) applies, the amount paid by the taxpayer in respect of the debt shall, subject to subsection (4B), be an allowable deduction to the extent that it does not exceed so much of the debt as the Commissioner is satisfied was taken into account in ascertaining the amount of the loss.”

It is clear that the net effect of these legislative provisions is to deny deductions for prior year losses in any year of income following the year in which a taxpayer had become bankrupt other than in respect of any subsequent payment of debts taken into account in calculating such losses.

  1. The clear result of the legislation is that the applicant is not entitled to a deduction in the year ended 30 June 1995 for any losses incurred prior to 29 July 1993. It was not suggested by the applicant that any part of the $1,300,000 claimed related to losses incurred after that date. In fairness to the applicant, he did not seek to argue that the legislation did not produce the result contended by the respondent. However, he contended that the respondent should be estopped from applying the legislation when neither the verbal advice from an officer of the respondent, nor the publications on which he was urged to rely, referred to the possible application of such legislation. Unfortunately, such a contention cannot succeed before this Tribunal. It is clear that no conduct on the part of the Commissioner can operate as an estoppel against the operation of the Act (see Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Wade [1951] HCA 66; (1951) 9 ATD 337 per Kitto J, at p. 344). The provision of publications such as Tax Pack whilst providing assistance to taxpayers cannot be a substitute for the legislation. An opening statement by the Commissioner in the 1995 Tax Pack says “Tax Pack 95 has been produced to help you get your tax return right”. Whilst the use of such publications may provide an appropriate defence against the imposition of additional tax on any tax shortfall for failure to take reasonable care to comply with the Act, it can be no defence against the proper assessment of income tax in accordance with the Act.
  2. In addition to the substantive matter of the deductibility of losses, the applicant had objected to the imposition of interest on the amount payable on the notice of assessment issued on 19 September 1996. This interest was imposed pursuant to section 170AA of the Act. Subsection 170AA(6) applies to the position, as in this matter, where a notice of nil taxable income is issued and a subsequent assessment of taxable income and tax payable is issued. For the purpose of the application of interest under the section, the subsequent assessment is deemed to be an amendment allowing interest to be charged. The Tribunal has no power to consider an objection decision relating to remission of such interest. Pursuant to section 14ZZA of the Taxation Administration Act 1953, the Tribunal may review a “reviewable objection decision”. A reviewable objection decision does not include an “ineligible income tax remission decision” which, under section 14ZS of that Act includes interest imposed under section 170AA.
  3. Whilst the foregoing results in a finding that the decision under review must be affirmed it is appropriate to comment on another aspect of the applicant’s claim. During the course of the hearing it was put to the applicant by the Tribunal, that, if the Tribunal was of a mind to accept his argument on estoppel, the question would still arise of whether the amount of $1,300,000 or some part of such sum could be an allowable deduction in the year in dispute. From the information before the Tribunal it appeared that the amount of $1,300,000 was based on the excess of liabilities over assets shown on the statement of affairs. When asked for detail of the amount claimed, the year incurred and evidence that it represented an excess of allowable deduction over assessable income of a prior year, the applicant was somewhat vague. However, he did state that further deductions had not been claimed in view of his understanding that the size of the loss made it unnecessary to consider any further deductions. His correspondence to the respondent and his evidence at the hearing indicated that the business as insurance agent was carried on in the name of a company. Initially the company involved the applicant and another agent but the other agent, in the words of the applicant, “decamped with most of the money and my secretary”. Following this the applicant apparently continued to carry on the business through the same company. He said that he continued using the company name as all of the stationery, etc., had that name. It was somewhat disappointing that a person who stated that his agency had sold some $28 million in sums assured in one year appeared to have no grasp of the difference between a company and an individual carrying a business or details of how the excess of liabilities at the date of bankruptcy represented a loss deductible under the Act. The Tribunal was left with the view that the liabilities listed in the statement of affairs were either private or domestic liabilities, liabilities under a guarantee of company debts or liabilities under lease of motor vehicles and equipment after cessation of business activities. It is, therefore, unlikely that any of these liabilities represented allowable deductions.
  4. The further deductions referred to by the applicant appeared to be:

1. A bad debt of $85,000;

2. Interest of $300,000;

3. Loss of an investment bond of $87,000;

4. Outstanding commissions of $57,000; and

5. Home office expenses.

The bad debt represented a loan to a client company to meet premiums on policies. The applicant stated this was done to ensure premiums were paid and to protect the commission earned. Unfortunately, the client company failed and the debt was not recoverable. Again it is not clear when the loan was made or when it became bad. The applicant thought that the loan was made in 1991 or 1992 and thought that the company was “bankrupted in 1993”. In addition, whether the loan was made by the applicant personally or by his company is unclear. In a letter to the respondent dated 27 August 1996 the applicant stated “I had one major debt owed to my company - $85,000 owed by an organisation...The debt related to insurance premiums I had paid on their behalf.” When the loss was incurred, whose loss it was and whether it represents an allowable deduction, is in doubt.

  1. The interest of $300,000 appears to be interest charged by the insurance company on a development loan. As such it would seem that it is included in the liability to the insurance company of $1,103,542 shown in the statement of affairs. Again such loan inclusive of interest was likely to have been a liability of the company which carried on the agency business and the interest not an expense of the applicant. Further, it would be necessary for the applicant to demonstrate that the loan upon which interest was payable was used for the purpose of producing the applicant’s assessable income.
  2. The alleged loss caused by the forfeiture of the investment bond is easily dealt with. Firstly, it was the loss of an investment and has all the hallmarks of a loss of capital. Secondly, the bond was in the name of the company, not the applicant. The alleged loss of commission was not backed up with any detail and the Tribunal has no knowledge of when the alleged loss was incurred, whether it was a bad debt of commission owed and previously returned as assessable income and whether it was commission of the company or the applicant.
  3. Home office expenses were not detailed although the applicant, at the hearing, indicated that the amount may have been some $500. However, in the return of income lodged by the applicant for the year ended 30 June 1995, he claimed $3,715 as work related deductions. These were car expenses $2,200, car hire $118, papers and periodicals $657, contribution phone and fax $500 and brokers licence $240. Assessable income disclosed was a salary as a night porter at a hotel and a superannuation pension. The applicant stated that he was working on a project which he hoped would develop into a profitable business. Again it must be a matter of some doubt as to whether the expenses claimed and allowed constituted allowable deductions in that year.
  4. Whilst it is possible to have some sympathy with the applicant who was denied a deduction to which he thought he was entitled and who was unaware of the effect of subsections 79E(8) and 80(4), the law is clear and the decision under review must be affirmed. It may well be considered that, on the other hand, the applicant has been fortunate in having allowed as deductions $159,680 of losses in the 1990 to 1994 years and $3,715 of deductions in the 1995 year, none of which may have stood up to a test of deductibility if they had been before the Tribunal.
