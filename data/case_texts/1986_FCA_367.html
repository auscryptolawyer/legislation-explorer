Re Commissioner of Taxation v Marshall and Brougham Pty Ltd [1986] FCA 367 (5 November 1986)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

And: MARSHALL and BROUGHAM PTY. LTD.

No. G47 of 1986

Practice and Procedure

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

SOUTH AUSTRALIA DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Fisher J.

CATCHWORDS

Practice and Procedure - application for leave to appeal under Income Tax Assessment Act (Cth) 1936 - relevant considerations - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss. 187(1)(6), 196 and 200.

Practice and Procedure - stay of execution pending appeal - Rules of Court - relevant provisions of Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

Federal Court Rules O 19 r 2, O52 r 17.

Income Tax Assessment Act s.201.

HEARING

ADELAIDE

5:11:1986

ORDER

Leave to appeal be granted to the applicant.

Execution of the judgment of the Supreme Court of South Australia dated 11 August 1986 be stayed until the hearing and determination of the appeal to this Court or further order.

Cost of these two applications be costs in the cause.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

In this matter the Commissioner of Taxation ("the Commissioner") seeks leave to appeal against a judgment of a single judge of the Supreme Court of South Australia. He also by notice of motion seeks a stay of execution and a stay of any enforcement proceedings under that judgment. It was necessary for the Commissioner to obtain leave in this matter to appeal to this Court because of the provisions of s.196 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. The parties agreed that this section applied because Marshall and Brougham Pty. Ltd. ("the taxpayer") had earlier requested the Commissioner in pursuance of para.187(1)(a) of the Act to refer his decision to disallow its objection to a Taxation Board of Review for review. If the taxpayer had requested the Commissioner in pursuance of para.187(1)(b) to treat its objection as an appeal and to forward it direct to the Supreme Court of South Australia the Commissioner is empowered by sub.s.200(a) to appeal to this Court as of right and without obtaining leave. It is pertinent to note that consequent upon the transfer of the jurisdiction of the Taxation Boards of Review to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal as from 1 July 1986 all of the aforementioned provisions of the Act, with the exception of s.200, have been repealed.

2. The relevant facts can be stated shortly. The taxpayer, in the words of the trial judge, acted as "banker" for a group of building companies of which it is a member. In this capacity it was in the habit of depositing surplus funds with Associated Securities Limited ("A.S.L.") as interest bearing loans on short term or at call. On 8 February 1979 A.S.L. was placed in receivership and subsequently into liquidation. It is common ground that the balance of monies then owing by A.S.L. to the taxpayer is irrecoverable. The taxpayer wrote off this balance, namely $500,000, as a bad debt prior to 30 June 1979 and claimed an allowable deduction of that amount under para 63(1)(b) or alternatively sub.s.51(1) of the Act. The Commissioner disallowed the deduction under both provisions and by his assessment increased the taxpayer's taxable income for that year by $500,000. The taxpayer lodged an objection, which was disallowed, and being dissatisfied with this decision of the Commissioner requested that it be referred to a Board of Review for review. The Board of Review on 31 July 1985 upheld the Commissioner's decision and confirmed the assessment.

3. The taxpayer appealed pursuant to sub.ss.196(1) of the Act to the Supreme Court of South Australia against the decision of the Board of Review. Subsection 196(1) empowers such an appeal "from any decision of the Board that involves a question of law". The trial judge noted in his reasons that it was not disputed that the appeal "involves questions of law - the construction of the statute and its application to the particular facts of the case". On 11 August 1986 he allowed the appeal on the ground that the taxpayer was entitled to deduct in accordance with sub.s.51(1) of the Act the sum of $500,000 from its assessable income for the year of income ending 30 June 1979. He dismissed the taxpayer's contention that this amount was deductible under para.63(1)(b) of the Act.

4. The course that the proceedings took in the Supreme Court was that the oral evidence and admitted documents before the Board of Review comprised the evidence before the trial judge. This evidence was not challenged on the hearing of that Court but the trial judge was invited by both parties to draw his own conclusions of fact from that evidence, as on a re-hearing. In his reasons the trial judge set out in detail the manner in which the taxpayer conducted its business operations and it is unnecessary for me to repeat these findings. It suffices to say that progress payments due in respect of building work performed by other companies in the group were collected by the taxpayer. These monies, the trial judge said, were "earmarked or destined" for payment to sub-contractors or to discharge current liabilities within the group. Pending making such payments these funds were placed on short-term loan as earlier mentioned. It is unnecessary for me to go into this evidence in any greater detail for two reasons. The issue under para.63(1)(b) upon which the Commissioner was successful was for obvious reasons not before me. Furthermore the only issue under sub.s.51(1) before the trial judge was whether the loss of the sum of $500,000 was a loss of capital or of a capital nature. The seemingly difficult question whether the loss was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or necessarily incurred in carrying on business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income was conceded by the Commissioner. He advanced no argument in denial of the contention of the taxpayer that its entitlement to a deduction could be established under either limb.

5. This left for determination the question whether the loss was a loss of capital or of a capital nature. The trial judge acknowledged at the outset of his reasons on this aspect that it was a difficult question. He concluded by stating that the case was not an easy one. I agree with both of these observations. In order to answer the question, he gave consideration to the critical features of the taxpayer's business activities which provided the setting for the loss and in particular the nature and source of the funds that were lost. His conclusion was that at the time they were lost the monies could not be identified in whole or in part as being capital or of a capital nature. His view was that this conclusion was justified by the approach adopted in cases such as Commissioner of Taxation (N.S.W.) v Ash 61 C.L.R.273, Charles Moore & Co (W.A.) Pty. Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 95 C.L.R.345 and Reids Brewery & Co. v Male (1891) 2 Q.B.1. The appeal was therefore allowed on this aspect of the matter.

6. The Commissioner's application for leave to appeal to this Court was strenuously opposed by counsel for the taxpayer. His contention was that the trial judge's conclusion was based exclusively on a factual analysis of the taxpayer's business activities and that his findings of fact were not challenged. He argued that the grounds of appeal were misconceived in the sense that they comprised "factual issues dressed up as questions of law". I am unable to agree that this is necessarily the case. Moreover I would have thought that there are few more difficult questions in this area of the law than the determination whether losses and outgoings are on capital or on revenue account (e.g. Avco Financial Services Ltd. v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1982) 150 C.L.R. 511 and Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Hunter Douglas Ltd. [1983] FCA 229; (1983) 50 A.L.R. 97). The question must at least be a mixed question of fact and law. The trial judge correctly stated it as a difficult case involving questions of law, the construction of the Act and its application to the peculiar and exceptional facts of the case. I wholly agree. Furthermore there is an element of public interest involved in having a further decision on these facts, because when interest rates are high it is a common occurrence for all surplus funds of a group to be collected by one company, which acts as banker for the group (e.g. Commissioner of Taxation v Gwynvill Properties Pty. Ltd. (1986) 86 A.T.C. 4512.) In my opinion it is proper that I grant leave to the Commissioner to appeal. I was not asked to make any further order on this aspect of the case.

7. The Commissioner also seeks a stay of the trial judge's order that the assessment be amended to deduct the amount of $500,000 from the assessable income of the taxpayer for the year ending 30 June 1979. This application is made by notice of motion under O.19 r.2 of the Rules of this Court. Section 29 of the Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 empowers this Court to grant a stay. Order 52 r.17 provides that an appeal shall not operate as a stay of execution or of proceedings under the judgment appealed from, except so far as the Court or a Judge or the Court below may direct. The general principle is undoubted, namely that the discretion to order a stay will only be exercised where special circumstances exist which will justify departure from the general rule that a successful litigant is entitled to the fruits of his litigation pending determination of an appeal.

8. There are however two grounds upon which it may be proper to place the Commissioner as an apellant in a special category. First s.201 entitles the Commissioner to collect payment of tax notwithstanding the fact that an appeal or reference may be pending. It may be said that this special position is presently acknowledged by the deletion of s.202 and the enactment this year of s.200B of the Act. This latter section provides that the Commissioner is obliged not later than 60 days after a decision becomes final to take such action, including amending the assessment, as may be necessary to give effect to the decision. There are particular provisions indicating when decisions become final. The Commissioner also relies upon the problem that was identified by Dawson J. on a similar application to the High Court in Commissioner of Taxation v Myer Emporium Ltd. (1986) 60 A.L.J.R.300. Under the Taxation (Interest on Overpayments) Act 1983 the Commissioner would be obliged, it was contended, if there was no stay, to repay not only $230,000.00 of overpaid tax but also $77,154.41 of accrued interest on that tax. The Commissioner argued that this amount of interest would not be recoverable once paid even if he is ultimately successful on his appeal. Dawson J. was of opinion that there was sufficient force in this argument of the Commissioner to cast doubt on the power of the Court to order on the appeal repayment of interest paid under the Taxation (Interest on Overpayments) Act. In my opinion I should adopt the same approach. In doing so I note that if the taxpayer is ultimately successful he will under the last-mentioned Act receive interest calculated at 14.026% per annum, admittedly not as high a rate as it can currently earn.

9. I have had the opportunity to peruse the reasons of Northrop J. in The Commissioner of Taxation Ex parte Just Jeans Pty. Ltd. an unreported decision delivered on 17 March 1986 wherein he ordered the Commissioner to repay both overpaid income tax and interest. This matter was however heard and his decision given prior to the hearing by Dawson J. of the Myer Emporium case supra and also at a time when s.202 on which Northrop J. substantially relied still had a place in the Act. The deletion of that section came into operation on 1 July 1986 prior to Jacobs J. delivering his decision in this matter.

10. For these reasons leave is granted to the Commissioner to appeal and there will be a stay on the execution of the judgment of the Supreme Court of South Australia dated 11 August 1986 until the hearing and determination of the appeal by this Court or further order. It is appropriate that the costs of these two applications be costs in the cause.
