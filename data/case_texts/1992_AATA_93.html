DECISION

The applicant seeks review of objection decisions in relation to the years of income ended 30 June 1976 to 1980 and 1982. In each year of income, except the year ended 30 June 1982, the principal question to be decided is whether the applicant was presently entitled to income of a trust estate and therefore whether the net income is assessable to the applicant. There are also a multitude of different claims arising with which I will deal in due course.

2. The applicant conducted his own case and Mr Howe of counsel appeared for the respondent. At the applicant's request the Tribunal heard the evidence in each application separately.

3. I will deal with the principal question first which is the only matter of substantial dispute between the parties. The applicant's relevant income tax returns disclosed income derived from various sources but did not disclose income derived from the trust.

4. The applicant gave evidence, which I accept, that he was not aware of the creation of the trust nor that he was a beneficiary under the trust until he received notices of assessments which included amounts described as "Share of income from the (applicant's name) Trust included in assessable income (1976 year T7) or simply included the amount of the alleged distribution in the calculation of taxable income (T27, T46, T66, T141).

5. In lengthy and detailed notices of objection the applicant objected against each assessment. The essence of each objection is that the applicant had not consented to nor accepted the benefit of the trust or that he had assigned the benefit of the trust income to another unnamed person or that he was not presently entitled to income of the trust or that the alleged income was not received from the trustee.

6. In evidence the applicant stated that he did not have any knowledge of the existence of a trust for his benefit during the year ended 30 June 1976 and first became aware of the trust when the respondent assessed income from the trust to him. Although the applicant was unable to remember the date it must have been on or about 16 March 1979 when the respondent notified an assessment in respect of the year ended 30 June 1976 which included an amount of $2,891 as assessable income being income from the trust. I accept the applicant's evidence as being truthfully given according to his recollection. He formed the view "that the trust was, I guess, some sort of fiddle for my father". He acknowledged that upon turning 25 years of age in 1980 he received the corpus of the trust estate from his father but was apparently unaware of the details of the trust until several years after that. The applicant had formed the view that his father had deliberately concealed the trust from him for altruistic reasons. The applicant was unable to say how much he received from his father and was also unable to say when he received the distributions of income.

7. I have come to the conclusion that the applicant did not know initially of the creation of the trust in his favour but I am unable to determine from the evidence as to when he did become aware of the trust.

8. The trustee was called to give evidence and identified Exhibit 5 as the relevant trust deed. The deed is unexceptional in that it creates trust obligations for the benefit of the applicant with income to be applied or accumulated for the benefit of the applicant until his twenty-first birthday, thereafter to pay the income to the applicant until he reached twenty-five years and then to hold the fund for his benefit absolutely. The trustee's evidence was that the trust fund was paid over to the applicant when he reached twenty-five years of age.

9. Income tax returns, lodged in the applicant name, being Exhibit 6, were identified by the trustee as income tax returns signed by him as trustee. Those returns did not disclose any distribution to the applicant. His explanation for this was that he had decided to accumulate the income until the applicant turned 25 years of age. He had made a conscious decision not to tell the applicant of the existence of the trust until the applicant turned twenty-five years of age. He could not be precise as to exactly when he had informed the applicant of the existence of the trust fund. I formed the impression that the trustee did not really understand the obligations imposed upon him by the settlor and adopted the approach that he would hold the trust estate and the income from it until his son reached twenty-five years without any real regard to his obligations as trustee.

13. Document T6 is copy of a notice of assessment in respect of the year of income ended 30 June 1976 assessing the applicant on a taxable income of $4,473 by adding the amount of $2,891 as income from the trust (T6 and 7). The applicant objected to the assessment effectively raising objections against the disallowed deductions. At the hearing the respondent conceded the claims in relation to depreciation of instruments and books. The applicant also objected against the failure to allow a deduction of $500 in respect of "my Curran Scheme deduction". No evidence was led by the applicant to support the claim for $500.

14. The claim for postage and also a claim for "solicitors fees" related to a deceased estate in the United Kingdom in which the applicant had an interest. There is nothing before the Tribunal to indicate that the applicant derived or was likely to derive assessable income from this estate. The contrary indication is given in the return of income which was affirmed in the applicant's oral evidence. There is therefore no basis for allowing the deductions sought. Related to this claim is a claim for a deduction of $30 said to have been incurred in procuring money to finance the purchase of investment property. The reality is that this amount was paid for solicitors fees in relation to the deceased estate. There is no apparent connection with the derivation of assessable income. The evidence does not support the contention that the fees can be characterised as borrowing expenses.

Year ended 30 June 1977

15. In relation to this year of income the applicant lodged an objection raising claims in respect of the trust income, a "Curran" scheme, the health insurance levy and a claim for $50 deduction in respect of travel expenses. Other claims made in the notice of objection were allowed to the applicant in the objection decision. At the hearing counsel for the respondent conceded the claim in respect of travel expenses. The applicant led no evidence in relation to the grounds of objection relating to the health insurance levy.

Year ended 30 June 1978

16. The applicant's objection for this year of income relevantly raised claims in relation to the trust income, the health insurance levy, $587 said to have been incurred in travel expenses, a desk and additional depreciation on a reference library. The applicant did not pursue the claim relating to health insurance levy.

17. In evidence the applicant said the travel expenses were incurred, in the main, on travel between the United Kingdom and Australia. The basis of the claim was that as the applicant had derived income in both countries. Apparently the applicant worked as an intern in a hospital in the United Kingdom for one week. Whether he derived any assessable income (as compared with exempt income) from such employment I do not know. His income deriving activities in Australia upon his return were investment related. So far as I can tell from the evidence the travel expenses had nothing to do with those investment activities. I am not satisfied that the travel expenses were incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable income. I am satisfied that the travel expenses were outgoings of a private nature and therefore the applicant cannot succeed under section 51 of the Act. I am not aware and was not directed to any other relevant statutory provision. Furthermore the applicant was unable to give evidence as to how the amount of $587 had been incurred. There is therefore no basis for allowing the claim.

18. At the hearing I explained why the decision in relation to the depreciation allowed on the library will be affirmed. I do not need to reiterate that discussion, found at pages 25/27 of the transcript.

20. At the hearing the respondent conceded the deductibility of the University union fee. The applicant did not pursue the claim re the health insurance levy.

21. The applicant gave evidence that during the year of income he rented a flat at Artarmon and sublet the garage belonging to the flat to Armstrong-Nylex. On this basis he claimed expenditure said to relate to the garage. In his oral evidence the applicant opined that no income had been derived prior to the 1980 year of income.

22. So far as I can tell from the evidence before me the applicant did not own the Artarmon property in the 1979 year of income. He was, however, in possession of the property and he may therefore have sub-leased the garage. However, I should not speculate in circumstances where the taxpayer is unable to give a coherent explanation or lead other evidence. I am not satisfied that the garage was used for income producing activities during the year of income.

23. The applicant also claims that the premises at Artarmon were used as business premises. However, he did not conduct any medical practice or other business from these premises. He did conduct his very limited investment activities from these premises but there is no basis in the evidence before the Tribunal for holding that the decision in Handley v F.C.T. 81 ATC 4165 does not apply. The expenditure regarding the home office was not of the character for outgoings incurred for the purpose of producing assessable income.

24. During the year of income the applicant was a salaried medical officer with the Department of Veterans Affairs commencing employment on 9 October 1978 after return from overseas. In his return of income the applicant describes himself as "Worker Inter Alia". However, I am prepared to infer that he was at that time a medical officer. As such he was a salary earner.

25. The basis of the claims for the motor vehicle is that because he was on call at the hospital he was required to use his car getting to and from the hospital. It is now settled by a whole line of cases starting at Lunney v F.C.T. (1958) 100 CLR 478 to the effect that the costs of getting to and from an employee's place of employment are not allowable deductions.

26. There is no evidence that the car was actually used in the course of employment - it was used for getting to employment. I am not satisfied that the outgoings including depreciation in respect of the motor vehicle have been incurred in the course of producing assessable income.

27. As to the claim for the deprecation of lecture notes ($100) there is no basis known to the Tribunal upon which such notes can be depreciated. In relation to the depreciation claims generally, for the reasons given at the hearing, the basis adopted by the respondent will be affirmed.

28. The claim for repair of spectacles relates to an incident which apparently took place in the course of the applicant's employment. While it is prima facie the case that the outgoing was incurred in the course of employment, the nature of the outgoing is private. It is hard to think of anything more personal than prescription spectacles designed to correct a person's vision. It was not suggested that the spectacles were in any way special to the applicant's profession as a medical practitioner or that they were worn for protection. On this basis I am bound to find that the cost of repair of the spectacles was an outgoing of a private nature and not an allowable deduction.

29. In relation to the claim in respect of termination payments the applicant failed to lead evidence in this matter and invited the Tribunal to draw its own conclusions. For reasons which I will outline later, the onus lies on the applicant to establish that the assessment is excessive. It is not sufficient to make an assertion and then invite the Tribunal to "jump" to its own conclusion. The disallowance of the claim will be affirmed.

30. The evidence in relation to the depreciation of staff amenities established that there were no relevant staff amenities if only because there was no staff member employed and none intended to be employed. The decision to disallow the claim will be affirmed.

31. The claim for telephone expenses in the applicant's return was calculated as 25% of $140 = $35. It was made clear in the return that the $140 was for installation of the phone and no bill for calls had been received in the 1979 year of income. In the notice of objection the applicant claimed that 60% of the $140 was allowable because the phone was used for calls to and from the hospital regarding the applicant's patients. That was claimed to account for 50% of the installation fee paid. An additional 10% of the fee was said to be attributable to the applicant's investment activities. I accept that the applicant used the telephone to receive incoming calls from the hospital at which he was employed but I do not know any details of the frequency of such calls. In view of the conflicting statements made in the return and the notice of objection and the vague oral evidence before the Tribunal, once again I am left to speculate as to the factual situation. In these circumstances I can only affirm the respondent's decision.

The applicant assumed that because the one solicitor handled all the work some apportionment may be necessary.

33. The copy documents in Exhibit 2 are a certificate of title, a transfer under the Real Property Act and a Memorandum of Mortgage. Those documents establish that the date of transfer of the Artarmon property was 26 July 1979 and the mortgage bears the same date. On the evidence before me I must conclude that the transfer of the property and the grant of a loan secured by mortgage occurred on or about 26 July 1979. Without more I cannot make a finding that the borrowing expenses were incurred prior to 1 July 1979. Furthermore I am not satisfied that the applicant used the Artarmon premises for the conduct of a business.

The applicant said in evidence that the purchase was partly financed by borrowing on his bankcard account. He was unable to say how much was borrowed but claimed that $72 of interest was incurred in the financial year. In fact three bankcards from different banks were used and the make up of the amount claimed is set out in the return of income as follows:-

While I am prepared to accept the evidence that the Beecroft house was financed in part by funds drawn from Bankcard accounts, given that the purchase was effected on 20 June 1980, I am not prepared to assume that the amounts claimed were incurred during the year of income.

37. As to the claim for council rates and water rates paid on purchase of the Beecroft property to the vendor - those amounts are part of the capital consideration paid on purchase of the property and are not allowable deductions. The same applies to the cost of title searches and other fees paid out in the course of purchasing the property. Such amounts form part of the capital cost of the building.

38. The claim for paint at $20 and other items for repairs and expenses in connection with those repairs of the Beecroft property are clearly not allowable deductions on the authority of the Law Shipping Case. Nor is the cost of purchase of tools purchased for effecting repairs to the Beecroft property. Clearly such outgoings are on capital account as also are the search fees and other expenses incurred at the time of purchase of the property. The claim for a deduction of $23 for old clothes written off because of paint spilt on them may be novel but it is not allowable.

39. The stamp duty payable on the purchase of the Beecroft property is likewise an outgoing on capital account and not allowable. The claim for investment allowance in respect of the stove installed in the Beecroft house is disallowed. There is simply no basis for such an allowance. The amount of $620 is a figure dreamed up by the applicant. The figure of $844 for the hot water service was established on the same basis. Claims in respect of the stove and the hot water service are fanciful and without foundation. The claim for depreciation of water pipes will be rejected on the same basis.

40. As to the claim for borrowing expenses in relation to the purchase of the Beecroft house the applicant gave evidence that the following amounts were incurred:-

$

There is no evidence before me which establishes the character of the outgoings claimed. In particular I am not satisfied that the $1,802 claimed in respect of stamp duty is the duty payable on the mortgage. It is not established to whom the other amounts were paid so that the Tribunal is left to speculate as to the character of the outgoings. As I have already indicated it is not a function of this Tribunal to speculate as to the facts.

41. In relation to the Artarmon property, the Tribunal ruled at the hearing that certain claims made in the 1979 year would be considered as allowable deductions in the 1980 year of income in accordance with paragraph 190(b) of the Act. As to cleaning of the leased garage the claim for $64 seems to relate to the cost of brooms, garbage bags and gravelling from the applicant's place of employment. The latter claim is made notwithstanding that the leased garage is contiguous to the applicant's residence. I am not satisfied that the travel claim can in any way relate to the sweeping of the garage. As I do not know the cost of garbage bags and brooms it would be mere speculation to allow any deduction.

42. The evidence about the claim for insurance on the property was so vague as to not even allow me to be satisfied that an insurance premium had been incurred, much less that some portion of the premium applied to the Armstrong-Nylex stock stored in the garage. The claim cannot be allowed.

43. The applicant claimed a deduction for interest paid to the St George Building Society in relation to the Artarmon property. The claim related to the garage, the home office and 1 per cent for the occasional use of the toilet. The respondent has already allowed a deduction for the interest attributable to the garage and I am not satisfied that there is a sound basis to ground a claim for the home office and the toilet. The evidence indicates that the applicant saw a very small number of patients at home. Any use of the home in these circumstances would be de minimus and I do not propose to make any allowance for such use. To the extent that the home office was used by the applicant as distinct from seeing patients then the decision of the High Court in Handley's Case applies to deny the deduction. The Artarmon property was the applicant's home and outgoings incurred in connection therewith were essentially of a private or domestic and therefore non allowable character. In any event there is no evidence before the Tribunal which would satisfy me that it is more likely than not that a portion of the Artarmon premises (other than the garage) was used as a home office or for other income producing activities.

44. The claims for travel between the hospital and the applicant's residence fail as being outgoings incurred in travel between the applicant's home and place of employment (Lunney's Case). To suggest that the travel expenses became deductible because the applicant cleaned the income producing garage prior to entering his residence upon his return to work is fanciful and is totally unconvincing to establish that the assessment is thereby excessive.

45. Fees paid to a solicitor in respect of the purchase of the Beecroft property are not allowable deductions under any provision of the Act being of a capital nature. Section 64A of the Act allows a deduction of $50 in relation to the costs incurred in the purchase of the Beecroft property.

46. I accept that an employed medical practitioner may be required to use his private telephone from time to time in the course of his work. There is, however, no evidence, other than a general assertion, that the applicant incurred telephone expenses in connection with his employment. There is therefore no basis for allowing the claim for 21% of the cost of the telephone service at the applicant's residence even allowing for the fact that the applicant managed his real estate and share investments. In respect of the latter, on the evidence before me such usage must have been minimal.

47. In the course of his employment at the Concord Hospital the applicant used his motor vehicle for travelling in the hospital grounds. The applicant's evidence is that it was often necessary for him to travel to distant parts of the hospital in the course of his employment and he used his vehicle for this purpose. Although the basis for the claim is not before the Tribunal I have come to the conclusion that the amount claimed of $1 should be allowed. The decision in Burton means that I cannot allow a deduction for amounts incurred in merely having the motor vehicle at work in the event that it may be required for use in the course of employment. The claim for a deduction of $54 in respect to damage to the vehicle caused by the hospital smoke stack emissions while the vehicle was parked at the Concord Hospital will therefore be rejected.

48. 48.The claim for spectacle repairs and depreciation of lecture notes are not allowable for reasons already given.

49. The claim for a deduction of $10 in respect of a white coat although not substantiated will be allowed on the basis that I am satisfied that the protective or uniform coat was required to be used in the course of the applicant's employment and that it is more likely than not, on the evidence, that the applicant incurred the outgoing of $10 claimed.

50. The claim for depreciation in relation to the Beecroft property has already been dealt with in part but to the extent that I have not done so I am unable to be satisfied on the evidence before me that these items were depreciable in the year of income. Furthermore the values assigned to these articles were merely wild guesses by the applicant based upon replacement values. That is not a sound basis for determining the value for depreciation purposes and for that reason also I am unable to uphold the applicant's claims.

Year ended 30 June 1982

51. In this year of income the applicant claims deductions for legal fees in relation to a motor vehicle accident and for depreciation of fly screens. The latter claim involving fly screens was conceded by the respondent so that an additional deduction of $7 is allowable.

52. In relation to the motor vehicle accident the applicant was unable to give any evidence about the circumstances of the accident other than to assert that it occurred while he was travelling in the course of business. Like so many other claims made by the applicant the Tribunal is left to speculate in a vacuum of evidence. As I have already indicated the Tribunal will not do so, especially where the onus of proving that the assessment is excessive lies on the applicant.

The Trust

53. I return to the claims in relation to the net income of the Trust. In each of the years of income before the Tribunal the respondent has increased the taxable income reported by the applicant by adding an amount said to be the applicant's share of the net income of a trust estate in the applicant's name.

54. The applicant contends that the income was not his because at least in the earlier years of income he was not aware of the existence of the trust. In relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1976 his evidence was that he had no knowledge of the trust, that he was a beneficiary of a trust, that there was any money in a trust, or that he was to get income from a trust. His first knowledge of the trust came via the respondent in the notices of amended assessment for the year of income ended 30 June 1976 notified by a notice dated 16 March 1979 which added $2,891 to the applicant's taxable income his "share of income from the . . . Trust . . . ".

55. The applicant seems to have been some what cavalier in his response in that he seems not to have made any real enquiries within his own family as to the basis for the respondent's adjustment. The respondent had made it clear to the applicant that the trust was entitled with the applicant's own name so that this was unlikely to be a case of mistaken identity and the applicant does not appear to have regarded it as such himself. He now understands that the trust was settled by his grandmother and that his father was the trustee.

56. Upon the applicant turning 25 years of age in 1980 the trust fund was transferred to the applicant by his father in accordance with the terms of trust (Exhibit 5). It has not been suggested to me that the applicant has elected to disclaim the benefit of the trust - only that he was not aware of its existence until the respondent assessed income of the trust to him for the first time in 1979.

57. The trustee, who is the applicant's father, was called to give evidence. He recognised the trust deed (Exhibit 5) and the income tax returns lodged in respect of the trust for the years of income ended 30 June 1976 to 30 June 1980 (Exhibit 6). His evidence was to the effect that no distribution of the net income of the trust had been made to the applicant until 1979 when the trust fund was made over to the applicant. Prior to 1979 the net income had been accumulated for the benefit of the applicant.

58. The trustee had deliberately refrained from telling the applicant of the existence of the trust prior to the applicant turning 25 years of age although he did not explain his reason for this.

59. In Case W36 89 ATC 363 this Tribunal decided (per the headnote) that upon any determination of the trustee to distribute income to the taxpayer she only became entitled to the benefits of the distribution if she chose to accept them. Thus the amounts in question could not become her "income" until she consented to the status of being a beneficiary and she determined to accept the distribution. As the taxpayer was not at any time aware of the existence of any trust or of her status as a beneficiary of the trust eligible to benefit at the discretion of the trustee, she had not given the necessary consent and therefore there was no derivation of trust income. That summary of the decision in the headnote uses other words but effectively summarises the case put by the applicant in the present case. Without more it might be expected that the applicant should succeed if this Tribunal was to adopt a consistent approach.

60. I am not required to consider whether I should adopt a consistent approach to the decision in Case W36 because in F.C.T. v Vegners 89 ATC 5274 the Federal Court reversed the Tribunal's decision. That decision by Gummow J. was the subject of a further appeal in Vegners v F.C.T. 91 ATC 4213. A full court of the Federal Court held that a beneficiary is entitled under a trust notwithstanding that the beneficiary has no knowledge of the trust. Section 101 of the Act deemed the taxpayer to be presently entitled to the trust income received and therefore the beneficiary was assessable to that income in terms of section 97 of the Act.

61. There is an important factual distinction between the present case and Vegners Case. In the decided case the taxpayer actually received the distribution each year although she thought that she was receiving a payment under a court order for payment of maintenance. In that case the Commissioner's counsel specifically stated that he would only seek to support the assessments as to the amount actually received and not as to amounts distributed by the trustee but not paid over to the taxpayer. I cannot glean from his Honour's reasons the basis upon which this concession was made.

62. Turning to the law and with the benefit of the decision in Vegner's Case I have come to the conclusion that the law applies as follows.

63. Sub-section 95(1) of the Act defines "net income" of a trust estate to mean the total assessable income of the trust estate and if the trustee were a taxpayer in respect of that income and were a resident from which is deducted all allowable deductions.

64. Pursuant to sub-section 97(1) where beneficiary of a trust estate is not under any legal disability and is presently entitled to a share of the income of the trust estate then the assessable income of that beneficiary shall include so much of that share of the net income of the trust estate.

65. The present case involves a non-discretionary trust because clause 3 of the deed (Exhibit 5) provides that upon the beneficiary (the applicant) attaining the age of 21 years the trustee shall hold the settled fund upon trust to pay the income thereof to the beneficiary until the beneficiary attains the age of 25 years. At all relevant times the applicant was 21 years of age or older. It does not seem to me to be relevant that the trustee applied the income for the benefit of the applicant rather than pay it over to him. Clearly the applicant was sui juris and could call for payment of the income and for that matter the trust estate to himself (Saunders v Vautier [1841] EngR 629; (1841) 49 ER 282). The net income of the trust vested in the applicant by the trust deed. It was therefore income to which the applicant was presently entitled and assessable to him in accordance with section 97 of the Act.

66. In proceedings under Part V of the Act on a review before this Tribunal the burden of proving that an assessment is excessive lies upon the applicant (section 190). "In FC. of T. v. Dalco 90 ATC 4088 at 4090; [1990] HCA 3; (1990) 20 ATR 1370 at 1372 Brennan J. posed the questions in these terms:

". . . does the taxpayer discharge the burden of proving

that the assessment is excessive where (a) he does not

prove that the amount assessed as his taxable income in

fact exceeds his taxable income, but (b) he shows that the

Commissioner formed a judgment as to the amount of his

taxable income on a wrong basis?"

67. The answer appears at pages 4093-4; 1375-6 of the judgment of Brennan J. (with whom the other judges agreed) where his Honour stated:

"But where, as here, the taxpayer has not proved that his

actual taxable income is less than the amount assessed, the

Court does not know all the material facts and it cannot

find that the amount assessed in wrong. A taxpayer who

shows on the facts that are known a mere error by the

Commissioner in assessing the amount of the taxpayer's

taxable income does not show that his objection should have

been allowed or that the appeal against the assessment must

be allowed . . . Unless the amount of the assessment is

found to be excessive in the sense of being greater than

the taxable income on which tax ought to have been levied,

the taxpayer fails on his appeal."

68. The same rule applies in respect of claims for deductions which cannot be explained or substantiated by evidence. The taxpayer is the person who best understands his own affairs. If he cannot provide a proper explanation and evidence to support the claim then it is not for this Tribunal to fill the gaps. In such a case the taxpayer fails because he has not made out his case and also because he has not discharged the onus imposed by section 190.

69. As Vegner's Case shows, the fact that the applicant did not know of the existence of the trust until 1979, does not alter the operation of section 97.
