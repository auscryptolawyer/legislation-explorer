ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - assessable income - audit – alleged failure by applicant to make a true and full disclosure - penalties -whether applicant could discharge onus: s 190(b) - additional tax - rate of culpability factor

INCOME TAX ASSESSMENT ACT 1936 ss 170, 190(b),

Case U36 87 ATC 266

Respondent

Tribunal : Mr B J McMahon (Senior Member)

Decision : The Tribunal decides that -

(1) The objection decisions are set aside and the matter is remitted to the respondent with the direction that income tax of the applicant for the three years in question be re-assessed in accordance with the findings in these reasons.

(2) Liberty to apply is reserved to both parties generally.

B J McMahon

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT87/2974-6

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re: <>

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

30 August 1988 Mr B J McMahon (Senior Member)

  1. Following a taxation audit of the applicant's affairs, his assessments for income tax for the years ended 30 June 1981, 1982 and 1983 were amended. The taxable income for each of those years was increased by $25,000, $31,400 and $39,720 respectively. Because of an alleged failure by the applicant to make a true and full disclosure of his assessable income, the amended assessments were issued pursuant to s 170 of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act"). They included provision not only for additional tax on the additional income, but also for interest and for what is sometimes referred to as a penalty.
  2. Thereafter negotiations took place at length between the taxpayer and his representative on the one hand and officers of the respondent Commissioner on the other hand. A document had been prepared as the basis for each of the amended assessments. It lists funds available at the relevant balance date and compares it with funds expended at the subsequent balance date and arrives at an understatement which is assumed to be taxable income. Although 3 assessments were involved, I understood the Commissioner's representative to say that only one "T statement" (as he called it) was prepared and that this was in respect of the year ended 30 June 1985. The assumed shortfalls for the earlier years are extrapolations from the conclusions reached in this one statement. It was agreed that resolution of the present issues, to which I will later refer, would govern the way in which the earlier assessments would be amended, if necessary. The statement, which was tendered in evidence as Exhibit 1, showed under the heading of capital receipts, the sale of a sauna for $1,000. The applicant's case was that the shortfall could be accounted for by the sale of other assets. At the time of the hearing of this application, the respondent Commissioner was satisfied to add the proceeds from the sale of some assets to this item, but not to add the asset in question, namely, a collection of household furniture. The applicant says he sold this collection for $17,985, receiving that sum on 18 September 1982. The main issue in the proceedings therefore was, whether the applicant could discharge the onus cast upon him by s 190(b) of the Act to disprove the existence of a fact or assumption upon which the amended assessment relied. It was agreed that if he had discharged this onus, then the funds available column should be amended, which would result in a diminution of the total understatement shown on the document. There would still be a shortfall of $11,835 to which I will later return.
  3. The applicant gave evidence that between 1980 and 1983 he was the co-proprietor with his wife and was managing director of a company that dealt in office stationery. He had married in 1960, and moved to Newcastle in 1965 residing at 3 houses in turn. The last house was quite large. The applicant and his wife also had a weekender. They separated in June 1982 and were divorced in August 1983.
  4. During the 23 years of marriage, furniture was purchased by the applicant from retail stores in Sydney, Newcastle and Hornsby. Prior to June 1982, both the large house and the weekender were sold and the applicant and his wife moved into a much smaller house. As they intended to build a new house, the surplus furniture from the 2 previous houses was stored at the warehouse of the applicant's company. While it was located in the 2 previous houses, the furniture was insured for $20,000 and $10,000 respectively. Certificates of insurance were tendered in support of this evidence.
  5. After the separation, the applicant agreed to marry another woman. It was agreed between them that he would move into her house and that, in order to make a new start, he should sell the furniture which had been intended for the new house with his first wife, but which was now in store in his warehouse. As part of the divorce settlement, it had been arranged that his first wife should retain the furniture in the small house, and that he was at liberty to sell the remainder of it. He had noticed a sign for a company on the main road that advertised itself as used furniture dealers. He arranged for their representative to visit the warehouse and to inspect the furniture. He gave detailed evidence of the negotiations that went on with that representative whose name he could not recall. He gave him a hand written list of the furniture and asked for payment of around $20,000. He said that negotiations went on, item by item, and that eventually the company agreed to pay the sum to which I have referred. The list of furniture was later typed out by the applicant from memory. It was given to the respondent's officers during negotiations. There is no evidence to suggest that it did not correspond in all material respects with the handwritten list. The typed list was tendered in evidence as Exhibit 4. The dealer company sent a truck to collect the furniture. Corroborative evidence of the existence of the furniture and of its subsequent removal was given by a former employee of the company.

6. The applicant asked for payment of $17,985 in cash as he did not know the credit or standing of the dealer. This was agreed to reluctantly. The following day, 18 September 1982, he attended the dealer's premises and received the cash. There was tendered in evidence a copy of a numbered folio from a receipt book signed by the applicant and acknowledging to the named dealer receipt of the relevant amount. The applicant denied that there was any element of secrecy about the fact that payment was made in cash. He proposed to deal with it in an open way. In fact he paid it into an account he had with a building society in Newcastle. A copy of the ledger sheet of that account was tendered in evidence and confirmed that on the same day that he received the cash, an identical sum was paid to his credit with that account.

7. This account of the facts was given to investigators during the tax audit but was not accepted because it could not be independently verified. In order to assist the investigators further, the applicant undertook more enquiries. He began by looking for the furniture dealing company and found that it had gone into liquidation and that the liquidators were in Sydney. He made an appointment to see them and travelled to Sydney to enquire about what evidence the liquidator could make available. He was told by the liquidator that the records were incomplete and that the liquidator was unable to help him. He did however, give the applicant the name of a person who, the liquidator believed, was the manager. On his return to Newcastle, the applicant checked the phone books without success to find that manager. He then went to Dunn & Bradstreet and paid them a fee in order to search for that person. They were unsuccessful. He then went to the Taxation Department in Newcastle and asked for their help in locating former employees of the furniture dealing company. He thought that perhaps names and addresses shown on group certificate returns could be made available and that he could pursue his enquiries through one or more of these employees. At first he was told that he needed the authority of the liquidator for this purpose. He therefore wrote to the liquidator and obtained that written authority. On presenting this to the Taxation Department, he was then told that the information could not be made available to him because of secrecy provisions in the Act. All these inquiries are corroborated by correspondence that was tendered in evidence during the hearing of these proceedings. Apart from the efforts of the applicant personally, evidence was also given that his accountants had also spoken with the liquidators. Correspondence between them was also tendered in evidence. The absence of independent corroborative evidence does not, of itself, mean that I should reject the whole of the applicant's evidence, particularly when the absence of such corroboration has been fully explained.

  1. If a criminal standard proof were to be adopted, I would agree that the evidence produced did not prove beyond all reasonable doubt that the applicant sold the furniture for the stated sum on the stated day. However, on the standards applicable, in the present proceedings, I believe that he should succeed. I am satisfied that he received the stated sum as a capital receipt on the day and from the source stated.
  2. His evidence was quite unshaken in cross-examination. No suggestion has been made by the Commissioner or his representative of any other basis for explaining the items and documents to which I have referred. Nothing that was said in cross-examination in any way detracted from the probative value of the evidence given by the applicant. I accept the applicant on this issue as a man of truth. The account that he gave of the sale of the furniture is probable and believable. The way in which it was accounted for is also credible. At the time it must be remembered that he had not only lost his marriage of 23 years, he gave evidence that in 1984, his company also went into liquidation. Having regard to these handicaps and to the intensive efforts made by him to seek and put forward corroboration of his version of events, I have no difficulty in finding that a sum of $17,985 should have been brought into account as a capital receipt on 18 September 1982. The applicant's evidence as regards the sale of other assets has already been accepted by the Commissioner. I believe that his evidence concerning the furniture should also be accepted.
  3. The extent of the burden of proof to be undertaken by the taxpayer in circumstances such as this, was recently referred to in a decision of McCauley v The Commissioner of Taxation (unreported - Sydney 22 July 1988). In his judgment, Lockhart J reviewed the authorities and reiterated the heavy burden which taxpayers disputing assessments of this nature must undertake. In my view, the applicant has discharged the onus so far as the furniture item is concerned.
  4. A shortfall of $11,835 however, still remains unexplained. The applicant considered that it could be accounted for by the sale of other assets. He said that at the time, he had two garage sales from his home that he had sold a portable generator, a box trailer, a welding outfit and other pieces of furniture. He could however give no details as to the person to whom he had sold these items, the amounts he received, or the dates of the sales. In cases of this nature it is not sufficient for imprecise evidence or guesses to take the place of hard positive evidence. The evidence attempting to account for the shortfall of $11,835 is by no means as strong as the evidence concerning the furniture. I am not prepared to accept in fact that the applicant has discharged the onus upon him in relation to that shortfall. On the evidence put before me, there is no basis upon which to alter the capital receipts item of the calculation, except in relation to the furniture and for the amount to which I have referred.

12. There remains the question of penalties. Additional tax will be calculated in the ordinary way, depending upon the ultimate extent of the assessed understatement. The interest rate of 20% per annum should not be disturbed. The only aspect of the penalties calling for comment is the rate of the culpability factor.

13. Although the applicant has not satisfactorily accounted for the shortfall, it could not be said that there was a deliberate failure on his part to disclose taxable income. Indeed in his own mind, he still believes that he has fulfilled all his obligations to make such a disclosure. There is no suggestion that assets have been hidden or that the applicant has engaged in any conduct that could be described as guilty. Whilst it is no answer to an assessment of additional tax to rely on an honest error, honesty is most material when it comes to determining the appropriate amount of additional tax. The evidence is that he co-operated to a quite extraordinary degree with the investigator and in fact was successful in convincing him of the existence of certain facts that were favourable to the applicant. Co-operation during the course of an investigation must also be considered in determining the amount of additional tax.

  1. Having regard to the various examples of culpability-rates considered and described in Case U36 87 ATC 266 and having regard to the principles stated in that decision that are applicable in cases of this nature, I consider that an appropriate culpability factor rate would be 20%.
  2. The objection decisions are therefore set aside and the matter is remitted to the respondent with the direction that income tax of the applicant for the 3 years in question be re-assessed in accordance with the findings in these reasons. In case there should be any difficulty in implementing this decision both parties will have leave to apply.
