Re Taxation Appeals [1990] AATA 8; 21 ATR 3197 (25 January 1990)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

No. NT88/1450

AAT No. 5634

Income Tax

[1990] AATA 8; 21 ATR 3197

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

P.M. Roach(1) (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Assessable Income - Derivation - Whether income derived by husband or partnership of husband and wife.

Income Tax - Burden of proof - Inferences drawn from conduct and records of parties - Failures to call witnesses.

Income Tax - Practice and Procedure - Whether proceedings adversarial in nature - Special responsibilities of Respondents.

Income Tax Assessment Act

Sutton v F.C.T. [1959] HCA 3; (1959) 100 CLR 518

Jones v Dunkel [1959] HCA 8; (1959) 101 CLR 298

Fletcher and Ors v F.C.T. (88 ATC 4834)

Australian Postal Commission v Hayes and another (MG 121 of 1989)

Case U225 (87 ATC 1253).

HEARING

SYDNEY

25:1:1990

ORDER

The order of the Tribunal will be that the decision of the Commissioner upon the objection under review shall be varied and that the taxable income of the Applicant for the years of income ended 30 June 1982 to 1986 inclusive shall be reduced by $9,805, $11,625, $12,636, $15,576 and $16,798 respectively; and that the spouse's rebate as assessed and allowed shall be reduced accordingly.

DECISION

In about 1970 the Applicant negotiated with an acquaintance for the purchase of a truck . The vendor introduced the Applicant to the Mayne Nickless organisation of carriers as a prospective "owner-driver". The Applicant was accepted by a division of Mayne Nickless now known, and hereafter referred to, as "Railex". As a result the Applicant and his wife purchased the truck with funds borrowed from the bank. Later, similarly funded, they purchased a second second-hand truck and later still, they jointly acquired a new truck under lease from a finance company. It was a ten tonne truck suitable for the carriage of goods. Acting on a view that, as a married couple they were partners in all things, they presented income tax returns to the Commissioner on the basis that the business was conducted by a partnership of husband and wife. Then in the late 1970's they were made sharply aware that, in the eyes of the law, the fundamentally important social partnership of marriage did not automatically extend to commercial activity or the taxation system. Amended assessments issued which resulted in the payment of something of the order of an additional $13,000 in tax and, presumably, additional tax. No details were made available.

2. Then, acting on the advice of their accountant, on 1 June 1979, the couple entered into a partnership agreement in writing. It recited an agreement between the parties that they:

"shall be and become partners in the the business of

haulage contractors," and

the agreement provided that:

(1) The partnership shall be deemed to have commenced

on the first day of July 1977 and shall continue

from that date until determined in manner

hereinafter provided."

The attempt to give retrospective effect to the agreement is of no moment in relation to the issues which now need to be resolved.

3. Having entered into the agreement, I accept that, by their accountant, the couple gave notice of the agreement and of its execution to the Commissioner, but I am not persuaded that either of them, or any person representing them, ever directly brought the existence of that document to the attention of Railex. Subject to some considerations yet to be mentioned, on one view all that had happened was that the husband and wife had brought into existence between themselves a new legal relationship: that of partnership. But that of itself did nothing to change the relationships between Railex and the person or persons with whom it was dealing.

4. Thereafter the income of the business was returned, as before, as income of the partnership. Several years passed and then there was a further investigation which resulted in the Commissioner issuing notices of amended assessment on 22 October 1987 relating to the years of income ended 30 June 1982 to 1986 inclusive. The adjustment sheet which attended the notices of assessment disclosed the following:

5. Liability to tax was increased accordingly but the Commissioner exercised his discretion and wholly remitted additional tax.

6. The Applicant objected to the amended assessments. Upon the objections being disallowed, at the request of the Applicant, the matters were referred to this Tribunal for independent determination. Upon the hearing the only issue which the Tribunal was called on to decide was whether the assessments were excessive by reason of the Commissioner attributing the entire earnings of the business to the Applicant, rather than to the partnership.

7. A single issue arose for determination. If an issue of such magnitude had arisen for determination on a dispute between Railex and the Applicant, the community of New South Wales would have made access to the institutions of justice available on payment of a fee of $25, payable by the claimant. But, this being a Commonwealth tax matter, it is the person against whom the claim is made who has to pay the fees - and the fees in this instance amounted to $1,500. A system which exacts such fees as the price of seeking a just determination, a determination to be made independently of the institutions of government making claim upon a citizen, does not deserve to be called a system of justice.

8. All preliminaries to ensure, first, that the matter needed to be heard and, secondly, to set conditions for the fair hearing of the matter were conducted by telephone to the convenience and advantage of all and with substantial savings to the community and the individuals concerned.

9. Upon the hearing it was not contended by Counsel for the Commissioner that the couple could not have been in partnership; or that it was not possible for the partnership to have derived the income in question. To that extent, the issue is different from that which arose in Case U225 (87 ATC 1253). In that case, the Commissioner had attributed to a taxpayer all of the income generated by his activities as a timber faller and which he claimed had been derived by his wife and himself in partnership. It was argued for the Commissioner that the nature of the husband's duties to the contractor were such that he was at all time standing as a servant in a master/servant relationship to the contractor. I rejected the submission, and then addressed the more serious difficulty facing the Applicants. I went on to say:

"The evidence presented before me is such that I cannot

identify any arrangement made with the contractor for

whom work was being done in May 1983 which would amount

to a novation substituting the partnership for the

individual. Nor is there any evidence before me which

satisfies me that at any later time, whether in dealing

with that contractor or the contractor who in time

succeeded the first contractor, there was brought about

a contractual relationship between the contractor and

the partnership."

As a result I concluded that on the evidence the assessable income claimed to have been derived by the partnership was derived by the husband. The same issue is the central issue in the present reference.

10. The greatest difficulty in face of the Applicant is the circumstance that no direct evidence has been presented that the parties to the original arrangement - Railex and the Applicant - ever changed that arrangement or even that it was ever proposed to Railex that the identity of the party contracting as "owner-driver" should be changed. A second level of difficulty pointed to by Counsel for the Commissioner is that, as the Transport Workers' Union and Railex came over the years to be parties to awards in relation to the work of "owner-drivers" such as the Applicant; and as Railex came to pay remuneration and provide other benefits such as sick leave, long service leave, annual leave, workers' compensation and the like for the Applicant, in many respects as if he were an employee, then the contract was at least in some sense a personal one. I do not consider the latter contention to be substantial. Examination of the relevant awards discloses that they purported to embrace as "owner-drivers" all persons who, whether as independent contractors to Railex or otherwise, provided services to Railex as carriers using vehicles which they provided and that the awards so made were not by any means limited to "employees" standing in a master/servant relationship. They expressly represented the awards as embracing contracts with individuals; with partnerships; and with bodies corporate.

11. So it was that the case as argued turned on a question as to the identity of the person or persons deriving the income generated by service to Railex. There was no argument advanced to suggest that what was attributed through the partnership accounts to the wife of the Applicant constituted a loss or outgoing deductible to the Applicant. As no contention to that effect was raised, I shall not address the question (Fletcher and Ors v FCT 88 ATC 4834). That being so it is unnecessary to consider whether relief on such grounds might have been available (cf. the decision of the Tribunal of 18 December 1989 VT87/2951).

12. As to the identity of the person deriving the income, clearly at the outset that person was the Applicant. That was not changed by the execution of a partnership agreement or by communicating the fact of the partnership agreement to the Commissioner. There was no direct evidence that the fact that the partnership agreement was ever brought to the attention of Railex and no direct evidence from any officer of Railex that Railex has ever considered itself to be dealing with the partnership in succession to the Applicant trading as an individual.

13. However, the records of Railex produced in evidence and other evidence also show a number of other things. First, commonly but not invariably, the Applicant in completing "bundy" cards recorded the claimants as both husband and wife, using the initials of each coupled with the family surname. Secondly, the evidence of the Applicant and his wife was that payments were made by cheques made out in favour of both the Applicant and his wife. Without more, such evidence would not have persuaded as to the identity of the contracting parties. But there was more to be learned from those records. The computer printout of the company's record of payments identified the payees as being "J & G M ......". The same records identified some other payees in a manner suggesting some other dealings with partnerships of husband and wife and yet other dealings with parties identified by names suggestive of "business names" or of being imprecise references to the names of corporations. The information so recorded appears on a form recording financial information as to payments to "owner-drivers" - a phrase attributed to all persons providing services to Railex and, because "owner-driver" is a term used in reference to a wide range of contracting parties, appropriate to identify individuals, partnerships or companies. Finally, the records of Railex, confirmed by the adjustment sheet which was issued by the Commissioner (cf. pa. 4 ante) show that Railex remitted moneys on account of the partnership in accordance with the obligations arising under the Prescribed Payment System.

14. However there was yet a further source of available information, available through documents produced to the Tribunal but not tendered in evidence by either party, although both were free to do so. That the existence of that evidence came to be known at all to the Tribunal is attributable to the limited resouces of personnel available to the former Taxation Board of Review Members as Senior Members of this Tribunal. Unlike Deputy Presidents of the Tribunal and most other Senior Members, they have never had available to them the services on an ongoing basis of any particular attendant during hearings. With rare exceptions, even during prolonged hearings, the orderlies appointed to the hearing have changed daily and sometimes more frequently. In consequence, in my experience there has been no uniformity or consistency in dealing with documents produced to the Tribunal in answer to summonses.

15. In these proceedings it happened that the orderly of the day had gathered together the documents exhibited and the documents produced under summons which had not been exhibited. The result was that when the time came to examine the exhibits, there came to hand materials not sighted during the hearing, which had not been exhibited in evidence. In that way I observed through a transparent folder cover documents which I considered to be of some possible relevance. I made a cursory examination of them which confirmed that view. Further, in examining a folder of documents, some only of which had been exhibited in evidence, I observed the existence of other documents seemingly of similar effect.

16. Acting on a view that the principal objective of this Tribunal is to attain a correct result, I decided that it was desirable that the additional information should be received into evidence. However, acting on the view that any decision to be reached should only be reached under conditions fair to both parties, I invited Counsel to indicate whether they consented to those documents being received in evidence - as I indicated I thought they should be - or whether the hearing should be reopened. Counsel were prompt in their responses, for which I express gratitude. What followed thereafter indicates the importance at all times and for both parties of ensuring that all relevant evidence is presented at the appropriate point of time during a hearing. The importance of doing so becomes the greater when it is remembered that, unlike times past when an unsuccessful party before a Taxation Board of Review could elect to re-run his case upon appeal using different witnesses and tactics, such a second chance is no longer available.

17. What thereafter transpired indicates the tension between the administrative processes directed to ensuring that all taxpayers are correctly assessed - no more and no less - and the litigious processes established to determine matters in dispute upon the evidence presented under fair conditions at a trial. It also raises questions as to the nature of proceedings before this Tribunal as distinct from proceedings before the Courts. Such questions are important because it is this Tribunal rather than the Courts which must expect to bear the brunt of applications for independent review presented by taxpayers, either appearing alone or aided by amateur advocates.

18. Three matters emerged out of the proceedings which followed my communication to Counsel. They were:

(a) whether the Tribunal should receive the documents

referred to and if so, on what basis, whether as to the

recall of witnesses or permitting the parties to call

other witnesses;

(b) whether the Applicant should be provided with an

opportunity to re-open his case to call a further

witness who might have been called upon the hearing; and

(c) the significance of the fact that, as to the matters

raised by me, and as to the witness referred to, both

parties had had an opportunity to present the evidence

upon the hearing and neither had chosen to do so.

19. Proceedings before the Tribunal are often spoken of as non-adversarial. For my part I do not understand the concept. The parties are opposed to each other. What one hopes to gain, the other seeks to deny. If a determination upon such matters in dispute is to be made justly, it is essential that both parties are provided with a full opportunity to present their cases under fair conditions and as they choose to present them. Further, as Wilcox J. pointed out in Australian Postal Commission v Hayes and another (MG 121 of 1989 - an unreported decision of 18 May 1989), since mendacity in witnesses is always possible, the fair determination of proceedings may require a vigorous and skilled cross-examination: even on occasions called on for the cross-examination of unrepresented and inexperienced taxpayers at the hands of Senior Counsel. Such proceedings can be as fiercely contested as any Court proceedings. They demonstrate clearly the adversarial process.

20. But what is special about proceedings before the Tribunal is that the respondents who come before the Tribunal as the representatives of government, whether charged with administering the collection of the Revenues (as with the present Respondent) or the distribution of the Revenues (as with some other respondents), have a special responsibility to both the individual and the community at large. That is because, as successive Commissioners of Taxation have accepted, it is the responsibility of the Commissioner by his officers to correctly assess taxpayers; and to assist the Tribunal in its endeavours to that end. So it was that in a joint judgment a Full Bench of the High Court of Australia in Sutton v FCT ((1959) [1959] HCA 3; 100 CLR 518), while confirming that the former Taxation Boards of Review were administrative tribunals not exercising judicial power, nontheless said that the relationships between those Boards and the Commissioner

"should not and doubtless do not begin and end with

legal obligation. As an administrative body reviewing

his decisions upon objections, if the Board finds that

it needs the assistance of this or that information as

to what the Commissioner did, there is no reason why the

Board should ask for it, nor is there anything to

prevent it asking for the information in some specific

form, which the Board might find useful for the

facilitation of the proceedings. Natural justice, it is

needless to say, requires that the taxpayer shall know

the course that is taken and what is placed before the

Board. But the procedure of the Board is not

necessarily a formal or technical affair. The

Commissioner is unlikely to withhold any information or

assistance which the Board might reasonably require, and

it would serve no purpose to speculate as to the effect

that might legitimately be produced upon the Board's

determination of the case by a refusal on the

Commissioner's part to supply information in his

possession without which the Board could not get at a

just conclusion on the merits".

That special obligation on the part of those representing Government to assist the Tribunal in reaching a proper determination was recently emphasised in Ermolaeff v Commonwealth of Australia (N88/500: an unreported decision of 22 August 1989) when Deputy President McMahon criticised the respondent in proceedings before him under the Commonwealth Employees Compensation Act for seeking to have excluded from a hearing evidence from its own experts which happened to favour the Applicant.

21. Nonetheless Counsel for the Respondent in these proceedings protested at what he described as a situation in which the Respondent was prejudiced because, he suggested, sympathy for the Applicant on the part of the Tribunal would result in gratuitous advice from the Tribunal and an invitation to the Applicant - represented by Counsel - to re-open his case. The submission would have been more felicitously and accurately put if it had acknowledged that there would be no disadvantage or prejudice to the Respondent unless the course proposed would result in less tax being levied than should have been levied; and had recognised that what was referred to as "sympathy for the Applicant", was in fact a concern on the part of the Tribunal to ensure a correct assessment.

22. That there was power in the Tribunal to receive additional evidence was not disputed. The substance of the argument for the Commissioner was that the discretion which existed should not be exercised. In the event the seemingly simple question which had been raised by the Tribunal was not one to be determined in isolation from the other issues which came to be subsequently canvassed.

23. The first question having been raised by the Tribunal, the Applicant then sought leave to call a particular officer from Railex. That officer could have been called and presented as a witness in support of the Applicant's case upon the hearing. That was not done. What is more, I accept that Counsel for the Applicant knew that that particular witness had been summonsed to appear before the Tribunal by the Respondent and that Counsel for the Respondent had indicated to his opponent plainly that he had not decided whether or not to call that witness. In the event, having conferred with the witness, Counsel for the Respondent chose not to call him.

24. In those circumstances the question was no longer one of only receiving some documents which might be taken into account in weighing the evidence. If the reception of the documents was to be allowed under fair conditions, there would be a substantial re-opening allowing at least the Applicant and, possibly, in consequence the Respondent an opportunity to present the case in a substantially different manner. In the face of those factors, I declined to re-open - whether to receive evidence of the witness now sought to be called by the Applicant; or the documents to which I had directed attention; or any other evidence which either party might seek to present in consequence. That being so I propose to make the findings which are appropriate based on the evidence which the parties chose to place before me upon the hearing.

25. The pivotal issues and the identification of the parties to the contract during the relevant period. There is no suggestion in the evidence that any contract was formally entered into. The terms of the contract and identification of the parties to it could have been proved by evidence from either or both parties or from any other competent witness who could have given evidence that such an agreement had been entered into prior to the rendering of the services in question; and that there had been no material variation thereafter. On the other hand it might have been proved by evidence of the conduct of the parties and of communications between them during or even following the period under review, the evidence being such as to persuade the Tribunal as a tribunal of fact that the arrangements by which the parties conducted themselves was sufficiently cogent evidence to provide proof of the terms of some antecedent contractual arrangement between them.

26. In the present case it is appropriate to find that initially the Applicant alone was a party to the contract with Railex. That is not disputed. Rather the question is whether in the absence of direct evidence of a change substituting the partnership for the individual prior to the commencement of the period under review, one should find more probably than not that such a change in the contractual relationship had occured. The evidence presented by the Applicants is weak. But it does derive some support from the evidence of the conduct of the parties: the relevant industrial award recognised partnerships and companies as possible "owner-drivers"; by the use of the "bundy" cards, the Applicant commonly claimed payment on behalf of the partnership; payment for services was made by cheques in the favour of the partnership; the records of Railex identifying creditors for cartage services often identified partnerships and others than individuals as creditors for those services; the Applicant and his wife were so identified as persons entitled to payment; and Railex remitted moneys to the credit of the partnership to the Commissioner under the Prescribed Payment System. All of those practices point to the partnership being a party to the contract during the relevant period.

27. But those indicators have to be weighed in the balance against such contra-indicators as there are. As the evidence stands, there was no direct evidence to the contrary. The contra-indicators are only that the original arrangement did not involve the Applicant's wife; that there is no direct evidence that the fact of the partnership was communicated to Railex on any particular occasion or by any particular method; and there is no direct evidence that on any particular occasion or by any particular method Railex communicated to the partnership its acceptance of the partnership as a contracting party.

28. On the other hand if the original arrangement as to the identification of contracting parties remained unchanged, it is difficult to offer any reasonable explanation as to why Railex conducted itself as it did, not only in effecting payment by cheques in favour of the partnership, but also in maintaining its own records as showing itself as indebted to the partnership and effecting P.P.S. remittances as it did.

29. The judgment called for is a fine one. Bearing in mind that a witness from Railex would not have had any interest in the outcome - particularly as all relationships with the Applicant had been severed prior to the hearing - it would have been more appropriate that such a witness should have been called on behalf of the Respondent than be called by the Applicant (Jones v Dunkel ((1959) [1959] HCA 8; 101 CLR 298). I also note that, following the submissions as to the re-opening of the hearing, it is no longer a matter of the absence of any witness from Railex being unexplained. Instead the Tribunal has it on the authority of Counsel for the Respondent that he elected not to call the witness under summons to the witness box. In the absence of any such witness to speak either directly to the contractual relationship or as to the significance of the documents of Railex, I consider that those circumstances provide confirmation of the view I have formed that it is more probable than not that during the relevant period Railex was dealing with the partnership and knew itself to be dealing with the partnership as a contracting party. In consequence I find that the income in question was derived by the partnership.

30. The order of the Tribunal will be that the decision of the Commissioner upon the objections under review shall be varied and that the taxable income of the Applicant for the years of income ended 30 June 1982 to 1986 inclusive shall be reduced by $9,805, $11,625, $12,636, $15,576, $16,798; and that the spouse rebates as assessed and allowed shall be reduced accordingly.
