REASONS FOR DECISION

18 May 1998 Justice Mathews, President

BACKGROUND

1. This is an application to review a decision of the Veterans' Review Board (VRB) dated 13 June 1996, upholding a decision of the respondent which in turn refused the applicant's claim for a pension under s 13 of the Veterans' Entitlements Act 1986 (the Act). The applicant claimed that the death of her husband, in June 1994, was war-caused within the meaning of the Act. Both the respondent and the VRB found that his death was not war-caused, and thus refused the claim.

2. The cause of the veteran's death was certified to be myocardial infarction due to ischaemic heart disease. Accordingly, the applicant's success in these proceedings will be dependent upon a finding that the veteran's ischaemic heart disease was war-caused under the Act. As the veteran's war service was operational service, the matter falls to be determined by reference to ss 120(1) and 120(3) of the Act.

3. The applicant's claim for a pension was made after 1 June 1994. Accordingly, the amendments introduced by the Veterans' Affairs (1994 - 1995 Budget Measures) Legislation Amendment Act 1994 (the 1994 amendments) apply in this case. However at the time of the lodgement of the claim, namely on 12 September 1994, there was no Statement of Principles (SoP) relating to ischaemic heart disease in existence. The first SoP relating to this condition (Instrument No. 85 of 1995) did not come into effect until March 1995, that is some fourteen months before the applicant's claim was determined. The Commission, in rejecting the applicant's claim, referred to the factors outlined in the SoP and concluded that a reasonable hypothesis connecting the veteran's death with his war service had not been established.

ISSUE FOR DETERMINATION IN THIS CASE

4. There are a number of issues that must be dealt with before this matter can be resolved. However, the parties have isolated a preliminary question of law to be determined at this stage. That question is as follows: given that there was no SoP in relation to ischaemic heart disease in force at the date of the claim for pension, and given that there is now one in force, should this matter be determined as if there were no relevant SoP, that is by reference to subss 120(1) and (3) alone, or should it be determined by recourse to the relevant SoP?

5. The applicant says that she has an accrued right to have her claim dealt with according to the law which applied at the time of the making of her claim, and that the SoP relating to ischaemic heart disease is not applicable in this case. The respondent relies upon the legislative scheme which, it is said, makes it clear that a decision-maker is to apply the law as it exists at the date of its decision. As there is now a SoP relating to the veteran's terminal condition, the respondent submits that it must be applied in these proceedings.

6. Whatever my finding on this question may be, it will not produce a final resolution of this case. If I were to find in favour of the applicant, it is agreed that there are outstanding factual and medical issues which would need to be explored before a final determination can be made. Similarly, a finding in favour of the respondent on this issue will be likely to leave outstanding one, and possibly two, further questions of law. First, whether the legislative scheme requires that a hypothesis connecting a veteran's war service with an intermediate cause of death (as opposed to an ultimate cause of death) must be upheld by a relevant SoP. One of the factors listed in the SoP relating to ischaemic heart disease is hypertension. The veteran is known to have suffered from hypertension. There will thus be a question as to whether a hypothesis connecting the veteran's hypertension with his war service will need to be upheld by the SoP relating to hypertension; or is this a matter to be determined by subss 120(1) and (3) alone without reference to any SoP?

7. There may also be a question as to which of the several versions of the SoP in relation to ischaemic heart disease should be applied in this case. The case of Lee v Secretary, Department of Social Security (1996) 68 FCR 491 would appear to be authority for the proposition that the SoP which was in force at the date of the primary decision should be applied by the AAT, provided that that SoP was more favourable to an applicant than the SoP currently in force. However that was a majority decision and the issue might to some extent still be at large (see Repatriation Commission v McLean (Federal court, 17 April 1998, NG946/1997)). However, given the issues involved in the present case, it is unnecessary to address either of the above questions here.

LEGISLATIVE SCHEME

8. In order to explore the matter currently at issue, it is necessary to cite the relevant legislation. Subsections 120(1) and 120(3) relate to the standard of proof in claims arising from the death of veterans who have rendered operational service. They are in the following terms:

Standard of proof

120. (1) Where a claim under Part II for a pension in respect of the incapacity from injury or disease of a veteran, or of the death of a veteran, relates to the operational service rendered by the veteran, the Commission shall determine that the injury was a war-caused injury, that the disease was a war-caused disease or that the death of the veteran was war-caused, as the case may be, unless it is satisfied, beyond reasonable doubt, that there is no sufficient ground for making that determination.

Note: This subsection is affected by section 120A.

...

(3) In applying subsection (1) or (2) in respect of the incapacity of a person from injury or disease, or in respect of the death of a person, related to service rendered by the person, the Commission shall be satisfied, beyond reasonable doubt, that there is no sufficient ground for determining:

(a) that the injury was a war-caused injury or a defence-caused injury;

(b) that the disease was a war-caused disease or a defence-caused disease; or

(c) that the death was war-caused or defence-caused;

as the case may be, if the Commission, after consideration of the whole of the material before it, is of the opinion that the material before it does not raise a reasonable hypothesis connecting the injury, disease or death with the circumstances of the particular service rendered by the person.

Note: This subsection is affected by section 120A.

...

9. These provisions applied, without modification, to all claims made before 1 June 1994. They still do so in relation to claims made since that date where there is no SoP applicable to the injury, disease or death in question. The application of these provisions has given rise to significant legal debate and to a steady stream of judicial decisions. To summarise the effect of the major decisions in this area (Bushell v Repatriation Commission [1992] HCA 47; (1992) 175 CLR 408, Byrnes v Repatriation Commission [1993] HCA 51; (1993) 177 CLR 564 and East v Repatriation Commission [1987] FCA 242; (1987) 16 FCR 517), they establish that the following situation will apply in cases involving s 120(1) and (3): a reasonable hypothesis will be "raised" by material before a decision-maker, if the material points to, and not merely leaves open, the hypothesis as being a reasonable one. On the other hand, a hypothesis may be reasonable without being proved on the balance of probabilities. Accordingly, an asserted connection between a veteran's war service and a subsequent death or incapacity may be reasonable even though it is theoretical as opposed to proven. Nevertheless a hypothesis will not be reasonable if it is contrary to proved scientific facts or if it is obviously fanciful, impossible, incredible, or too remote or too tenuous. But as the majority observed in Bushell ( at p 415), it will be a rare case where a hypothesis which is put forward by a medical practitioner who is eminent in a relevant field of knowledge will be found not to be reasonable, notwithstanding that it is in conflict with other medical opinion on the issue.

10. Accordingly the situation was reached, before the 1994 amendments, where a hypothesis was likely to be treated as reasonable if it was put forward by a medical practitioner who was eminent in a particular field of knowledge, notwithstanding that it might be in conflict with other medical opinions on the subject. The raising of a reasonable hypothesis thus depended, in some cases, upon the applicant being able to find a single eminent practitioner who could support the hypothesis, notwithstanding that the practitioner's views were in conflict with the bulk of medical opinions on the subject. Once a reasonable hypothesis was raised, the claim would be successful unless the factual foundation upon which the hypothesis rested was disproved beyond reasonable doubt.

11. It was this situation which the 1994 amendments were designed to address. As relevant here, the amendments consisted of the insertion of the notes at the end of subss 120(1) and (3) and the insertion of ss 120A and 196A et seq. Section 120A provides as follows:

Reasonableness of hypothesis to be assessed by reference to Statement of Principles

120A. (1) This section applies to any of the following claims made on or after 1 June 1994:

(a) a claim under Part II that relates to the operational service rendered by a veteran;

(b) a claim under Part IV that relates to:

(i) the peacekeeping service rendered by a member of a Peacekeeping Force; or

(ii) the hazardous service rendered by a member of the Forces.

Note 1: Subsections 120 (1), (2) and (3) are relevant to these claims.

Note 2: For "peacekeeping service", "member of a Peacekeeping Force", "hazardous service"

and "member of the Forces" see subsection 5Q (1A).

(2) If the Repatriation Medical Authority has given notice under section 196G that it intends to carry out an investigation in respect of a particular kind of injury, disease or death, the Commission is not to determine a claim in respect of the incapacity of a person from an injury or disease of that kind, or in respect of a death of that kind, unless or until the Authority:

(a) has determined a Statement of Principles under subsection 196B(2) in respect of that kind of injury, disease or death; or

(b) has declared that it does not propose to make such a Statement of Principles.

(3) For the purposes of subsection 120 (3), a hypothesis connecting an injury suffered by a person, a disease contracted by a person or the death of a person with the circumstances of any particular service rendered by the person is reasonable only if there is in force:

(a) a Statement of Principles determined under subsection 196B (2) or (11); or

(b) a determination of the Commission under subsection 180A (2); that upholds the hypothesis.

Note: See subsection (4) about the application of this subsection.

(4) Subsection (3) does not apply in relation to a claim in respect of the incapacity from injury or disease, or the death, of a person if the Authority has neither determined a Statement of Principles under subsection 196B (2), nor declared that it does not propose to make such a Statement of Principles, in respect of:

(a) the kind of injury suffered by the person; or

(b) the kind of disease contracted by the person; or

(c) the kind of death met by the person;

as the case may be.

12. Section 196A established the Repatriation Medical Authority (RMA) and Section 196B set out its functions. Section 196B, as relevant here, provides as follows:

Functions of Authority

196B. (1) This section sets out the functions of the Repatriation Medical Authority.

Determination of Statement of Principles

(2) If the Authority is of the view that there is sound medical-scientific evidence that indicates that a particular kind of injury, disease or death can be related to:

(a) operational service rendered by veterans; or

(b) peacekeeping service rendered by members of Peacekeeping Forces; or

(c) hazardous service rendered by members of the Forces;

the Authority must determine a Statement of Principles in respect of that kind of injury, disease or death setting out:

(d) the factors that must as a minimum exist; and

(e) which of those factors must be related to service rendered by a person;

before it can be said that a reasonable hypothesis has been raised connecting an injury, disease or death of that kind with the circumstances of that service.

Note 1: For "sound medical-scientific evidence" see subsection 5AB (2).

Note 2: For "peacekeeping service", "member of a Peacekeeping Force", "hazardous service"

and "member of the Forces" see subsection 5Q (1A).

Note 3: For "factor related to service" see subsection (14).

...

Investigation

(4) If the Authority:

(a) receives a request under section 196E to carry out an investigation in respect of a particular kind of injury, disease or death; or

(b) of its own initiative, decides that a particular kind of injury, disease or death ought to be investigated for the purposes of this Act to find out whether a Statement of Principles may be determined in respect of it;

the Authority must carry out an investigation to obtain information that would enable the Authority to establish:

(c) how the injury may be suffered, the disease may be contracted or the death may occur; and

(d) the extent (if any) to which the injury, disease or death may be war-caused or defence-caused.

Note 1: For "war-caused" see sections 8 and 9.

Note 2: For "defence-caused" see section 69.

(5) If, after carrying out the investigation, the Authority is of the view that there is sound medical-scientific evidence on which it can rely to determine a Statement of Principles under subsection (2) or (3), in respect of that kind of injury, disease or death, the Authority must do so as soon as practicable.

Note: This subsection does not mean that the Authority must carry out an investigation before it

can determine a Statement of Principles under subsection (2) or (3).

(6) If, after carrying out the investigation, the Authority is of the view:

(a) that there is no sound medical-scientific evidence on which it can rely to determine a Statement of Principles under subsection (2) or (3) in respect of that kind of injury, disease or death; or

(b) that the sound medical-scientific evidence on which it can rely is insufficient to allow it to do so;

the Authority must make a declaration in writing:

(c) stating that it does not propose to make a Statement of Principles; and

(d) giving the reasons for its decision.

...

13. Subsections (7) to (13) relate to the subsequent investigation and review of SoPs both by the RMA and by the Specialist Medical Review Council, a body established under s 196A of the Act. Section 196D provides that a determination of the RMA under s 196B is a disallowable instrument for the purposes of s 46A of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901.

14. Section 196E provides that any one of the following: the Repatriation Commission, a person eligible to make a claim for a pension, or a veterans' organisation, may ask the RMA to carry out an investigation under s 196B(4) in respect of a particular kind of injury, disease or death. Section 196G provides that as soon as practicable after either receiving such a request, or deciding on its own initiative to carry out such an investigation, the RMA must publish in the Gazette a notice stating that it intends to carry out such an investigation and inviting written submissions. It is the giving of this notice which activates s 120A(2), and requires the Commission to defer the determination of any claim relating to the injury or disease specified in the notice until the RMA has either determined a SoP in respect of that injury or disease, or declared that it does not propose to make one. Section 120A(2) is particularly relevant to the issues raised here for reasons which will emerge shortly.

DISCUSSION OF ISSUES

15. I turn now to the arguments adduced in this case. Mr Vincent's primary submission is that the applicant had an accrued right under s 8 of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 (the AI Act) to have her claim dealt with according to the law which was in force at the time of its lodgement. At that time there was no SoP in relation to ischaemic heart disease. If the claim had been determined before the first such SoP came into effect it would have been dealt with under the pre-1994 system. Mr Vincent's submission is, as I have said, primarily based on s 8 of the AI Act. However, before I turn to discuss that section, I should briefly mention a further argument raised by Mr Vincent, based upon s 48(2)(a) of the AI Act.

SECTION 48(2)(A) OF THE ACTS INTERPRETATION ACT 1901

16. As mentioned earlier, SoPs are disallowable instruments under s 196D of the Act and thus the provisions of Parts XI and XII of the AI Act apply. By virtue of s 46A(1)(a) (contained in Part XI of the AI Act), SoPs are treated as if they were regulations for the purposes of ss 48, 48A, 48B, 49 and 50. Subsections 48(1) and (2) stipulate the date upon which regulations will take effect. These subsections provide as follows:

Regulations

48. (1) Where an Act confers power to make regulations, then, unless the contrary intention appears, all regulations made accordingly:

(a) shall be notified in the Gazette;

(b) shall, subject to this section, take effect from:

(i) a specified date;

(ii) a specified time on a specified date;

(iii) the date, or date and time, of commencement of a specified Act or a specified provision of an Act; or

(iv) in any other case - the date of notification; and

(c) shall be laid before each House of the Parliament within 15 sitting days of that House after the making of the regulations.

(2) A regulation, or a provision of regulations, has no effect if, apart from this subsection, it would take effect before the date of notification and as a result:

(a) the rights of a person (other than the Commonwealth or an authority of the Commonwealth) as at the date of notification would be affected so as to disadvantage that person; or

(b) liabilities would be imposed on a person (other than the Commonwealth or an authority of the Commonwealth) in respect of anything done or omitted to be done before the date of notification.

...

17. Mr Vincent argues that s 48(2)(a) provides a legislative bar to the retrospectivity of delegated legislation to which it applies. By virtue of this provision, he says, a SoP cannot be backdated if to do so would be disadvantageous to the rights of a person. He relies on passages in Watson and Another v Lee and Another [1979] HCA 53; (1979) 144 CLR 374 to support this argument. In particular Barwick CJ spoke strongly against the retrospective operation of regulations, describing such a concept as "fundamentally unjust" and "tyrannical" (at p 379).

18. Whilst accepting the force of these comments, I do not see how they apply here. SoPs, being disallowable instruments, constitute part of the legal framework in which an administrative decision-maker is required to make his or her determinations. It is not suggested that SoPs will have retrospective operation, but that once determined, they will be applied from then on in the decision-making process. For it is well established that administrative decision-makers are obliged to apply the law as it exists at the time of decision (Esber v The Commonwealth of Australia and Another [1992] HCA 20; (1992) 174 CLR 430; Re Costello and Secretary, Department of Transport (1979) 2 ALD 934). In Esber, Brennan J made the following observation:

The appellant's application to the A.A.T. invoked an administrative jurisdiction to review his application under s. 49(1) for a redemption payment. Exercising an administrative jurisdiction, the A.A.T. determines applications for review on a rehearing de novo, acting on the materials before it when it makes its determination...[Builders Licensing Board v. Sperway Constructions (Syd.) Pty. Ltd. [1976] HCA 62; (1976), 135 CLR 616, at pp. 620-621; Drake v. Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (1979), 24 ALR 577, at pp. 589, 599; Re Costello (1979), 2 ALD 934, at pp. 940, 943.] Where, on a rehearing de novo, the question for decision is whether an applicant should be granted a right, the law as it then exists is applied, not the law as it existed at an earlier time ...[Harris v. Caladine [1991] HCA 9; (1991), 172 CLR 84, at p. 125; Re Costello (1979), 2 ALD, at p. 944.] By contrast, in a judicial proceeding brought to enforce an alleged right accrued at the time when the proceedings were instituted, the question for decision is determined according to the law existing when the proceedings were instituted unless statute otherwise provides. (pp 448-449)

19. It follows that, from the moment that a SoP comes into effect, it constitutes the medical/scientific "template" which, as a matter of law, any administrative decision-maker will be bound to apply. The SoP does not have retrospective effect. It becomes part of the legal framework which must thenceforth, in the ordinary course of events, be applied by administrative decision-makers.

SECTION 8 OF THE ACTS INTERPRETATION ACT

20. I turn now to s 8 of the AI Act. This section, as relevant here, operates to prevent claimants being disadvantaged by reason of recent changes in the law which would otherwise deprive them of a right which they enjoyed at an earlier relevant time. This provision is crucial to the applicant's case, and I turn now to discuss it. It is in the following terms:

Effect of repeal

8. Where an Act repeals in the whole or in part a former Act, then unless the contrary intention appears the repeal shall not:

(a) revive anything not in force or existing at the time at which the repeal takes effect; or

(b) affect the previous operation of any Act so repealed, or anything duly done or suffered under any Act so repealed; or

(c) affect any right privilege obligation or liability acquired accrued or incurred under any Act so repealed; or

(d) affect any penalty forfeiture or punishment incurred in respect of any offence committed against any Act so repealed; or

(e) affect any investigation legal proceeding or remedy in respect of any such right privilege obligation liability penalty forfeiture or punishment as aforesaid;

and any such investigation legal proceeding or remedy may be instituted continued or enforced, and any such penalty forfeiture or punishment may be imposed, as if the repealing Act had not been passed.

21. Section 8 applies only when an enactment has been repealed. Mr Vincent urges that the extended definition of "repeal" in s 8A of the AI Act is sufficiently wide to encompass the change in the law which occurs when a SoP comes into effect. I think he is correct in this. Under s 8A the "repeal" of an enactment includes the "limitation of the effect" of the enactment. Applying that to the legislation here, I accept that s 120A(3) of the Act limits the effect of s 120(3) so as to constitute a "repeal" under this section once a SoP has been determined.

22. Mr Vincent further urges that as the repeal in this case was disadvantageous to the applicant, she has a right to have the AAT determine her claim according to the law as it existed when the claim was first made.

23. I have the gravest of reservations as to whether the entitlement of an applicant to have the reasonableness of a hypothesis determined according to the test propounded under s 120(3), as opposed to the test propounded under a combination of s 120(3) and s 120A(3), constitutes a right under s 8. A "right" under s 8 means a substantive right, not a procedural one (Esber at p 440). The application of the SoP system does not, on its face, affect a claimant's substantive rights. Rather it regulates the manner by which those rights are to be ascertained. The SoP system removes from administrative decision-makers the task of determining medical issues as to the aetiology of injuries and diseases, and confers it upon members of the RMA, who are taken to be experts in this field. The primary object of the amendments was to create consistency in decision-making on matters of medical and scientific expertise, not to deprive claimants of their remedies. (See Second Reading Speech, Hansard 9 June 1994, pp 1808-9).

24. All this supports the proposition that the introduction of the SoP system brought about procedural changes only, so that no substantive rights were affected. On the other hand, there are undoubtedly some claimants who, under the pre-SoP system could have established entitlement to a pension by relying on the evidence of a single eminent practitioner, and who now cannot do so because the relevant SoP fails to uphold the hypothesis they seek to rely upon. It is difficult to say, in relation to these claimants, that the right they have lost is a procedural one only.

25. I am nevertheless inclined to the view that the introduction of the SoP system effected procedural changes only, and thus did not affect pre-existing substantive rights. However it is unnecessary to reach a concluded position on this issue. For there are other reasons why the applicant's submissions cannot succeed. In the first place, the majority judgment in Lee would suggest that any "right" of an applicant before the AAT accrues, for the purposes of s 8, only at the time when the primary decision is made. Both Cooper and Moore JJ said, following the majority of the High Court in Esber, that the right of an applicant before the AAT is a right to have a claim determined in his or her favour if the primary decision-maker has wrongly refused the claim. It is thus a right to have AAT review undertaken by reference to the law which was in force at that time.

26. At the time of the primary decision in the present case, a SoP had already been determined in relation to ischaemic heart disease. Therefore the law, as relevant to the issues here, was the same as it is now. The relevant "repeal" of the law, for the purposes of s 8, had already occurred.

27. The third and final consideration which compels me to find in favour of the respondent on this issue is one which was subject to considerable attention during the hearing. It is that, even if (in spite of the above considerations), the applicant had an accrued right under s 8 which could avail her in these proceedings, the 1994 amendments to the Act evinced a clear intention to exclude the operation of the section. This argument was put by Mr Hanks, who appeared for the respondent, and in my view it is an argument which must prevail.

28. Much of the argument on this issue revolved around the construction of s 120A(2). Pursuant to that section, the RMA's notice under s 196G, stating that it intends to investigate a particular injury, disease or death, will lead to compulsory deferral by the Commission of the determination of any claim relating to that injury, disease or death. Mr Hanks urges that this provision shows a very clear legislative intention that any SoP, once it comes into effect, will be binding on an administrative decision-maker. For it must follow that, if the investigation leads to the making of a SoP, the Commission will be bound to apply it. Were it to be otherwise, there would be no point in deferring the decision-making process in the first place. Mr Vincent, however, says that this argument begs the question of the applicability of s 8. For, he says, s 8 will operate to protect applicants against the effect of s 120A(2) as well as protecting them against the adverse effect of any subsequent SoP. This means, he says, that if no notice under s 196G has been given when a claim is first lodged, then a subsequent notice will, by virtue of s 8, have no effect upon the determination of that claim.

29. There are several reasons why this argument cannot succeed. First, s 120A(2) is not, on its face, susceptible of this restricted interpretation. This section creates a clear embargo upon the determination of any claim respecting the injury or disease mentioned in the notice. No distinction is drawn between pending or new claims. In my view, the section, in its ordinary meaning, must apply to all outstanding claims, regardless of when they were made.

30. Moreover, s 120A(2) is, by its nature, purely procedural in operation. It says nothing as to how any claim will be determined, but merely operates to defer the consideration of certain claims. As such it is not capable of affecting substantive rights, and s 8 of the AI Act can have no part to play in relation to this provision.

31. The real question is whether, consideration of the claim having been deferred under s 120A(2), and a SoP in relation to that injury or disease having subsequently been determined, the decision-maker is then required to apply that SoP, even if it operates disadvantageously to the claimant.

32. Mr Vincent urges a negative answer to this question. But this poses yet another problem. How will it be known to a decision-maker that a SoP operates adversely to a particular claimant? It will immediately be clear if a SoP fails to uphold a hypothesis advanced by a claimant. But it does not follow that the SoP is depriving the claimant of a pre-existing "right". It might well be that there would be no reasonable hypothesis connecting the injury or disease with the veteran's war service, even without the imposition of a SoP. The only way of ascertaining this would be for the decision-maker, having determined that the relevant SoP did not uphold a particular hypothesis, to then embark upon an inquiry as to whether a reasonable hypothesis was raised under s 120(3) according to the pre-SoP principles.

33. This is precisely what the 1994 amendments were seeking to avoid. It would undermine the entire object of the amendments if this exercise were required to be undertaken in all cases where a claim was made before a relevant SoP came into existence, and the SoP did not uphold the claim. If there were any doubt about this aspect of the legislative intention, it is laid to rest by the following extract from the Second Reading Speech:

The bill will, in effect, define by reference to such statements of principles the concept of `reasonable hypothesis', as it appears in subsection 120(3) of the Veterans' Entitlements Act. The result will be that a medical hypothesis linking particular kinds of injury, disease or death with war service that does not have a sound medical-scientific base will no longer be sufficient to constitute a `reasonable hypothesis'. This will be a matter solely for the expert medical authority to determine. I stress that the opinion of a single medical expert may still be sufficient to constitute a `reasonable hypothesis', provided that such opinion has a sound medical-scientific base, as determined by the authority. (Hansard 9 June 1994, p 1808)

34. Section 120A(1) provides that the 1994 amendments apply to "any of the following claims made on or after 1 June 1994:", and then refers to "a claim under Part II that relates to the operational service rendered by a veteran". It would perhaps have made it clearer if the section had referred to "all of the following claims". However in my view the intention of the legislature was that the amendment should apply to all such claims. This is made clear by a reading of the totality of the provisions and is reinforced by an examination of the Explanatory Memorandum and the Second Reading Speech.

35. It follows that the applicant's claim falls to be considered within the SoP system. There is, as I have already indicated, at least one further outstanding question in this case, namely whether a hypothesis linking an intermediate cause of death (as opposed to a immediate or direct cause of death) with war service will only be reasonable if it fits within the terms of any relevant SoP. That question is for another day. It is in any event, as I understand it, currently under consideration in the Federal Court.

36. Accordingly the only order I can make is that the matter should proceed on the basis that the SoP (or one of them) relating to ischaemic heart disease is to be applied.
