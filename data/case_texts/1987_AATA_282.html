ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Allowable Deductions - Retiring allowances paid by private company to Directors as former employees - Whether payments reasonable - Level of deduction permitted to paying company - Relevance of Commissioner's guidelines on "Early Retirement Benefits" - Relevance of superannuation entitlements -Deduction Allowed.

Income Tax - Assessable Income - Retiring allowance received by Director/shareholder and former employee from private company controlled with another - To what extent payment taxable to employee - Whether payment excessive and assessable as a deemed dividend.

REASONS FOR DECISION

29 July 1987 Mr Peter M Roach (Senior Member)

1. In June 1983 a company (Firstco) paid $40,000 to each of two persons who until 30 June 1982 had been employees (Arthur and Phillip). The payees were, and were to continue to be, Directors of Firstco and were the persons with control of its affairs. The share-capital in Firstco was held by Arthur and Phillip (whose interests were equal) and by another company which Arthur and Phillip controlled and of which they may have been beneficial owners. The payments were made to give effect to a resolution of Directors of Firstco recorded in its Minute Book as a resolution passed at a meeting of Directors held on Thursday, 1st July 1982. The resolution so recorded read

"Gratuities: A gratuitous payment of $40,000 be made to each of Mr (Arthur) and Mr (Phillip) following their retirement from the company's employment on 30 June 1982 and that such payment be in lieu of any long service leave payment otherwise due to them under the Long Service Leave Act and any other claim against the company arising from their service as employees of the company".

2. In December 1983, Firstco presented its return of income to 30 June 1983 to the Commissioner under a new name which it had adopted and under which it had been registered from February 1983. By its Profit and Loss account it showed total assessable income of $202,110 of which $190,774 was derived by way of fees for its services. It claimed deductions for $80,000 "retiring allowances" and other deductions totalling $99,403 and returned a net profit of $22,706 (taxable income $23,287). 3. By letter of 10 July 1984 the Commissioner made enquiries of Firstco as to the retiring allowances so paid and the company replied on 10 August 1984.. On 8 February 1985 the Commissioner issued an original assessment against the company increasing taxable income from the figure returned ($23,287) to $98,087 by adding what was described in the adjustment sheet accompanying the notice of assessment as follows:

"Deduction: claim for retiring allowances paid of $80,000 allowed to the extent of $5,200 the balance of $74,800 deemed a dividend in terms of section 109 of the Income Tax Assessment Act."

Tax was assessed at $45,120.02. Firstco objected to the assessment and, upon the objection being disallowed, requested reference for review.

4. In December 1983 - Phillip - the younger of the two men - presented his income tax return. In doing so, he disclosed the receipt of $40,000 and of a "bonus" of $5,000, both derived from Firstco. In disclosing the receipt of the lump sum Phillip stated:

"On 1 July 1982 the taxpayer received a gratuitous payment from (Firstco by its new name) in consequence of his retirement from employment by that company on 30 June 1982. The payment was made in recognition of his service to the company in an executive capacity for nine years, from the company's formation in 1974 until it ceased trading on 30 June 1982".

5. Phillip acknowledged $2,000 of the $40,000 to be "5% assessable lump sum payments". By that return of income, Phillip acknowledged his only other personal exertion earnings to be by way of salary and allowances from another company (Newco) which has not yet been mentioned, but which was identified in the return of income as having the same name as that originally held by Firstco. The Commissioner, on 18 January 1984, assessed the return as lodged, but on 6 June 1985, issued an amended assessment which increased the returned taxable income of Phillip in two respects. Only one is material upon this reference, the other adjustment having been reversed after objection made. The material adjustment was to increase the taxable income of Phillip by $38,000 described in the adjustment sheet which accompanied the notice of amended assessment as follows:-

"Amount of $40,000 received on your retirement from (Firstco - described by the new name) has been deemed a dividend in terms of section 109 of the Income Tax Assessment Act;

$40,000

Less amount previously returned 2,000

$38,000."

6. As a result the Commissioner contends that the taxable income of Phillip for the year of income ended 30 June 1983 stands at $75,945. The assessment so made was objected to by Phillip; the objection was disallowed; and upon its disallowance Phillip requested that the disallowance be referred on for review.

7. There is no reference before the Tribunal as to the assessment of Arthur. However, the evidence did disclose that he too claimed to be assessable on no more than $2,000 of the $40,000 "retiring allowance" which he received and that that claim was accepted by the Commissioner.

8. Both applications were forwarded for review. By consent they involve a joint hearing of applications by two quite distinct, although nonetheless inter-related, entities: Firstco, (the company); and Phillip (the individual). Phillip contends that by reason of s26(d) of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act") only 32.000 of the $40,000 received constitutes assessable income and Firsoco contends that the entirety of the $80,000 paid to Arthur and Phillip was allowable as a deduction by force of section 78(1)(c) of Act. An alternative submission by Firstco based on section 51 of the Act was abandoned. In addition Phillip alternatively contends that, having regard to the disclosures which were made prior to the issue of the original assessment against him, the Commissioner acted beyond power in issuing an amended assessment. Against that the Commissioner contends that even if the amounts of $40,000 so paid were paid by way of "retiring allowances" or as gratuities or other remuneration so as to satisfy the requirements of section 51 of the Act or fall for consideration under section 78(1)(c), the payments were excessive and to that extent section 109 of the Act applies. If so, it is contended that the consequence is that, to the appropriate extent, the payments constitute a "dividend" not deductible by the company and, as such, constitute assessable income in the hands of Phillip. To understand the issues fully requires that consideration be given to the history in business of Arthur and Phillip.

  1. Arthur was aged 60 years at 30 June 1982. He was a University educated engineer who had been employed in an advisory role in architectural practice. Phillip was aged 53 years at the same date. He was by training a tradesman electrician and was at all material times a licensed builder. In 1968 Arthur and Phillip came together in partnership and commenced business providing specialist consulting services within the construction industry. The basis of their partnership arrangement was one of equality between them save that Arthur, as the more highly qualified, would be entitled to a higher salary from the business than Phillip. At all material times from the inception of the business their wives were engaged in performing secretarial and clerical duties within the practice.
  2. In 1974 Arthur and Phillip caused Firstco to be incorporated. Firstco thereupon took over conduct of the practice and upon doing so it assumed an obligation to Arthur and Phillip to pay them $100,000 for "goodwill". In the years following 1974, Firstco conducted the business with Arthur and Phillip as its principal operatives. The fortunes of the business fluctuated, influenced by the rise and fall of activity in the industry and, perhaps, by other competitive factors such as play a part in determining what measure of success (if any) will be had in any business venture. Some of those difficulties can be seen in the circumstance that, for a period of about 18 months embracing the entirety of the year of income ended 30 June 1977, there was not enough work to enable Firstco to continue employing both men. Phillip, as the holder of a builder's license, went off to try to earn a living as a builder. In that period he received no salary from the company.
  3. However, things were not always so difficult. Indeed, in the course of his evidence Arthur indicated that his earnings from the business for the year of income ended 30 June 1986 were of the order of $160,000. However, in the late 1970's and early 1980's such prospects were remote. Even so, the evidence before the Tribunal gives some indication of the development of the company in terms of gross fees as hereafter recorded. In that regard it is to be noted, for it is of considerable significance, that the company returned its income on a "cash basis" so that it did not record as income of any particular year accounts rendered before the close of the year but unpaid by that date, or the value of work-in-progress at the close of that year.
  4. Apart from the eighteen month absence of Phillip from service of the company, both men continued in the employment of Firstco and drew salaries in accordance with decisions made from time to time. Understandably salary levels were set having regard to the capacity of Firstco to pay. Arthur and Phillip were not in a situation in which they could decide what levels of income they wished to have, and then determine what work they would undertake and what fees they would charge to enable them to satisfy those ambitions. Similarly, contributions to a superannuation fund established for the two men and their wives (as employees) in 1975 were not made on a regular basis.

15. The nature of the business was such that revenues likely to be derived from major commissions and retainers could be foreseen for up to two years in advance. So it was, that by early 1982 the partners had reason to be optimistic about business for the coming eighteen months. They also had hopes of retirement by 1988, when Arthur would be aged 66 years and Phillip 59 years. To achieve that they wanted to introduce two younger men to the business, but the problem they saw was that neither of the proposed purchasers was in a position to pay any significant purchase price. Recognising that and, realising that if they did not do something, the business would simply run down when they personally ceased to work in the practice, they consulted their accountant. I accept that he was a person upon whom they relied completely for financial advice. Having heard the evidence of Arthur and Phillip, I am quite satisfied that neither of them had any particular gift for the intricacies of company law or taxation law or, in the case of Arthur at least, insurance law. When consulted, the accountant considered that the business had substantial value: a value of such magnitude that there was no prospect that the proposed purchasers could afford to simply purchase a half-interest in Firstco. It was left to the accountant, as the trusted guide of the partners in financial matters, to devise a feasible and convenient means of introducing the purchasers to the business while ensuring to Arthur and Phillip a fair, but attainable, value for the interest they were to dispose of.

16. The advice was that it would be best to have a new company (Newco) to be controlled by the partners and their purchasers jointly as Directors and that Newco should take up conduct of the business from 1 July 1982. As a means of introducing the purchasers to the business they should be allotted redeemable preference shares in Newco with a view to taking up a half-interest after a probationary period. The concept was that the "business" would be preserved as an ongoing entity so far as those with whom it dealt were concerned, but that to effect the introduction of the purchasers there should be a clear point of demarcation at 30 June 1982 - with all profit earning activities of the business being conducted thereafter by Newco.

17. What happened thereafter is less clear. The evidence of the witnesses, such as it was, was vague and in some respects inconsistent. In particular one person, who was present throughout the hearing and who might have given helpful evidence by reason of his involvement in relevant matters, was not called. Further, documentation such as wages records; bank statements; notices to creditors and clients, were not produced in evidence. Clearly there were difficulties in implementing the commercial objective of effecting the transfer of the business from Firstco to Newco while preserving the image of continuity by preservation of the name by which Firstco had originally been known. The evidence discloses that Newco was incorporated in February 1982 as a "shelf" company. It was a subsisting entity at all material times thereafter, but for some months existed only under the name attributed to it upon incorporation. That could not change so long as. Firstco was registered by its original name; and that name-change was not effected until February 1983. Thereafter the way was clear for the shelf company to adopt the original name of Firstco as its own. It did so.

18. As to the transfer of the business, all that was necessary was to transfer across at depreciated value the few physical assets of Firstco; and for Firstco, Arthur and Phillip to suffer Newco to take the benefit of all new business and, possibly, partially completed business which otherwise would have been carried out by Firstco. Evidence from Arthur was that Firstco took to itself the value of all accounts rendered and unpaid and all work in progress at 30 June 1982 as those monies came to hand, thus generating receipts for the year of income ended 30 June 1983 of the order of $190,000. On the other hand, the accountant gave evidence that Firstco kept the benefit of all fees derived under all commissions in hand at 30 June 1982. If so, it would seem that Firstco arranged that any services necessary to be rendered by Arthur or Phillip or others in order to complete those retainers should be rendered by employees of Newco. I find that such services (if any) were not rendered by either Arthur or Phillip as employees of Firstco. That could account for $25,000 for Flrstco from management and consulting fees in the year of income ended 30 June 1983. In this regard, what is more significant is that the goodwill attaching to the reputation of Firstco and to the reputations of Arthur and Phillip was effectively transferred to Newco but, insofar as the retained value of that asset exceeded the value of work in progress and, possibly, commissions in hand, it was "transferred" without compensation to Firstco.

19. It was conceded by the accountant that all of the foregoing could have been achieved - and in my view achieved much more simply - without the need to introduce any new entity to carry on the business. The concession was correctly, if reluctantly, made. Nonetheless the evidence of the accountant was that the proposal to start afresh with a new company from 1 July 1982 was entirely his idea, and that it was devised by him as the most appropriate means of introducing the purchasers to the enterprise. I accept that. His further evidence was that, after the idea was formed by him, it was proposed to and accepted by Arthur and Phillip before any consideration was given to any question of payment of any retiring allowance by Firstco to either Arthur or Phillip. His further evidence was that the possibility of paying retiring allowances was first brought to his attention by a colleague in his firm; that the proposal for the payment of retiring allowances was recognised as being in all respects proper for all concerned and advantageous to Firstco and to Arthur and Phillip; that with the benefit of draft accounts and projections to 30 June 1982, it was decided prior to 30 June 1982 that the retiring allowance would be paid and that it would in each case be $40,000; and that at a meeting of Directors on 1 July 1982 the proposal was formalised by the passage of a resolution of the Directors as recorded in minutes bearing that date. I accept that evidence. I also find that on and from 1 July 1982 Arthur and Phillip were employed by Newco, which carried on the business with the results noted in para 14.

The Company's Claim

20. The company claims an income tax deduction in the sum of $80,000, in lieu of the $5,200 allowed. It claims that it has satisfied the requirements of s78(1)(c) which provides:

"78. (1) The following shall, subject to section 77B, Slab-section 77D(11) and section 79C, be allowable deductions:

(c) Sums which are not otherwise allowable deductions and are paid by the taxpayer during the year of income as pensions, gratuities or retiring allowances to persons who are or have

beer employees or dependants of employees, to the extent to which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, those sums are paid in good faith in consideration of the past services of the employees in any business operations which were carried on by the taxpayer for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income.

It is not claimed that there is any entitlement to a deduction pursuant to s51(1).

21. The Commissioner disputes the claim so made and says that not only are the requirements of s78(1)(c) not satisfied, but also that no more than $5,200 of the amount paid is allowable as a deduction by reason of s109 which provides:

"109. So much of a sum paid or credited by a private company to a person who is or has been a shareholder or director of the company or a relative of a shareholder or director, being, or purporting to be -

(a) remuneration for services rendered by that person; or

(b) an allowance, gratuity or compensation in consequence of the retirement of that person from an office or employment held by him in that company, or upon the termination of any such office or employment, as exceeds an amount which, in the opinion of the Commissioner, is reasonable, shall not be an allowable deduction and shall, for the purposes of this Act other than the purposes of Division 11A of Part III and Division 4 of Part VI, be deemed to be a dividend paid by the company on the last day of the year of income of the company in which the sum is paid or credited."

24. The differences in approach between the parties can be explained by reference to the different "guidelines" published from time to time by the Commissioner. In December 1976 the Commissioner issued guidelines, inter alia, for the purpose of determining whether the benefits provided for employees pursuant to section 23F superannuation funds were excessive. Then on 22 March 1983, the Commissioner issued Tax Rulings IT 294 and IT 2026. In each case the ruling was said to have effect from 1 July 1982. (How what was allowable or reasonable in July 1982 could be altered in March 1983 was not explained.) The guidelines expressed in IT 294 were said to be:

"for the purpose of determining the income tax deductions allowable for contributions by employers to superannuation funds established and maintained to provide superannuation benefits for employees and/or their dependants".

IT 2026 was described as

"a ready reckoner which has been prepared for use in the calculation of contributions to s23F superannuation funds which may be allowed as income tax deductions"

and goes on to deal with the subject of early termination benefits stating:-

"7. Where a member of a superannuation fund withdraws from the fund as a result of the termination of his employment prior to normal retirement age, it is necessary to ensure that the benefits payable from the fund to the member on the early termination of his or her employment are not excessive."

25. I do not propose to refer in detail to any of those guidelines or the calculations said to have been made as a result of them. They undoubtedly serve a useful purpose in as much as they proclaim to the community norms by which the Commissioner has instructed his staff in relation to the subject matter of the rulings, but the rulings are statements as to administrative practice and do not necessarily accurately provide for the application of the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act. I also observe that when the text of such rulings is largely given over to the presentation of statistical tables (14 pages out of 26 in IT 294; and 13 out of 16 in IT 2026), the caveats and reservations expressed early in the rulings tend to be forgotten. The preamble to IT 294 states clearly:-

"It will be appreciated that the guidelines contained in this statement are in the nature of general rules appropriate to the general run of cases and are in no way intended to supplant the express provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act. Cases may arise in which, because of particular circumstances, it would not be appropriate to apply the guidelines. Such cases will have to be determined in the light of their own particular circumstances."

On the subject of early termination benefits, IT 2026 states:

"It will be readily appreciated that it is not wholly practicable to make a general ruling in this area which will apply to all funds".

26. That is not to suggest that the guidelines and rulings have no relevance. As will be seen later (Comber v FCT 85 ATC 4450) is an authority to the contrary. But for the company the question to be determined is whether in the first instance the provisions of s78(1)(c) are satisfied and, if they are, whether deductibility is to be denied by the operation of s.109. As, despite differences between s78(1)(c) and s109, both call for determinations as to the reasonableness of sums paid by way of retiring allowance, I first address that question of reasonableness. I commence by taking into account the Rulings of the Commissioner in so far as they accept that it would have been reasonable for the Company to have provided total benefits by way of superannuation only for Arthur in the range $61,298 and $87,096 and for Phillip $48,930 to $52,228 depending upon which guidelines are applied. Those sums are considered reasonable by the Commissioner on the assumption that one only has regard to the age of the employee; the salary actually paid; service with the employer to the date of early retirement; and years to elapse between early retirement date and age 65. It also assumes that the salary actually paid was the full salary which might have been paid in an arm's length situation and gives no recognition to the possibility that one of the persons deriving an equal salary may be more deserving of additional benefits than others. If the period to elapse between retirement and age 65 is excluded from consideration as being unrelated to the service rendered by the employee, and the value of those services to the employer, the figures rise substantially. Similarly, of the "bonus" paid in the following year as a reward for service in the last year, of services, the reasonable amount on the Commissioner's calculations would rise by about 20%.

27. Insofar as the rulings are based upon "superannuation" benefits, they are only considering "entitlements" arising, possibly upon the exercise of a discretion, pursuant to trust deeds which cast strict obligations upon the trustees, including upon those who direct the decisions of corporate trustees. (Fouche v Superannuation Fund Board [1952] HCA 1; (1952) 88 CLR 609). Such entitlements stand in sharp contrast to retiring allowances gratuitously paid by an employer. I find nothing in s78(1)(c) or s.109 suggesting that it is unreasonable to pay any sum to the most deserving employee on the occasion of his retirement which would exceed the maximum amount payable under an approved s.23F superannuation scheme to the least deserving employee upon his retirement. I also observe that in rewarding employees for their service, it is appropriate that regard should be had to their actual service and that it is not appropriate that the reward should be "discounted" because the employee chooses to retire early. The reward should be measured by his service to the employer. The idea that early retirement calls for a "discounting" of superannuation benefits otherwise available would seem to reflect social and industrial concepts rather than fiscal. It is also reasonable that an employer, in determining to gratuitously pay a "retiring allowance", should have regard to the extent, if any, to which the employee has been underpaid either by comparison with employees rewarded according to the standards of an industrial award, or for working additional hours without claiming remuneration.

  1. In this instance I am well satisfied that both men worked well beyond the standard hours likely to have been prescribed by any industrial award; that the salaries (including bonus) actually paid to them were less than would have been payable for similar services rendered by a person claiming all entitlements pursuant to any industrial award; and that the quality and intensity of service rendered to the company by Arthur and Phillip was superior to what might have been expected of any "arm's length employee". I accept that the value of "fringe benefits" (eg use of motor vehicles) might have been taken into account but nothing in the evidence suggests any foundation for that. Furthermore, I observe that if "salary" in the Rulings was to be increased to take account of such considerations, the retiring allowance "permitted" by the Rulings would be increased rather than reduced.
  2. Another matter which, in my view, is of particular significance is that, if the company had remunerated both men at their "market rate" entitlements throughout their service, on the application of the Commissioner's rulings it seems likely there would have been no challenge. The totality of superannuation benefits on retirement and retiring allowances as paid probably would have been less than the Commissioner's calculations would have allowed. However, of even greater significance, is the circumstance that, having regard to the financial performance of the company during those nine years, if the company had paid its directors those "market rates" in the early years it would have failed financially, probably long before 1982. The exceptional effort of the shareholder employees and their willingness to accept less than market rates created the ultimate profitability of the company, but only as a result of the efforts of several years.
  3. As I have indicated, goodwill - valued in the 1983 Balance Sheet at $100,000 - was effectively transferred to Newco. No consideration was received for the transfer. In the accounts to 30 June 1984 that goodwill was written off, thus leaving exposed on the accounts as presented, a deficiency in shareholders' funds amounting to $33,260. It was proposed for the Commissioner that if the $80,000 "retiring allowances" had not been paid, there would have been no such deficiency of funds. I agree. It was then suggested that, therefore, the payment of the "retiring allowances" was unreasonable and was not made in "good faith" as required by s78(1)(c). I am not persuaded that that is so. The Balance Sheet at 30 June 1982 had failed to bring to account the then value of the goodwill of a prospering business which would generate income for years to come. I am satisfied that insofar as "goodwill" would have represented the market value of (inter alia) those expectations, it substantially exceeded $100,000. In the view I have formed, the cause of the deficiency in shareholders' funds was not the payment of retiring allowances, but the transfer to Newco for no consideration of so much of that goodwill as was transferred to Newco on 1 July 1982: a goodwill value diminished substantially by the reservation to Firstco of some future revenues from the business - the $190,773 received in the year of income ended 30 June 1983. In my view the deficiency in shareholders' funds on the accounts as presented to 30 June 1984, and which was not disclosed at 30 June 1983 only by reason of preserving as a Balance Sheet asset "goodwill $100,000" when it had become valueless, does not establish that the retiring allowances were unreasonable, or not paid in "good faith", or were not allowable pursuant to s.78(1)(c).
  4. As to s78(1)(c) I am satisfied that the sums paid were paid by the company during the year of income; were paid as retiring allowances; were paid to persons who had been employees of the company; were paid in consideration of the past services of those employees in business operations carried on by the company for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income; and were paid in good faith and in consideration of those past services. However, as the company was a "private company" as defined by Division 7 of the Act, the company will not be entitled to the deduction otherwise allowable under s78(1)(c) unless it satisfies the requirements of s109.
  5. Like every other section in the Act s109 needs to be construed in its context, particularly as part of Division 7. The Act provides that companies are liable to tax on their taxable incomes; and shareholders are liable to tax on dividends derived from companies. Since nothing in company law obliges companies to pay dividends except as directed by their members, it follows that there is a capacity in companies, particularly those with closely held shareholdings, to adopt policies in relation to dividend distribution and other matters to meet the convenience of company members, at least in the short term, and possibly to the long term detriment of the revenue.
  6. In Stocks and Holdings (Constructions) Pty Ltd v FCT 73 ATC 4053 Stephen J (with whose judgment Barwick CJ and Menzies and Mason JJ agreed), in referring to s103 to 107A of the Division, said (at p 4056):

"Together these sections make up a complete legislative scheme for the imposition of additional tax upon private companies making an insufficient distribution. They operate to discourage, by that means, the retention of those profits by those companies carried on from profit which, of their nature would otherwise be likely extensively to retain profits. Because companies are, as to the bulk of their income, taxed at a flat rate of tax which is lower than the upper ranges of the graduated rates of tax payable by individual taxpayers those companies which are closely held corporations would, but for Division 7, provide a ready means for the accumulation of profits which have borne a relatively light tax burden. It is for those closely held companies that Division 7 directs its attention and, by means of the threat of additional tax, seeks to deter the undue accumulation of profits which, if it nevertheless occurs, attracts that tax."

The scheme so laid out in s103 to 107A is filled out and supported by s108 and 109 which complete the Division. Section 103 has as its subject attempts to effect "distributions of income" in the form of loans to members or payments to the use of members. Section 109 has as its subject payments to members by way of "remuneration for services" or "retiring allowances" to the extent to which the amounts so paid exceed a reasonable amount. In the form in which those two sections were cast prior to 1952, s108 provided that any amount so paid:

"shall., for all purposes of this Act, be deemed to be dividends paid by the company to those shareholders out of profits derived by it"

and s.109, because it operated in relation to payments which on the face of it would entitle the paying company to deductions, operated both by providing that the excess "shall not be an allowable deduction" (thereby preventing pro tanto a reduction in taxable income) and also by providing that:

"the excess shall, for all purposes of this Act, be deemed to be a dividend paid out of profits derived by it to the recipient and received by him as a shareholder of the company".

In 1952 the entire Division was repealed by Act No. 90 of 1952 and re-enacted. In the course of doing so the Parliament altered the terms of s108 and 109. Initially commentators such as Challoner (Income Tax Law and Practice (Commonwealth)) and Gunn (Commonwealth Income Tax Law and Practice 4th ed.) did not see the recast sections as having any less scope than previously. The sections were still seen as being effective to implement a policy whereby "such distributions of income" to members and related persons in the guise of loans or payments to their use (s108) or as overpayments of remuneration for services or by way of retiring allowance (s109) would be treated as dividends in the hands of the recipient in addition to being, in the latter caso disallowed as deduction.

34. Later when the sections came under close scrutiny by the courts, it was held that they were in terms which were not necessarily apt to wholly give effect to any such intent. In R W Rutherford & Anor v FCT 76 ATC 4303 Sheppard J. held that s.108 (as recast) did not have the effect of deeming the imputed dividend to have been paid "out of profits" - an attribute of a dividend called for by s44(1) - and that although "paid out of the earnings of the companies ... it does not follow' that they were paid out of profits. The moneys were paid during an income year and not out of any fund which could be regarded as a profit or dividend fund" (p 4310). More recently, in Comber v FCT 85 ATC 4450 David Hunt J followed the same approach in construing s109 (as recast) and held it was not effective to deem the excess of a retiring allowance to be deemed to be "a dividend paid out cf profits derived by (the company) to the recipient and received by him as a shareholder of the company" as had been provided for by s109 prior to 1952.

  1. Although those decisions established that the policy of the Division was less effectively enacted than it might have been, in my view the decisions do not alter the policy objective of the Parliament whereby s109 was intended to prevent a company from unreasonably reducing its taxable income by making excessive payments to members and related persons in the guise of remuneration for services or retiring allowances.
  2. In addition it is necessary to consider s109 in this relationship to both s78(1)(c) (which authorises deductions in relation to retiring allowances) and S23F (which relates to superannuation benefits). In the former case that is because without s78(1)(c) in many cases no entitlement to a deduction would arise at all. In the latter case that is because the Commissioner relies on it to contend that in a case such as the present the amounts paid by way of retiring allowance to former employees were excessive. In relation to s78(1)(c) it suffices to say that for reasons already given, the requirements necessary to satisfy the section have been satisfied. But it does not automatically follow that, because the requirements of s.78(1)(c) are satisfied, there is no scope the operation of s.109 (cf. Henry Comber Pty Ltd v FCT 85 ATC 4450).

37. In relation to S23F David Hunt J. in that same case held that (at p 4460):

"The matters to which s2 is directed are, however, clearly relevant to the opinion to be formed under s109. The inclusion of (iv) - 'any other matters that the Commissioner considers relevant' - permits the flexibility which s109 requires, provided of course that the amount identified in that scale (which is necessarily based only upon the matters enumerated in s23F(2)(h) as (i), (ii) and (iii) is increased or decreased according to the additional matters so considered to be relevant."

Although the instructions reflected in the preambles to the Commissioner's Guidelines point to the need for flexibility, the tables to the guidelines are themselves inflexible. His Honour in that passage was not giving a blanket endorsement to any proposition that employees entitled to receive the maximum superannuation benefits permitted by guidelines issued by the Commissioner could not be the recipients of retiring allowances which would not infringe s109. After all s23F only operates to determine whether or not certain employee superannuation funds will be exempt from income tax (s23F(15)). Prerequisites to that privileged status are (inter alia) that the funds are for employees and their dependants (sub-section (2)(a)); that benefits are fully secured (sub-section (b)) and defined (sub-section (e)); and that the benefits that any employee has the right to receive pursuant to the scheme are not excessive (sub-section (h)). If all of the criteria of the section are satisfied the fund is exempt from income tax to the advantage of all of its members, be they few in number or many.

  1. Arising from the matters referred to in the previous paragraph, it is appropriate to say that s.109 does not provide that any payment of a retiring allowance to an employee is excessive if the same person receives superannuation benefits up to the limit provided for in mathematical tables incorporated in the Commissioner's guidelines from time to time. Further, s.109 does not provide that, if a person who receives maximum superannuation benefits (not being excessive benefits within the meaning of s23F(2)(h)), any amount paid to him "pursuant to s78(1)(c) of the Act will be unreasonable for the purposes of ..........
  2. What s109 does is to rank as unreasonable any amount paid by way of a retiring allowance if in the opinion of the Commissioner it exceeds a reasonable retiring allowance. The consequence then is that, to the extent of the excess, it is not allowable to the employer-company as a deduction, and is likened to a distribution of accumulated earnings.
  3. In the circumstances of this case I do not find it unreasonable that Arthur and Phillip should have been rewarded with retiring allowances which would give to them on termination of their employment a larger sum than would be "permitted" by the Commissioner's rulings, however viewed. Further, I am satisfied that, in as much as $40,000 was paid to each man, it was not unreasonable. Nor did the payment of $40,000 to Phillip come to be unreasonable by reason only that Arthur was paid no greater sum.

The Employee's Claim

41. This issue related only to the determination of the taxable income of Phillip. I have already found that on the occasion of his retirement from the employment of Firstco he received $40,000 by way of a "retiring allowance" paid by his employer in good faith in consideration of past services, I am also satisfied that only 5% of the amount so received constituted assessable income (s.26(d) (Reseck v FCT 75 ATC 4213). Accordingly, the claim of Phillip is to be allowed.

Jurisdiction: the $2 Question

42. What has most troubled me in relation to these matters is the question whether the Tribunal has jurisdiction to make any determination upon the objection of the company. The Tribunal is the creature of statute and has only the powers and authority conferred by statute. (Adams v Tax Agents Board (1976) 7 ATR 87) The reference could properly be before this Tribunal only by force of s.223 of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act (No. 48 of 1986). That Act, as its title suggests, provided for this Tribunal to assume and become responsible for the exercise of jurisdiction previously the responsibility of Taxation Boards of Review. However, the particular reference will only properly be before this Tribunal if it was properly before a Board of Review immediately prior to that transfer of jurisdiction. The reference will have properly been before a Board of Review if it was the subject of a request for reference in accordance with the provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act as it stood at the time when the request for reference was made. At all material times the relevant provisions of that Act (omitting immaterial parts) provided as follows:-

"185. A taxpayer dissatisfied with any assessment under this Act, may, within SO days after service of the notice of assessment, post to or lodge with the Commissioner an objection in writing ...

186. The Commissioner shall consider the objection, and may either disallow it, or allow it either wholly or in part, and shall serve the taxpayer by post or otherwise with written notice of his decision.

187(1). A taxpayer dissatisfied with the decision may, within sixty days after such service, in writing request the Commissioner either -

  1. to refer the decision to a Board of Review for review; or

b)

189. If within 60 days after receiving the request accompanied by the fee of $2 the Commissioner does not refer the decision or forward the objection, the taxpayer may at any time thereafter give him notice in writing to do so, and the Commissioner shall within 60 days after receiving the notice refer the decision or forward the objection to a Board or Court accordingly."

43. Under those provisions a question arises as to whether the objection of the company is duly before this Tribunal. The difficulty arises in this way. On 8 February 1975 the Commissioner gave notice to the company of his assessment of it for the year of income ended 30 June 1983. On 18 March 1985, within the time strictly allowed, the company delivered written notice of its objection to the Commissioner. The Commissioner, having considered the objection, and having decided to disallow it, served the company with written notice dated 6 June 1985 of that decision in accordance with the requirements of s186. Within the defined period of 60 days strictly allowed for the purpose, on 24 July 1985 the accountants to the company wrote on their own letterhead to the Commissioner with reference to the company (referring to it by name and citing the Commissioner's file reference) stating:

"We refer to your communication dated 6th June 1985 conveying your decision on the objection lodged by the Company against its income tax assessment based on income derived by it during the year ended 30th June 1983. The Company considers your decision on the objection to be unsatisfactory, and hereby requests that you refer it to a Board of Review for review."

  1. On or about 7 August 1985 the period of 60 days allowed for requesting such a review expired.
  2. Then on 6 September 1985 the Commissioner wrote to the agent for Firstco in relation to the company and said:

"Your letter of 24 July 1985 sought to have the decision on your 1983 objection referred to a Board of Review for review.

It is advised that whilst your letter has satisfied the requirements of section 187 of the Income Tax Assessment Act, there is no record of receipt of the $2 fee required in terms of section 188. It follows that in terms of the income tax law your request for reference cannot be complied with in the absence of the payment of the prescribed fee.

It has been decided, on this occasion, to treat your request as valid on the understanding that a $2 fee is remitted to this office by return mail. If the fee is not paid promptly, the request will be regarded as invalid. If on the other hand the fee has been paid, please forward a copy of our receipt evidencing the payment."

46. By letter of 11 September 1985 the agent responded to the Commissioner:

"We enclose herewith a cheque for $2 to cover the referral fee referred to in your letter dated 6th September 1985.

We thank you for your consideration in this matter, and apologise for any inconvenience." <

On this occasion the cheque was actually enclosed. It was received by the Commissioner and on 7 May 1986 the Commissioner referred the matter to a Taxation Board of Review for independent review. On 1 July 1986 such authority to and responsibility for the determination of the matter as there was passed to this Tribunal.

  1. At the hearing, there was no submission for the Commissioner that the Tribunal lacked jurisdiction and the question would not have come under notice had it not been for the fact that the Commissioner's letter of 6 September 1985 had been included with the documents referred to the Tribunal. However, as it has arisen, it must be addressed. Despite a suggestion in the Commissioner's letter of 6 September 1985 that a choice might be exercised as to whether or not to treat the request for reference as invalid, there is no choice for the Tribunal. If it has no jurisdiction, no action of the Commissioner or determination of the Tribunal can confer it.
  2. Conventional wisdom for fifty years was that a request for reference would be ineffective unless both the $2 fee and the request itself were delivered to the Commissioner within the 60 days allowed. If that view is correct then the Tribunal is without jurisdiction in this matter. It is odd that such a question should only arise for determination after the obligation to pay the fee had been abolished (s.80 Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986, repealing and substituting s.187 of the Tax Act)) and the fee had been refunded (ibid s.229).
  3. By the same legislation, provision was made to empower (inter alia) this Tribunal to relieve Applicants from the consequences of such trifling omissions, should it be necessary, by authorising the granting of extensions of time in such cases (S188B of the Tax Act), but subject to a restriction which would deny that relief to the Applicant company because s221 of the Transfer of Jurisdiction Act had the effect of providing that the harsh restrictions of the past should continue in full force and effect against those for whom the period within which objection or request for reference for review might be made had ended before 1 July 1986.
  4. Before the calendar year was out, there was yet a further change with the passage of amendments to the Taxation Administration Act (effected by Taxation Laws Amendment Act (No 4) 1986, No 154 of 1986), proclaimed to commence 1 March 1987. Those amendments provided:

"14ZAC(D Where a person who is dissatisfied with an objection decision lodges with, or sends to, the Commissioner (whether or not within 60 days after service on the person of notice of the decision) a request to refer the decision to the Tribunal or a court under a relevant enactment, the request shall be accompanied by the required fee.

14ZAC(2) If the request is not accompanied by the required fee, then, notwithstanding any provision to the contrary contained in the relevant enactment, the request shall be taken not to have been lodged with, or sent to, the Commissioner."

  1. It is clear that, if that legislation had been in force when the request for reference in question was made, the omission to tender the fee would have rendered the request for reference totally ineffective, but it is also clear that so slight an omission could have been rectified by extending time pursuant to S.188B of the Tax Act once the omission had come under notice.
  2. If conventional thinking about the matter has been correct over fifty years, then the result for the company is that the omission to pay the $2 within time will be fatal to its claim and will be incapable of being rectified by this Tribunal or any Court. It would, of course, still be open for the Commissioner to act under s170(4) to issue an amended assessment in accordance with these reasons and it would be open to the Treasurer to effect payment of the tax on behalf of the company to avert a gross injustice.
  3. In considering whether conventional thinking has been correct, I note that s187(1) of the Act did not require payment of a fee at all. The references to the fee appeared only in s188 and s.189. Section 188 obliged the Commissioner to forward the objection for review pursuant to a request "if the request is accompanied by a fee of $2". Section 189 cast on the Commissioner an obligation to so refer within 60 days of a further notice in writing requesting him to do so, "if within 60 days after receiving the request (for reference) accompanied by the fee of $2" the Commissioner had not done so. In my view, two matters then become clear. First, the Commissioner has no duty to refer a request for reference unless the fee has been paid; secondly, that the fee must "accompany" the request.
  4. It is nowhere expressly stated that if the fee must be paid to the Commissioner within the period allowed for making the request for reference. At most, such an obligation is to be inferred from the notion that the fee must be paid at such a time, and perhaps place, in relation to the lodgment of the request for reference as to be aptly said as to "accompany" the request for reference. I also note that Counsel for the Commissioner could not direct attention to any decision of the Courts which would deny jurisdiction only because of "late payment" of the fee.
  5. Unquestionably the rules proclaimed by the Parliament in force prior to 1 July 1986 (and still in force in relation to some matters) were harsh. They were out of keeping with the norms accepted throughout the community in relation to all other fields of contention between individuals; and between the individual and the community interest. However, I do not think it appropriate that one should seek to adopt a harsher than necessary construction on a doubtful point touching the operation of already harsh provisions. I am of opinion that the failure to pay the $2 within the period allowed for requesting the reference and, a fortiori, the failure to present to the Commissioner a cheque for $2 (or cash) at the moment of presenting the request for reference, should not be allowed to deny the company the benefit of adjudication upon its claim.

56. I therefore conclude that the Tribunal does have jurisdiction in the matter, at the same time recognising that if that view is incorrectly formed, this order can be set aside by a Court of competent jurisdiction as an order made in excess of jurisdiction (cf. my Reasons for Decision in NT36/2299).

57. Accordingly, the order of the Tribunal will be that the taxable income of the company for the year of income ended 30 June 1983 be reduced by $74,800; and the taxable income of the individual Applicant for the year of income ended 30 June 1983 be reduced by $38,000.
