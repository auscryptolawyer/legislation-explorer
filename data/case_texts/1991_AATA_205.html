Re Taxation Appeals [1991] AATA 205; 22 ATR 3437 (6 September 1991)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: TAXATION APPEALS

Nos. NT88/698-701

AAT No. 7352

Income Tax

[1991] AATA 205; 22 ATR 3437

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION

B.J. McMahon(1) (Deputy President)

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Assessable income - partnership carrying on business of investing in real estate and property - discretionary trust with family beneficiaries - assignment of share in partnership to trust - whether assignment of income from partnership not carrying on activity of professional services is valid for tax purposes - Income Tax Assessment Act ss 102B, 260

Conveyancing Act 1919 (NSW) s 12

Income Tax Assessment Act ss 102B(1), (2)(b), 260

Act No 110 of 1964 102B

Booth v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1987] HCA 61; 164 CLR 159

Davis v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 86 ALR 195

Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Purcell [1921] HCA 59; 29 CLR 464

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Everett [1980] HCA 6; 143 CLR 440 78 ATC 4595

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Galland [1986] HCA 83; 162 CLR 408 and 84 ACT 4890

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Gulland [1985] HCA 83; 160 CLR 55

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Patcorp Investments Limited [1976] HCA 67; 140 CLR 247

Newton v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1958] UKPCHCA 1; 98 CLR 1

United Builders Pty. Limited v Mutual Acceptance Ltd. [1980] HCA 43; 144 CLR 673

HEARING

SYDNEY

6:9:1991

ORDER

The Tribunal sets aside the objection decisions under review and remits the matters to the respondent with the direction that, in the years ended 30 June 1985 and 30 June 1986, that part of the income of the trusts referable to the assigned interests in the partnerships should not be treated as income of the applicants.

DECISION

The applicants are partners in a partnership established by deed on 6 March 1979 to carry on a business of investing in real estate and other property. The deed is of a general nature and contains no clauses one would not expect to find in a normal partnership arrangement.

2. On 30 June 1980 a deed established a discretionary trust in favour of the A and B class beneficiaries therein named. The A class beneficiaries consisted principally of the members of the family of the first applicant, the B class beneficiaries those of the second applicant. In a similar deed entered into on the same day, the entitlements of A and B class beneficiaries were reversed.

3. Two deeds of conveyance and assignment were executed in the Australian Capital Territory on 30 June 1980 by attorneys for each of the applicants and for the trustee of the discretionary trusts. In each deed, each of the applicants assigned nine tenths of his share in the partnership to the trust. The operative parts of the deeds are as follows -

"NOW THIS DEED WITNESSETH that in consideration of the

premises the Vendor as beneficial owner DOTH HEREBY CONVEY

AND ASSIGN to the Purchaser nine-tenths (9/10ths) of the

Vendor's share in the partnership as aforeasid together

with all those rights including the right to receive an

appropriate share of the profits of the partner to which

an Assignee of a share in a partnership is entitled by

virtue of Section 31 of The Partnertship Act 1892 as from

the date of these presents to hold the same unto the

Purchaser absolutely PROVIDED HOWEVER that nothing herein

contained shall entitle the Purchaser to any share of the

prospective profits accumulated in the books of the

partnership and held subject onoy (sic) to distribution

thereof (as reflected in the partnership books) until such

time as the share therein of the Vendor at the date hereof

has been distributed to the Vendor AND IT IS HEREBY

DECLARED that the Purchaser shall not by reason of these

presents or otherwise become a member of or a partner in

the said partnership or be entitled to interfere in its

business or affairs or to require any account of the

partnership transactions or to inspect the partnership

books but he shall only be entitled (subject as aforesaid

as to prospective accumulated profits) to receive the

portion of the share of the profits and other moneys or

property referable hereto as and from the date of these

presents to which the Vendor would otherwise have been

entitled had this assignment not be made."

4. It will be seen that the terms of the deed of conveyance and assignment are identical with those of the deed considered in the Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Everett [1980] HCA 6; 143 CLR 440. The terms of the deed in that case are reproduced in the judgment of Deane J in the Federal Court at 78 ATC 4601.

5. The partnership assets comprised residential premises owned jointly by the 4 partners, shares in non-listed companies and moneys deposited in banks. The partnership income was generated from the rental of properties owned jointly by the 4 partners and the rents received from non-listed companies and interest on moneys deposited in banks. The present application deals with income derived during the years ended 30 June 1985 and 30 June 1986, some 5 and 6 years after the date of the deeds of assignment. In each case, the adjustment sheet issued by the respondent increased the applicants' income from the partnership by the amount returned as its share by the discretionary trust, stating in the adjustment sheet that "an assignment of income from a partnership not carrying on the activity of professional services is not considered valid". In the statement of reasons provided when these applications were referred to this Tribunal, the respondent alleged that the assignment of the applicants' individual interest in the net income of the partnership was "ineffective for income tax purposes". These applications are brought to consider objection decisions of the respondent affirming these assessments.

6. No oral evidence was called by either the applicant or the respondent. Both parties agreed that the documents tendered in evidence were genuine and spoke for themselves. Both parties relied upon a construction of the documents and their legal effect, having regard to certain authorities to which reference will be made. It was submitted on behalf of each of the applicants that a nine-tenths proportion of their respective interests in the partnership was effectively assigned in the same way that the assignment was carried out in Everett and they relied on the reasoning of the High Court in that case to support their contention. The respondent argued that the provisions of s 102B of the Act applied to render the assignment invalid for income tax purposes. In the alternative, the respondent argued that the arrangements made had the purpose or effect of altering the incidence of income tax and of relieving the applicants from liability to pay income tax and accordingly were absolutely void as against the Commissioner pursuant to s260 of the Income Tax Assessment Act.

7. It seems clear from the decision of the High Court in Everett that a share in a partnership may be assigned. At pages 446 and 447 their Honours said -

"Although a partner has no title to specific property

owned by the partnership, he has a beneficial interest in

the partnership assets, indeed in each and every asset of

the partnership (Canny Gabriel Castle Jackson Advertising

Pty.. Ltd.. v Volume Sales (Finance) Pty.. Ltd.. (1974) 131

C.L.R. 321, at pp 327-328; Livingston v Commissioner of

Stamp Duties (Q.) [1960] HCA 94; (1960) 107 C.L.R. 411, at p 453. His

share in the partnership consists of a right to a

proportion of the surplus after the realization of the

assets and payment of the debts and liabilities of the

partnership (Bakewell v Deputy Federal Commissioner of

Taxation (S.A.) [1937] HCA 11; (1937) 58 C.L.R. 743, at p 770; Bolton v.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1965) A.L.R. 481, at

p 485; (1964) 9 A.I.T.R. 385, at p 389). Historically

the interest of a partner in a partnership has been

considered to be an equitable interest because it is a

right or interest enforceable in equity and not at law

(Bolton's Case)."

8. An assignment of the nature presently under consideration is therefore not an assignment of an interest in partnership assets, but in the chose in action constituted by the partnership itself. This distinction is emphasised in United Builders Pty.. Limited v. Mutual Acceptance Limited [1980] HCA 43; 144 CLR 673 at 688. The concept outlined in Everett has also been endorsed and followed in a number of High Court cases. An example is Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Galland [1986] HCA 83; 162 CLR 408. It must be assumed, therefore, that an assignment of an interest in a partnership can be validly effected in the manner chosen by the applicants in this case.

9. The respondent however seeks to draw a distinction between the assignment of the whole of an interest and of a fraction thereof and relies on a number of passages in Everett. At pages 447 and 448 the Court said -

"However, the weight of authority is against the view that

part of a debt or a chose in action can be assigned under

that section: In re Steel Wing Co. (1921) 1 Ch 349;

Williams v. Atlantic Assurance Co Ltd.. (1933) 1 KB 81,

at pp 100, 108; Walter and Sullivan Ltd.. v. J. Murphy and

Sons Ltd.. (1955) 2 QB 584; Norman v. Federal

Commissioner of Taxation [1963] HCA 21; (1963) 109 CLR 9, at p 29;

Shepherd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1965) 113

CLR 385, at pp 390, 396. Consequently, the assignment

in the present case had effect as an equitable assignment.

..........

Before the introduction of the Partnership Acts it was

well settled that "a person who agreed to buy a portion of

the interest of a partner in a partnership did not become

a partner, but that his vendor, while remaining a member

of the partnership exactly as before, become a trustee for

him of the interest agreed to be sold": Hocking's Case

(1909) 9 CLR, at pp 743-744; See also p 749, per

Griffith C.J. In that case the Court considered that the

counterpart of s 31 of the Partnership Act, 1892 (N.S.W.)

did not alter the law relating to the assignment of part

of a partner's interest in a partnership.

Does this trust relationship come to an end when the

contract of sale is completed and a formal equitable

assignment is executed? The question must, we think, be

answered in the negative, though the nature of the trust

changes once the vendor receives payment under the

contract. Our reason for saying that a trust continues is

that the assignment does not constitute the assignee a

partner or pass to him the powers of management,

administration and inspection of books and accounts which

repose in the assignor as a partner. What is more, legal

title to the assets of the partnership continues to vest

in the partners to the exclusion of the assignee and he

has no access to the assets. The extent of the assignee's

equitable interest is ascertainable only on dissolution.

These considerations lead us to the conclusion that the

assigning partner continues to stand in the relationship

of a trustee to the assignee, notwithstanding that the

assignee may be entitled to receive payments from

partnership profits direct from the partnership."

10. As I understand their Honours' reasoning, however, an equitable assignment of a fractional interest would, for present purposes, be effective to assign the income producing property, and not merely the income derived from the income producing property. At pages 448 and 449 Their Honours said -

"It is to be observed that s. 31 appears to be speaking of

an assignment of the entire share of a partner. This was

the view taken at least by Griffith C.J. and Barton J., if

not by all the members of the Court, in Hocking's Case

(1909) 9 CLR, at pp 743, 749. Although s 31(1)

applies only to an assignment of the entire interest of a

partner, there is no reason to doubt that the assignment

of portion of a partner's interest of itself likewise

entitles the assignee to receive the appropriate

proportion of the partner's interest in the income of the

partnership whatever that may be. The partnership

agreement may provide that a partner's share of

partnership profits is greater or less than his share in

the partnership itself. None the less a share in a

partnership carries with it the right to receive the

proportion of the partnership profits to which the partner

is entitled by virtue of the partnership agreement.

Consequently, when the share is assigned, it carries with

it the right to receive the assigning partner's proportion

of those profits. In the same fashion, when a portion of

a share is assigned, the portion carries with it the right

to receive the proportion of profits attributable to that

portion. As the right to receive profits is inherent in

the partner's interest in the partnership, unless it be

excluded by the partnership agreement, it is carried on

assignment of the share, even though no mention of it be

made in the assignment.

Consequently, we are unable to agree with the appellant's

submission, based on the dissenting judgment of Deane J.,

that the right to receive profits is separate from the

partner's interest in the partnership as such. We do not

doubt that a partner may enter into a contract or otherwise

bind himself to deal with his future profits from the

partnership so that others may acquire enforceable rights

to those profits as and when they are derived. Whether he

can sever his entitlement to receive future profits from

his interest in the partnership so as to confer an

immediate entitlement on an assignee with respect to those

profits as distinct from assigning future profits and

thereby binding those profits if and when they arise, is

another question. It was answered in the negative by

Woodhouse J. in Kelly v. Inland Revenue Commissioner

(N.Z.) (1969) 1 ATR 380, at p 383."

11. The fact that the court refers specifically in the last passage to the assignment of "portion of a share" makes it clear that both the portion and the rights attributable to that portion may be effectively assigned, whether that assignment takes place in law or in equity. For all material purposes I see no distinction between the facts in the present case and those in Everett. The court explicitly rejected the submission that what had been achieved was merely an equitable assignment of income.

12. The respondent pointed out that neither s 102B nor s 260 were referred to in the High Court judgment and that this may have indicated that neither section was argued before the court. The summary of arguments in the report indicates that Part III Division 5 of the Act relating to partnerships was the statutory provision to which most attention was addressed. It was further submitted (as it would have to be) that if the Everett transaction had been attacked under either of those sections, then the court's decision would have been different. I do not consider that I am entitled to assume this.

13. Section 102B and s 260 were in the Act when Everett was argued through the various courts to the High Court. It is inconceivable that their Honours would have been unaware of the existence of these sections. I am not entitled to assume that they were not argued for any particular reason. It is, for example, quite possible that they were not argued because counsel on both sides considered them irrelevant. I simply take note of the fact of their existence and of the reasons for the court's decision which I consider bind me. In short "the point" has been determined. Where there are no distinguishable facts (as distinct from distinguishable arguments) a High Court decision must be followed, particularly at the level of this Tribunal.

14. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Patcorp Investments Limited [1976] HCA 67; 140 CLR 247 at 311 Jacobs J. said -

"The question, then, is whether this decision is

distinguishable. It has primarily been submitted that the

applicability of s260 was not argued in the IMF Case

[1971] HCA 35; (1971) 125 CLR 249. But if the relevant facts are not

distinguishable I do not think that this is a valid reason

for not following the earlier decision it being one on the

applicability of a fiscal Act. What such a case actually

decides is that in certain circumstances there is a

certain liability, or freedom from liability, to

assessment of income tax, whatever be the expressed

reasons for that decision. If in another case the

circumstances cannot be relevantly distinguished then the

same result should follow if a necessary and most

desirable uniformity in the principles of assessment of

tax is to be achieved and preserved. The question is not

so much whether the applicability of s260 was argued but

whether it was overlooked and the decision given per

incuriam. That is not suggested. It is true that the

applicability of s260 involves a degree of factual

inquiry and inference and therefore to a considerable

extent its applicability is often a question of fact. But

if the facts in one case are not relevantly

distinguishable from those

in another the same conclusion should follow partly

because the ultimate conclusion is, or largely depends on,

a question of law as to the true construction and legal

effect of s260 and partly for the reason to which I have

earlier adverted, the need for uniformity in application

of a taxing Act. It seems to me that this need is

particularly strong in the case of a statutory provision

as notoriously difficult to intepret and apply as s260.

If the court has held that a particular course of

operations has a particular fiscal effect and if it has

thereby expressly or even impliedly found that it is not a

contract, agreement or arrangement falling within s260

it is for the legislature to make whatever special

provision it thinks is necessary to displace the effect of

the decision....."

15. Having concluded therefore, that there are no grounds for distinguishing the facts in this case from those in Everett, I conclude that the applicants must succeed. In case it should be found that I am wrong in this conclusion, however, I will deal with the other arguments put on behalf of the respondent.

16. His primary submission was that s 102B had application. At the relevant time, it was in the following terms -

"102B(1) Subject to this section, where, after 22

October 1964, a right to receive income from property is

transferred, otherwise than by a will or codicil, by a

person to another person for a period that will, or may

for any reason other than the death of any person or the

other person's becoming under a legal disability,

terminate before the prescribed date, any income derived

from the property that is paid to, or applied or

accumulated for the benefit of, the other person by reason

of the transfer, being income that, but for the transfer,

would have been included in the assessable income of the

transferor, shall be treated for the purposes of this Act

as if the transfer had not been made.

(2) This section does not apply in relation to a

transfer of a right to receive income from property where -

(a) the right was not a right that arose from the

ownership by the transferor of an interest in the

property; or

(b) the right arose from the ownership by the

transferor of an interest in the property and the

transferor has transferred that interest to the

transferee or another person.

(3) Where a person who has transferred a right to

receive income from property dies or, being a company

ceases to exist, this section does not apply in relation

to income derived from the property after the date of the

death or the date on which the company ceased to exist, as

the case may be."

17. Section 102B was inserted by Act No 110 of 1964, following recommendations made by the Ligertwood Committee. Its genesis was referred to by Mason C.J. in Booth v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1987] HCA 61; 164 CLR 159 at 164 -

"....As the general law did not restrict the capacity of

an owner to alienate his right to income from his

property, many taxpayers exercised this capacity by

alienating their right to income from property for short

terms. Section 102B(1) was directed to this situation.

It strikes at transfers, otherwise than by way of will or

codicil, of a right to receive income from property for a

period that will or may, for any reason other than death

or legal disability, terminate within seven years of the

date on which the income is first paid to, or applied or

accumulated for the benefit of the transferee. But the

sub-section did not apply where the right to receive

income "arose from the ownership by the transferor of an

interest in the property and the transferor has

transferred that interest": s102B(2)(b). In other

words, s102B(1) has no application when the transfer of

the right to receive income takes place as the result of

the transfer of the underlying proprietary interest which

generates the right to receive income."

18. I was referred by counsel for the respondent to the Second Reading Speech. Although I do not consider that the terms of the section are so obscure as to require the assistance of the Speech to interpret it, I will quote the following to indicate the mischief at which the amendment was directed -

"Alienation of income, not accompanied by a transfer of

the assets producing the income, is another matter dealt

with in the Bill. The Ligertwood Committee recommended

that income assigned in this way to a minor unmarried

relative of a taxpayer should bear the tax it would have

borne if the assignment had not been made. Most people,

among them employees on wages and persons in receipt of

professional fees are unable to transfer their income so

as to avoid or reduce the tax liability on it. The use of

assignments for tax purposes thus provides a sectional

advantage for a relatively small proportion of taxpayers.

In the light of present practices the Committee's proposal

would have a very limited effect. After the most careful

consideration, the Government now proposes that a person

who makes an assignment of income to another person, but

retains the assets that produce the income, shall be taxed

as though the assignment had not been made. The measures

proposed by the Bill in this respect will, however, apply

to assignments that are, or may be, for a period of less

than seven years and are made subsequently to today."

Thus the section was aimed at assignments of income where the assignor "retains the assets that produce the income".

19. The respondent argued that the entirety of a partner's interest in a partnership may be assigned at law under s212 of the Conveyancing Act 1919. It was agreed that the interest, being a chose in action, falls within the expression "deed or other legal chose in action". It was submitted, however, that part of a chose in action may not be so assigned and that consequently the assignment of a part only of a partnership share has effect only in equity. The effect of such an assignment (it was submitted) is that the assignor, while remaining a member of the partnership, becomes a trustee for the assignee of the interest assigned. It does not confer on the assignee any rights as against the partnership. Legal title to the assets of the partnership continues to vest in the partners to the exclusion of the assignee (Everett at page 448). Therefore, it was said, the assignor had not assigned "the underlying proprietary interest" in whole. He had retained legal title to part of that interest.

20. It seems to me however that such a submission is simply not consistent with the decision of the High Court in Everett. Whether a fractional interest in a partnership is assignable at equity or at law, seems to me to be beside the point. The fact is that a beneficial interest in income producing property has been held to be validly assigned in Everett in circumstances indistinguishable from the present circumstances. The fact that the assignor retains a a legal estate and holds it in trust is not effective to separate the incoming flowing from the asset itself. The assignor has assigned the beneficial interest in both the tree and the fruit to the assignee. The draftsman of s 102B has, significantly, chosen to use the neutral term "transfer", rather than the more technical word "convey". This is wide enough to encompass an equitable assignment.

21. The respondent placed reliance upon the fact that only a nine-tenth part of the assignor's interest in the partnership was assigned. Such an assignment, it was submitted, is not an assignment of "the property" referred to in s 102B(1) or "that interest" in sub-section (2)(b). It was, the respondent submitted, an assignment of something else and not the property from which the right to income arose.

22. This seems to me an altogether too narrow reading of the section. It must surely be intended that "property" is to be read distributively to mean "all or any part of the property". Any other interpretation could lead to absurd results. Thus, if the assignor derived income from a portfolio of shareholdings in various companies and assigned only one or two of those holdings, it would be said, if the respondent were correct, that the assignor had not effectively divested himself of the whole of his port folio and that therefore the income from dividends on the shares that had been transferred are to be treated as the assignor's income. Merely to state the proposition is to demonstrate the unlikely nature of this interpretation being correct. Applied to the present circumstances, it may be said that ten-tenths of the assignor's share generated the right to receive income and nine of those tenths were transferred to the assignee.

23. It seems to me, therefore, that having determined that property (being nine-tenths of the assignor's share in a partnership) has been effectively transferred, there is no room for the application of s 102B(1). For the same reason, the exception in sub-section (2)(b) would have effect. That being the case, there is no need to examine the submission that the partnership, being terminable on notice, the assignment could have been defeasible within the prescribed 7 year period. The observations of Hill J in Davis v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 86 ALR 195 at 225 have no application in the present circumstances.

24. The respondent then argued that s 260 applied. Although no oral evidence was given, it was submitted that the purpose of the agreement (being the establishment of the discretionary trust and the execution of the assignment) was to be ascertained from the circumstances. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Gulland [1985] HCA 83; 160 CLR 55 at 65, Gibbs C.J. reaffirmed the principle that the section does not refer to the motives of the taxpayer or other person who entered into the arrangement which it is sought to impugn. The purpose or effect of the arrangement, he said, must be ascertained from the terms of the arrangement itself and from the overt acts by which it was carried into effect.

25. The agreed circumstances in the present application are that income accrued to each applicant and he was taxable on it. A discretionary trust was established, the beneficiaries being the children and spouse, as well as the applicants, and an assignment to deflect income (and the tax) from the applicants to the beneficiaries under the trust was entered into. This, it was said, was sufficient to invoke the voiding provisions of the section. As Gibbs J. continued (at page 67) "...the avoidance of tax (by which I mean to include any of the purposes mentioned in s 260) need not be the sole purpose of the arrangement". It is now established that it is sufficient to attract the operation of the section that tax avoidance was a purpose, at least so long as it was not merely an "incidental" or "unessential purpose". (Davis at page 227). It was further submitted that the dealings could not be categorised as "ordinary business or family dealings" (Newton v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1958] UKPCHCA 1; 98 CLR 1 at page 8).

26. In my view, s 260 would have no application to the present facts for two reasons. Firstly, it is not concerned with transactions which merely shift the burden of taxation. In Gulland Gibbs C.J. said at pages 65 and 66 -

"Not every arrangement which results in a saving of tax

will be struck down by this section. For example, if one

person disposes of income-producing property to another so

as to reduce the burden of taxation, the section will not

avoid the transaction, for the incidence and burden of the

tax will fall precisely as the Act intends, on the new

owner".

I have found that what the applicants did was to dispose of income producing property. As his Honour says, s 260 will not avoid this transaction.

27. Secondly, it can not be said, in my view, that, as a general principle, the establishment of a discretionary trust for the benefit of a family and the assignment of assets to form part of the corpus of that trust, can not be said to be "an ordinary business or family dealing". In the early case of Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Purcell [1921] HCA 59; 29 CLR 464, such an arrangement was held to be just that. The reasoning in that case has been approved on many occasions. In Gulland, Gibbs C.J. refers to it with approval at page 66. At page 79, Brennan J. said -

"A disposition of income-yielding property could not have

been intended to be within the net of s 260 merely because

the disposition has the effect of relieving the disponor

of tax liability which would have been imposed on him had

he continued to receive the income yielded by the

property."

28. He then went on to quote with approval the reasoning of Gavan Duffy and Starke JJ in Purcell at page 473. More recently, Hill J in Davis at page 234 again affirmed the principle that "Where an arrangement consists of no more than an assignment of income, the arrangement could not fall within the provisions of s 260". It must follow a fortiori that an assignment of corpus together with relevant income could not fall within the provisions of the section.

29. The respondent submitted that the fact that the applicant could, through control of the appointment and removal of the trustee, secure the return of corpus to himself meant that there was no prima facie advancement of the beneficiaries and that consequently the statutory purpose of s 260 was again demonstrated. This argument was dealt with in Galland in the Federal Court reported at 84 ATC 4890 at page 4895 in the following terms -

"The Commissioner's final submission was that whilst the

assignment might be effective under the general law it was

ineffective for tax purposes. It was said that because of

the powers the taxpayer retained and the possibility that

he could himself become a beneficiary, the assignment was

ineffective for income tax purposes. Counsel for the

Commissioner relied upon the decision of Hardie Boys J. in

Arcus v. Commr. of IR (N.Z.) (1962) 13 ATD 101 and

sought to distinguish Purcell v. F.C. of T. (1920) 28

CLR 77, Tunley v. F.C. of T. [1927] HCA 27; (1927) 39 CLR 528 and

C. of T. v Hedges (1935) 3 ATD 255. He also

endeavoured to support this contention by arguing that in

truth the taxpayer was the settlor of the partnership

share upon his wife and children, he having expressly

assigned that share to Galland Services in its capacity as

a trustee.

There are a number of grounds which deny validity to these

submissions. In the first instance it is clear that, with

the exception of sec. 260, there is no provision of the

Act which enables the Commissioner to treat as void an

assignment which is valid under the general law. There

are of course sections of the Act which empower the

Commissioner to assess the assignee or beneficiary to tax

at the rate applicable to the donor or assignor (cf.

subsec. 102(2) and sec. 102B) but these provisions do not

advance the contentions of the Commissioner. Likewise he

gains no assistance from the reasoning of Hardie Boys J.

in Arcus's case, where his Honour had before him a

voluntary assignment of future property, a transaction

ineffective in this country by virtue of the decision in

Norman v. F.C. of T. [1963] HCA 21; (1963) 109 CLR 9. In our opinion

the Commissioner's attempt to distinguish the cases of

Purcell, Tunley and C. of T. v. Hedges cannot succeed."

30. For these reasons it seems to me that the applicants are entitled to succeed. The objection decisions are therefore set aside and the matters are remitted to the respondent with the direction that, in the relevant years, that part of the income of the trusts referable to the assigned interests in the partnerships should not be treated as income of the applicants.
