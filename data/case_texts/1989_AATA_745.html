REASONS FOR DECISION

19 December 1989 Mr Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. By an agreement in writing made the 2nd day of April 1984 the Applicant company (App-Co) purchased all of the assets of a business carried on by a manufacturing company (Vend-Co). The price was $3,700,000. In doing so it purchased the entirety of the plant and equipment of Vend-Co as used in that business at a price of $1,601,227. Plant and equipment for that purpose included leasehold improvements and motor vehicles. (cf. post). It also purchased for $1,650,000 all "Stock" (defined to include (inter alia) "goods in transit"; and with special provisions made as to payment in defined circumstances). By an earlier "Shareholder's Agreement" there would have been no obligation to proceed with the purchase of "Stock" at settlement had valued at less than $1,500,000 or more than $1,900,000. (The reason for reserving a right to withdraw if the value of the stock being purchased for $1,650,000 exceeded $1,900,000 was explained. In that situation it was contended that the advantages of getting the additional stock at an under-value would have been out-weighed by the long-term need of the business to carry the additional stock necessary to maintain the business). Thereafter App-Co carried on the business. In doing so it presented its returns of income for the years of income 30 June 19B4 and 1985 and claimed depreciation on all of the items of plant and equipment which it had so purchased. In making its claims to depreciation is attributed a cost price to each item and claimed a percentage rate of depreciation which it contended to be appropriate having regard to the nature of the item. There is no dispute between the parties as to the cost attributed to the several items or as to the rates of depreciation claimed in respect of those items. However, it is contended for the Commissioner that it is of some relevance that App-Co had acted unilaterally in apportioning the total purchase price it had paid to the items which it had purchased.

4. That written-down value relied on by the Commissioner had flowed through into the accounts of Vend-Co from an earlier company (Pre-Vend-Co) from which Vend-Co had taken title on 1 July 1983. During the hearing it became evident that the plant and equipment which had been itemised by App-Co following its purchase was substantially more comprehensive than the plant and equipment which had been depreciated by Vend-Co and, before that, by Pre-Vend-Co. That arose because some of the plant and equipment acquired by App-Co at substantial cost had been written off as a current year expense and its cost claimed and allowed as deductions to Pre-Vend-Co or Vend-Co (as the case may be) pursuant to Section 51(1) of the Act; and had not been claimed as depreciation. That those practices had been followed was made clear to Trade-Co by the report of accountants of 29 November 1984 which was incorporated in the Sale of Assets Agreement.

5. To determine the issues, it is appropriate to consider the context in which Section 60 of the Act appears. Taxpayers are liable to be taxed upon their taxable incomes (Section 166). "In calculating the taxable income of a taxpayer, the total assessable income derived by him during the year of income shall be taken as a basis, and from it there shall be deducted all allowable deductions". (Section 48). Allowable deductions extend ordinarily to all losses and outgoings incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, provided they are not losses or outgoings of capital or of a capital nature (Section 51(1)). Although expenses of capital, or of a capital nature, are not so allowable, provision is made that "plant or articles owned by a taxpayer and used by him during (the) year for the purpose of producing assessable income" is depreciable (Section 54(1)). To determine the amount of depreciation to be allowed requires that the Commissioner make an estimate of:

"the effective life of the unit assuming that it is maintained in reasonably good order and condition, and the annual depreciation per centum shall be fixed accordingly" (Section 55(1).

That estimate of life is commonly measured in years, but may be one year or less. The amount to be claimed as depreciation is then determined according to the diminishing value method or, at the option of the taxpayer, the prime cost method (Section 56(D). The rates may be as high as 100%. The method used may not be changed without the leave of the Commissioner (Section 57). Some property never depreciates in market value but most does. Commonly written-down values differ from market values - a difference realised upon sale at market value. So the Act makes provision (inter alia) that on disposal, any amount realised in excess of the written-down value of depreciated property is to be treated as assessable income to the extent to which depreciation has been allowed to the vendor (Section 59(2)). Ordinarily any additional amount will not constitute assessable income of the vendor. All of the foregoing is subject to a number of provisions which entitle the Commissioner to make his own determinations and substitute them for those of the parties, particularly in circumstances where the parties have not been dealing at arm's length.

6. Two consequences follow which need to be noted. The first is that it is common place that, upon the sale of any business as a going concern, the vendors will provide that plant and equipment is to be sold at depreciated values as per the books of account of the vendor. In that way, commonly no tax liability will arise by reason of recoupment by the vendors of depreciation previously allowed. In such circumstances the vendors can avoid the consequences which would flow from selling such plant and equipment at an under-value by "writing up" the value of other assets being sold, being assets of no fiscal significance. The second consequence is that, to quote the Commissioner's explanation in Income Tax Order 1217 (1956 revision);

"It is commonplace for businesses owned by individuals, partnerships or companies to be transferred to a company, or a new company formed to take over the businesses or going concern. In such cases it frequently happens that the assets are written up in value far above the value to which their cost had been reduced by depreciation-under the Act. The written up values generally represent in these cases the estimated cost of replacing the assets. The purchases, in such cases, if allowed a deduction for depreciation based on the written up values at which they nominally acquire the assets, would obtain an unfair advantage over those owners who continue to conduct their businesses without alteration of legal ownership. In no case is the deduction for depreciation calculated by reference to the possible cost of replacing any plant or machinery etc. A deduction is always calculated by reference to actual cost - i.e. actual money expended to acquire the asset. This necessitates excluding all added value represented by a writing up of the book values of any items of plant etc. and the greatest care should, therefore, the exercised to see that the depreciation allowance is not calculated upon a written up value".

7. In addition, even in transactions between parties dealing at arm's length, the way is open to purchasers to gain a fiscal advantage by bargaining with vendors (inter alia) to have the cost of depreciable plant and equipment increased substantially above written-down value, and even above market value, while at the same time bargaining for a reduction in the price to be attributed to non-depreciable capital assets such as goodwill. Such problems are addressed by Section 60 which at all material times - it has since been amended - read as follows (immaterial words omitted):

"(1) Where ... a person has acquired any property in respect of which depreciation has been allowed or is allowable ...., he shall not be entitled to any greater deduction for depreciation than that which would have been allowed to the person from whom the property was acquired if that person had retained it;

Provided that, where under Section 59 an amount is included in the assessable income of the person selling the property, the property shall be allowed depreciation calculated on the sum of that amount and the depreciated value of the property under this Act immediately prior

(1A)

(2) This Section shall not apply where the Commissioner is of the opinion that the circumstances are such that depreciation based on the actual consideration given should be allowed".

9. The fundamental contention for the Commissioner is that in the circumstances of this case, full effect should be given to the provisions of Section 60(1) of the Act by confining the "cost" to App-Co for depreciation purposes to the written-down value which had appeared for Vend-Co in the books of account of the latter company. On the other hand, for App-Co it is contended that:

(a) In so far as Section 60 could have any application, the Commissioner should have determined that the Section did not apply; and

(b) that, in so far as much of the plant and equipment sought to be depreciated by App-Co did not appear in the depreciation schedules of Vend-Co and had not been depreciated by Vend-Co, the consequence is that there was no scope for the application of Section 60(1) of the Act by the Commissioner.

10. To place the issues to be resolved in their full context I propose to commence with the early fiscal history of the equipment in question. The business commenced only in 1972. By June 1983 it was being conducted by a company which I have identified as Pre-Vend-Co. It was controlled by a father and son (Rex and George) who, according to the income tax returns of Pre-Vend-Co to 30 June 1984, held 70% and 30% of the issued capital respectively. In the course of carrying on its business, Pre-Vend-Co had acquired, by purchase or by construction, a vast range of plant and equipment. Some of that plant and equipment had been treated as depreciable, but some of it - including some substantial equipment acquired at considerable cost - had been "expensed". In the latter case that involved claims to deductibility under Section 51(1) of the Act for the full cost of construction or acquisition. I find that the same practices were continued later while the business was being conducted by Vend-Co. At all material times I am satisfied that Rex and George exercised control over the affairs of Pre-Vend-Co.

  1. With effect from 1st July 1983, Pre-Vend-Co transferred the entirety of the business (including all of the plant and equipment used in the business, whether depreciated or expensed) to Vend-Co. The entirety of that plant and equipment was transferred at the "written-down value" of so much of that plant and equipment as had been depreciated. I am satisfied that Vend-Co was also controlled by Rex and George. But, according to the income tax return of Vend-Co - a return only brought into existence long after all the material events had occurred - the entire share capital was held by a proprietary limited company. According to the evidence of George the reorganisation had been to effect a redistribution of beneficial ownership within the family, with a larger share coming to be held by George and his family. I accept that. At the same time I recognise that the rearrangement had the advantage for Pre-Vend-Co that there would be no S.59 recoupment of depreciation; and for Vend-Co that any future recoupment of depreciation would be limited.
  2. By mid-1983, the business had been undergoing a progressive, rapid and substantial expansion. In some areas of activity, competitors were withdrawing from the field. In other instances, the business was being subjected to litigious harassment from rivals. Its performance and growth were such as to attract the interest of some who were interested in acquisition of sound businesses. For its part, Vend-Co, because of the limited financial capacity available to it, was having difficulty funding its expansion. All in all it was a likely and promising "take-over" target, its success making it an attractive prospect to others and its rate of expansion being so great as to make the possibility of sale increasingly desirable to its shareholders. Some negotiations of no immediate relevance took place prior to mid-1983. But the presently relevant moves which resulted in the sale of the business commenced in July 1983.
  3. That first step was taken when a commercial facilitator ("Felix") made an approach to Vend-Co. Felix specialised in identifying closely controlled companies with a view to negotiating their sale. He had been previously successful in persuading Trade-Co - a substantial company in itself, and a subsidiary of a major public company - to extend the scope of its activities. Several acquisitions had taken place. However, some of his efforts had failed. But, for all that, Felix knew the range of his client's interests as expressed by the Managing Director of Trade-Co. Having failed in one attempt to introduce Trade-Co into the field of Vend-Co's activities, he made an approach by letter to Vend-Co. His approach was ignored. That was because, in the very difficult competitive environment then faced by Vend-Co, it was suspected by George that Felix, in making his approach, was embarking on some commercial espionage for rivals.
  4. But a personal approach by Felix was more successful. Even so the first priority of Rex and George was to be assured of the identity of Felix's principal. They were not satisfied on that account until they were provided with a letter on the letterhead of Trade-Co acknowledging its interest and recording its authority to Felix for him to negotiate on its behalf. That was followed by a substantial meeting attended by Rex and George and by Felix. Rex and George provided a written outline of their company's activities, being an update of a document which had been prepared earlier for the information of others. They provided a description of current undertakings and of the history of the company. In doing so they mentioned that in June 1983 Pre-Vend-Co had been sold to Vend-Co and that "the sale to (Vend-Co) was to enable (George) to increase his share of the business from one-third to 49%"; the other 51% being held by Rex. At that meeting George advised that nett assets would be in excess of $2,600,000 and nett profit before tax would be in excess of $800,000. Felix responded by indicating that the price at which Trade-Co would be looking would be $3,200,000.
  5. Felix was a person given to maintaining most detailed records of his negotiations. They were in the form of typewritten diary notes and commonly formed a basis for his correspondence. Having heard the man and read his notes I am satisfied that the notes provide a very reliable record of the negotiations in which he engaged. Whenever there is any conflict or disparity between the evidence of Felix and that of others, I accept the evidence of Felix as the more reliable.

16. By 2 August 1983 Rex and George had made it known that the value they attributed to the "plant and equipment" to be sold would be $1,601,227. That was a figure which father and son had compiled by appraising each and every item of "plant and equipment" (including leasehold improvements and motor vehicles) of value used in the business and attributing their own value to it. On that date, Felix was provided with a set of accounts of Vend-Co to 30 June 1983 (with comparative figures for Pre-Vend-Co to 30 June 1982) which showed (inter-alia) a gross manufacturing profit of $2,822,372; a nett profit before tax of $617,243; income tax payable $283,932; nett profit after tax $333,311; a written down value of "plant and equipment" (other than leasehold improvements and motor vehicles) of $198,522 and of leasehold improvements at $39,027 and motor vehicles at $8,651; and share capital and reserves of $1,087,306.

17. From the first meetings in July the negotiations moved forward. Initially they were under the control of Felix. Things did not always go smoothly but some matters emerged quite early as being "non-negotiable" so far as Rex and George were concerned. They were (inter alia) $1,601,227 for plant and equipment; $1,650,000 for stock; and $3,700,000 in total. As negotiator Felix collaborated with and took his instructions from the Managing Director of Trade-Co. I am satisfied that Felix was sufficiently experienced and astute to appreciate that the "plant and equipment" price was a matter of significance to Vend-Co, just as he was sufficiently experienced and astute to realize that the rapid growth of the company and the prospect of a large tax liability had generated substantial liquidity problems for it. I am also satisfied that he believed that Vend-Co did have tax problems pending and was sufficiently astute not to enquire what they were.

  1. I am satisfied that Felix received approval from Trade-Co to negotiate for the purchase of the assets of Vend-Co rather than the shareholding of Vend-Co; that the proposal that it should purchase assets rather than shares emanated from Rex and George; that the purchaser would be a company to be incorporated (it was to prove to be App-Co); that Vend-Co would be able to take up as it pleased a quarter-interest in the new company with minority representation on the Board; that App-Co would retain the services of Rex and George under contract, with an obligation in Trade-Co to buy out the minority interests in App-Co which Rex and George would control on the expiration of their periods of service; and that Trade-Co would obtain guarantees from Rex and George as to future profitability of the1 business. The latter requirement was appropriate and called for because of the short financial history of the company in its expanded condition. In due course Felix was instructed to obtain an independent valuation of the "plant and equipment" - an exercise which he considered to be significant, not only for itself, but as an indication of the competence of Rex and George in assessing the worth of what Vend-Co had to sell and of their honesty in putting forward their claim to the price proposed.
  2. By early October 1983 Rex and George had made available to Felix, and Felix had made available to Trade-Co, a detailed inventory under a number of categories and in a Schedule.   2. I find that, on the instructions of Trade-Co, Felix arranged for a valuation of the "plant and equipment" to be made by a wholly independent firm and that such a valuation was carried out in a completely independent way by the firm engaged for the purpose. I further find that the valuers who actually carried out the work of valuation were not even appraised of the circumstances giving rise to the commission they received. They valued all that they could, placing a "going concern value" of $1,313,354 and an "auction realisation" value of $694,465 on the "plant and equipment". Leasehold improvements were similarly valued by the same valuers at figures of $148,000 and $29,900.

22. By early December, Trade-Co was finalising a letter of intent. It proposed that a new company, App-Co, be incorporated with the shareholding to be held 75% by Trade-Co and 25% by Rex and George in equal beneficially-held shares. After providing for the transfer of stock (with special provision for "stock in transit") and fixed assets, it was provided:

"The value to be paid for goodwill is to be the difference between the value of stock (finished goods, working progress and raw materials) and the valuation of fixed assets and the sum of $3,700,00 and (Vend-Co's) accrued liability for a holiday pay and long service leave, which shall be met under the heading "General Warranties".

The letter of intent proposed that "our interest in acquiring (Vend-Co's) businesses (was) on the faith and basis of" accounts of Pre-Vend-Co and a "charitable trust" (sic); of management profit and loss accounts prepared by George; the income tax returns and assessments of Pre-Vend-Co for the years ended 30 June 1981 and 1982; and certain representations as to turnover and the value of orders in hand. The letter of intent also proposed that the agreements would be two-fold: a shareholder's agreement and a sale agreement.

  1. Before either of those agreements was executed the Managing Director of Trade-Co came to negotiate directly with Rex and George. He did not give evidence. But George did give evidence of a meeting which he and his father had attended with their solicitor at a time when the Managing Director was the only-representative of Trade-Co. Some insight into that meeting can be had from memoranda subsequently prepared by the Managing Director. But having heard the evidence of George in the matter, I am satisfied that whatever suspicions Trade-Co may have had as to the reasons as to why Rex and George were insisting upon the transaction being carried through as it was, neither Rex nor George disclosed anything more as to their reasoning, or their intent, or their affairs than is to be gleaned from a perusal of the accounts then available. I am satisfied that the principal objective of the Managing Director in those negotiations was to try to secure a reduction in the total price. I am equally well satisfied that his efforts were successfully resisted.
  2. Following the "Shareholders' Agreement" of 27 February 1984 the sale was finally settled contemporaneously with the execution of the "Sale Agreement" on 2 April 1984 with the entirety of the business assets passing to App-Co for $3,700,000; with Trade-Co taking up a 75% interest in the issued capital of App-Co amounting to $4,000,000; and with 12.5% of that issued capital passing to two proprietary companies acting as trustees for the respective family trusts of Rex and George. (There was some evidence, not developed, that the price was increased by $50,000 or so by reason of stock-in-transit). I find that Rex and George joined the board of App-Co but did not disclose any further information to App-Co as to any prior fiscally significant conduct of Vend-Co or Pre-Vend-Co. I am satisfied that, when the income tax return of Vend-Co to 30 June 1984 was prepared in November 1984, the information contained in it was not disclosed to App-Co or to Trade-Co. I am satisfied that in all respects throughout the negotiations and in all that followed the parties conducted themselves as parties completely at arm's length.

25. I also find that, following the purchase, Trade-Co continued to pursue its original course of ensuring that it enjoyed the goodwill and support of Rex and George throughout the transition period, but with a view to Trade-Co ultimately acquiring the entirety of the issued capital of App-Co." When Rex retired from the service of App-Co, App-Co exercised its entitlement to acquire the shares held by his family trust. As to George, Trade-Co acquired the interests held by his family trust while he continued in the service of the company as an employee: a service scheduled to come to an end shortly. With the latter purchase, the course embarked upon by Trade-Co during 1983 of achieving complete control of the business of Vend-Co was completed.

THE 'EXPENSED' ITEMS

26. Section 60 of the Act is not in its terms limited to plant and equipment as listed in the depreciation schedule of a taxpayer. It applies to "any property in respect of which depreciation has been allowed or is allowable ...". In relation to the items in this category, I accept that no depreciation had been claimed or allowed in respect of expensed items valued by Vend-Co at $627,072 ($510,260 plus $116,812) and so brought to account in the price of all "plant and equipment" purchased by App-Co. I accept that it is appropriate to apportion the purchase price for plant in accordance with the classification by and valuation by Vend-Co.

27. It may be that to some extent, perhaps wholly or substantially so, the items in question were properly expensed. But the evidence is insufficient for me to make any such finding. That possibility is one to be borne in mind in considering the construction of Section 60(2) of the Act. But, since I am not able to make such a finding, it follows that I am not persuaded that Section 60 has no application to the expensed items here in question acquired at a price of $627,072. Accordingly, no basis has been made out for distinguishing the treatment of the expensed items from that of other items of plant and equipment.

THE DEPRECIATED PLANT AND EQUIPMENT

28. I am satisfied that at the point of sale to App-Co, the plant and equipment (other than motor vehicles) had a value in the market place of the order of $1,461,354 ($1,313,354 and $148,000); and that a sale for $1,601,227 had been negotiated at arm's length as part of a larger transaction as to the sale of the entirety of the assets of the business previously carried on by Vend-Co. I am satisfied that the price so attributed to the plant and equipment was proposed by Rex and George as its market value as assessed by them and that it was no less the market value of that plant and equipment because the sale was of an entirety comprising all of the plant and equipment necessary for the manufacture of the particular products and, indeed, of all the components of plant and equipment necessary for the conduct of the business in its entirety. The market value of a set of the thirty-two pieces of a chess set is no less its value just because the value of the set exceeds the sum of the value of the thirty-two pieces considered each in isolation from the others. I am also satisfied that the price was not inflated, with the price of other components in the whole purchase being depressed to compensate. I accept that the nomination of the price was at the instance of Rex and George, and not Trade-Co; and that on both sides it was accepted that goodwill was a residual figure -an amount remaining after the identification and valuation of all other components of the sale: that it was a value over and above the sum of the values of the other assets of the business.

29. I find that the original cost of all of the relevant plant and equipment substantially exceeded its written down value in the books of account of Vend-Co; and that it had substantially exceeded the written down value of the plant and equipment acquired by Vend-Co from Pre-Vend-Co as it appeared in the books of account of Pre-Vend-Co. It is not possible to determine with accuracy what that original cost was, but some elements are known. It comprised the 'expensed' items which I accept by the material date had a value of the order of $627,072. It included the plant and equipment recently taken over by Vend-Co from a competitor retiring from the field at a price of $75,000. It included at least the "written down value" of the depreciable items acquired from Pre-Vend-Co plus the total value of depreciation allowed to Pre-Vend-Co in respect of those items from the time of their acquisition by Pre-Vend-Co. Bearing in mind that the history of the business began in 1972; the recent rapid expansion; and the circumstance that the sale was early in the 1984 year of income, it is not unreasonable to suppose that the original cost of the items now sought to be depreciated by App-Co may have been as high as the price paid for them by App-Co.

30. I am satisfied that at the point of sale to App-Co, the market value of the plant and equipment sold could have been greater than its original cost. However, for convenience of presentation, I shall assume that cost to have been equal to the sum of the amounts originally depreciable whether in the hands of Vend-Co, or Pre-Vend-Co, or any predecessor to Pre-Vend-Co. Again, for convenience I shall assume that the taxpayer first entitled to claim the depreciation was either Pre-Vend-Co or Vend-Co.

  1. That being so, it follows that there would have been no scope for the application of Section 60 of the Act if the entirety of the depreciation which had been allowed and the cost 'expensed' in relation to other items which ought to have been depreciated had been capable of being assessed to either Vend-Co or Pre-Vend-Co. But Section 59 of the Act did not make provision in that regard.
  2. It had no application in relation to the sale from Pre-Vend-Co to Vend-Co because, in conformity with the expectations of Section 60(1) of the Act, the depreciated property in the books of account of Pre-Vend-Co had been sold to Vend-Co at their written down values. Secondly, as to the sale between Vend-Co and App-Co, the operation of Section 59(2) against Vend-Co was limited to any amount recouped by Vend-Co which, having regard to the artificially reduced price at which it had acquired the plant and equipment from Pre-Vend-Co, was nominal. Thirdly, the operation of Section 59 was limited to depreciable items. As to the 'expensed' items it may be unlikely though it be - that all of the expensed items were correctly treated as Section 51(1) claims. Alternatively, it may be - although unlikely - that the correct characterisation was to have treated those expenses as capital expenditure on plant and equipment appropriate to be depreciated but, by reason of an estimate of life of one year or less, appropriate to be wholly depreciated in the year of acquisition. But such matters are matters to be resolved between the Commissioner on the one hand and Vend-Co or Pre-Vend-Co on the other.

33. In making my findings in relation to such matters, it is appropriate to say that:

(a) It may be that the expensed items were properly expensed, in which case there would be no basis for the application of Section 60(1) of the Act; and

(b) It may be that the amount assessed against Vend-Co by force of the operation of Section 59(2) of the Act was inadequate.

34. It is clear that the substantial reason why the maximum sum allowable to App-Co - assuming Section 60(1) applies - is as low as the Commissioner contends is the fact that Section 59 recoupment prospects for the Commissioner were quarantined by the sale by Pre-Vend-Co at written-down values – substantial under-values - less than twelve months before the sale by Vend-Co. Expressed more generally, what has caused what the Commissioner's officers perceived as a "loss to the Revenue" has been occasioned by a combination of :

* Provisions of the Act which encourage transfers at written down values, particularly between associated parties;

* The practices of Pre-Vend-Co and Vend-Co in "expensing" items of plant and equipment;

* The absence from the Act of provisions providing for recoupment on sale of "expensed items" of amounts previously allowed as deductions; and

* The absence from the Act of provisions providing for recoupment on sale measured against all depreciation previously allowed or allowable rather than only against depreciation allowed or allowable to the vendor.

None of those factors, nor a perception of "loss to the Revenue", provides a sound basis for giving to the relevant provisions a construction contended for by the Commissioner, not being the interpretation intended by the Parliament.

  1. In referring to the terms of the Act in that way I am not proposing that the provisions ought to be other than they are. That is a matter for others. The Tribunal is not a policy making body. Where it observes injustice being done by the terms of the substantive law, and a fortiori procedural law, it is proper that members should speak out. But beyond that it should be recognised that the precise definition of the scope and extent of the tax upon "income", and how it is to be assessed, is a matter for the Parliament.
  2. I am not persuaded that Trade-Co did not welcome the fact that such a high proportion of the cost of the purchase of the business would be attributable to matters of fiscal significance such as trading stock (including both "stock on hand" and "stock in transit") and to plant and equipment. Further, I am not persuaded that either Trade-Co or App-Co was under the impression that no fiscal significance was to attach to the relationship between on the one hand a price to be paid for plant and equipment and on the other to the matters of expensed items; to the earlier, low, written-down values; and to the effect of an earlier purchase by Vend-Co from Pre-Vend-Co. But I am satisfied that the full significance of such fiscal considerations was only known to Rex and George. As to that, however, I note that they came to be directors of App-Co. In making that observation I give recognition to two difficulties; the difficulty of precisely defining the duties of Directors appointed to a Board of Directors to represent minority interests, standing at 'arm's length' to Directors representing majority interests; and on the other hand of the commercial reality which has such Directors give prior allegiance to those whom they are appointed to represent.

37. I also have particular regard to the Commissioner's "Rulings" on this subject for, to the extent published, they constituted an information source as to the Commissioner's views on these matters. What was available to be known by the public at the relevant time was expressed in Income Tax Order 1217 (1956 Revision) previously quoted. As to that is to be noted that what the Commissioner was viewing with disfavour was the "writing up" of the value of plant and equipment to reflect a concept of replacement cost - a course which, had it been followed in this case, would almost certainly have remitted in a much higher "price" for plant and equipment. In that Ruling the Commissioner was insisting that future depreciation should always be "calculated by reference to actual cost - i.e. actual money expended to acquire the asset". Implicit in that was the notion that prices should not be inflated or artificial. By that standard prices determined by reference to market value, including reference to market value of plant and equipment being sold as part of a going concern, involves no element of "writing up" values to enlarge the prospects of future claims to deductions.

38. The second ruling which is relevant to the exercise of discretion is IT 2354, a Ruling of 14 August 1986, and as such, a Ruling not available to be known to App-Co or to the public at the time of App-Co's purchase. In the latter ruling, the rationale expressed in the earlier Income Tax Order was confirmed. By the Ruling the Commissioner went on to say;

"6. The sub-section reflects the fact that; in many ordinary commercial transactions, the market value of depreciable property to be sold exceeds the original cost to the vendor and that normal commercial or business practice requires that the purchaser account for depreciation on the cost of the depreciable property. Although the discretion in the sub-section must be exercised in each particular case, it is normally exercised in the ordinary arm's length sale of depreciable property, i.e. where there is a bona fide sale, where the purchase price represents the fair market value of the depreciable property and where the depreciable property is for use in the purchasers income producing activities and is no longer used in the income producing activities of the vendor".

39. If that were the only standard by which the discretion were to be exercised, in my view it should clearly be exercised in favour of App-Co. App-Co dealt at arm's length with the vendor; there was a bona fide sale; the purchase price did represent the fair market value of the property; the property was for use in the purchasers' income-producing activities; and the property was no longer to he used in the income-producing activities of the vendor. In my view the significance of those considerations is not outweighed by the circumstance that following the sale, interests identifiable with the families of persons who had held the beneficial shareholding in Vend-Co and controlled its activities were for a limited term to hold a minority interest in App-Co.

40. An exercise of discretion is called for. In making it I have regard to all of the foregoing considerations, but especially to:

(a) the arm's length nature of the transaction;

(b) the circumstance that the value of the plant and equipment was independently appraised and the value so obtained supported the bona fides of the dealing;

(c) the circumstance that App-Co in the short term was dominated by Trade-Co and in the long term would be wholly controlled by it;

(d) the fact that App-Co did not contrive the situation in order to gain any fiscal advantage;

(e) the finding that such "losses" as there may be to the Revenue were not occasioned by any conduct of App-Co; and

(f) the view I have formed that such "losses" have as their substantive cause the provisions of the Act and, possibly, the conduct of Vend-Co and Pre-Vend-Co.

41. In my opinion this was appropriate to be described as but one of:

"many ordinary commercial transactions, (in which) the market value of depreciable property to be sold exceeds the original cost to the vendor (and the vendor's predecessors) and that (in consequence) normal commercial or business practice requires that the purchaser account for depreciation on the cost of the depreciable property."

In my view the discretion conferred by Section 60(2) of the Act should be exercised in favour of the Applicant and the order of the Tribunal will be to that effect.

42. In view of that conclusion it is unnecessary to review the decisions of the Commissioner as to whether App-Co was liable to additional tax for incorrect retrun and, if so, whether penalties assessed at $12,220 and $13,598 should be further remitted.

43. The order of the Tribunal will be that the determinations of the Commissioner upon the objections under review shall be varied and the objections of the Applicant wholly allowed.
