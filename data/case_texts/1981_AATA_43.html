Re George Alfred John Hare and the Commissioner of Superannuation [1981] AATA 43 (5 June 1981)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: GEORGE ALFRED JOHN HARE

And: THE COMMISSIONER FOR

SUPERANNUATION

No. V80/5

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mr R.K. Todd (Senior Member)

Sir Ernest Coates (Member)

Dr M. Glick (Member)

CATCHWORDS

superannuation _ determination of retirement benefits _ medical examination _ whether failure to furnish full information _ whether furnishing of false information _ issue of benefit classification certificate _ nature and extent of duty to disclose information _ nature of relationship between a contributor and the fund

Superannuation Act 1922 s.5

Superannuation Act 1976 s.184(2),(3),(5)

HEARING

CANBERRA

5:6:1981

ORDER

The Tribunal decides to set aside the decision of the Commissioner for Superannuation:

(a) to issue pursuant to sub-section 184(5) of the Superannuation Act 1976 a benefit classification certificate in respect of the applicant specifying the condition 'nervous tension'.

(b) that the applicant's benefit entitlement on ceasing to be an eligible employee on 22 November 1977 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity fell to be determined under sub-section 66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976.

DECISION

1. In this application for review the Tribunal is required to give further consideration to the benefit entitlement of the applicant pursuant to the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act"). Such entitlement was previously considered by the Tribunal as then constituted in relation to the application for review numbered 78/12027, reported as (1979) 2 ALD 233 and 2 ALN 662 Note 95.

2. In its previous Decision the Tribunal decided that the incapacity that was the ground for the retirement of the applicant on 22 November 1977 was severe nervous tension. A benefit classification certificate was deemed to be in force in respect of the applicant on 1 July 1976 specifying the condition 'hypertension'. The Tribunal found itself unable in all the circumstances to conclude that the severe nervous tension that was the ground for retirement was caused by hypertension, or that it was substantially contributed to thereby. The nervous tension, the Tribunal said, 'had its own causes and was nourished by the inability of the applicant's personality to cope with a very difficult situation at work'. Accordingly, the Tribunal decided to set aside the decision of the Commissioner that the benefit entitlement of the applicant should be determined under s.66(2) of the 1976 Act.

3. At the previous hearing evidence had emerged which led the Commissioner's representative to contend that in the proved circumstances the provisions of s.184(3) of the 1976 Act, relating to the issue by the Commissioner of a benefit classification certificate where a person at or in connection with a medical examination required by s.5 of the Superannuation Act 1922 failed to furnish any information, or furnished false information, should be applied by the Tribunal. Since however this matter had never been the subject of a decision by the Commissioner, and had not been reconsidered as provided for by s.154 of the 1976 Act, the Tribunal declined to embark upon that question. The Tribunal having stated this, and having pronounced its findings upon the primary question before it, the Commissioner indicated that he consented to his decision being set aside, while indicating that he proposed to apply the provisions of s.184(5) of the 1976 Act, referring to s.184 as amended by s.77 of the Superannuation Acts Amendment Act 1978, which came into force on 24 April 1978. Section 184(5) provides as follows: '(5) Where _

(a) a person to whom this section applies (whether or not he was, immediately before the commencing day, a contributor to the Provident Account) ceases to be an eligible employee by reason of death or retirement on the ground of invalidity;

(b) his period of contributory service is less than 20 years and, on the day on which he ceases to be an eligible employee, he has not attained his maximum retiring age;

(c) the person was, immediately before the commencing day, a contributor to the Fund or, if the person was, immediately before the commencing day, a contributor to the Provident Account, the Commissioner is not of the opinion that the death of the person or the incapacity which was the ground for the retirement of the person was caused, or was substantially contributed to _

(i) by the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, by those conditions or one or more of those conditions; or

(ii) by a physical or mental condition or conditions of the person connected with the physical or mental condition of the person that was relevant for the purposes of the superseded Act or, if there was more than one such condition, with those conditions or one or more of those conditions; and

(d) the Commissioner is satisfied _

(i) that, at or in connexion with a medical examination which the person underwent for the purposes of the superseded Act, the person failed to furnish any information required to be furnished by him or furnished false information; and

(ii) that, if the person had not failed to furnish that information or had not furnished that false information and, in a case where the person would not have been a contributor to the Provident Account or a contributor to the Fund immediately before the commencing day but for the failure to furnish that information or the furnishing of that false innformation, the person had been a contributor to the Provident Account immediately before the commencing day, there would have been deemed to be in force in respect of the person, immediately before his death or retirement, a benefit classification certificate in which there would have been specified the physical or mental condition or conditions which caused, or substantially contributed to, the death or retirement, or a physical or mental condition or conditions connected with such a condition or conditions,

the Commissioner shall issue in respect of the person a benefit classification certificate in which there is or are specified the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, would have been the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person specified in the benefit classification certificate that the Commissioner would have issued, or, but for sub-section (4), would have issued, in respect of the person under sub-section (2) if the person had not failed to furnish that information or had not furnished that false information and, in a case where the person would not have been a contributor to the Provident Account or a contributor to the Fund immediately before the commencing day but for the failure to furnish that information or the furnishing of that false information, the person had been a contributor to the Provident Account immediately before the commencing day and, for the purposes of this Act, the certificate shall be deemed to have been issued under sub-section 16(11) and to have been in force in respect of the person immediately before his death or retirement.'

4. It was agreed by the representatives of the parties in the present application that the evidence before the Tribunal in the previous application should be before the Tribunal for the purposes of this review.

5. At the hearing of the present application, further oral evidence was given by the applicant and by Dr E.G. Bruce. From that evidence, from the oral evidence given at the previous hearing by the applicant, by Dr Bruce, and Dr M. Delaine, from the Tribunal documents in both applications, and from the exhibits formally tendered at the previous hearing, we make the following findings of fact that are relevant for present purposes. They should be read in the light of the additional narrative detail given in the Reasons for Decision dated 11 May 1979: (i) The applicant commenced temporary employment with the Commonwealth in 1970.

(ii) Soon after he commenced such employment an incident occurred in the course of his employment which gave rise to some nervous problems thereafter.

(iii) In about September 1972, the applicant was prescribed amytal by his then local doctor, Doctor Hamilton, who described the applicant as 'depressed'. In March 1973 Dr Hamilton described the applicant as depressed and prescribed valium. In April 1973 Dr Hura, a partner in the same practice, described him as 'still depressed' and prescribed concordin. In May 1974 Dr Hamilton prescribed concordin. The applicant said that he was told that the concordin was to quieten his nerves. The dose was described by Dr Bruce, who saw the applicant later, as 'pretty small by today's standards'. After that the visits were at fairly regular intervals and there were further prescriptions of concordin. The applicant appears to have been unimpressed with it as a treatment and said that he had had quite a lot of it stockpiled at home.

(iv) In February 1975 the applicant went for a medical examination in relation to his application for permanent appointment. He was examined by a Dr Bridges. In answering the questions set out in the Medical Report Form under the heading 'Personal' the applicant answered a number of questions including the following:

'Have you now or have you previously had any of the following conditions? Answer 'Yes' or 'No' in the space provided.

1(a) Nervous fatigue No

(b) Mental or nervous conditions No

(c) Anxiety state No

(d) Neurasthenia No.'

(v) The comments made by Dr Bridges on the Medical Report Form indicate that the applicant made full disclosure of a number of other treatments and operations, not presently relevant, that he had had.

(vi) Because of the applicant's blood pressure readings, Dr Bridges recommended that the applicant be rejected, but he also considered the possibility of deferment for six months. This recommendation came under the view of Dr Delaine on 12 March 1975, who noted that the application should be deferred for 11 months 'view Provident'.

(vii) On 1 August 1975 the applicant was examined by Dr Delaine. In the intervening period the applicant's hypertension had improved, the treatment having initially been aldomet and chlotride. From Dr Delaine's evidence it is clear that Dr Delaine was in August 1975 made aware that the applicant was at that time taking aldomet and navidrex for his hypertension, and that he was also taking concordin, which was described to us by Dr Bruce as a mild anti-depressant.

(viii)Dr Bruce was reluctant to use any such terminology as 'anxiety state' or 'nervous condition'. In the Tribunal's previous Reasons for Decision something of the same difficulty was experienced, and we spoke there of the applicant's 'nervous troubles'. The most accurate way, if accurate is a word that may in the context be used at all, to describe the applicant's condition between 1970 and 1975 is to describe him as having been depressed.

(ix) On the evidence however it would have been open to a doctor seen by the applicant in the period 1970 to 1975 to conclude that he had a 'nervous condition', but that term is not one that every doctor finds meaningful.

(x) When examined by Dr Delaine, with a view to retirement, on 14 September 1977, Dr Delaine's report contained inter alia the following statement:

'Since early 1975 under constant medical care for hypertension, nervous tension and persistent headaches....' (underlining supplied)

(xi) The applicant was never told by any of his own doctors that he had or had had nervous fatigue, a mental or nervous condition, anxiety state, or neurasthenia, nor for that matter does it appear that a diagnosis of any such conditions was ever made by any of his doctors. He went to the doctors principally for headaches.

(xii) When the applicant was prescribed concordin, he thought that it was for headaches. He did not know until 1978 that concordin was an anti-depressant. He did not in fact take concordin very often.

(xiv) Dr Delaine in August 1975 considered the concordin 'as a part of the treatment for the associated depression and (neurasthenia) with hypertension of considerable duration": see transcript of evidence at the previous proceedings p.65. It also appears from p.69 of that transcript that he regarded the concordin as having been for 'nervous tension', as he put it.

In these circumstances, we must consider what, if any, duty of disclosure rested in the applicant when he was examined by Dr Bridges in February 1975 and by Dr Delaine in August 1975.

5. In attempting to assess the nature and extent of the obligation of disclosure that rested on the applicant in the present context, we were referred to statements contained in text books in relation to the duty of disclosure in relation to contracts of life assurance. The principles that have been evolved in relation to life insurance that are relevant in the present context are those relating to disclosure of material facts. They are clearly summarised in Ivamy, Personal Accident, Life and other Insurances (1973) p.73, in relation to life insurance: 'As in other branches of insurance the insured is under a duty to disclose to the insurer all material facts and not make any material misrepresentations.

If the insured fails in such a duty, the insurer can avoid liability.

If, however, there is a 'basis clause' in the proposal form, the insurer may avoid liability even if the fact which is not disclosed or which is misrepresented is not a material one.'

A 'basis' clause is one whereby the accuracy of the answers given by a person who makes a proposal is made the basis of the contract, as to which see Babatsikos v. Car Owners' Mutual Insurance Co Ltd 1970 VR 297. But the matter is not as simple as it may seem at first sight. The principles developed in relation to life assurance are principles based upon the law of contract, for the person who has made the proposal and the assurer who accepts the proposal enter into a contract of assurance, normally evidenced by a policy. The lack of a contractual basis to the relationship in question at least renders inapplicable those rules which there apply in cases where there is a 'basis' clause. And the rules as to materiality must also be approached with care. Subject to some additional comments that we make later, the rules as to materiality are rules that have been evolved as part of the law of contract. We do not see how it can be said that the rights conferred and obligations imposed on a person who became affected by the provisions of the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act") can be said to arise out of contract. They are conferred and imposed, respectively, by statute. To the extent that any duty of disclosure can be identified, it must be identified by implication from the provisions of the statute. And in the case of the present applicant that means the 1922 Act, for that was the provision which was in existence in February 1975 when he was examined, and in August 1975 when he was further examined. It is almost trite to note that s.184, which made its first appearance in the 1976 Act, was not to be found in the 1922 Act, nor was there any provision to a similar effect in the latter Act.

6. May s.184 of the 1976 Act, in its original or amended form, itself be relied upon for the purpose of determining the ambit of the disclosure required to be made? It is true that s.184(5) may be said to have embodied in it an assumption that an examinee is required to furnish some information and that he should not furnish false information. The latter assumption is obvious, and the former has a further assumption embodied in it. But there are more difficulties. We accept that, once s.184 came into force, its provisions had as it were an ambulatory effect, subject to what we say in paragraph 12 below. But when the applicant went for his medical examinations in 1975, s.184(5)(d) did not exist. The 1976 Act itself did not exist, and we do not see how it could be said that any inference can be drawn from the terms of s.184(5)(d) as to what were the applicant's obligations upon the occasion of the examinations which he underwent pursuant to the provisions of the 1922 Act. It is on the question of the medical examination itself, and the provisions relating thereto in the legislation, that we must concentrate.

7. In the recent decision of the Tribunal in Re Reeve and the Commissioner for Superannuation (No.79/12014) the Tribunal set out the history of s.5 of the 1922 Act, which is the only provision in the 1922 Act which appears to be relevant for present purposes. That section required an intending employee to undergo a medical examination, but made no other relevant provision in relation to the duties of the intending employee. The provisions of s.5 effectively prevented contribution to the Superannuation Fund under Part III of the 1922 Act, or to the Provident Account, without the employee undergoing a medical examination.

8. As we see it, a medical examination does not consist merely of a physical examination. Blakiston's Gould Medical Dictionary, 4th ed. gives as the first meaning of 'examination' the words: 'Investigation or inspection for the purpose of diagnosis'. The notion of investigation embraces, in our opinion, the asking of such questions as the examining doctor considers relevant for the purpose of the examination, which in this case is not so much for the purpose of diagnosis as of a general examination for the purpose of assessing the health and physical fitness of the person such as to enable a judgment to be made as to whether he should be accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund, or to the Provident Account, or to neither. When such questions were asked, they demanded full and fair answers. Should an applicant be in doubt as to whether some possibly relevant fact should be disclosed, we think it plain that the doubt has, and always has to have been, resolved in favour of disclosure. The applicant was seeking a valuable right, no doubt a right involving mutual rights and obligations, but one which, while it would involve his own contributions, could also ultimately involve the interests of the Australian taxpaying community. The answering of the questions demands, and at the relevant time in this case demanded, the utmost good faith, and in this sense it is true that some analogy may be drawn with the principles applicable to contracts of life insurance. The words of Viscount Dunedin in Glicksman v. Lancashire and General Assurance Co 1927 AC 139 at p.143 are not, for the reasons already stated, directly relevant, but they may be adopted as a useful exposition for present purposes:

'There is the duty of no concealment of any consideration which would affect the mind of the ordinary prudent man in accepting the risk.'

9. A point to note in the circumstances is that it may be difficult to spell out any duty of disclosure beyond the confines set by the questions asked. In insurance cases it is said that the form of questions asked may render the proposer's duty more strict, in that it might direct his or her attention to the materiality of a fact or class of facts, but that it 'is more likely, however, that the questions asked will limit the duty of disclosure, in that, if questions are asked on particular subjects and the answers to them are warranted, it may be inferred that the insurer has waived his right to information, either on the same matters but outside the scope of the questions, or on kindred matters to the subject of the questions'; MacGillivray & Parkington on Insurance Law, 6th ed., 1975, paragraph 736. It follows that it is critical, out of fairness to both sides of the transaction, and in the interests of proper decision-making, that clear and comprehensive questions be asked. Particularly in the difficult area of nervous disorders, wherein there is so much difficulty of definition, it would be desirable to ask such a question as 'Have you lost any time from work, or have you consulted a doctor, because of any illness or injury?' The question could be related to a specified period of time. It would give the examining doctor the opportunity to have enquiries made, if necessary, and would not be dependent upon the examinee's recollection of symptoms or of conveyed diagnoses, or indeed upon some self diagnosis, perhaps the most unreliable touchstone of all.

10. We conclude that at a medical examination conducted pursuant to s.5(1)(a) of the 1922 Act for the purpose of a report being made for the purposes of s.5(1)(b) of the 1922 Act of the result of the medical examination, the prospective employee was obliged to answer all questions put to him fully and truthfully having regard to the knowledge which might fairly have been attributed to him of his then present condition and his past medical history. In this context we would however adopt as apposite for present purposes what Fletcher Moulton LJ said in Joel v. Law Union and Crown Insurance Company 1908

2 KB 863 at pp.884-5: 'But in my opinion there is a point here which often is not sufficiently kept in mind. The duty is a duty to disclose, and you cannot disclose what you do not know. The obligation to disclose, therefore, necessarily depends on the knowledge you possess. I must not be misunderstood. Your opinion of the materiality of that knowledge is of no moment. If a reasonable man would have recognized that it was material to disclose the knowledge in question, it is no excuse that you did not recognize it to be so. But the question always is, Was the knowledge you possessed such that you ought to have disclosed it? Let me take an example. I will suppose that a man has, as is the case with most of us, occasionally had a headache. It may be that a particular one of those headaches would have told a brain specialist of hidden mischief. But to the man it was an ordinary headache undistinguishable from the rest. Now no reasonable man would deem it material to tell an insurance company of all the casual headaches he had had in his life, and, if he knew no more as to this particular headache than that it was an ordinary casual headache, there would be no breach of his duty towards the insurance company in not disclosing it. He possessed no knowledge that it was incumbent to him to disclose, because he knew of nothing which a reasonable man would deem material or of a character to influence the insurers in their action. It was what he did not know which would have been of that character, but he cannot be held liable for non-disclosure in respect of facts which he did not know."

We have pointed out in paragraph 6 above that s.184 of the 1976 Act is not available as the foundation of any implications as to the extent of the duty of disclosure. But if we were wrong in this, and it were so available, what Fletcher Moulton LJ said above would be entirely consistent with one of the conclusions that should be drawn from that section, namely that what has to be revealed is something that has come to the mind of the person. It must be information. A person may be informed by his doctor that he has diabetes, and he then possesses that information. The person may have a very sore toe. He is informed of that fact by reason of the pain that is registered. But one way or another the pain has come to his notice. It is something 'of which (the person) is apprised or told' (see Shorter Oxford Dictionary). The section clearly requires this. This disposes of a submission that was made at one stage by Mr Huttner to the effect that the duty of disclosure is absolute, so that if it should be later found that there was some pre-existing condition that had not come to the notice of the person, then nevertheless the provisions of s.184(5) would operate to permit the issue of a benefit classification certificate accordingly. It seems clear to us that if there were such an absolute duty it would have been expected that the legislation would have taken an entirely different form, but in any event our conclusion, if we were to base it on s.184, would be based simply upon the meaning of the word 'information'.

11. In the case of the present applicant we consider that he made adequate disclosure having regard to the state of his own knowledge. He made it clear that he had been under medical treatment and he revealed that he had been prescribed concordin, the nature of which was known to the examiner. He had never been told that he had a nervous condition, and Dr Delaine drew his own conclusion that in 1975 the applicant was subject, inter alia, to 'nervous tension' or 'depression and neurasthenia'. This being so we do not think that any further disclosure was required of the applicant nor did he, so far as anything was known to him, have anything further to disclose. It was open to the examining doctor to make such further enquiries as might have appeared requisite, but what appears to have happened is that attention came to be concentrated on the raised blood pressure, which had been detected at the first medical examination early in 1975, to the exclusion of any enquiries being directed to the depression which the applicant had undoubtedly experienced for some time prior to 1975 and which, as appears from paragraph 4(xiv) above, was apparent to Dr Delaine in August 1975. We will not again traverse here the extent to which the raised blood pressure, and what ultimately emerged as severe nervous tension, were related. We refer to and repeat what we said in our previous Reasons for Decision. Suffice to say that we repeat the finding that we there made that the incapacity that was the ground for the retirement of the applicant on 22 November 1977 was severe nervous tension. We now find that the applicant did not at either of the medical examinations conducted in 1975 fail to make any relevant disclosure or make any untruthful disclosure. He answered questions put to him fully and truthfully having regard to the knowledge which might fairly have been attributed to him of his then present condition and his past medical history. If the test be whether he failed to furnish any information, or furnished false information, we find that he did not at any medical examination which he underwent for the purposes of the 1922 Act so fail to furnish any information required to be furnished by him, and that he did not thereat furnish any false information.

12. The conclusion to which we have come makes it unnecessary to deal with some very difficult problems that Mr Huttner raised, and very fully and frankly discussed, in relation to s.184 of the 1976 Act. As has already been seen, reference was made at the previous hearing to s.184(3) of the 1976 Act in its original form. Mr Huttner however conceded that the effect of s.77(1) of the Superannuation Acts Amendment Act 1978, which, as from 24 April 1978, repealed s.184 of the 1976 Act and substituted a new s.184, was to deny to the Commissioner the possibility of invoking the provisions of the old s.184 after that date, notwithstanding that the applicant had retired before 24 April 1978. Mr Huttner invited the Tribunal to consider the validity of this concession, but in our opinion it was clearly correct, with the consequence that the action taken by the Commissioner to issue the benefit classification certificate which is here in issue must be based on the provisions of the new s.184(5). It follows from what we have already said that we do not consider that there can be satisfaction of the matters set out in s.184(5)(d)(i), with the result that it is unnecessary to consider the operation of s.184(5)(d)(ii) or of the words which follow it. In fact there are very great difficulties in the language of these latter provisions, which were expounded to us by Mr Huttner and may be found referred to in detail in the transcript.

13. Briefly, the problem initially arises from the wording of s.184(5)(d)(ii), the expression of which appears to have run into difficulties of grammar and punctuation. The sense of the provisions in question is however fairly evident, namely that in cases of failure to furnish information, or of the furnishing of false information, but for which a benefit classification certificate would have been deemed to be in force immediately before the commencing day (namely 1 July 1976), certain consequences should follow. The problems of grammar and punctuation could we consider be made to bow to the clear purpose so expressed. A second problem however may be more intractable. It relates to the formulation of the consequences that follow. For the section directs the Commissioner to issue a benefit classification certificate in which there is or are specified the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person that, in the opinion of the Commissioner, would have been the physical or mental condition or conditions of the person specified in the benefit classification certificate that the Commissioner would have issued .... in respect of the person under sub-section (2) if the person had not failed to furnish that information or had not furnished that false information..." Now it is perfectly true that the sub-section as a whole acknowledges the existence of the 'deemed' benefit classification certificates that have been explained so often in Tribunal decisions: see s.184(5)(d)(ii). But the provision that is made, as is set out above, is for issue of the certificate 'that the Commissioner would have issued', whereas between 1 July 1976 and 24 April 1978 there was no provision for the issue of benefit classification certificates. They were, in the appropriate circumstances, deemed to have been in force, although the situation is confused by the circumstance that it was in fact the custom of the Commissioner between 1 July 1976 and 23 April 1978 to issue actual certificates where he deemed it warranted. The question is therefore whether s.184(5), which came into force on 24 April 1978, can apply to a situation in which the person in question retired after 1 July 1976 and before 24 April 1978 and in respect of whom it could never be said that the Commissioner 'would have issued' a benefit classification certificate. The problem is not, we consider, one of retrospectivity. Where a benefit classification certificate is issued under s.184(5) it operates in future but is deemed to have been in force in respect of the person in question immediately before his death or retirement. Such is the position in relation to a benefit classification certificate issued under s.184(2). Where the problem does arise in a critical form is in relation to the possibility that the critical provision is operative only in cases where an actual certificate would have issued under s.184(2). The reference to sub-section (2) of s.184 of course emphasises the danger that the gap in the legislation which this situation would involve does in fact exist. We do not consider that we should come to any conclusion about the matter. We confine ourselves to saying that the need for an amendment of the legislation to clarify the uncertainty is very apparent and probably urgent.

14. In what has gone before we have discussed the question of the source of the obligation to make full disclosure. On one view the cases on insurance law may have gone beyond the ordinary rules of mistake and misrepresentation that apply in the law of contract. The rules are commonly stated as an application of the principle that, a contract of insurance being a contract that involves uberrima fides, full disclosure is required of such material facts as are known to the assured. As Lord Mansfield said in a famous passage in Carter v. Boehm (1766) 3 Burr 1905 at 1909: 'Insurance is a contract upon speculation. The special facts, upon which the contingent chance is to be computed, lie more commonly in the knowledge of the insured only: the underwriter trusts to his representation, and proceeds upon confidence that he does not keep back any circumstance in his knowledge, to mislead the underwriter into a belief that the circumstance does not exist, and to induce him to estimate the risque as if it did not exist. The keeping back such a circumstance is a fraud, and therefore the policy is void. Although the suppression should happen through mistake, without any fraudulent intention; yet still the underwriter is deceived, and the policy is void; because the risque run is really different from the risque understood and intended to be run at the time of the agreement ... The governing principle is applicable to all contracts and dealings. Good faith forbids either party by concealing what he privately knows, to draw the other into a bargain, from his ignorance of that fact, and his believing the contrary...'

15. These considerations have led us to consider the basis of the doctrine of uberrima fides. The Courts have evolved rules of conduct in this area which are commonly expressed as contractual rules, but the basis of them may deserve further identification. In a previous edition of Cheshire & Fifoot's Law of Contract, namely the 4th ed., 1956, the learned authors confessed to some difficulty in deciding at what stage contracts uberrimae fidei should be discussed, but considered that 'they seem to fall most naturally under the general heading of fraud. They are cases in which one party requires confidence in the other, and a misuse of that confidence may properly be regarded as a further example of constructive fraud.' Constructive fraud, as the authors point out, was an equitable doctrine to be distinguished from the term fraud at common law. It 'embraces cases in which there has been no dishonesty. It is directed against conduct which, though free perhaps from moral turpitude or from actual evil design, may tend to the deception of third persons, or to the abuse of a confidential relationship, or to the injury of the public interest.' Reference is then made to Story, Equity Jurisprudence, 2nd English ed., section 216. We would refer also to s.218 of that work which states as follows:

'But by far the most comprehensive class of cases of undue concealment arises from some peculiar relation,, or fiduciary character between the parties. Among this class of cases are to be found those which arise from the relation of client and solicitor, principal and agent, principal and surety, landlord and tenant, parent and child, guardian and ward, ancestor and heir, husband and wife, trustee and cestui que trust, executors or administrators and creditors, legatees or distributees, appointer and appointee under powers, and partner and part-owners. In these, and the like cases, the law, in order to prevent undue advantage, from the unlimited confidence, affection, or sense of duty, which the relation naturally creates, requires the utmost degree of good faith (uberrima fides) in all transactions between the parties. If there is any misrepresentation, or any concealment of a material fact, or any just suspicion or artifice or undue influence, courts of equity will interpose, and pronounce the transaction void, and, as far as possible, restore the parties to their original rights.'

16. We do not need to rely upon the point for the purpose of coming to our decision as to how the relevant duty of disclosure should be formulated, but from the foregoing it appears that there are grounds for asserting that a duty of disclosure may be capable of being discerned from the nature of the relationship existing between two parties to a transaction, that relationship not necessarily being of a contractual nature. If this is so, the relationship between an applicant in relation to the Superannuation Fund and those who have responsibility for the administration of the Fund would in our opinion be enough to enable the discernment from that relationship of the duty of disclosure which we have decided is to be implied from the terms of the relevant legislation.

17. For the reasons stated, our decision will be that the decision of the respondent to issue a benefit classification certificate pursuant to the provisions of s.184(5) of the 1976 act should be set aside. Given this it follows that it is unnecessary for us to consider the second question of whether the applicant's benefit entitlement fell to be interpreted under s.66(2) of the Act. That decision of the respondent we also formally set aside.
