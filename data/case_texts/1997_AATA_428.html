Hinch and Commissioner of Taxation [1997] AATA 428; 37 ATR 1128; 97 ATC 2171 (28 October 1997)

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No VS97/9

Sitting as Small Taxation Claims Tribunal )



Re DESMOND JOHN HINCH

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



AAT No 12352



DECISION



Tribunal Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member

Date 28 October 1997

Place Melbourne

Decision The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.



.......(Sgd) B. H. Pascoe......

Senior Member



CATCHWORDS

INCOME TAX - home office expenditure - interest on mortgage - whether incurred in deriving assessable income - whether private or domestic - cost of new dinner suit - purchased to attend function overseas - whether private expenditure

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s. 51

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Forsyth 81 ATC 4157

Handley v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 84 ATC 4165

Swinford v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 84 ATC 4803

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Cooper 91 ATC 4396

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Edwards 94 ATC 4255

Case Q31 83 ATC 135

Case 48 94 ATC 422

Case 72 96 ATC 640

REASONS FOR DECISION

28 October 1997 Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member



  1. This is an application to review a decision of the respondent to disallow an objection lodged by the applicant against an amended assessment of income tax based on income for the year ended 30 June 1994. The amended assessment was issued on 24 April 1996 following an audit and disallowed $4,269 of deductions claimed in the return. After objection the respondent allowed a further deduction of $804. At the hearing the applicant conceded the disallowance of the expenses claimed other than the amount of $1,440 claimed as home office rent and $673 for purchase of a dinner suit.
  2. At the hearing the applicant, Mr Hinch, was represented by Mr D McLennan, an accountant and tax agent, and the respondent was represented by an officer of the respondent. Mr Hinch gave evidence.
  3. Mr Hinch said that during the major part of the year ended 30 June 1994 he was responsible for the management of two restaurants. He said that these responsibilities included ordering supplies, monitoring bookings, staff management, bookkeeping and the general day to day operations. The restaurants opened seven days a week from 6.00 pm to midnight. He maintained that there was no appropriate space available at the restaurants for much of the work and it was necessary to do this work at his home. In addition, Mr Hinch said that there were security problems in the restaurant area late at night so that it was appropriate to depart with the takings as quickly as possible and to do all of the paperwork at home. He stated that he was well-known in the area in which the restaurants were located so he acquired a residence in a suburb well away from that area.
  4. The restaurants were owned and operated by a company, Juracom Pty Ltd, in which Mr Hinch was a 25% shareholder and a director. Mr Hinch said the company had incurred considerable losses on the restaurants and that he was not paid any salary. He maintained that he had a responsibility in the relevant year to manage and maintain the restaurants for the company and to seek purchasers for them. One restaurant was sold in November 1993 and the other in April 1994. Mr Hinch said that the result of his involvement in the restaurant business was a personal loss of between $170,000 and $200,000.
  5. Mr Hinch said that, in April 1994, he commenced full-time employment with Just Jeans Pty Ltd as Australian Sales Manager. This involved a substantial amount of travel and evening work examining daily sales reports and contact with regional or state managers in areas as distant as Perth and New Zealand. Although he was provided with an office by his employer, Mr Hinch said that much of his work was done outside normal business hours, much of the reporting was provided by facsimile to his home and a considerable amount of telephone contact was done at night at his home. He stated that it was common to arrive in Melbourne by plane in the evening, go home and work, then catch another flight the next morning. He said that after April 1994, it was necessary for him to continue some work on the restaurant business and it had been difficult to manage within his commitment to Just Jeans Pty Ltd.
  6. Mr Hinch provided a sketch plan of his residence on which were marked two rooms used as an office and a working area for his computer, fax and report filing. He considered that the area devoted solely to a home office represented some 35% of the total area. The claim of $1,440 was said to be 20% of rental paid, $7,200. The respondent considered that the plan indicated that only 13.5% of total space was occupied by the two rooms. Mr Hinch said that the sketch plan was from memory and not to scale and that the respondent’s calculations had not included a further area used for storage. Mr Hinch said that he shared the residence with a friend with an understanding that the friend would not go into the rooms in which confidential information was held.
  7. In relation to the claim for the cost of a dinner suit, Mr Hinch said that this was purchased in New York to enable him to attend a function in that city which stipulated black tie as the required dress. He said that he was in the United States at the request of Just Jeans Pty Ltd. He had with him a dark blue business suit and had expected that, as an overseas visitor, he could wear that suit. He said that a few days prior to the New York function he had torn the trouser leg of the suit when leaving a men’s room, making the suit unwearable. In order to properly represent his company and to respect the other business attendees, he said that it was necessary to buy a new dinner suit to wear at the function. Mr Hinch said that he already owned another dinner suit at home and the cost of the new one was incurred solely for the purposes of his employment.
  8. Mr Hinch was asked by the Tribunal to clarify the amount claimed for a home office. The return of income and other documentation referred to the amount as “rent”. However, the Tribunal noted that in his evidence and in a letter to his accountant Mr Hinch had referred to having bought the residence. Mr Hinch confirmed that he had purchased the residence and that the description of rent was inappropriate. Subsequent checking by representatives of both parties indicated that the claim of $1,440 represented 20% of $7,200 which Mr McLennan had noted as rent. The respondent advised that copies of cheque butts held by the respondent showed payments of $600 per month which appeared to be mortgage repayments and could be assumed to include principal and interest. Mr McLennan advised that the $7,200 represented 50% of $14,400 being Mr Hinch’s share of home loan interest calculated as 11.2% of $129,000.
  9. It was submitted by Mr McLennan that whether the expenditure was interest or rent did not effect an entitlement to a deduction for a proportion of that expenditure relevant to the use of part of the premises for the purpose of producing assessable income. It was argued that the circumstances of Mr Hinch were abnormal or unusual. Given his concern about security and the unavailability of suitable working conditions at the restaurant, it was said to have been necessary for him to use his home as his place of business. In addition it was argued that the need for him to work in evenings in his employment with Just Jeans Pty Ltd made the use of an office at home necessary. Mr McLennan submitted that the growth in use of electronic equipment meant that people in Mr Hinch’s position could and did carry out much of their work at home and outside normal business hours. It was said that the expenditure on the dinner suit was necessarily incurred in relation to his employment where, in order to properly carry out his duties as a representative of his employer, he had no option other than to incur the expenditure on the purchase of a second dinner suit which had no value to him for personal use.
  10. For the respondent it was submitted that the interest expenditure was not deductible as being of a private or capital nature. It was said that the applicant chose to work at home for reasons of personal convenience and could not be said to have been carrying on any business from home or using the home as a place of business. It was submitted that the purpose of the expenditure was to acquire a residence jointly with a friend and the essential character was payment for provision of a private residence. No part of the expenditure could be said to have been directly related to the provision of a place of work and from which assessable income was derived. It was further argued that the applicant derived no income from the restaurant activity and, given the losses being incurred by the restaurants, had no prospect of deriving income from that source. Consequently, it was said, the activities of Mr Hinch were directed at protecting his investment, minimising losses and seeking to sell the restaurants. It was further argued that, if any apportionment was appropriate, the proportion of time in which part of the premises were used for business out of the total time of a normal day should be relevant, particularly in relation to the latter part of the year when, on the evidence of Mr Hinch, he spent a substantial amount of time away from home. In relation to the claim for expenditure on the dinner suit, it was submitted that this was private in nature being an item of normal conventional clothing, not limited to use in earning assessable income and not required as a condition of employment but a matter of personal choice.
  11. It would be easy to affirm the respondent’s decision in relation to the use of a home office on the grounds that the applicant claimed a deduction for rent paid and it is clear that no rent was paid. However, the objection lodged on behalf of the applicant used the expression “home office expenses” and it would be inappropriate to deny the applicant’s claim out of hand because of an incorrect labelling of the expenditure. It would appear that the claim relates to interest expenditure relevant to money borrowed to purchase the applicant’s residence. If any deduction is to be allowed, it will be necessary to remit the matter to the respondent to confirm the exact amount of that interest.
  12. The deduction claimed by the applicant is governed by the provisions of subsection 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”) which states:
“51(1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income.”

The question of deductions for a proportion of rent or interest has been considered on many occasions by the Courts and this Tribunal. Two of the leading cases were Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Forsyth 81 ATC 4157 and Handley v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4165. Both decisions were handed down on the same day, both were decisions of the same majority of the High Court and both concerned barristers claiming deductions for use of a home study. In Forsyth’s case the claim was for rent or licence fee for use of the study paid to the family trust which owned the residence and in Handley the claim was for a proportion of interest, rates and insurance premiums. Both were disallowed. In Handley, Wilson J ( at page 4176) said:

“In my opinion, the appeal cannot succeed. The room used as a study does not cease to be part of the taxpayer’s home merely because as a matter of convenience he uses it for professional purposes for 20 hours per week during 45 weeks of the year. It is true that in choosing for purchase in 1969 this particular residence as a home for himself and his family the taxpayer was influenced by the fact that there was in it a room which he considered to be suitable for use by him as a study. But it remained essentially part of his home. The payments for mortgage interests, rates and insurance premiums were of a kind which in the circumstances of this case cannot be apportioned between home and office expenses. They related to the building and/or land as a whole, and are not affected in any way at all by reason of the fact that the taxpayer performs professional work on the premises. They would remain the same whether or not he worked at home.

In view of the foregoing considerations, and for the reasons I have given in Forsyth, I conclude that no part of the outgoings possess the requisite character of outgoings incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, nor are they necessarily incurred in carrying on the taxpayer’s profession. In any event, I believe that they must be regarded as outgoings of a capital, private or domestic nature, thus coming within the exception to sec. 51.”

In agreeing with the decision of Wilson J, Mason J said (at pages 4172 and 4173):

“I do not agree that in determining the ‘essential character of the expenditure’ we should look only to the use to which the study is put, though use is obviously a matter of great importance. It is necessary to look to the character of what is said to have been acquired by means of the expenditure, namely the study, and to its relationship to the home of which it forms part, in order to decide whether the expenditure falls within the exception.

The outgoings consisted of interest payable on moneys borrowed to purchase a home. The fact that the home included a study and that the taxpayer proposed to use the study predominantly for professional purposes are quite consistent with the expenditure being of a capital, private or domestic nature.”

In a subsequent decision of the Supreme Court of New South Wales, Swinford v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 84 ATC 4803, David Hunt J decided that a self-employed script writer having no other place of work was entitled to a deduction for a proportion of rent of her flat. His Honour considered the High Court decisions in Forsyth and Handley and drew a distinction between interest on borrowings and rent when he said (at page 4808):

“...If need be, moreover, there is, it seems to me, a valid distinction to be drawn in relation to the payments of interest upon moneys borrowed to purchase premises (when the essential character of the payments of interest are usually related back to the character of the expenditure upon the purchase of the premises) and the payments of rent for those premises (when the essential character of those payments of rent may more readily be related to the use of the premises at the time of each such payment).”
  1. In this case the residence in question is a three bedroom unit which was purchased jointly by Mr Hinch and his friend. The area said to be used as a home office consisted of two of the bedrooms, one of which was said to be used as an office and the other for a computer, a facsimile machine and record storage. Whilst Mr Hinch maintained that the choice of residence was influenced by the availability of an area suitable for work, the residence was purchased as a residence to be used jointly as such with another person. In my view the essential character of the interest expense relates to the purchase of that residence. Mr Hinch stated that the choice of the unit to buy and its location was dictated also by how much he could afford to pay. Although it may be said that, for part of the year, Mr Hinch was required to do work for the restaurant at home because no other suitable space was available and security considerations meant that home was safer, there was a significant element of personal convenience in the decision to do the work and maintain records at home. This was, clearly, a major element whilst employed by Just Jeans Pty Ltd. Nevertheless, the dominant factor in arriving at a decision that a proportion of the interest is not deductible is that the interest relates to the purchase of a jointly owned residence. The facts that it was purchased jointly with a friend, that Mr Hinch derived no income from his activities with the restaurant and had no expectation of deriving income in the relevant year or subsequently and the home was not a place of business of Mr Hinch, reinforce the view that no proportion of the interest is an allowable deduction.
  2. In relation to the claim for the cost of a dinner suit, it is again necessary to determine the “essential character of the expenditure” (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Cooper 91 ATC 4396 at 4401-4402). In the majority of circumstances expenditure on a conventional item of clothing, such as a dinner suit, which can be worn on many occasions other than those related solely to employment must be regarded as private and not having the necessary nexus with the gaining or producing of assessable income. Here, although the case was not referred to by Mr McLennan, the applicant seeks to argue that buying an additional dinner suit acquired for one work-related function, the expenditure falls within the category of clothing expenditure allowed in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Edwards 94 ATC 4255. In that case the taxpayer obtained a deduction for a proportion of expenditure on formal wear required to attend functions as personal secretary to the Governor’s wife on the grounds that there was demonstrable additional clothing expenditure incurred solely to enable the taxpayer to fulfil the obligations of her employment. Mr Hinch maintained that he purchased the dinner suit solely to enable him to properly represent his employer on a particular occasion. It was not a condition of his employment that he wear particular clothing to a particular function and his position is little different to the taxpayer in Case Q31 83 ATC 135. In that case a claim for a dinner suit was made by a senior public servant and the Board of Review No. 3 disallowed the claim. The Chairman, Mr Hogan, considered that the taxpayer had put the claim no higher than a personal obligation to honour the convention of wearing a dinner suit and found the expenditure to be private. Dr Gerber, then a member of the Board, expressed his opinion in his inimitable manner in saying:
“...A dinner suit is still merely a spectrum variance of conventional clothing and thus readily distinguishable from the attire worn by, say, liftboys or professors of Greek on ceremonial occasions. In short, it remains an expenditure of a private or domestic nature.”

In this case, I am of the opinion that the same result must ensue. The suit in question is an item of conventional clothing and, although it may well have been an additional dinner suit purchased to satisfy Mr Hinch’s feeling of obligation to arrive at a function conventionally attired, it still remains expenditure of a private or domestic nature. The expenditure here can be distinguished from that in Edwards’ case and is more akin to that in decisions of this Tribunal in Case 48 94 ATC 422 and Case 72 96 ATC 640.

  1. If follows from the foregoing that the decision under review should be affirmed.
