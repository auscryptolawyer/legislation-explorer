Michael John Chadwick and Commissioner of Superannuation [1993] AATA 245; (1993) 30 ALD 699 (6 August 1993)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

MICHAEL JOHN CHADWICK v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V92/1274

AAT No. 8910

Number of pages - 16

Superannuation

[1993] AATA 245; (1993) 30 ALD 699

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

I. R. THOMPSON (DEPUTY PRESIDENT), A. ARGENT (MEMBER) AND J. BRASSIL (MEMBER)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - entitlement to early retirement benefit - duties of employment profoundly altered - employee left employment - alteration had caused position to cease to exist - ceased to be eligible employee by reason thereof - breach of contract by employer entitled employee to rescind

Superannuation Act 1976 ss. 58, 154

Commissioner for Superannuation v Bayley [1979] FCA 76; (1979) 28 ALR 293

Mann v Capital Territory Health Commission [1981] FCA 160; (1981) 54 FLR 23

Quinn v Jack Chia (Australia) Ltd. [1992] VicRp 37; (1992) VR 567

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 26, 28 June and 8 July 1993

6:8:1993

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr N. Green

Solicitors for the Applicant: Sly and Weigall

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr P. Hanks

Solicitors for the Respondent: AGS

ORDER

The Tribunal decides to set aside the decision under review and to remit the matter to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with a direction that the rate of the applicant's early retirement benefit payable to him under the provisions of Division 2 of Part V of the Superannuation Act 1976 is to be calculated on the basis that he is to be deemed to have retired involuntarily.

DECISION

I. R. THOMPSON, A. ARGENT AND J. BRASSIL The applicant was employed by ANL. On 15 January 1991 he ceased to be so employed. He subsequently applied for payment of benefit under the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act"), electing to be paid a lump sum in lieu of pension. A delegate of the respondent decided that the applicant was not eligible for the benefit that would have been payable to him if he had retired involuntarily. That was a reviewable decision, as that expression is defined in section 154(1) of the Act. The applicant's solicitors requested the respondent to reconsider the decision. One of his delegates did so and, pursuant to section 154(4) of the Act, confirmed it. The applicant then applied to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal, pursuant to section 154(6) of the Act, for review of the decision so confirmed.

2. At the hearing of the application the applicant was represented by Mr N. Green, of Counsel; the respondent was represented by Mr P. Hanks, of Counsel. In addition to the documents lodged with the Tribunal pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the T documents"), Mr Green tendered in evidence a set of 19 documents, mainly correspondence within ANL to or about the applicant, and also a number of other documents of a similar nature. Oral evidence in support of the applicant's case was given by the applicant himself. Mr Hanks called as witnesses Mr J P. McCormack, Mr S. G. Bradford and Mr R. I. Anderson. At the commencement of the hearing the parties tendered also a written statement of certain agreed facts.

3. The applicant entered the employment of ANL's predecessor, the Australian Shipping Commission ("ASC") in January 1981 at the age of 31 years. ASC was an approved authority for the purposes of the Act and the applicant became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act on 4 June 1982. Act No. 127 of 1988 converted ASC into a public company, ANL. It provided, however, that staff members of ASC continued to be employed by ANL on the terms and conditions on which they were employed by ASC. The applicant remained an eligible employee for the purposes of the Act up until the time when he ceased to be employed by ANL in January 1991.

4. The evidence of Mr McCormack, Mr Bradford and Mr Anderson, establishes that in 1989 Mr Bradford was the General Manager of the International Liner Trades Division ("ILT") of ANL, that Mr Anderson was the General Manager of the Sales and Marketing Unit of ILT and that Mr C. Jacob was the Victorian Regional Manager of ILT. All three had their offices in Melbourne. In Mr Jacob's office were staff responsible for ILT sales, for ILT freight, bookings and containers and for ILT accounting. The evidence of Mr Bradford and Mr Anderson establishes that by the middle of 1989 problems had developed in the Victorian Region of ILT; the problems related to the freight, wool, bookings and containers area of Mr Jacob's responsibilities. It was decided to increase the staff of the Regional Office by appointing an Agency Services Manager to supervise all those activities.

5. At that time the applicant was working in the New South Wales Regional Office of ILT, engaged in sales and marketing, not management of the agency services; there was an Agency Services Manager in the New South Wales Regional office. The applicant had, to that date, had a successful career in ASC and ANL. He had begun work in 1981 as a sales representative. He had been promoted by 1987 to be the Regional Sales Manager in the New South Wales Regional Office. It appeared to Mr Bradford and Mr Anderson that he had the potential to become a senior manager; they considered that the experience of supervising the agency services work in the Victorian Regional Office would assist him to develop that potential. He was, therefore, invited to travel to Melbourne to discuss the possibility of his being transferred from Sydney to Melbourne to become the Agency Services Manager in the Victorian Regional Office. Following that visit he was offered the position and accepted it by a letter dated 8 September 1989. He then moved to Melbourne and began work as Agency Services Manager there on 25 September 1989.

6. On 11 September 1989 Mr Jacob provided the applicant with a copy of a job description for the position of Agency Services Manager in the Regional Office. It was in the following terms:-

"PRIMARY OBJECTIVE:

1. Provide efficient and effective management of Victorian Regional

operational areas viz:-

- Freight

- Wool

- Bookings

- Transport

2. Provide representation at Victorian State Level Meetings with ANL

Consortia partners consistent with ANL aspirations in International

Liner Trades as required.

3. Provide investigative and analytical assistance to Regional

Manager Victoria in negotiation of contracts on behalf of Principals

in respect of wool dumping/cartage, LCL Depots, FCL storage/bonding

etc, and make recommendations as appropriate.

4. Maintain and improve procedures for the handling of transhipment

cargo.

5. Take responsibility for preparation of statistical data/reports

in regard to ANL Sales activities (bookings) relative to Victorian

Region.

6. Liaise with the Regional Accountant and enhance, through

co-operation and improved procedures, financial controls.

7. Ensure that the operational departments are structured, staffed

and managed effectively and efficiently.

8. Through effective employment procedures, employ, train, develop

and motivate staff to produce a stable, happy workforce.

9. Ensure that all operational personnel are:

i) Customer orientated in their dealings with shippers, and

ii) Constantly seeking t find ways of improving the Region's service

to clients.

10. Maintain staff turnover levels at less than 15% per annum.

11. Ensure that operational errors are kept to a minimum.

12. Monitor the needs of hardware and software facilities and

recommend on further development to ensure that the Region has the

appropriate equipment and technology to enable it to provide

superior service.

13. Undertake any other responsibilities as may be required from

time to time.

DIRECT SUBORDINATES:

1. Bookings Supervisor

2. Freight Supervisor

3. Transport Co-ordinator

OTHER REPORTING RELATIONSHIPS

Refer to attached organisation chart."

7. That job description is dated 1 September 1989. Although a copy was not provided to the applicant until three days after he had accepted the offer of the position of Agency Services Manager in the Victorian Regional Office, there is no doubt that it described accurately the responsibilities of the position at the time when the applicant accepted the offer and at the time when he commenced work in it on 25 September 1989.

8. However, by April 1990 Mr Jacob was concerned with the functioning of the part of his office which the applicant was required to manage and by November 1990 the applicant was regarded by Mr Bradford and Mr Anderson as being unable to handle that management effectively. On 16 November 1990 Mr Jacob advised the applicant that he was transferring responsibility for freight from the applicant to another employee. The responsibility for wool had always accompanied the responsibility for freight; the applicant therefore ceased to be responsible for wool. On 21 December 1990 he was relieved of his responsibility for bookings. From then on he was responsible only for transport, which essentially was the movement of containers, and for three general office staff, namely the word processor operator, the switchboard operator and the telex operator. The extent of the supervision required by those three general office staff was minimal. Effectively, the applicant was left in charge of six operative staff dealing with containers. When he had taken up the position of Agency Services Manager in September 1989 he had been responsible for the supervision of approximately thirty staff.

9. In spite of the reduction of the applicant's responsibilities from 16 November, his title remained Agency Services Manager and his remuneration was unchanged. However, having received advice from his solicitors, he took the view that ANL had breached his contract of employment by taking away most of the responsibilities of his position. The solicitors wrote to ANL on 8 January 1991 that, unless the situation was rectified before 15 January 1991, the changing of the responsibilities would be regarded as a repudiation by ANL of the applicant's contract of employment. On 15 January 1991 ANL wrote to the applicant's solicitors stating that it had acted within the terms of the applicant's contract of employment in varying his responsibilities and pointed out that the applicant remained employed in the position of Agency Services Manager. At the close of business on that day the applicant handed to Mr Jacob the credit card and the keys of the car supplied to him by ANL and said that he regarded himself as no longer employed by ANL. On 18 January 1991 the Personnel Manager of ANL wrote to the applicant stating that his actions were regarded by ANL as an abandonment of employment.

10. Section 58(3) of the Act sets out circumstances in which an eligible employee is to be deemed for the purposes of the Act to have retired involuntary. The only circumstance relevant to the cessation of the applicant's employment in the present case is paragraph (d). The statutory provision is in the following terms:-

"58. (3) Where a person ceases to be an eligible employee

because:

. . . . .

(d) except in the case of a temporary employee - his position or

office ceases to exist, whether by reason of its being abolished or

otherwise;

. . . . .

the person shall, for the purpose of this Act, be deemed to have

retired involuntarily."

11. Both parties informed the Tribunal that the only issues in these proceedings were:-

(1) whether on or before 15 January 1991 the applicant's

position of Agency Services Manager in the Regional Office of

ANL in Melbourne had ceased to exist; and

(2) if so, whether the applicant ceased to be an eligible

employee because his position ceased to exist.

12. Essentially, the decision in respect of the first of those issues must depend on whether or not, as the respondent's witnesses asserted, the responsibilities of the position had been reduced only temporarily with the intention that they should subsequently be restored when the applicant had got on top of the job, or whether, as Mr Green submitted, there was no such intention. There is, in our view, no doubt that, if there was no such intention, the position to which the applicant had been appointed in August 1989 was not the same position which he occupied on 15 January 1991, even though it bore the same title.

13. It was suggested by Mr Hanks that the inclusion of item 13 in the list of responsibilities contained in the Job Description authorized ANL to alter the responsibilities of the position as it wished from time to time, including altering them to the extent to which it in fact altered them in the applicant's case. We totally reject that submission. Item 13 refers to the undertaking of other, i.e. additional, responsibilities, not to the cessation of any of the listed responsibilities. Mr McCormack gave evidence that, although the responsibilities of various positions in ANL were changed from time to time to meet its organizational requirements, such alterations were discussed with the persons holding the positions concerned and the alterations are made with their agreement.

14. In order to reach a conclusion about the intentions of ANL and its senior management in January 1991 with regard to the future of the position of Agency Services Manager in the Victorian Regional ILT Office, it is necessary to examine the sequence of events which led to the reduction in the applicant's responsibilities. As part of ANL's personnel management procedures, management development reports were required to be made on managerial staff every year. Each such report was made by the person next in line in the managerial hierarchy above the person in respect of whom the report was made. On 28 May 1990 Mr Jacob wrote such a report about the applicant. The report can best be described as raising some doubt as to the applicant's ability to perform the duties of his position. On 2 April 1990 Mr Jacob had sent the applicant a letter in which he had referred to recent falling standards in the operational areas of the Victorian Regional Office and had expressed his opinion that the minimum levels of service required were not being met. In the report Mr Jacob sought to identify shortcomings in the applicant's supervision of those who were carrying out the work in the operational areas.

15. On 5 June 1990 the applicant responded to the letter dated 2 April by pointing out some deficiencies in ANL's system of employing and rewarding staff in the operational areas, deficiencies for which he considered that he could not reasonably be held responsible. He put forward some suggestions for ways in which that system might be improved. On 5 July 1990 he wrote to Export Freight, that is to say one of the sections of the office under his supervision, setting out proposals for restructuring. Mr Jacob endorsed on the memorandum a note: "This is an excellent communication] I wish you and OF well and look forward to hearing how it all goes.".

16. On 23 July 1990 the applicant wrote to Mr Jacob regarding the management development report stating that he did not concur with the assessment in it. He set out at some length his reasons for not doing so. He explained in considerable detail the difficulties which he had encountered, most of which were of the types to which he had referred in his memorandum of 5 June 1990. He observed that:-

". . . . . in certain areas of this organisation there has been

. . . . . an attitude that the operational areas of the organisation

should run themselves with inadequately trained and junior staff. I

believe that the efforts required to improve these areas and to

improve attitudes (in terms of this Management Development Report)

have been underestimated."

He then gave details.

17. On 31 July 1990 the applicant wrote to Mr Jacob referring to recent conversations and correspondence on the lack of quality service from the operations area in the Victorian Regional Office. Again, he set out the reasons why the services were not satisfactory and proposed certain changes. On 13 August 1990 Mr Anderson, who was immediately above Mr Jacob in the management hierarchy, wrote to the applicant and referred to the response which he had made on 23 July to the management development report. Mr Anderson wrote that, although a task to which the applicant had referred in his letter had been achieved by him, "there is a lot more required of you". He continued:-

". . . . . we expect a higher pace of work, improved overall

supervision and coaching, more pro-active involvement, stronger

motivation and attention to urgency and detail."

He wrote that he was still awaiting the applicant's "30 day task list and evaluation of the last 30 days" and required him to attend to those matters "with the urgency they demand". On 16 August 1990 the applicant responded to that memorandum. He sent copies of his "30 and 60 day initiatives" which he said had been discussed with Mr Jacob on 3 July; he apologized for the fact that Mr Anderson had not previously received a copy. He then reported briefly on the 30 day tasks and the 60 day tasks. On 19 October 1990 he wrote to Mr Jacob suggesting some changes to the operational structure in the part of the regional office for which he was responsible.

18. Meanwhile, the applicant had been told that a further management development report was to be prepared in respect of him. On 31 August 1990 he completed the management self assessment form which was to accompany the report. Then on 5 November 1990 Mr Jacob completed the report. It was more favourable than the first report. In particular, whereas in the first report Mr Jacob had ticked the box marked "Provisional" under the heading "Overall Evaluation of Performance in Present Job", in the second report he ticked the box marked "Acceptable". However, that was not the view held by Mr Anderson. On 13 November 1990 he returned the review to Mr Jacob stating "(I) confirm our verbal discussion that I find it unacceptable". He wrote that the applicant's performance was unsatisfactory and that during discussions with the applicant he had advised the applicant of that. He continued:-

"There should be no doubt in his mind that I find his

performance totally unsatisfactory and in view of the costs of

his considerable errors, that we have sufficient cause to

terminate his employment."

He concluded:-

"It is my view that we have given Mr Chadwick every chance to

improve and he is obviously incapable. I would ask that if he

has not resigned by Friday, November 16th, that we terminate him

on grounds of poor performance."

19. On the previous day, 12 November 1990, Mr Jacob had written to Mr Anderson a memorandum which commenced as follows:-

"Mike Chadwick's imminent resignation has necessitated my giving

some thought to the supervision of our operations."

When Mr Anderson gave evidence, he said that on 13 November 1990 he had not considered the applicant to be incompetent. Rather he had thought that the applicant had a lot of problems. He said that each person needed to be treated differently. He had discovered that the applicant had had marital problems and then problems within the office. He believed that the applicant did not realize the urgency of the need for him to improve and would not do so if advice given to him was couched in gentle terms. He acknowledged that on that day he took the view that the applicant's employment was to be terminated if he did not resign before 16 November 1990. He said that he had regarded the resignation as imminent because of the memorandum dated 12 November 1990 which he had received from Mr Jacob. Mr Anderson gave evidence that by 16 November 1990 the matter had been taken out of his hands by Mr Bradford who had assumed direct responsibility for dealing with it.

20. Mr Bradford gave evidence that on 8 November 1990 he met the applicant at the applicant's request. He said that the applicant was concerned about the performance reviews. He said that he told the applicant that they were not satisfactory. There was then a discussion of other positions in ANL which might be available. The applicant was concerned about his ability to perform the duties of Agency Services Manager at an acceptable level. Mr Bradford said - and we find this remarkable in view of Mr Anderson's evidence that talent was short in ANL - that, if an employee who had been promoted to a position was unable to perform the duties of that position, it was normal "to go to dismissal" and that it was not normal for the employee to return to his previous position, as invariably that position had been filled by someone else.

21. He said that the only "lower position" available in ANL in November 1990 was in the Bulk Trades Division, that is to say outside the ILT Division. There could therefore be no straight transfer into it and it would have been necessary for the applicant to apply for the position. Mr Bradford said that there was no position available for the applicant in the ILT Division "because his performance was not satisfactory". He said that the applicant had told him that he would "follow up the other job". He said that there had been discussion of the applicant's resigning. Although the applicant had not completed the period of service necessary for him to be granted long service leave, he was getting near to it. So, Mr Bradford said, he had agreed that it would be unfair for the applicant to lose the entitlement and that, if he resigned, he could be paid cash for the long service leave which he had accrued.

22. Mr Bradford gave evidence that the reduction of the applicant's responsibilities on 16 November 1990 was decided upon by Mr Jacob in consultation with Mr Anderson. He said that the intention was to allow the applicant to attain a satisfactory level of performance with a reduced workload. He said that the adoption of such a course was not unusual but was not done in every case; it was not the standard practice. In the letter which was sent to the applicant by Mr Jacob informing him of the change in his responsibilities reference was made to specific areas in which the applicant's performance was regarded as not meeting the required standards. The letter concluded:-

"Your performance will be reviewed once again on December 7th,

1990 and your progress will be discussed with you.

It is indeed unfortunate that this action has been necessary and

I urge you to raise your performance levels as required to avoid

any further action being taken."

23. On 5 December 1990 Mr Jacob wrote a memorandum to Mr Anderson in which he stated:-

"The dragging out of the Mike Chadwick affair is not doing

anyone any good, least of all the Victorian Regional Office."

He suggested two possible courses of action. The first was "to negotiate a deadline for (the applicant's) departure, say the end of April". The second was "to say to him that if he achieves the objectives that we have set him he would be able to stay, albeit on different terms". He concluded the memorandum as follows:-

"I wish to end the situation as it currently pertains as it

impedes my ability to manage him, it impedes his ability to

perform and is certainly disruptive to the Regional staff."

(Emphasis added)

24. At that time ANL received advice from a firm of consultants regarding the possible restructuring of the Victorian Regional Office. Apparently the consultants advised that there should be a position with the responsibilities broadly the same as those of the Agency Services Manager at the time when the applicant was appointed to that position. However, a different structure was devised by ANL management which did not include such a position. Instead, there was a position bearing the title "Regional Operations Manager", the responsibilities of which were essentially those with which the applicant was left after 20 December 1990.

25. A copy of the proposed structure was sent by Mr Jacob to a number of members of the staff of the Victorian Regional Office, including the applicant. Mr Jacob requested their general opinion of the structure and any specific concerns which they would have with the implementation of it. On 12 December the applicant replied; after criticizing the staffing numbers and the particular positions proposed and commenting on the proposed structure, he concluded his memorandum with the following comment:-

In concept I don't have a problem with the structure.

Operationally it is virtually unworkable. We currently have 3

computer systems, none of which work well and none talk to each

other. We would require all 3 systems in each of the

Booking/Freight areas plus Operations which would be a

requirement for additional equipment."

26. Among the T documents is a file note dated 19 December 1990, apparently written by Mr Jacob. The first four lines read as follows:-

"Reviewed the November 16th performance letter with Mike.

Due to the changes in structure and responsibilities taking

place a number of the performance goals set are not applicable.

In addition he has taken care to comply with all the goals set

him." (Emphasis added)

However, on 21 December 1990 Mr Jacob wrote to all members of the staff of the Victorian Regional Office informing them that the management structure of the office had been changed and that the change would be implemented over the next few weeks. As a result of that restructuring of the office the applicant, while retaining the title of Agency Services Manager, was to be responsible for six operational staff and the three general office staff, namely the receptionist, the word processor operator and the telex operator. The applicant was then on leave over Christmas and the New Year. On his return Mr Jacob wrote to him on 4 January 1991 telling him that his responsibilities as Agency Services Manager under the new structure would be vessel cargo clearance, container control, transport operations and claims.

27. On 7 December 1990 the applicant's solicitors had written to ANL noting that it had "unilaterally removed the Freight area from (the applicant's) responsibilities" and that the applicant did not accept that unilateral variation and was continuing to perform the duties of "his new position" under protest. They wrote also that they were instructed that ANL had "also sought (the applicant's) resignation". They continued:-

"We believe that any forced resignation would be regarded as a

constructive dismissal. Further, a resignation by our client

may jeopardise superannuation entitlements that he would

otherwise have if his employment had been terminated by the

(ANL)."

They said that the applicant was prepared to resign on condition that ANL acknowledge that the termination of his employment was effectively a dismissal and, more particularly, "fell within the definition of involuntary retirement in section 59(3) (sic)" of the Act.

28. On 10 December 1990 the Personnel Manager of ANL replied to the applicant's solicitors letter. He wrote: "Your letter incorrectly states that the company has sought Mr. Chadwick's resignation." He said that the applicant had been given the option to continue a counselling process in the formal sense or tender his resignation and had chosen to pursue the formal counselling option. In that regard a formal warning had been issued to him in writing. The responsibility of the Freight Department had been taken away from him to assist him to perform in the remaining areas, as well as to provide for an immediate increase in efficiency and productivity of the Freight Department. The Personnel Officer wrote that:-

"(ANL) is not prepared to falsely acknowledge that a resignation

(by the applicant) would fall within the definition of

involuntary retirement in section 59(3) (sic) of the

Superannuation Act 1976."

Finally, the Personnel Manager observed that if the current counselling process failed to realize an acceptable level of performance, that might result in a lawful termination of employment based on inadequate performance.

29. For the sake of completeness, we note that at all relevant times the Shipping Officers' (ASC) Consolidated Award 1987 was in force and was binding upon ANL in respect of the applicant as its employee. However, the award contained no provision of any relevance to the issues with which we have to deal in these proceedings.

30. Mr Jacob's memorandum of 21 December 1990 set out not only the title of the position to be held by the applicant and named him as the person to hold it, but also the titles of the other supervisory positions in the Regional Office and the names of the persons who were to hold them. Whereas previously there had been three sections, the Sales Section, the Accountancy Section and the Agency Services Section, there were to be four sections, the Sales Section, the Accountancy Section, the Agency Services Section and the Customer Services Section. The position of the person responsible for supervising the Customer Services Section bore the title "Customer Services Supervisor"; the person to hold that position was named, that is to say R. Hyde. All the staff for whom he was to be responsible were performing functions of staff for whose work the Agency Services Manager had been responsible at the time when the applicant was appointed to that position.

31. In view of the assertions made in the documents, and also by Mr Bradford when he gave evidence, that the applicant was being given an opportunity to prove himself with a reduced workload with a view to his taking back all his previous responsibilities if successful, we regard it as significant the fact that another position, that of Customer Services Supervisor had been created and an employee appointed to hold that office. That appeared to us to be inconsistent with those assertions. We, therefore, questioned Mr Bradford about it. He acknowledged that Mr Hyde was occupying the new position and that two people were being paid to do what had previously been one job. He said that that had been necessary because ANL had been losing market share and the alternative would have been to allow the applicant to remain with his previous responsibilities but with doubts about his ability to perform them. It had not been possible to allow the company to go down hill but he had hoped that some day the applicant would come good. However, if his performance had not improved, the company would have dismissed him.

32. Mr McCormack gave evidence that, after the applicant ceased to be employed by ANL, the position of Agency Services Manager was not filled either substantively or on an acting basis until shortly before the hearing in these proceedings in June 1993. Then the salary had been decreased by $6,000-$7,000 per annum from what had been paid to the applicant, approximately $45,000 per annum. That, he said, indicated that the responsibilities were less than they had been when the applicant was appointed; however, Mr McCormack was unaware precisely what the responsibilities of the position were now.

33. One of the documents tendered in evidence by Mr Green was an undated file note written by an ANL Personnel Officer, Mr G. Toniolo. In it he wrote that he had been notified that "it appeared that ILT could no longer sustain Mr. Chadwick in his present role and no other suitable positions were available to which Mr. Chadwick would be acceptable" and that he was informed that "Mr. Chadwick was given an inducement to resign in the form of three months in lieu of notice". Mr Toniolo described a meeting with union representatives on 14 November 1990. He wrote that the Division's position had been reiterated and that the union was advised that "we would not negotiate upward of our offer and that, in fact, it may be withdrawn and formal warnings were likely to proceed" (emphasis added).

34. We are satisfied that in the early part of November 1990 the management of ANL were encouraging the applicant to resign. Both Mr Anderson and Mr Bradford were expressing themselves as dissatisfied by his performance of his duties. However, we note that that opinion was at variance with the opinion which Mr Jacob expressed in the second management development report. It is likely that the problems in the Victorian Regional Office which had existed before the applicant's appointment as Agency Services Manager had not all been solved as the result of his appointment. That was no doubt a matter of concern to Mr Bradford, Mr Anderson and Mr Jacob. However, there appears to have been a difference of opinion between them whether the blame could fairly be placed on the applicant. There is no doubt that Mr Anderson wanted the applicant to go. However, the decision about what should be done with him was taken out of Mr Anderson's hands by Mr Bradford. From then on Mr Bradford was the mind of ANL insofar as actions were taken by it in respect of the applicant.

35. He insisted at the hearing that his intention had at all times been to give the applicant an opportunity to prove himself so that he might resume the responsibilities of the position to which he had been appointed in September 1989. Evidence tending to show that that was his intention is the fact that the applicant's salary was not reduced. On the other hand, the manner in which the Victorian Regional Office was restructured and the fact that Mr Hyde was appointed substantively as Community Services Supervisor afford strong evidence that Mr Bradford had no real expectation that the applicant would ever again exercise all the responsibilities of the position to which he was appointed in 1989. Mr Bradford told us that, if the applicant's performance had not improved, ANL would have dismissed him. From the manner in which the Regional Office was restructured and appointments made, we are satisfied that it was Mr Bradford's expectation that that would be what would happen.

36. We regard as of some considerable significance the evidence given by Mr Anderson to which we have already referred that he did not consider the applicant to be incompetent but rather thought that he had a lot of problems, first marital problems and then problems within the office. The applicant gave evidence that he had had marital problems at the time of his transfer to Melbourne and that in Melbourne he had developed a relationship with an employee in the section of the Regional Office for which he was responsible. However, he said, that employee had left the employment of ANL in February 1990. Mr Anderson alleged that there had been a further similar relationship later in the year; that allegation was not put to the applicant. Asked whether he had discussed the applicant's personal problems with him, Mr Anderson said that Mr Jacobs had been asked to do that; however, he did not recall getting any feedback about the matter. Mr Bradford was asked by the Tribunal about any personal problems of the applicant that had been within his knowledge. He said that there had been office gossip and rumours of personal problems but that he had chosen to ignore them. He said that no other members of ANL's management had expressed to him their concern about any such problems.

37. We have to say that we find that evidence quite extraordinary. If the applicant had developed a relationship with a member of the staff for whom he was responsible, which could arguably have affected the discharge of his supervisory functions, we would have expected any person senior to him in the management hierarchy to whom that information became known either to have spoken to the applicant about it or to have taken action at the very least to ensure that someone in authority did so and then to be informed of the result. For similar reasons we have difficulty in accepting Mr Anderson's evidence that, although he knew of the problems, he left the matter entirely to Mr Jacob and did not insist on obtaining from Mr Jacob any account of action taken by him. It is most unsatisfactory that the matter was not dealt with at the proper time as a disciplinary matter. We regard it as more likely than not that the minds of both Mr Bradford and Mr Anderson were affected adversely to the applicant by the information in their possession regarding his involvement with a member of the staff under his supervision.

38. Having had regard to all the evidence, we have come to the conclusion that, when Mr Bradford caused the reduction in the responsibilities of the applicant in November and December 1990, he was not doing so in the expectation that, because of the reduced workload, the applicant would prove himself and then resume the responsibilities of the position of Agency Services Manager as they had been when he was appointed to it in 1989. We find that it was not Mr Bradford's intention that the reduction in the applicant's responsibilities should be only temporary.

39. The first question we have to decide is whether the position to which the applicant had been appointed in September 1989 had ceased to exist by 15 January 1991. Most persons who are eligible employees, as that expression is defined in the Act, are members of the Public Service and the language used in the Act can fairly be described as appropriate generally to persons employed in the Public Service. Neither of the expressions "position" and "office" in section 58(3)(d) of the Act is defined. However, we are satisfied that they are to be construed by reference to the types of persons for whom the Act provides to be contributors and beneficiaries.

40. When the applicant was appointed to be the Agency Services Manager in September 1989, he was appointed to a position having particular functions and responsibilities; the responsibilities were those set out in the Job Description of which a copy was supplied to him and the nature of the functions can be ascertained by reference to those responsibilities. By 15 January 1991 the position bearing the title Agency Services Manager involved only a very small part of the responsibilities and functions of the position having that title when the applicant was appointed to it in September 1989. We have found that that reduction in responsibilities was not merely a temporary measure. There had been a restructuring of the office and most of the responsibilities or functions that had previously been those of the Agency Services Manager had been transferred to another position. Having in mind the context of section 58(3)(d) of the Act, and of the Act itself in the scheme for the employment of persons by the Commonwealth, we have no doubt whatsoever that the position of Agency Services Manager in January 1991 was not the same position as that which bore the same title when the applicant was appointed to it and which he occupied until 16 November 1990. It is not necessary that the position be abolished in a formal sense; it is sufficient that the position otherwise ceased to exist. Our decision in respect of the first of the issues in these proceedings is, therefore, that the applicant's position had ceased to exist by 15 January 1991.

41. The second question which we have to decide is whether the applicant ceased to be an eligible employee because his position ceased to exist. It is not in doubt that he ceased to be an eligible employee when he ceased to be employed by ANL. In Commissioner for Superannuation v Bayley [1979] FCA 76; (1979) 28 ALR 293 at 301 Smithers J observed:-

"It would be a matter for surprise that under the scheme an

employee whose position is abolished by the Commonwealth should

be deprived of the entitlement otherwise arising thereon by the

simple expedient of the Commonwealth offering him a permanent

position, at any level, at any place, on any terms and which he

is under no obligation to accept."

Although the circumstances of that case were substantially different from those with which we are concerned, the comment is, in our view, properly to be understood as not related simply to the facts of that case but as being applicable generally. We are satisfied that, whether or not the respondent's conduct constituted a repudiation of the applicant's contract of employment, it was a breach of an essential term that contract which entitled him to rescind it, as he did on 15 January 1991. He had no obligation to continue in the employment of ANL after his position ceased to exist; in that regard it is immaterial that ANL was willing to continue to pay him at the same rate as previously. His position having ceased to exist, he was entitled to leave the employment of ANL at any time without him being in breach of his contract of employment. He chose to leave the employment. The reason for the choice which he made was that his office ceased to exist. We find, therefore, that he ceased to be an eligible employee because his position ceased to exist and that by virtue of the provisions of section 58(3)(d) he is to be deemed to have retired involuntarily.

42. However, in case we are wrong in the view which we have taken that, without repudiation of his contract of employment by ANL, the applicant was entitled to rescind the contract and to leave that employment without breaching the contract, we will deal with submissions made by both parties on the question whether ANL had repudiated it. In Mann v Capital Territory Health Commission [1981] FCA 160; (1981) 54 FLR 23 the Full Court of the Federal Court, by a majority, came to the conclusion that the respondent had not repudiated a contract of employment with a senior specialist in general surgery by not furnishing him with surgical work of the kind and quantity suitable for a senior general surgeon. There was no express term in the contract that it should do so. Their Honours rejected a submission by the surgeon that such a term was to be implied. At pages 29 and 30 they said:-

"The question in this case is whether the particular term

alleged should be implied. In some employment situations, it may be

correct to imply a similar term. There are a number of cases

dealing with actors and producers to whom actual performance was

regarded as vital: see, for example, Marbe v. George Edwardes

(Daly's Theatre) Ltd. (1928) 1 KB 269; White v. Australian and New

Zealand Theatres Ltd. [1943] HCA 6; (1943) 67 CLR 266. In Collier v. Sunday

Referee Publishing Co. Ltd. (1940) 2 KB 647 in a passage often

referred to, Asquith J said: 'It is true that a contract of

employment does not necessarily, or perhaps normally, oblige the

master to provide the servant with work. Provided I pay my cook her

wages regularly she cannot complain if I choose to take any or all

of my meals out. In some exceptional cases there is an obligation

to provide work. For instance, where the servant is remunerated by

commission, or where (as in the case of an actor or singer) the

servant bargains, among other things, for publicity and the master,

by withholding work, also withholds the stipulated publicity: see,

for instance Marbe v. George Edwardes (Daly's Theatre) Ltd.; but

such cases are anomalous, and the normal rule is illustrated by

authorities such as Lagerwall v. Wilkinson, Henderson and Clarke

Ltd. (1899) 80 LT 55 and Turner v. Turner v. Sawdon and Co. (1990) 2

KB 653, where the plaintiffs (a commercial traveller and a salesman

respectively, retained for a fixed period and remunerated by salary)

were held to have no legal complaint so long as the salary continued

to be paid, notwithstanding that owing to their employers' action

they were left with nothing to do. The employers were not bound to

supply work to enable the employee, as the phrase goes, to 'keep his

hand in', or to avoid the reproach of idleness, or even to make a

profit out of a travelling allowance. In such a case there is no

breach of contract, but the result is much the same as if there had

been, because in either event the plaintiff is entitled to a sum or

sums which are measured prima facie by the amount of salary in

respect of the unexpired period of service' (1940) 2 KB, at pp

650-651. (See also Halsbury's Laws of England ((4th ed.), vol. 16,

par. 557; 53 American Jurisprudence (2d), par. 133; Fridman, The

Modern Law of Employment (1963), pp 82-85.)

In Langston v. Amalgamated Union of Engineering Workers (1974) 1 WLR

185, an action under the Industrial Relations Act, 1971 (Eng.), Lord

Denning MR, after commenting that time had marched on since

Collier's case, said: 'In these days an employer, when employing a

skilled man, is bound to provide him with work. By which I mean

that the man should be given the opportunity of doing his work when

it is available and he is ready and willing to do it' (1974) 1 WLR,

at p 192. (See also Langston v. Amalgamated Union of Engineering

Workers (No. 2) (1974) 1 ICR 510, at pp 521-522.)

This formulation does not involve a duty as absolute as that relied

on here. In many situations, contractors and other employees, and

perhaps professional employees in particular, make inquiries and

seek information to see whether a risk as to the volume or

continuity of work is acceptable.

There are a number of matters which lead us to the conclusion that

in the present case the term should not be implied. There is the

difficulty of applying the term in question. Some of the

considerations show up more clearly if the term is assumed and

matters of breach and damage are considered. There is for example,

no period stipulated during which compliance is to be tested. It

would not be a once and for all situation; the term could be

complied with at some times, and not at others, and so on. A

variety of external factors could become operative. If the term

created an absolute obligation on the Commission, some allowance

would have to be made for the work received privately, and this

could vary considerably. These and other matters are not, as

counsel suggested at one stage, merely a matter of making cross

implications. They go to the implication of the term relied on, and

of that particular term. It is perhaps one way of putting this

aspect of the matter to say that the vicissitudes attending the

employment were too many and too varied to allow the term to be

implied."

43. In our view the facts of the present case are so different from those in Mann's case that the reasons for which the majority of the Court came to the conclusion that the term suggested there should not be implied are quite inapplicable. There is no difficulty in implying a term that the functions and responsibilities of the position will remain substantially unchanged. The nature and extent of the functions and responsibilities of the position to which the applicant was appointed were matters of concern to him not dissimilar to that described by Lord Denning MR, in Langston's case.

44. In Quinn v Jack Chia (Australia) Ltd. [1992] VicRp 37; (1992) VR 567 the Supreme Court of Victoria held that, where there was a change in the duties of an employee, it was a question of fact whether the change brought the initial contract of employment to an end and brought into being a new contact of employment or whether it simply amounted to a variation of the original contract. Where the alteration in the duties and responsibilities was profound, a new contract could more readily be taken to have replaced the old one. In our view the change in the applicant's responsibilities can properly be described as profound. It is clear that he did not at any time accept the change; his solicitor's letters of 7 December 1990 and 8 January 1991 are evidence of that. Accordingly, no new contract of employment came into existence but the old contract came to an end when the applicant declined finally to continue in ANL's employment.

45. The respondent's delegate made the decision which is under review in these proceedings in accordance with the information supplied to him by ANL that the applicant's position had not ceased to exist and that he had resigned from his employment. Accordingly, the delegate decided that the only amount payable to the applicant under the Act was the amount of his accumulated contributions. However, Division 2 of Part V of the Act provides for the payment of early retirement benefit to an eligible employee who is deemed to have retired involuntarily. Such a benefit was not paid to the applicant. He is entitled to have it paid to him. We have, therefore, set aside the decision under review and remitted the matter to the respondent to make the appropriate payment of early retirement benefit.
