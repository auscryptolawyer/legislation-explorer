ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



INCOME TAX - motor car racing - whether sponsorship fees assessable income - whether carrying on a business - deductibility of expenditure - depreciation on car under construction



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s.51(1), 54

REASONS FOR DECISIONS



11 May 1994 Mr B.H. Pascoe, Member



1. This is an application for review of decisions by the Commissioner of Taxation to disallow objections lodged by the applicant against amended assessments of income tax based on income for the years ended 30 June 1989, 1990 and 1991.



2. In each of the three years the applicant had claimed expenditure related to motor car racing activities. In the latter two years he had also disclosed assessable income in the form of sponsorship fees. Following an audit of the applicant in 1992, amended assessments were issued for each of the three years increasing taxable income by $4570 in the 1989 year, $14,911 in the 1990 year, and $12,201 in the 1991 year. In a letter to the applicant advising him of the decision to issue amended assessments the respondent stated that adjustments would disallow deductions claimed relating to racing activities as the applicant "has been deemed not to be carrying on a business of car racing, but has been deemed to be pursuing a hobby".

The 1989 adjustment is particularly unclear as no sponsorship fees were shown separately in the details included in the return of income lodged by the applicant and evidence was given that the sponsorship fees were incorrectly included under salary and wages received from the applicant's employer. No evidence was provided as to the quantum in that year and, from evidence provided at the hearing, it would appear likely that the amount shown as sponsorship fees in the 1990 year is understated.



4. Evidence was given by the applicant and by Mr C, the managing director of a printing company which employed and sponsored the applicant. No witnesses were called by the respondent.



5. In his evidence the applicant stated that he had commenced motor racing as a hobby in the 1970s whilst conducting a printing business. In 1978 he decided to seek to become a professional racing driver. Subsequently he approached a large motor vehicle manufacturer and in the early 1980s the manufacturer provided him with a body shell from which he built a racing car. He then became an employee of the manufacturer in its motor sports division designing, building and racing cars. Subsequently he ceased employment and received sponsorship moneys from the motor company's spare parts division. In 1985 he commenced employment with the printing company which also provided sponsorship in the form of monthly payments, additional payments for special events and time away from normal duties to pursue his car racing related activities such as car preparation, testing and racing. The precise description of his duties as an employee for which he was paid a salary was not canvassed by either party at the hearing. In 1989 the applicant ceased racing the car built in the early 1980s as he considered that he needed a more competitive vehicle. He negotiated with the same motor company to acquire a special racing engine and a new standard road car from which he could construct a new racing car. The car was purchased for $1000.



6. The applicant explained that the new racing car resembled the standard road car and was described as such, but the only parts of the original standard road car used in the racing car were the roof and two doors. All other body parts were fibreglass made from moulds taken from the original car and, apart from the motor and gearbox, all components were hand made. The building of the new racing car took four years and it did not commence to be used for racing until after the period with which this review was concerned.



7. It was stated by the taxpayer that, from the early 1980s, he had formed an intention and commenced working toward a full time career in motor racing. His purpose was to establish a motor racing team employing additional people, to design and build racing cars and employ other drivers. He gave examples of other people in the industry who had accomplished the goal he had set himself. He also stated that, subsequent to the period under review, he had entered into a contract to design and build a racing car for a third party.



8. Included in the expenditure claimed and disallowed by the respondent were leasing costs and depreciation of equipment acquired to construct the new vehicle, depreciation on the costs of manufacture of the vehicle and interest and borrowing costs relating to funds borrowed to construct a large garage and driveway where the new vehicle was constructed and housed. In each of the years to which these applications apply the major part of the deductions claimed and disallowed related to the cost of facilities and equipment used to construct, test and modify the new vehicle which did not actually race during the period.



9. The basic question argued in the hearing was whether the activities of the applicant during the three years could properly be described as a hobby or as the carrying on of a business. For the respondent, it was submitted that the applicant was not engaged in any business activities during the period under review but pursuing a hobby. At best, if any intention of carrying on a business had been formed, the activities undertaken in the period were in the way of preparation for a potential future business. It was said that the applicant was not involved in any racing activities during the period, the activities during the period were not of a trading nature, they resulted in losses and were not capable of generating a profit and the sponsorship moneys were not capable of being income and were simply a financial contribution to the cost of constructing a new car. On the other hand, Mr M. Sheedy, representing the applicant, argued that the activities carried on by the applicant during the relevant three years were an integral and continuing part of a business commenced by the applicant some years previously with the intention of making profits. It was submitted that the interim suspension of actual racing whilst building and preparing a new car is a normal incident of such a business and is necessary and relevant to maintaining and increasing a reputation as a designer and builder of successfully competitive racing cars. It was further submitted that the sponsorship fees were assessable income as payment for services provided by the applicant in promoting the printing company and as proceeds of the business carried on by the applicant. It was said that the primary aim of a business such as carried on by the applicant is to attract sponsorship fees with prize money as a secondary aim. Mr Sheedy sought to show that the applicant had carried out his activities in a professional manner with full record keeping and subsequent to the period under review, computerisation of his financial records.



10. It is appropriate to consider separately the questions of the assessability of the sponsorship fees and the deductibility of the amounts claimed. It is difficult to understand and accept the respondent's view that the sponsorship fees were not capable of being income. Evidence of both Mr C, the managing director of the printing company which paid the fees, and the applicant was that the fees were paid in consideration of the applicant ensuring clear signage on both the old and new racing car and ensuring that applicant represented himself within the motor racing industry as being associated with the sponsor company. The company used photographs of the vehicle in its advertising seeking to indicate a relationship between the speed of the vehicle and the speed and efficiency of its printing. As indicated previously, there was no clear evidence adduced as to the appropriate break up of total payments received from the printing company into salary for services provided as an employee of a printing company and payment for sponsorship of his racing and car building activities. In a letter dated 11 December 1992 addressed to the respondent and signed by Mr C, the company confirmed that the applicant had been employed since May 1985 and since that date the company had actively sponsored his motor racing business. The sponsorship had been in the form of time away from normal duties to pursue testing and racing of his vehicle, and since 1986 with cash payments. This letter stated that "In 1989 year, the $700 per month was inadvertently included in his group certificate". By letter of 11 June 1990 addressed to the applicant, the company stated:



"This letter confirms our recent sponsorship agreement. The company will sponsor your motor sport business by a monthly payment of $656.00 or $8528.00 per annum.



We will supplement this with lump sum payments, to be negotiated, depending on exposure and importance of the event (eg. Sandown 500 dependant on television).



Included within this package is 'time sponsorship', that is to say any time necessary to further your motor sport business will be accommodated."



These figures do not appear to tie in with the amounts treated as sponsorship fees in the returns lodged and adjustments made by the respondent in the amended assessments. It appears likely that in each of the years in question, not just the 1989 year, there remained confusion between salary and sponsorship fees. However, I am of the view that it is unnecessary to be overly concerned to arrive at the precise allocation because I am of the firm view that the sponsorship fees and some part of the salary were paid for services provided, or to be provided to the printing company in relation to motor racing activities and were assessable income of the applicant. The oral evidence which indicated that sponsorship by the printing company commenced in 1989 is at odds with the letter of December 1992 which indicated 1985 as the commencement of sponsorship. Whether this conflict of dates relates to the commencement of specific monthly sponsorship payments or the building of the new car, the apparent inconsistency does not appear critical given my view of the assessable nature of the sponsorship fees and the apparent overlap of salary and sponsorship.



11. The next question for consideration is the claim for deduction of expenses pursuant to sections 51(1) and 54 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). For the purposes of section 51(1), argument at the hearing was limited to whether or not the applicant was carrying on a business, and, therefore, the second limb of the section. No consideration was given to the question of whether any or all of the expenses claimed would be deductible under the first limb if there was a finding that the sponsorship fees were assessable income. In cases such as these there is a fine line between activities constituting a hobby and those which constitute a business. The respondent brought to the attention of the Tribunal numerous Court, Tribunal, and Board of Review decisions, including three involving motor racing activities, which sought to determine on which side of that line a taxpayer's activities fell. Both parties sought comfort for their submissions in the decision of the Federal Court in Ferguson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 79 ATC 4261 and in particular the joint judgment of Bowen CJ and Franki J where they listed a number of factors to be considered in saying (at pages 4264 and 4265):



"There are many elements to be considered. The nature of the activities, particularly whether they have the purpose of profit-making, may be important. However, an immediate purpose of profit-making in a particular income year does not appear to be essential. Certainly it may be held a person is carrying on business notwithstanding his profit is small or even where he is making a loss. Repetition and regularity of the activities is also important. However, every business has to begin and even isolated activities may in the circumstances be held to be the commencement of carrying on business. Again, organization of activities in a business-like manner, the keeping of books, records and the use of system may all serve to indicate that a business is being carried on. The fact that, concurrently with the activities in question, the taxpayer carries on the practice of a profession or another business, does not preclude a finding that his additional activities constitute the carrying on of a business. The volume of his operations and the amount of capital employed by him may be significant. However, if what he is doing is more properly described as the pursuit of a hobby or recreation or an addiction to a sport, he will not be held to be carrying on a business even though his operations are fairly substantial."



I would doubt whether either of the learned Judges would have considered that this list of factors was exhaustive and all of the decisions cited in that case and before this Tribunal make it clear that each case depends upon its own facts and a consideration of all of the circumstances surrounding the activities of a particular taxpayer. The test was described by Webb J in the decision in Martin v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1953] HCA 100; (1953) 90 CLR 470 when he said (at page 474):



"The test is both subjective and objective: it is made by regarding the nature and extent of the activities under review, as well as the purpose of the individual engaging in them, and as counsel for the taxpayer put it the determination is eventually based on the large or general impression gained."



12. In this case I am satisfied that the applicant's activities over several years prior to and including the three years with which these applications are concerned have amounted to more than the pursuit of a hobby or an addiction to a sport. His evidence of having moved away from his original printing business and from another State in the early 1980s in order to pursue an intention to become involved full time in motor racing and car design and building, the experience and study in car design and building, and the degree of involvement in the industry all point to having commenced a business of deriving income from motor racing related activities. I accept that the break from racing whilst constructing a new and more competitive car was an integral and continuing part of that business. The attainment of the applicant's goal to establish a reputation sufficient to attract sponsorship and obtain contracts for car design and building depended upon success in the field. Consequently I am of the view that expenditure incurred in carrying on that business during the years in question was allowable as a deduction under section 51(1) of the Act. I have considered and distinguished three decisions of the former Board of Review reported as 17 CTBR (NS) Case 15, Case C 18 71 ATC 77, and Case D15, 72 ATC 81. In my view the degree of involvement in motor racing activities of the three taxpayers concerned in these decisions fell short of the involvement of this applicant.



13. It was submitted on behalf of the respondent that if the Tribunal determines that the applicant was carrying on a business during the relevant years, certain expenses and deductions claimed were not allowable. In particular, the claims for interest and borrowing expenses on money borrowed for the garage and driveway and depreciation on the new car were disputed. It was argued that the building additions were of a private or domestic nature being additions to the capital value of a private residence. Evidence was given that the land on which the garage and driveway were constructed were owned by a Miss G, an associate and friend of the applicant and with whom the applicant lives. The loan application was made in both names and the applicant stated that an agreement had been entered into between himself and Miss G setting out their respective rights in the property. Although the agreement was not produced, I am prepared to accept that the applicant has tenant rights in the garage and driveway and would be entitled to compensation for the expenditure. As I am also satisfied that the additions were primarily and principally for use in the applicant's business, the interests and borrowing expenses are deductible under section 51(1).



14. The argument against deductibility of depreciation on the new racing car is more persuasive. Under section 54 of the Act depreciation is allowable on plant or articles used by a taxpayer during the year for the purpose of producing assessable income. From the evidence, it was clear that the new racing car was in the course of construction during the whole of the three years in question. Depreciation was claimed in each of the years on the progressive costs to each year end and amounted to $5592 in 1989, $5114 in 1990 and $5662 in 1991. In my view the applicant is not entitled to a deduction for such depreciation as the partially constructed car could not have been said to have been used for the purpose of producing assessable income in any of the relevant years. During cross-examination of the applicant, some confusion existed as to the amounts carried forward from year to year as the written down value of the new car. For the sake of completeness, it should be said that it is clear that in the 1990 year the $1000 purchase price of the standard road car was incorporated in the costs of the new partially constructed racing car. In the 1989 year the $1000 was shown as a separate asset. Given the evidence of the destruction, copying and minimal incorporation of parts of the standard road car it would appear that this accounting treatment was appropriate. Given no argument on the quantum of other deductions claimed, I am satisfied that the amounts claimed are deductible under either section 51(1) or section 54.



15. During the hearing, some time was spent on the question of the relevance of the registration by the applicant of a business name in February 1989. From that date, bank accounts, records, etc were maintained in that business name. Whilst the use of that business name may be a factor among many others in determining whether a business is being carried on, it was not seen as a significant factor in this decision.



16. The applicant had also objected against additional tax imposed pursuant to section 223 of the Act. The amended assessments for the years ended 30 June 1989 and 1990 included culpability penalties equal to five per cent of the additional income tax payable for those years. No culpability penalty was imposed for the year ended 30 June 1991. In each year a per annum penalty was imposed to reflect the delay in payment of the tax. Whilst the applicant will be partially successful in his applications to this Tribunal, it is not unreasonable that the same rate of penalties be imposed on the income tax payable on the disallowance of the depreciation of the new racing car.



17. The decisions under review are varied to include sponsorship fees as assessable income and to allow as deductions the amounts previously disallowed with the exception of depreciation claimed on the new racing car. The effect will be to increase taxable income for the year ended 30 June 1989 by $1022 and decrease taxable incomes for the years ended 30 June 1990 and 1992 by $9797 and $6539 respectively.
