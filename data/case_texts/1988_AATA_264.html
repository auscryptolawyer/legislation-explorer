WT85/28, WT85/29 and Commissioner of Taxation [1988] AATA 264; (1988) 19 ATR 3865, 88 ATC 852 (2 September 1988)

Last Updated: 3 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Assessable income - Arrangement by unit trust to provide services of taxpayer to a company of finance brokers -Whether employed by trust or finance broker - Trust paid for services rendered by taxpayer - Whether income of trust or assessable income of taxpayer - Whether arrangement void against Commissioner - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1981, sec. 260.

Case K50 (1978) ATC 471, 489

Casuarina v FC of T (1971) ATC 4068 Mullens v FC of T (1976) ATC 4288

Patcorp v FC of T (1977) ATC 4225

Slutzkin v FC of T (1977) ATC 4076

Cridland v FC of T (1977) ATC 4538

Gulland v FC of T [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 160 CLR 55

Watson v FC of T [1985] HCA 83; (1985) 160 CLR 55

Pursuant to subsection 43(1)(a) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the Tribunal affirms the objection decision in relation to the assessments for the years of income ended 30 June 1982 and 1983.

(Sgd) P. Gerber

DEPUTY PRESIDENT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) WT85/28-29

TAXATION APPEAL'S DIVISION )

Re:

APPLICANT

And: Commissioner of Taxation

RESPONDENT

REASONS FOR DECISION

Tribunal: Dr P. Gerber (Deputy President)

Date: 2 September. 1988

1. The taxpayer in this application ("T") was for many years employed as a company executive, Accumulating a considerable amount of expertise in the area of finance, as well, as" making valuable contacts in the money market. In October 1980 he severed his ties with his employer, having conceived the idea that he could exploit his talents and his contacts to better advantage. A few days before T resigned his job as a company executive, a Unit Trust was formed - Ferrari Enterprises Unit Trust - ("Ferrari") with Fiat Pty Ltd ("Fiat") as trustee and at the same time, the Ferrari Property Trust was formed with Renault Pty Ltd ("Renault") as trustee being the sole unit holder of Ferrari. Having these various trusts in place, T said goodbye to his employer and took a well-earned holiday, a fact on which he placed considerable reliance.

2. On 3 November 1980, that is, after T had returned from his holiday, he entered into an oral contract of employment with Fiat at an agreed annual salary of $17,680. Fiat agreed to provide the usual benefits accruing to employees, such as workers' compensation cover, long service leave, etc. At or about the same time, a written agreement was executed between Fiat and a firm of mortgage and finance brokers ("Mercedes"), pursuant to which Fiat agreed to provide Mercedes with certain specified services, involving, in the main, hiring T out for a fixed charge in much the same way as other people hire dinner suits. The nature of the services and charges were set out in a schedule attached to the agreement:

"Contractor's Remuneration and how payable: The sum of ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR DOLLARS ($104.00) per day in which the Contractor shall provide the Service to the Principal payable within seven (7) days of the Contractor's Invoice to the Principal without deduction PROVIDED that where the Service shall continue on a daily basis for consecutive periods of five (5) or more days then upon the Invoice of the Contractor in respect of each successive five (5) day period payable by the Principal to the Contractor in the sum of FIVE HUNDRED AND TWENTY DOLLARS ($520.00)

The Service: On a daily basis in. accordance with the express instruction of the Principal in that regard during the continuation of this Contract for Service of a Consultant and Financial Advisor in the person of T together with or in substitution therefor such further or other expertly qualified person as shall be expressly agreed in writing between the Contractor and the Principal from time to time to advise the Principal with respect to all matters of the Principal's financial and investment business.

Contractor's Costs: The Contractor shall at its sole cost and expense absolutely be responsible for all of the costs of the Contractor in providing the services of the said T or any further or additionally expertly qualified person as expressly agreed in writing between the Contractor and the Principal from time to time and without limiting the generality thereof the Contractor shall be responsible for wages, salaries, taxation, Workers' Compensation, holiday, sick leave long service leave and all such other normal and usual costs in respect of the Contractor's employees, servants agents and independent contractors."

3. The services to be provided - in the words of T - involved -

"the preparation of financial applications on behalf of. borrowing clients to lending institutions and intermediaries, It involved liaison with finance of business or homes, inspection of properties, liaison with accountants and sometimes solicitors. Having concluded all. the necessary interviews and inspections, proposals were then completed and then presented to the relevant lending institution and involved much time being spent away from home or Mercedes' office. I make the point that it was hardly the occupation of a full-time employee in an office. Now, let me turn to the functions that were not performed by Fiat. They were the hiring and firing of staff, the writing up of financial records such as the firm's trust account for the annual order or liaison with that firm's auditors. It did not cover the drawing of cheques and payment of accounts. It did not cover the furnishing of annual returns to the Finance Brokers Supervisory Board. There was not representation of the firm at the Institute of Finance Brokers. There was not the preparation of the annual accounts. This was done by the firm's accountants, and there was not the preparation of income tax returns. All of the above functions could be expected to have been performed by a firm's accountants, and there was not the preparation of income tax returns. All of the above functions could be expected to have been performed by a firm's employee, such as an accountant or financial controller or administrator None of these was performed by Fiat. Furthermore, and I must stress this, the hours of attendance at the office were flexible.

There was no set nine-to-five routine. The main criteria of attendance was to ensure that the consulting work be handled as expeditiously as possible."

4. It was conceded that Fiat would occasionally provide similar services to other companies, although T's absences on these occasions did not affect the remuneration received from Mercedes. Again, it was conceded that clients of Mercedes, in their, dealings with that firm, would be. unaware that, when T became involved in their, dealings he was, in fact, employed by Fiat.

5. In the 1982 and 1983 tax years, the Commissioner treated the income - returned as management fees derived by Fiat as trustee for Ferrari - as the. assessable income of T. He also submitted that the agreement between Fiat and Mercedes was void for illegality in reliance on sections 26(1) and 28(c) of the Finance Brokers Control Act 1975 (WA) which provide:

"26(1)' On and after the appointed day a person shall not carry on business, or by any means hold himself or itself out, as a finance broker unless he or it is licensed as such under this Act and holds a. current annual certificate in respect of the licence.

28(c) where the firm is constituted by not more than 3 persons at least one of them (shall be) licensed or where the firm is constituted by more that 3 persons at least 2 of them (shall be) licensed, and in either case the person in bona fide control of the business operated under the licence is licensed."

6. What in fact occurred was that the trustee of Ferrari decided in the, relevant years, that the net income of the trust be set aside and applied 100% to the sole unit, holder Renault Pty Ltd, the trustee of the Ferrari Property Trust. That trustee, in turn, resolved that in each of the two years now before the Tribunal, the three children of T would receive $1040 each, the balance to be paid to T.

7. Dealing with the "illegality" argument, I have not been persuaded by the Crown that T held himself out as a finance broker and/or committed any offence under the Finance Brokers Control Act (WA) and, even if he did, that this could afford the respondent any comfort.

8. On the other hand, I have no doubt that T was seen by the outside world as an employee of Mercedes, and no persuasive reason has. been advanced why, as against the Commissioner, he should be seen as something else. Furthermore, I am satisfied that the. internal arrangement inter se had as its objective purpose the reduction of T's tax. The question thus arises: is this arrangement void as against the Commissioner, or is it saved by the "choice principle" as discovered and explained by the High Court, which was generally believed to provide that if a scheme or arrangement can be attached to Sme other provision of the Act, it cannot be annihilated by recourse to sec. 260?

9. Section 260 has bedevilled the law almost from the time of its enactment. A decade ago, in Case K50 78 ATC 471, 489, I was moved to observe that whilst the provision might once have been intended to cover the whole body of deliberate tax avoidance, after being washed by Casuarina 71 ATC- 4068, Mullens 76 ATC 4288, Slutzkin 77 ATC 4076, Patcorp 77 ATC 4225 and. Cridland 77 ATC 4538, the fabric bad shrunk to an extent where there was not enough cloth left to protect Michelangelo's David from a charge of indecent exposure. It is fair comment to observe that sec. 260 had, by the end of the 70s, become the Adam and Even of the Income Tax Assessment Act, in the sense that there were the creationists who believed in the literal interpretation of the section and worked hard and tythed, albeit at 60 cents in the dollar. Dogmatically opposed to these fundamentalists stood the afisealopalians who regarded sec. 260 as merely symbolic and neither spun nor toiled. Instead, they worshipped the "choice principle" and hitched their wagons to the silk merchants who went forth into the world opening new trade routes and erecting shrines to this art nouveau on exotic islands.

10. And behold, there came Three Wise Men from the East called Gulland, Watson and Pincus who steered a narrow course between the Scylla of Newton and the Charybdis of Slutzkin with jurisprudential seamanship denied to lesser mariners. As I read the various views expressed by their Honours of the majority, it seems that section 260 does not provide a taxpayer with the choice of entering into an arrangement which has the effect of diverting income derived by him to another by resorting to a trust or other device, such as a contract of agency.

11. Applied to the instant case, I find that this taxpayer sought. by way of a discretionary trust, to divert income, derived from personal exertion, to his family. In the years now under review, this was achieved by effecting the maximum tax-free distribution to his three children. It was a modest and respectable arrangement, but nevertheless a scheme designed to alter the incidence of tax and - post-Gulland - is henceforth void as against the Commissioner. Furthermore, as I understand their Honours of the majority, such an arrangement is not saved by reference to "ordinary business or family dealings". Nor is there any magic in the existence of an antecedent transaction between the parties. Thus an arrangement will be caught by sec. 260 if it alters the incidence of tax "in a case in which the only relevant antecedent circumstance is that the taxpayer is in receipt of income." per Gibbs CJ at p.73.

12. In the result, I am satisfied, that the purpose of the arrangement resorted to in this case was to divert income, derived by T, for the benefit of members of his family in order to avoid tax on the income thus diverted, income which would otherwise have been subject to tax if it had first been derived by him and then applied for the benefit of his family. Post-Gulland, sec 260 operates to annihilate such an arrangement. It follows that so much of the income returned as trust income forms part of the assessable income of the taxpayer.

13. For the above reasons, the Tribunal will affirm the objection decision under review.
