Re Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v Tully Co-Operative Sugar Milling Association Limited [1983] FCA 155; (1983) 68 FLR 39 (25 July 1983)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

And: TULLY CO-OPERATIVE SUGAR MILLING ASSOCIATION LIMITED [1983] FCA 155; (1983) 68 FLR 39

No. G101 of 1982

Income Tax

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

QUEENSLAND REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Fox(1), Lockhart(2) and Fitzgerald(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Investment Allowance - Deduction for Expenditure in respect of Construction of New Unit of Property - Meaning of "unit of property" - Whether unit Constructed by Taxpayer - Meaning of "construction", "incurred" - Whether expenditure incurred on or after particular date.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, sections 51, 53H, 54, 55, 56 57AA, 57AC, 57AE, 57AG, 57AH, 57AJ, 58, 62AA, 62AB, 75B, 82AA, 82AB, 83AE, 82AF, 82AQ.

Income Tax - Allowable deductions - Investment allowance - Meaning of "eligible units of property" - Whether improvement to sugar mill units of property - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), ss 82AA, 82AB. The taxpayer conducted a large sugar mill. In the process of upgrading its mill, it expended some $3,181,157. It claimed a deduction for this amount as an investment allowance under s. 82AB of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), and the Commissioner disallowed the claim as to $2,439,914. The expenditure was on a mixed juice pumping station, two crushing mills and juice heaters and effet vessels. The taxpayer appealed to the Supreme Court of Queensland, which upheld most of the taxpayer's claims. The Commissioner appealed.

Held: that the appeal be dismissed. For an allowable claim, each item must be a unit of property. Whether an item is a unit of property is tested by its function. A unit will usually be capable of operating separately and have a separate existence, although it may not necessarily be self-contained. (Ready Mixed Concrete (Vic.) Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1969] HCA 12; (1969) 118 CLR 177; Wangaratta Woollen Mills Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1969] HCA 39; (1969) 119 CLR 1.)

There was no reason to disturb the trial judge's findings on the facts as to which items were "eligible units of property" within s. 82AB. (The question of whether certain of the expenditure on these items had been incurred on or after 1 January 1976 was remitted to the Supreme Court for determination.)

HEARING

Brisbane, 1983, February 9, 10; July 25. 25:7:1983

APPEAL.

Appeal from the decision of Thomas J. of the Supreme Court of Queensland allowing in part the taxpayer's appeal against the disallowance by the Commissioner of its objection to an assessment in respect of the year of income ended 30th April 1977.

M. Morley Q.C. and I. Diehm, for the appellant.

I. Gzell Q.C. and K. Cullinane, for the respondent.

Cur. adv. vult.

Solicitor for the appellant: B.J. O'Donovan, Commonwealth Crown Solicitor.

Solicitors for the respondent: Flower & Hart.

J.H.T.

ORDER

1. The question whether the relevant expenditure in respect of construction was incurred on or after 1 January 1976 or what part thereof was so incurred be remitted for determination to the Supreme Court; the Supreme Court may make such modifications to the orders already made by it including the order for costs as may be necessary in the light of such determination. 2. Otherwise the appeal be dismissed.

3. The appellant pay the respondent's costs of the appeal. Appeal dismissed.

DECISION

This is an appeal from a decision of the Supreme Court of Queensland (Thomas J.) respecting an objection to an assessment of taxpayer's income tax for the year (i.e. the taxpayer's income tax year) ending 30 April 1977. His Honour allowed the objection in part and the Commissioner of Taxation has appealed.

The respondent conducts a large sugar mill in Queensland. In 1976 and 1977 it carried out extensive alterations or additions to its plant and it claimed to have expended on them during the period 1 January 1976 to 30 April 1977 an amount of $3,181,517. By the time the appeal came on for hearing before Thomas J. the Commissioner had allowed a deduction of $741,603 of this amount, leaving a balance of $2,439,914.

The taxpayer called the only witnesses and their evidence was accepted by the Judge. No evidence was called for the Commissioner. The statements of fact of the learned Judge respecting relevant matters have not been challenged. He outlined the more basic facts as follows:

"Before 1976, the milling train consisted of some six mills. The end result of the up-grading was a conversion to five mills and the replacement or up-grading of various component sections of the milling process.

The system involves the processing and reprocessing of cane through the various mills. Juice is thereby extracted which finds its way into the various juice tanks. Ultimately the clarified juice is pumped to two effet sets, which operate in parallel with one another. The effet station increases the sugar content of the juice by evaporating water from the solution. The product from the effet station, now called liquor or syrup, is pumped to storage tanks. There is then a process of crystallization of sugars and the production of other products at the pan station. The overall scheme of things is much more complex than the primary course which I have described. It includes a re-processing of by-products which are collected along the way. These include recovery of the eventual fibres which are used to fuel the mill itself: and the collection of 'mud' which is then reprocessed so as to extract sugar bearing liquids which it still contains.

The relevant expenditure related to equipment at various stages of the processing, as can be seen from the following summary.

A. Mixed juice pumping

station - $ 59,226.50

B. First crushing mill - 886,649.30

C. Third crushing mill

(now called the number - 998,658.12

5 mill)

D. Mud filter station - 442,282.23

E. Juice heaters and

effet vessel - 814,701.04."

The claim for deduction was made in reliance upon sections 82AA and 82AB of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). Section 82AA is in part as follows:

"82AA. Subject to the following provisions of this Subdivision, this Subdivision applies in relation to a unit of eligible property acquired or constructed by the taxpayer that is -

(a) in the case of any taxpayer, for the use by the taxpayer wholly and exclusively -

(i) in Australia: and

(ii) for the purpose of producing assessable income otherwise than by -

(A) the leasing of the eligible property;

(B) the letting of the eligible property on hire under a hire-purchase agreement; or

(C) the granting to other persons of rights to use the eligible property;

. . ."

Sub-section (1) of s.82AB is as follows:

"82AB. (1) Subject to this subdivision, where -

(a) on or after 1 January 1976, a taxpayer has incurred expenditure of a capital nature (in this section referred to as 'eligible expenditure') in respect of the acquisition or construction by him of a new unit of eligible property in relation to which this Subdivision applies:

(b) the eligible expenditure exceeded $500:

(c) the eligible expenditure was incurred -

(i) in respect of a unit of property acquired by the taxpayer under a contract entered into on or after 1 January 1976 and before 1 July 1985: or

(ii) in respect of a unit of property that was constructed by the taxpayer and the construction of which commenced on or after 1 January 1976 and before 1 July 1985; and

(d) the unit of property was first used or installed ready for use before 1 July 1986.

there shall be allowed as a deduction from the taxpayer's assessable income of the first year of income during which that unit was either used for the purpose of producing assessable income, or installed ready for use for that purpose, an amount (in this section referred to as the 'relevant amount') ascertained in accordance with the following provisions of this section."

Some definitions in s.82AQ(1) of the Act are relevant:

"'construction' includes manufacture and 'constructed' has a corresponding meaning;

'eligible property' means plant or articles within the meaning of section 54 and includes earth tanks constructed for the purpose of conserving water for use in carrying on a business of primary production;

'new' means not having previously been either used by any person or acquired or held by any person for use by that person, but does not include reconditioned or wholly or mainly reconstructed."

Paragraphs (a) and (b) of s.82AQ(3) are also applicable:

"(3) In this Subdivision -

(a) a reference to the acquisition of property by a person is a reference to -

(i) the person becoming the owner of the property or taking the property on hire under a hire-purchase agreement; or

(ii) the construction of the property for the person by another person or other persons on premises of the first-mentioned person:

(b) a reference to property being installed ready for use is a reference to property being installed ready for use and held in reserve:

The claim, in relation to each of the particulars "A" to "E" set out above in the quotation from his Honour's judgment was made in reliance upon s.82AB(1)(c)(ii), that is to say, on the basis that the capital expenditure was in relation to the "construction" of the units of property. The alternative, of "acquisition", was not relied upon. The learned Judge disallowed the objection in respect of the used filter station, and there is no need to consider the position in relation to it. This appeal relates to the other four items.

The grounds of appeal are that the four items I have mentioned were not "units of property", that they were not "constructed" by the taxpayer, and that the relevant expenditure was not "eligible expenditure" in that it was not incurred on or after 1 January 1976, as required by the section. No question arises as to whether particular expenditure was incurred in the income tax year (1 May 1976 to 30 April 1977), because the deduction is allowable in, -

". . . the first year of income during which that unit was either used for the purpose of producing assessable income, or installed ready for use for that purpose, an amount (in this section referred to as the 'relevant amount') ascertained in accordance with the following provisions of this section." (vide concluding part of s.82AB of s.82AB(1))

The Mixed Juice Pumping Station

The only expenditure disallowed with respect to this item was $16,190, which comprised $488 spent on foundations and $15,702 spent on pumps and motors. The learned Judge made findings as follows:

"The evidence establishes that all the costs respecting the foundations were indeed incurred after 1st January 1976. The foundations were necessary for the functioning of two Kelly and Lewis pumps acquired and installed at the mixed juice pumping station. The relevant equipment included two starters and two electric motors to start and drive the Kelly and Lewis pumps. These items were ordered from Crompton Parkinson (Australia) Proprietary Limited on 19th November 1975.

It is first necessary to identify what is the 'new unit of eligible property' with respect to which the taxpayer incurred expenditure. The appellant submits that the mixed juice pumping station itself comprises the relevant unit. Whilst it is plain that an electric motor, or a starter may in appropriate circumstances itself be a unit, the evidence here shows that each of these components has become an integral part of the pumping station. It may be noted that the Kelly and Lewis pumps (in respect of which expenditure has apparently been allowed) were delivered to the site with the relevant motors (in respect of which expenditure has not been allowed) attached to them. The station obviously would not function without the pumps, and the pumps obviously could not function without the motors or the starters. Two pumps are necessary because during the crushing season the milling must continue on a 24 hour basis."

A question which arises is the identification of the "unit of property", more fully described in the section as the "new unit of eligible property". Having in mind the definition of "eligible property" in s.82AQ(1) the starting point is with s.54, which deals with depreciation on plant and articles, and gives "plant" a very wide meaning. It is not disputed that the items in question here, including, for example, the concrete foundation in question, are "plant or articles".

Plainly, difficulties can arise in particular cases in determining what is a unit of property. An examination of other sections of the Act is helpful. Section 53H provides for a deduction for the cost of converting oil-fired plant. Reference is made in that context to the conversion of adaptation of a "unit of property". The term is defined for the purposes of the section as meaning plant or an article within the meaning of s.54, not being trading stock. Plainly the units there in mind are, or include, large and complex ones. Section 55 deals with depreciation in respect of "a unit of property". Section 56 relates to the calculation of depreciation and sub-section (3) provides a connection between s.82AB and other provisions giving deductions, by way of depreciation or otherwise, in respect of units of property. Section 57AA deals with special depreciation allowances on units of property in respect of which depreciation is allowable under s.54. The units include structural improvements, and structural improvements which are provided for residential accommodation. Section 57AJ allows special depreciation on storage facilities for petroleum fuel, and relates to "units of property" on which depreciation is allowable under s.54. It has been thought necessary, or desirable, to exclude (by sub-secn. (3)), inter alia, ships, a unit of railway rolling stock and a pipeline. There are many other uses of the phrase in the Act. Those which I have examined suggest that the term "unit" is not used so much to limit, or require preciseness of definition, as to distinguish between the generality and something more specific, which is capable of being separately regarded and treated. On each occasion the phrase must of course be construed in its context, but the repetition of it in the variety of contexts to which I have referred certainly suggests that it is not to be understood in s.82AB in a narrow or technical sense.

Several items, each of which at some stage could for presently relevant purposes be regarded as a unit, can be combined, or linked or associated together so as to form a larger unit. When one looks to see whether there is a unit, one normally looks to see whether there is a whole something. Whether there is a whole will normally be judged by the intended function or purpose of that which is being looked at. On the other hand, there can be a unit when some part is missing without which the larger whole cannot function in the intended way. As used in the section (and other sections) the matter is more one of identification than of structure. The provision was intended to confer a benefit on taxpayers by way of giving them a special deduction in respect of new plant acquired or constructed during a stated period, and the benefit should not be defeated by an illiberal approach. Having in mind one submission made on behalf of the Commissioner, I should state that what might otherwise be regarded as a unit, does not cease to qualify as such because it comprises one or more, or many, integers, each of which could, at some stage of the development of the whole, be regarded as a unit.

The learned Judge took the view that the whole pumping station comprised a unit, and that the items in question were part of it, indeed an essential part. He was looking at the larger whole, of which the items had become an integral part. This was essentially a finding of fact, and I see no reason to disturb it.

The Judge found that the construction (by the taxpayer) of the pumping station commenced after 1 January 1976 (s.82AB(1)(c)(ii)), and this finding has not been challenged. The result is, in my view, that the appeal under this heading fails.

The First Crushing Mill

I set out a statement of facts by the learned Judge regarding this matter:

"This mill is now called the number 2 mill, and is the second in a chain of five.

The appellant expended $866,649.00 on this mill, and the Commissioner has disallowed $648,201.00. In effect, two six foot mills were replaced with a new seven foot mill. The present five mills operate in train to the end result that the juice is extracted from the sugar cane.

I have no doubt that this mill can and does comprise a new unit for the purposes of s.82AB.

Once again the appellant submits that it has 'constructed' a new unit. It cannot claim for acquisition of units, because the relevant orders were placed with Walkers Limited on 3rd December 1974.

The equipment acquired from Walkers was substantial, and included a turbine gear box roller crusher, gearing and associated parts.

The work undertaken by the appellant in creating this mill was also very substantial. It involved the removal of two existing six foot milling units, the removing of intermediate carriers, the removal of gearing forming part of the crushing units, and removal of a low speed unit, high speed unit turbines, access platforms, staging, and pipe work. Such items had to be removed to provide access to the site before demolition of the existing foundations could commence. Once back to ground level, the area for excavation was set out, breaking of concrete and excavation proceeded, three months being involved in forming the foundations. The various components supplied by Walkers Limited were slow in arriving. Their installation was a complicated process involving levelling, tensioning, aligning and assembling. The appellant's boilermakers spent a fortnight on welding work, their employed electricians (five or six men) spent a fortnight with much overtime, and the installation of the steam piping to the turbine took at least two weeks."

Questions arise in relation to this item concerning the relationship between the terms "construction", "acquisition" and "installation" as used in the legislation.

The allowance given by the section depends upon the new plant being constructed or acquired. "Construction" includes manufacture, but is not confined to it. One can construct by building or assembling, and it plainly accords with the policy of the section, as with sections in similar terms, that this can be done by a contractor for the taxpayer, as well as by its employees. It is intended, in my view, that the term "construction" should be given a wide meaning.

"Acquisition" is itself a broad term; its width in operation is sufficiently limited by the need for expenditure by the taxpayer in respect of it. It includes acquisition from a lessee (s.82AB(7)), the taking on hire under a hire-purchase agreement and the construction of the property by another person on the taxpayer's premises. In my view the last-mentioned example cannot reasonably give rise to the conclusion that construction by a contractor under a contract with the taxpayer must be treated as an acquisition. Rather it shows the intended width of the notion of acquisition, so that work done by another, if not to be regarded as construction, is included. The subdivision (Subdivision B of Division 3) does not of course have application unless there is expenditure, at some stage, in respect of the acquisition.

As I see it, the intention of the legislature is that "acquisition" and "construction" are between them intended to cover all cases, although they will commonly have application at different times in the development of a project. Problems can then arise as to whether an "acquisition" intended as part of a "construction" is a "unit", but these problems are capable of resolution, in accordance with the purpose of the legislation. If it is conceivable that there is some situation which would not come within either of the terms, the legislative intention may to that extent miscarry.

It is submitted that the legislation draws a distinction between "construction" and "acquisition" on the one hand, and "installation" on the other. If there is a new unit in respect of which the taxpayer has incurred expenditure of a capital nature, there would not seem to be any purpose or rationale in any such distinction, and in my view there is none. The purpose of the relevant reference in s.82AB, as in a number of other sections, (see s.57AC(1); s.57AE(1); s.57AG(2); s.57AH(5); s.58; s.62AA(5) and (7); s.75B(2)) is to require that that which has been acquired or constructed is at the relevant time in use or installed ready for use and held in reserve (s.82AO(3)(b)).

It is the case, as the learned Judge noted, that "installation" may not amount to "construction". This is not to say that there is a strict dichotomy between the terms. What s.82AB refers to is the unit being "installed". The passive is used, to indicate a state of affairs. The trial Judge would seen to have found that, in relation to the first and third crushing mills, construction was commenced on or after 1 January 1976. I do not understand the finding, as to time, to have been challenged before us.

In my view, for the reasons I have given, the learned Judge was correct in his conclusion that the amount in issue under this heading was deductible (subject to the relevant expenditure having been incurred on or after 1 January 1976; see later).

The Third Crushing Mill

On this matter the Judge found as follows:

"This is now called the number 5 mill. The evidence with respect to this issue is parallel to that concerning the first crushing mill and I do not think that there is any feature of sufficient importance to produce a different result. Consequently, I consider that the Commissioner was in error in disallowing $793,121.00 of this claim for deduction.

It has not been submitted that the considerations in this case are different from those respecting the First Crushing Mill, or that, given the conclusions respecting that mill, the view of the learned Judge in this case was wrong.

Juice heaters and effet vessel

His Honour was of the view that the juice heaters and the effet vessels could be regarded separately as units. In relation to the former, his Honour said:

"The installation of the juice heaters involved work by Walkers and also by the mill. Walkers initially performed the majority of pipe work associated with the heaters. Later additional pipe work was performed with the mill supplying materials and Walkers supplying the labour. The mill's work involved the design installation and foundation to support the staging on which the heaters were eventually mounted; the purchase and installation of instrumentation to control the system, particularly with respect to temperature control; and the purchase and installation of valve work associated with the steam pipe work. The work carried out by the mill cost approximately $187,000.00. The foundations prepared by the mill did not serve any function in relation to the structure of the mill building, and were for the sole purpose of the construction of the new juice heaters.

In my view the overall work was organised and effected by the appellant. It matters not that relatively substantial sub-contract work was obtained from third parties.

I also find that the appellant commenced its construction work after 1st January 1976.

I am prepared to declare that to the extent to which the assessment disallows deductions in respect of expenditure incurred in the construction of the juice heaters, the assessment was in error."

There was a problem in apportionment of cost which was later resolved by agreement between the parties.

There has been no challenge to the findings of fact of the learned Judge, and what I have already said supports the conclusion reached by him.

In relation to the effet vessels, his Honour said that he was not satisfied that their construction had been commenced on or after 1 January 1976, and that the objection in respect of them should be disallowed. There has not been any challenge to this conclusion.

Eligible Expenditure

Section 82AB(1)(a) requires the expenditure, that is to say, the expenditure in respect of construction, to have been incurred on or after 1 January 1976. It is submitted that, with some exceptions, the taxpayer did not satisfy the onus of proof in this regard. The exceptions are expenditures of the following amounts:

(a) $488.00 in relation to the mixed juice pumping station:

(b) $35,085.00 in respect of the first crushing mill:

(c) $36,485.00 in relation to the third crushing mill: and

(d) $9,884.00 in relation to the juice heaters.

Although the onus did lie on the taxpayer, it appears that there was some justification for its counsel to believe, at the hearing, that expenditure on or after 1 January 1976 was not in issue. It is significant that the learned Judge did not deal with the matter in his reasons for judgment. Counsel for the Commissioner has said, however, that in the circumstances, if it remains relevant, the taxpayer should have an opportunity of proving the matter fully. This will involve referring the issue to the trial Court for further hearing, and determination. This is the course which it seems to me should be followed.

The meaning of "incurred" in s.82AB was discussed before us. It is a term which is very familiar from its place in s.51 of the Act, and in my opinion it should have the meaning in s.82AB that it has in s.51. This meaning has been authoritatively dealt with in New Zealand Flax Investments Limited v. The Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1938] HCA 60; (1939) 61 C.L.R. 179, Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. James Flood Pty. Limited [1953] HCA 65; (1953) 88 C.L.R. 492, and Nilsen Development Laboratories Pty. Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 6; (1980-1981) 144 C.L.R. 616.

I am of the view therefore that the appeal fails on the matters raised by the Commissioner on all points except this last matter, and that in relation to that, the matter should be remitted to the Court at first instance for determination. The Commissioner should pay the taxpayer's costs of this appeal.

I have read the reasons for judgment of Fox J. and agree with them. I would add some remarks of my own.

The expression "a unit of property" is central to this case. It appears in Subdivision B of Division 3 of Part III of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Assessment Act") which comprises ss. 82AA to 82AQ and was inserted by Act No. 50 of 1976. My references to sections of the Assessment Act are generally to the form which they took during the relevant year of income namely, the year ended 30 April 1977. The subdivision provides a deduction in the form of a special investment allowance in respect of capital expenditure incurred by a taxpayer on "a new unit of eligible property" on or after 1 January 1976. Section 82AA provides that, subject to the other provisions of Subdivision B, the subdivision applies in relation to "a unit of eligible property" (an expression defined in s.82AQ) acquired or constructed by the taxpayer that is for use by the taxpayer wholly and exclusively in Australia for the purpose of producing assessable income otherwise than by leasing, letting on hire purchase, or granting rights to use the property.

The operative provision of the subdivision is s. 82AB. It allows a deduction in respect of capital expenditure exceeding $500.00 incurred by a taxpayer on or after 1 January 1976 in respect of the acquisition or construction by him of "a new unit of eligible property" provided the requirements of s.82AA are satisfied. If acquired, the property must be acquired under a contract entered into on or after 1 January 1976 and before 1 July 1985. If constructed, the construction of the property must have commenced on or after 1 January 1976 and before 1 July 1985. Whether acquired or constructed, the property must be first used or installed ready for use before 1 July 1986. These references to dates are, for convenience, to the Assessment Act in its present form.

Section 82AE excludes structural improvements from the operation of subdivision B other than certain specified exceptions which are, in the main, structural improvements on land used for the purpose of carrying on a business of primary production, for example, certain fences, dams, underground water pipes, and buildings or other structural improvements for the storage of grain, hay or fodder.

Section 82AF specifies property that does not qualify for the investment allowance including certain appliances of a kind ordinarily used for household purposes, motor vehicles, paintings, books, films, musical instruments, wharves, jetties or wearing apparel.

The expression "a unit of eligible property" is used in other provisions of subdivision B, for example, s. 82AA. The words "eligible property" are defined by sub-s. 82AQ (1) as meaning:-

". . . plant or articles within the meaning of section 54 and includes earth tanks constructed for the purpose of conserving water for use in carrying on a business of primary production;"

This definition thus provides a bridge between the depreciation provisions and Subdivision B relating to investment allowances. The expression "unit of property" appears in the depreciation provisions, for example, ss. 55 and 56 relating to the basis and calculation of depreciation of a "unit of property". Section 57AA provides for a special depreciation allowance to primary producers for "units of property" to which that section applies, for example, structural improvements for agricultural pursuits and structural improvements provided for residential accommodation. The expression "unit of property" appears in s. 62AA in respect of the manufacturers' investment allowance and in s. 62AB which provides a special deduction for primary producers who invest in certain units of property. The Legislature thought it necessary or desirable to exclude from s. 62AB items as diverse as buildings, wharves, fences, dams, wireless receivers and transmitters, cooking appliances, anchors, batteries and tents.

The large number of eclectic and diverse items capable of being "units of property" or "units of eligible property" which vary so considerably as to type, size and function and the fact that it is the intention of the Legislature to confer benefits on taxpayers by giving them a special deduction in respect of new plant, all point to the conclusion that in construing the undefined expression "unit of eligible property" a wide not a narrow approach is called for.

The Dictionaries provide some help in determining the meaning of the word "unit", but the variety of meanings given by them to it show the impossibility of assigning it an inflexible or precise meaning and likewise the larger expression "a unit of eligible property". Collins Dictionary of the English Language, Australian edition edited by G.A. Wilkes, Sydney, 1979, contains the most relevant definition of "unit" namely:-

". . . 1. a single undivided entity or whole. 2. any group or individual, esp. when regarded as a basic element of a larger whole. 3. a mechanical part or integrated assembly of parts that performs a subsidiary function: a filter unit. 4. a complete system, apparatus, or establishment that performs a specific function: a production unit . . . "

Helpful though the dictionaries are they do not provide much practical guidance in determining the meaning of the expression "unit of eligible property" for present purposes. It is true that ultimately the question what constitutes "a unit of eligible property" depends on the facts of the particular case, but some guidance to the Commissioner and taxpayers is called for. In my view, the nearest one can get to enunciating a test of fairly general application is that it is the function or purpose of the particular item to which one looks to see if it answers the description on the facts of the case of "a unit of eligible property". It is not necessary that it be functionally operative though in many circumstances this may be called for. For example, if five parts are installed in an assembly line and all that is needed to render the line operative is a sixth part, but until that part is installed no part may function or operate, the functional incompleteness does not necessarily deprive each of the five units of its character as "a unit of eligible property" for the purposes of the Assessment Act. It depends on the facts of the case. Yet, at other times a "unit" may not come into being until all the components have been assembled. For example, a farm fence is made up of a number of posts and rails or wires. It is difficult to conceive of any "unit" coming into being until the fence is erected. A concrete mixer installed on the back of a truck was held by Kitto J. in Readymixed Concrete (Vic.) Pty. Limited v. The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1969] HCA 12; (1969) 118 C.L.R. 177 to be a "unit of property". His Honour said (at p. 184):-

"Notwithstanding the mode and degree of annexation, the truck and the mixer are functionally separate and independent units of property. The function of delivery belongs to the truck. The use of the mixer is for mixing, as a step in the production of concrete in the condition required for pouring, and its nature is understated to the point of misdescription by saying that the machine - for that is what it is - is ordinarily used for delivery."

See also Wangaratta Woollen Mills Limited v. The Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1969] HCA 39; (1969) 119 C.L.R. 1.

The difficulty of identifying a "unit of property" for the purposes of the Assessment Act is that sometimes an item may be correctly described as a "unit" when it is one of a number of parts which upon assembly perform a subsidiary function. Sometimes each part may be correctly described as a unit before assembly and at other times after assembly. On other occasions there may not be a unit until a number of parts have been integrated into a complete system. Then the whole may answer the description of a unit. The possibilities and combinations are numerous. But purpose or function must generally be a useful guide to the identification of an item as answering the description of a unit of property in particular cases.

The learned primary Judge held that the whole mixed juice pumping station, including foundations, pumps and motors, was a unit of eligible property and that the components in question were part of it. His finding was one of fact and no grounds have been established to disturb it.

I agree with the orders proposed by Fox J.

This is an appeal by the Commissioner of Taxation from a decision of a single judge of the Supreme Court of Queensland. The respondent-taxpayer owns and operates a sugar mill at Tully in North Queensland. The Supreme Court held that certain capital expenditures by the taxpayer were allowable deductions under sub-s. 82AB(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act").

Subject to other requirements, a deduction is allowable under sub-s. 82AB(1) if a new unit of eligible property in relation to which Subdivision B of Division 3 of the Act applies is either acquired or constructed by a taxpayer. By sub-s. 82AQ(1) "construction" includes manufacture. By sub-s. 82AQ(3) the construction of property for a taxpayer by another person or persons on the taxpayer's land is an "acquisition" by the taxpayer.

As part of the modernisation of its sugar mill the taxpayer dismantled and removed entire stages of its milling process, even to the extent of removing the relevant foundations. In respect of each section, it purchased new plant and equipment, excavated and laid new foundations, installed and connected up the new plant and equipment, and carried out associated work. The work was performed under the taxpayer's supervision and direction by its own employees, tradesmen whom it engaged, and suppliers of the plant and equipment. The primary judge upheld the taxpayer's contention that the expenditures in respect of which deductions were claimed were incurred by it in the construction by it of new units of eligible property.

The first ground argued by the Commissioner was that the plant and equipment purchased by the taxpayer consisted of new units of eligible property which were acquired by the taxpayer and that, therefore, the deductibility of the expenditure incurred by the taxpayer in respect of the plant and equipment fell to be determinded on the basis of acquisition, not construction. In my opinion, given the correctness of the first limb of the Commissioner's propostion, the second limb does not follow. Neither the concepts involved in, nor the terminology adopted by, the relevant statutory provisions justify the conclusion that, in any given situation, it will be possible to identify but a single unit of eligible property which has been either constructed or acquired in one particular way or another. On the contrary, it seems to me plain that the task imposed by the subsection will often, perhaps more often than not, be to determine what is, in a particular fact situation, the appropriate characterization of what has occurred. Further, although the subsection speaks of construction or acquisition, there may be cases in which the proper conclusion is that the unit of eligible property, by reference to which a claim to a deduction is to be considered, was in part constructed and in part acquired. It is sometimes said that legislation intended to confer a benefit should be construed beneficially: see, e.g. PAP Printers and Publishers Pty Ltd v. Minister for Business and Consumer Affairs (1982) 40 A.L.R. 377, 399. It is well recognized that, where appropriate, "or" may mean "and". It is increasingly accepted, quite apart from statutory intervention, that undue literalism is to be avoided in the search for the legislative intent: see, e.g. Tickle Industries Pty Ltd v. Hann [1974] HCA 5; (1973) 130 C.L.R. 321. I see no reason to doubt that, in such a case as I have described, the Act often could and should be applied separately in relation to the acquisition expenditure and the construction expenditure. However, that does not mean that there will not be cases in which it is proper to consider that there is but a single unit of property constructed by the taxpayer although there have been purchases by the taxpayer of plant equipment or materials which have been incorporated into that unit. It may be that a similar result in such cases would arise from a liberal application of the phrase "in respect of" to which further reference is made below. Expenditure "in respect of" construction may in appropriate cases include costs of purchases, and expenditures "in respect of" acquisition might conceivably include costs other than the purchase price, including perhaps delivery and installation expenses.

In the present case, the judge recognized that questions of fact and degree were involved. He concluded, with the assistance of uncontroverted expert evidence, that the relevant units of eligible property were the discrete sections of the milling system and that each had been constructed by the taxpayer. He was clearly entitled, in my opinion, to conclude that the sections of the milling process constituted units of property. Without seeking to provide an exhaustive definition, I see no reason to doubt that there is, for present purposes, a unit of property if it is capable of independent existence, not necessarily self-contained, e.g. it may require power from an external source, not necessarily separately used, e.g. it may be incorporated into an operating system such as a machine or complex of machinery in a manufacturing process, but capable either of separate function, or of function in conjunction with different parts, or in a different context, from its current user. See the definition of "unit" in the Oxford Dictionary; cf Ready-mix Concrete Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1969] HCA 12; (1968) 118 C.L.R. 177 and Wangaratta Woollen Mills v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1969] HCA 39; (1969) 119 C.L.R. 1. It should, perhaps, be recorded that it was not submitted that any of the property in respect of which expenditure was incurred was not eligible property.

Insofar as the case for the Commissioner questioned the primary judge's conclusion that the units were constructed by the taxpayer I desire to say no more than that there was evidence upon which that view was open. Such limited analyses of the evidence as were undertaken before us did not persuade me that this Court should substitute its own different factual conclusion. Certainly, I do not accept that a conclusion that a unit of property was constructed by the taxpayer within the meaning of the legislation is excluded because parts of what were incorporated into a unit of property were manufactured by and purchased from other persons, or because part of the work of installation and erection etc. was performed by persons other than employees of the taxpayer.

The Commissioner's other argument was that the expenditures were not incurred on or after 1 January 1976. The debate in respect of this argument centred upon when, for relevant purposes, expenditure is incurred. The parties agreed that, perhaps due to a misunderstanding, the matter of whether the expenditures were incurred on or after January 1976 may not have been adequately litigated at first instance. Indeed, in my opinion, there are other aspects of the taxpayer's claims which do not seem to have been fully ventilated in the Supreme Court or indeed before us, as I understood the rival submissions.

In order to be deductible on the hypothesis advanced by the taxpayer and accepted below, expenditure must have been incurred in respect of the construction of the relevant new unit of eligible property. The phrase "in respect of" is capable of describing relationships over a wide range of degrees of proximity; the meaning to be attributed to it on a particular occasion depends on the context: State Government Insurance Office (Qld) v. Rees [1979] HCA 52; (1979) 144 C.L.R. 549, 553-554, 560-561. Further, the expenditure must have been incurred on or after 1 January 1976 and the construction must have been commenced on or after that date. The primary judge held, without elaboration, that the purchase prices of the plant and equipment were expenditures incurred in respect of the construction of the units of eligible property, and that such expenditures were incurred on or after 1 January 1976, although contracts to acquire the plant and equipment were entered into and perhaps other steps were taken prior to 1 January 1976. He also, however, held that the construction did not commence until after 1 January 1976 solely because he found that the foundations were not started until after that date. That finding assumed, without deciding, that the construction of each unit was confined to the work after that date; i.e. the foundations, the installation, erection, connection of the plant and equipment, and the associated work. Whilst the two conclusions in question may not be necessarily incompatible, they serve to emphasise the inter-related temporal questions raised by the Act.

It is by no means clear to me that the questions of when construction was commenced, what expenses were incurred in respect of construction, and when those expenses were incurred do not bear upon each other or can generally be considered in isolation from one another. Questions of when construction has been commenced have been elsewhere considered, often, but not always, in connection with whether or not there has been a substantial commencement of construction: see, e.g. Owendale Pty Ltd v. Anthony [1967] HCA 52; (1967) 117 C.L.R. 539; North Sydney Municipal Council v. Middle Harbour Investments Pty Ltd (1963) 81 W.N. (N.S.W.) 35; Parramatta City Council v. Industrial Acceptance Corp Ltd (1978) 1 N.S.W.L.R. 698; and Dav v. Pinglen Pty Ltd [1981] HCA 23; (1981) 55 A.L.J.R. 416. Of course, the different context in which the question of commencement of construction presently falls for consideration would have to be brought into account in any attempt to derive assistance from such cases. I desire to say no more here than that, in the context of conclusions that the taxpayer constructed units of property consisting of separate entire sections of the milling system, it seems to me that the questions of when the construction was commenced, of what expenditures were incurred in respect of construction, and of when those expenditures were incurred may have become inappropriately disassociated.

The taxpayer submitted that expenditure is incurred only when paid, relating this submission to the fact that sub-s. 82AB(1) is concerned with capital and not with revenue expenditure and seeking to place emphasis upon the dictionary definition of an expenditure. Whatever difference it may make that capital expenditure is presently material, it does not lead me to accept that submission for the taxpayer. I am unable to distinguish in any significant way between the reference to "outgoings incurred" in s.51 of the Act and the reference in sub-s.82AB(1) to "expenditure incurred", whether literally, conceptually, or by contrast, and I consider that the cases in relation to s.51 afford a useful guide as to when expenditure is incurred for present purposes: see, e.g. Nilsen Development Laboratories Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 6; (1981) 144 C.L.R. 616 and cases cited.

As is acknowledged by the parties, the factual issues relative to when the expenditures were incurred on this approach were not litigated. Further, I doubt whether, in the circumstances, the associated questions of when the construction was commenced and whether the expenditures were incurred in respect of construction were fully canvassed.

I am in agreement that the matter must be remitted to the Supreme Court. I would prefer to see the order of this Court formulated differently from what is proposed but no purpose would be served by elaborating.
