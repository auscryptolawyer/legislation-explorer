Re Fay Olive Scott and Commissioner of Superannuation [1986] AATA 135 (15 May 1986)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: FAY OLIVE SCOTT

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. Q85/107

AAT No. 2675

Superannuation - Practice & Procedure

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

I.R. Thompson (Deputy President)

F.A. Pascoe (Member)

Dr N.C. Davis (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - widow's entitlement - applicant widow and deceased contributor separated 6 months prior to death - whether applicant 'spouse' - whether applicant 'wholly or substantially dependent' on deceased at time of death - obligation to maintain spouse pursuant to Family Law Act - effect of registered maintenance agreement entered into by applicant providing for property distribution and forgoing future maintenance claims - legal right to support even though unfulfilled may still create dependency - circumstances surrounding signing of property agreement - intention of applicant to seek orders of court once emotions had cooled - relevant factors when Family Court orders maintenance to be paid - ability of applicant to maintain herself - reliance on social welfare benefits - relationship between spouse support and social welfare payments after separation - dependency a general concept not related to earnings alone - whole relationship to be looked at as at date of death to determine dependency - conduct of deceased pre- and post separation regarding monetary support - degree of dependency after separation - Re Schlatter considered and followed

Practice & Procedure - whether Tribunal should reconsider own decisions - inconsistency in decision-making undesirable - previous decision decided after full argument and full consideration of issues - decision should be followed unless basis of earlier reasoning faulty

Words and Phrases: "spouse" "wholly and substantially dependent"

HEARING

CANBERRA

15:5:1986

ORDER

The decision under review is set aside and the matter is remitted to the Commissioner for Superannuation for reconsideration in accordance with a direction that the applicant is entitled to a spouse's pension under the provisions of section 81 of the Superannuation Act 1976.

DECISION

Darrel Leslie John Scott was an eligible employee for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") and contributed to the Superannuation Fund established by section 40 of that Act. In 1964 he married the applicant and there were two children of the marriage. However, in April 1982 they separated and thereafter lived apart. On or about 11 October 1982 Mr Scott died. On 28 October 1982 the applicant made written application for spouse's benefit to be paid to her in accordance with section 81 of the Act. On 28 February 1983 a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner") rejected her application on the ground that she was not a spouse as defined in section 3(1) of the Act. Her solicitors sought on her behalf reconsideration of the delegate's decision. On 4 June 1985 the Commissioner informed the solicitors that he had affirmed the decision. The applicant then applied to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal under section 154(6) of the Act for review of the decision which the Commissioner had confirmed. As the applicant was regarded by the Commissioner as not being a spouse for the purposes of the Act, he decided that an orphan pension was payable under section 97 in respect of the two children. At the hearing of the application for review the Tribunal was informed that that benefit was currently being paid.

2. Section 81 of the Act provides that, where an eligible employee dies before attaining his maximum retiring age and is survived by a spouse, the spouse is entitled to a spouse's benefit. It is not in dispute that Mr Scott died before attaining the maximum retiring age. What is in issue is whether the applicant is a spouse. "Spouse" is defined in section 3 of the Act. It is not necessary for the purposes of this statement of the reasons for our decision to set out the whole of the definition. The only part of it which may be applicable to the applicant is the following:-

"'spouse', in relation to a person who has died and

was, at the time of his death, an eligible employee

or a retirement pensioner, means -

. . . . .

(b) a person who was legally married to the

deceased person at the time of the person's

death but who was not living with the

person on a permanent and bona fide

domestic basis at that time, and who, in

the opinion of the Commissioner, was wholly

or substantially dependent upon the

deceased person at that time;"

3. It is not in dispute that at the time of Mr Scott's death the applicant was legally married to him but was not living with him on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis. By virtue section 43(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") the Tribunal, when reviewing the decision of the Commissioner's delegate, has all the powers and discretions conferred on the Commissioner by the Superannuation Act 1976. What has to be determined, therefore, is whether in our opinion the applicant was wholly or substantially dependent upon Mr Scott at the time of his death.

4. At the hearing of the application the Tribunal had in evidence the documents lodged by the respondent with the Tribunal in pursuance of section 37 of the AAT Act and four other documents tendered in the course of the hearing. Oral evidence was given by the applicant, her solicitor, Mr W.B. Andrews, her mother, Mrs E.N. Golinski, and two officers of the Australian Postal Commission, Mr L.T. McKenzie and Mr R.J. Forster, the Divisional Manager for Brisbane North. Mr McKenzie was Mr Scott's supervisor for some years and Mr Forster was acquainted with Mr Scott's career in the Australian Post Office and the Australian Postal Commission and with the problems which he had encountered in that career.

5. On the basis of the documents tendered under section 37 of the AAT Act and the evidence of the two officers of the Australian Postal Commission we are satisfied that the applicant was a Postal Officer Grade 3 throughout the whole of 1982 until his death, that he was not then eligible for promotion, that, because of unsatisfactory aspects of his behaviour in his employment, his prospects of promotion were nil, that at the time both of his separation from the applicant and of his death his salary was $15,120 per annum and that, if he had not died and he had continued to be employed in the Commission without promotion his salary now would have been between $16,000 and $17,000.

6. A number of the documents lodged with the Tribunal by the respondent contained statements about Mr Scott's behaviour towards the applicant and his children and towards his brother. Section 3(2) of the Act provides that a person who would but for absence resulting from illness have been living with another person at any time on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis is to be deemed, for the purposes of the definition of "spouse" in section 3(1), to have been living with that other person on a permanent and bona fide domestic basis during that absence. Consequently, the Commissioner made enquiries to ascertain whether or not Mr Scott was suffering from mental illness. However, the general practitioner who attended him, Dr Miller, wrote that in his opinion "the marital separation was based upon personal differences; not medical disorder". The Commissioner concluded that there was no evidence of mental illness. At the hearing counsel for the applicant, Mr Westbrook, informed the Tribunal that the applicant did not seek to assert that Mr Scott was suffering from mental illness or that the provisions of section 3(2) of the Act were applicable in her case.

7. On 12 May 1982 the applicant entered into a maintenance agreement with Mr Scott; it was then registered in the Family Court under the provisions of section 86 of the Family Law Act 1975 ("the Family Law Act"). The agreement provided for division of the matrimonial property between Mr Scott and the applicant and contained the following provision:-

"The husband and the wife shall cease to have any

obligation if any so existed to pay maintenance to

each other."

8. Before the agreement was signed, the applicant, Mr Scott and the children were all occupying the matrimonial home. It is alleged that the applicant and Mr Scott were living separately in the house for a period of about a month before the agreement was signed. It is not necessary for us to make a finding whether that was so or not. Undoubtedly the maintenance agreement was being negotiated during that period and separation was envisaged if it had not occurred by then. Under the agreement Mr Scott was to move out of the matrimonial home and transfer all his interest in it to the applicant upon her paying him $23,000.

9. It is not in dispute that, when the agreement was signed and a sum of $23,000 paid to Mr Scott by the applicant in accordance with its terms, he vacated the matrimonial home; she and the children continued to occupy it. In due course his estate title and interest in it were transferred to her. Under the agreement Mr Scott also retained possession of a six-months-old Holden Commodore stationwagon which had been jointly owned by him and the applicant and of a colour television set, a stereo/radiogram and various items of furniture and tools. It is not in dispute that from then until his death Mr Scott had paid no money to the applicant for the maintenance of herself or the children.

10. Mr Andrews gave evidence that he was instructed by the applicant to seek sole use and occupancy of the matrimonial home. He then negotiated the maintenance agreement with a solicitor acting for Mr Scott. He tendered in evidence a valuation of the house at $50,000 which he had obtained from a valuer; no debt was owing in respect of any mortgage on the house. When Mr Scott transferred his interest in the house to Mrs Scott, stamp duty was assessed on the basis of that value. Mr Andrews said that he obtained advice from his brother-in-law, who was an executive with a major Holden dealer, as to the value of Commodore stationwagons and came to the conclusion that the car belonging to Mr Scott and the applicant had a value of $10,000; $2,000 was still owed on it. He said that the furnishings of the house and other chattels were discussed but not brought into the valuation; that is reflected in the agreement, where the items to be taken by Mr Scott are specified but no value is recorded in respect of them or of any other furnishings or chattels. Mr Andrews said that there was approximately $4,000 in a bank account in the joint names of Mr Scott and the applicant (he described it as an endowment account) and $5,800 in a bank account in the name of the applicant as trustee for the children; that money had been given by her parents. He had been aware that Mr Scott was a contributor to the Superannuation Fund and that he had a long service leave entitlement, both of which would normally be taken into account in a property settlement on breakdown of marriage. However, Mr Andrews said, he had had no information as to the amounts of them and, as the applicant urgently required to obtain sole occupancy of the matrimonial home, they had been left out of consideration when he negotiated the division of the matrimonial property with Mr Scott's solicitor. He said that he had subsequently learned that the value of Mr Scott's long service leave entitlement was approximately $8,000.

11. Mr Andrews gave evidence that he knew that the applicant was not getting as large a share of the matrimonial property as she would have been able to obtain if application had been made to the Family Court under the Family Law Act. She received approximately 40%; the court would have been likely to award her 60%. However, he had had to pursue in haste the negotiations for the agreement because the applicant and the children had told him that Mr Scott's behaviour towards them had included threats of violence and he had considered that it was imperative to get him out of the matrimonial home quickly. He had told the applicant that the maintenance agreement was "not binding on her as far as the property settlement was concerned". Application for a declaration of the rights of herself and Mr Scott to the matrimonial property could not at that time be made to a court; that had to wait until application for dissolution of the marriage had been made. So he had advised her to make the maintenance agreement, disadvantageous as its terms were, in order to get Mr Scott out of the matrimonial home, and then to commence proceedings for dissolution of the marriage as soon as possible (i.e. after one year) and to apply for a court order for division of the property and also for a lump sum maintenance payment. She had apparently acted on that advice and he was expecting her to give him instructions to commence dissolution proceedings in due course and to apply for a property settlement and lump sum maintenance.

12. Mr Andrews said that the applicant could have applied to the Family Court for maintenance at the end of twelve months; in the light of answers which he gave to Mr Anforth, who represented the applicant at the hearing, it seems that he was aware that the applicant might have applied for periodic payments of maintenance but considered that it was in her best interest that she should apply for lump sum maintenance. Although the Family Court could have made an order for payment of maintenance at any time, we think that it was probably more practical to make an application for lump sum maintenance at the same time as application was made for a property settlement; we accept that that was why Mr Andrews advised the applicant accordingly. However, periodic payments were appropriate in respect of maintenance for the children; he appears to have advised the applicant, correctly we are satisfied, that she could apply for that at any time. However, he said, the making of an application had been deliberately delayed, first because "they (Mr Scott and the applicant) were still very bitter and we were hoping that he would calm down a bit", and second because "Kaylene (the daughter) was going to be 12 in the near future and there was a question of access and she would have some say in access".

13. Mr Anforth referred Mr Andrews to a requirement of the Social Security Act 1947 that a supporting parent's benefit was not to be granted if the Director-General considered that it was reasonable that the supporting parent should have taken action to obtain maintenance from the children's father (section 83AAD). Mr Andrews replied that he thought that the Department of Social Security had not enforced that requirement strictly. That appears to be borne out by what occurred in the applicant's case. She was granted a supporting parent's benefit even though she had taken no steps to obtain maintenance from Mr Scott for herself or the children. Insofar as it may be relevant, it appears that Mr Andrews took the Department's attitude towards enforcement of the section into account in deciding what advice to give the applicant.

14. When the applicant made her application for spouse's benefit 17 days after Mr Scott's death she completed the "statement of particulars" on the application form. In answer to the question "Were you wholly or substantially dependent upon the deceased person at the time of his death?", she wrote "No". Giving evidence, she said that when she entered into the maintenance agreement she had acted on Mr Andrew's advice. He had told her that it was not binding, and she "had 12 months to, sort of, come back on it". She said that Mr Scott had himself told her the same thing, that it was not binding and she could "come back on him because it did not go through some other court". She had agreed to sign the agreement, even though its terms were disadvantageous to her, because of the way Mr Scott was behaving; she was scared of him and needed to obtain sole occupancy of the matrimonial home for herself and her children. She said that she had not applied for maintenance for the children immediately because of her daughter's age, as the question of custody and access would have arisen and she understood that Mr Scott would have been entitled to see the daughter. The daughter did not want to see him and had begged her not to make her do so; so she had delayed seeking maintenance for the children. She had intended to do so after 18 November 1982 when the daughter would become 12 years old and, she understood, would be old enough "to have a bit of a say in court" as to custody and access. She said that the maintenance which she had intended to seek at that time was for the children and not herself. Mr Andrews had told her that she "could come back on that one at a later date". She said that she thought that he had told her that she had to wait until she was divorced "or something like that".

15. With regard to her means of support, she said that she had not had any employment since she left Mr Scott. The last time she had been employed was 2-3 years before they separated; she had then had a cleaning job for periods of up to 6 months during "a couple of years" while the person who normally did the job was on sick leave and holiday. She had worked for one and a half years in a post office in the 1960s. Mr Scott had kept any money she had earned; he had used it "towards paying things off". She said that throughout the marriage he had made inadequate financial provision for her to buy the family's necessary food, clothing and household goods and that her parents had helped her to do so. They had owned a dairy farm until 1972 and then from 1972 to 1981 a farm where they had kept beef cattle. During the latter period they had sold one beast a year in her name and had given her the proceeds of the sale. They had also given her gifts of money from time to time and, whenever they visited, had brought with them large quantities of meat and vegetables, which would have cost up to $600 or $700 if bought in the shops. They had sent materials, clothes and money for clothes, in total about $220 a year; but they had not met all the clothing costs of herself and the children. When she and Mr Scott had separated she had had $5,000 in a bank account. She had saved it from what her parents had given her. She said that she had it as trustee for the children but "if I needed it . . to help buy things around the house, I would use it". In fact she had used all of it in paying Mr Scott the $23,000 under the maintenance agreement. After she and Mr Scott separated, she said, her parents had sent her in most weeks amounts of money varying between $20 and $50. Before Mr Scott died they had also bought her a motor car and a television set. Whenever they visited her, which was about once every three months, they had given her additional money, in amounts up to $100. However, they were on age pensions and no longer had the farm; so they could not have continued giving her money like that indefinitely.

16. Detailing her expenses, she said that the local council rates on the house were now about $300 per year and the electricity bill for the last quarter had been $120-$140. It now cost over $300 per year to insure and register her car; she could not recall what the cost was in 1982. Referred to an affidavit which she had sworn in February 1983 and in which she had stated her estimate of her weekly expenses in respect of the children since May 1982 as $62.50 per week for each child, she said that her own expenses would have been less than those for either of the children. (The $62.50 included $20 for tennis expenses; both the children were particularly talented tennis players.) After separating from Mr Scott she had received a supporting parent's benefit under the Social Security Act 1947 until his death; since his death she had received a widow's pension under that Act. She acknowledged that her expenditure on her children, herself, her car, the rates and electricity exceeded the amount which she received by way of supporting parent's benefit and widow's pension; she said, in effect, that her parents had made up the deficit. She pointed out that she had had no telephone as she could not afford one.

17. Of the $23,000 which she had paid to Mr Scott under the maintenance agreement about $14,000 had been provided by her parents. She said that, after she had separated from Mr Scott, he had taken no interest in her or the children. He had altered his will to cut out provision previously made in it for them. She had met him by chance in the street about two weeks before he died and he had asked about the children; but until then he had not contacted her and she considered that he would not have done so had he not met her by chance. Nevertheless, she considered that, if he had agreed or been ordered to pay maintenance to her, he would have done so, as he "always paid his bills". In any case, as she pointed out, it could have been taken out of his pay, if he had not paid it as required. She conceded that, when she was receiving the supporting parent's benefit, she knew that, if she had been paid maintenance by Mr Scott, it might have caused a reduction in the amount of the benefit she was paid.

18. Mrs Golinski gave evidence of the financial assistance which she and her husband had provided to the applicant both before and after the separation. She said that during the period when they had owned the beef cattle farm she had sold two bullocks in the applicant's name each year and sent her the money. She produced photocopies of the sale notes for a number of the years. The amounts varied between $131.49 in 1975 and $759.02 in a later year. She said that, in addition, whenever they had visited the applicant, which was about once every three months, they had filled their car with meat and vegetables for her. She estimated that they had given her less than $1,000 per year in cash before the separation. After the separation they had sent her each week amounts which varied between $20 and $50; they had also given amounts of up to $100 when they visited her and had continued to bring meat and vegetables. At the time of the separation they had contributed about $14,580 towards the $23,000 paid by the applicant to Mr Scott. After the separation they had bought her a new washing machine and a new stove, in place of her old one which had broken down. They had continued to provide her with such support as they could and would continue to do so for as long as they could. She said that they had obtained a substantial amount of money when they sold their farm but were "not very well off". They owned their own house but they received part age pensions under the Social Security Act.

19. As we have already stated, it is not in dispute that after Mr Scott left the matrimonial home in May 1982, he paid no money, and supplied no goods, to support either the applicant or their children. It is not in dispute that until his death she received a supporting parent's benefit under Part IVAAA of the Social Security Act 1947. That was paid at a rate of $200.30 per fortnight. There is no doubt that the amount which she spent on day to day living expenses, including tennis tuition for the two children, was of the order of $180 per week or $360 per fortnight. Of that amount $40 per week, or $80 per fortnight, was tennis expenses. So the cost of her other normal day to day expenses was approximately the same as the amount which she received by way of supporting parent's benefit. However, without financial assistance from her parents she would not have been able to afford to buy a motor car, television set, washing machine or stove, to pay the rates or to meet the children's tennis expenses.

20. For the applicant to be entitled to a spouse's pension it is necessary that at the time of Mr Scott's death she should have been wholly or substantially dependent on him. The meaning of that phrase in a practically identical context in the Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Act 1973 was examined in great detail by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal, constituted by Deputy President R.A. Layton and Mr B.C. Lock and Dr J.T.B. Linn, Members, in Re Schlatter and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (No. S84/123: decided on 19 August 1985). In that case the Tribunal, after referring to two landmark decisions of the House of Lords on the meaning of "dependency", noted that the question of whether or not a wife was at any time dependent on her husband was a question of fact. A matter relevant to the determination of that question was whether or not the husband had had a legal obligation to support the wife. The Tribunal came to the conclusion that section 72 of the Family Law Act 1975 imposed a legal obligation on each legally married spouse to support the other if, regard having been had for all such matters as were referred to in section 75(2) and as were relevant in the circumstances of the particular case, there was an inability of the other to support himself or herself adequately. In these proceedings Mr Anforth accepted that the question of dependency was one of fact but submitted that, whereas under the common law applicable in the two House of Lords cases a wife had a right to be supported by her husband, the Family Law Act overrode the common law and made such a right dependent on a maintenance order being made under Part VIII of that Act. He argued that the Tribunal in Re Schlatter had been wrong when it decided that that Act imposed an obligation to support when there was no order of a court.

21. This was the first of a number of matters decided by the Tribunal in Re Schlatter which Mr Anforth argued had been wrongly decided and in respect of which he asked the Tribunal in the present proceedings to come to a different decision. As Brennan J. (President) observed in Re Drake and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs (No. 2) [1979] AATA 179; (1979) 2 ALD 634 at 639:-

"Inconsistency is not merely inelegant; it brings the

process of deciding into disrepute, suggesting an

arbitrariness which is incompatible with commonly

accepted notions of justice."

Although that was said in relation to a consideration of whether or not it was proper for the Minister to have a policy and to make his decisions by applying that policy, it can properly be applied also to decisions of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal. One effect of the Tribunal's decisions is to establish administrative norms; they enable legislation to be administered consistently. For the Tribunal to make decisions inconsistent with its own previous decisions adversely affects that process. Doubtless, in some instances, where a matter has been decided by the Tribunal without full argument or full consideration and it is necessary for the Tribunal in later proceedings to examine the matter fully, it may then properly reach a conclusion different from the previous decision. In that event, because the later decision is the first made upon a full consideration of the matter, it is clear to administrators that that decision should be followed rather than the previous decision. More rarely there may be instances where, notwithstanding that the Tribunal has reached a decision on a certain matter after full consideration, it is nevertheless manifest that there was an error in the reasoning which led to that decision. To adapt what was said by Isaacs J. in Australian Agricultural Co. v Federated Engine-Drivers and Firemen's Association of Australasia [1913] HCA 41; (1913) 17 CLR 261 at 278, it is not better that the Tribunal should be persistently wrong than that it should be ultimately right. Nevertheless, where a matter has been decided by the Tribunal after full consideration of competing arguments, the decision is one which is reasonably tenable and there have been no changes to the legislation and no new decisions of the High Court of Australia or the Federal Court which may be relevant, it seems to us that it would be extremely unhelpful for the Tribunal in subsequent proceedings to decide the matter in a manner inconsistent with that decision, particularly when the arguments advanced are substantially the same as those advanced in the previous case.

22. That is what Mr Anforth has asked us to do in these proceedings. Although in Re Schlatter the Tribunal was concerned with the construction of a provision of the Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Act 1973 and not the provision which we have to construe in these proceedings, the two provisions are in pari materia. This appears to have been accepted by the respondent, who is responsible for the administration of both the Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Act 1973 and the Superannuation Act 1976. Mr Anforth did not suggest any reason for construing the provision in the Act differently from that in the former. We can find no reason for doing so. So it would be inconsistent for us to decide that the provision with which we are concerned in these proceedings was to be construed differently from the manner in which the Tribunal decided in Re Schlatter that the provision in the other Act should be construed.

23. In our view, where a decision-maker is strongly of the view that a fully considered decision of the Tribunal in relation to any matter is wrong, he should appeal to the Federal Court against that decision or, if it is not possible to do so, take the first opportunity to appeal against a decision in subsequent proceedings which follows and applies it, rather than attempt to persuade the Tribunal to make a decision inconsistent with its previous decision and so create a situation in which those relying on the Tribunal's normative function to make administrative decisions or to advise their clients with regard to the correctness or otherwise of such decisions are cast into confusion.

24. We appreciate that in Re Schlatter the Tribunal affirmed the decision under review, so that the respondent was unable to appeal against the Tribunal's decision. However, in Re Singleton and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (No. S84/116: decided on 19 August 1985, i.e. shortly after the decision in Re Schlatter) the Tribunal, constituted as it had been for Re Schlatter, adopted so far as was relevant (in Re Singleton the applicant was not legally married to the deceased, so that the provisions of the Family Law Act were not applicable) what it had said in Re Schlatter and set aside the decision under review. Mr Anforth informed the Tribunal that it had been decided not to appeal to the Federal Court in that case; no doubt there was good reason for that but, if it is the Commissioner's intention to continue to dispute the correctness of the Tribunal's decision in Re Schlatter on the construction of those provisions, it is to be hoped that he will pursue the matter soon in the Federal Court. Because Re Schlatter was decided after full argument and full consideration, we approach our task of construction on the basis that we should follow that decision unless we are satisfied that any part of the reasoning on which it was based was not reasonably tenable.

25. Section 72 of the Family Law Act is, and was in substance at all relevant times, as follows:-

"72. A party to a marriage is liable to maintain

the other party, to the extent that the

first-mentioned party is reasonably able to do so,

if, and only if, that other party is unable to

support herself or himself adequately whether -

(a) by reason of having the care and control of

a child of the marriage who has not

attained the age of 18 years;

(b) by reason of age or physical or mental

incapacity for appropriate gainful

employment; or

(c) for any other adequate reason,

having regard to any relevant matter referred to in

sub-section 75(2)."

26. Section 74 then makes provision empowering a court to make orders for the maintenance of a party to, or a child of, a marriage. Section 75(1) requires the court when exercising that jurisdiction to take into account only the matters referred to in sub-section (2) of section 75. Sub-section (2) sets out the matters to be taken into account. In our view, although the matters are set out in the context of the requirements of section 75(1), the reference to them in section 72 does not mean that the right of a spouse to maintenance depends on a court having made an order under section 74. Certainly an obligation of one spouse to maintain the other spouse which arises as a result of section 72 cannot be enforced except under Part VIII of the Family Law Act; but the situation was similar when the common law governed the husband's obligation to support his wife. That obligation could be enforced only by appropriate proceedings in a court. The provisions of section 72 are such that on occasions it may be difficult to be sure whether or not one party to a marriage has an obligation to maintain the other; but the obligation, as distinct from the enforcement of it, is dependent on the facts, not on a court having made a finding as to what those facts are. In most cases there will be no doubt as to its existence or non-existence, even when there is doubt as to its extent. We have come to the conclusion that the decision of the Tribunal in Re Schlatter was correct that section 72 of the Family Law Act imposes a legal obligation on each legally married spouse to support the other if there is an inability of the other spouse to support himself or herself adequately, having regard to the matters referred to in section 75(2), and that it is not necessary for a court to have made an order under section 74 of that Act for such an obligation to exist.

27. In New Monckton Collieries Ltd. v. Keeling (1911) AC 648, the first of the two landmark decisions of the House of Lords referred to by the Tribunal in Re Schlatter, it was implicitly accepted that a wife, having a legal right to be supported by her husband, might be dependent even though the husband had failed to provide the support. At page 653 Lord Atkinson said:-

". . . the existence of the obligation, the

probability that it will be discharged, either

voluntarily or under compulsion, the probability that

the wife will ever enforce her right if the

obligation be not discharged voluntarily, are all

matters proper to be considered . . . in determining

the question of fact whether, or not, the wife, . . .

looked to his earnings for her maintenance and

support . . ."

28. In the second case, Potts v Niddrie and Benhar Coal Company Limited (1913) AC 531 at 537. Viscount Haldane LC said:-

"If there was such a right, and there was no legal

difficulty in the way of enforcing it, then the mere

fact that a want of opportunity to resort to it,

which might have proved only temporary, had reduced

the mother and children for the time to living on

charity, cannot affect the conclusion that by the

father's death they lost something on which they

could depend."

At page 540 Lord Shaw of Dunfermline adopted a passage from Lee v Owner of Ship Bessie (1912) 1 KB 83 page 88:-

"In my opinion the effect of (the decision in

Keeling's case) is that legal obligations to support

must not be taken at their theoretic value, but at

their practical value. For instance, the mere fact

that a husband is bound to support his wife does not

establish that she is totally or at all dependent

upon him within the meaning of the Act if the

circumstances are such that there is no reasonable

probability that her rights would have been

practically and effectually asserted. But if on the

evidence there is any fair probability that the legal

rights would at any future time have been actually

and effectually asserted by the wife, then there is

evidence of dependency, and the compensation must be

regulated by an estimate of her practical loss

subject to the provisions of the Act."

29. Mr Anforth, who very helpfully supplied the Tribunal with a written statement of his submissions and of the cases on which he relied in support of them, accepted, rightly in our view, that although the decisions of the House of Lords were concerned with workers' compensation legislation, what was said in them about the nature of dependency was applicable to the construction of the phrase "wholly or substantially dependent" in the Superannuation Act 1976. In these present proceedings we are satisfied that the meaning of dependency was summarized accurately by the Tribunal in Re Schlatter where at paragraph 87 it said:-

"In summary therefore, dependency is a financial

concept which exists if the claimant satisfies the

Authority (or on appeal a tribunal), that as at the

date of death the deceased was either paying money or

providing support to the claimant for the basic

necessities of life or lower order needs;

alternatively, was under a legal obligation to

provide such support to the claimant pursuant to a

maintenance order which probably would be effectively

enforced if not paid voluntarily; in the further

alternative that the deceased had a prima facie legal

obligation to provide such support to the claimant as

at the date of death which probably would have been

effectively enforced if not paid voluntarily."

30. In paragraph 88 the Tribunal continued:-

"It would also seem from the cases previously referred

to that, in considering whether the above factors

exist, the Tribunal must look at the whole

relationship between the parties as it existed at the

date of death taking into account both past events

and future probabilities which would include matters

such as any financial arrangements, written or verbal

agreements, provisions in a will and all matters

which may affect the financial affairs between the

parties and not merely limited to the payment of

money but includes provision of support other than by

payment of money."

Subject to taking into account, if necessary, any possible effect of the requirement in section 72 of the Family Law Act to have regard to any relevant matter referred to in section 75(2) (which we consider below), we accept that the views expressed in paragraph 88 of Re Schlatter are correct, and we adopt them for the purposes of deciding in the present case whether dependency existed at the time of Mr Scott's death. We shall return to that question after considering the meaning of the expression "wholly or substantially dependent".

31. The meaning of the expression "wholly dependent" used in the Workers' Compensation Act 1927 (Tas.) was considered by the High Court of Australia in Kauri Timber Co. (Tas.) Pty. Ltd. v Reeman [1973] HCA 8; (1973) 128 CLR 177. There the majority held that a wife had been wholly dependent upon her husband's earnings for her support even though she had a small income of her own with which she was able to buy non-essential goods for her own enjoyment. The Tribunal pointed out in Re Johnson and Commissioner for Superannuation (1979) 2 ALD 825 that the Superannuation Act 1976 was concerned with dependence upon the spouse generally and not only upon his earnings, as is the case under workers' compensation legislation; however, subject to that distinction, it decided that the principles developed by the courts in cases under that legislation were applicable to the determination of dependency for the purposes of the Act. We accept that as correct.

32. In Re Schlatter the Tribunal examined at considerable length the meaning of "substantially dependent" in the context of the phrase "wholly or substantially dependent". It came to the conclusion that:-

". . rather than attempt to define what

'substantially' is, it is probably easier to say what

it does not mean. The dependency shown should not be

trivial, minimal or nominal (and to that extent

should be greater than 'partly' as previously used)"

(a reference to the previous provision of the Defence

Force Retirement and Death Benefits Act 1973

subsequently amended) "and should not be total but

something in between."

The Tribunal continued at paragraph 128:-

"It is important to note, however, that merely because

the payment or support cannot be aptly described as

trivial, minimal or nominal, nor is it total, it does

not ipso facto follow that the payment or support is

thereby 'substantial'. Each case must still be

looked at in context and on its facts."

33. In Re Schlatter Mr Anforth, representing the respondent in those proceedings, had submitted that "substantially" in its context in the definition of "widow" meant "of the greater part", or "in the main". The Tribunal considered his submissions and after a very thorough examination of relevant decisions and of the use of the words "substantial" and "substantially" in various pieces of legislation, decided to reject his argument. The arguments which he addressed to the Tribunal in the present proceedings appear to be identical in substance to those which he addressed to the Tribunal in Re Schlatter. A number of the submissions which he made in respect of this matter based on the combination of "wholly" and "substantially" had some attraction. But those very arguments were considered fully by the Tribunal in Re Schlatter and we are not satisfied that the conclusion which the Tribunal came to in that case was founded on any error. The meaning given to the phrase by the Tribunal in those proceedings is reasonably tenable. For the reasons we have already stated we consider that it would not assist those required to make decisions under the Act, and indeed would be positively unhelpful, if we were to decide in these proceedings that the phrase bore a meaning different from that which the Tribunal in Re Schlatter accorded to it.

34. On the question of the applicant's dependency on Mr Scott at the time of his death the Commissioner's case was, first, that she was not dependent at all because she had abandoned her right to be supported by him and, second, that, if she was dependent on him, she was not wholly or substantially dependent because she was in receipt of a supporting parent's benefit and of financial support from her parents. In respect of the first of those issues the Commissioner relied on the maintenance agreement which the applicant and Mr Scott had entered into and which had been registered under section 86 of the Family Law Act and on the fact that it had been acted upon by them. In response, Mr Westbrook submitted that, even though the maintenance agreement had been registered and had been acted upon, that did not deprive the applicant of her right to apply to the Family Court under section 74 of the Family Law Act to obtain an order for maintenance; in the circumstances in which it was entered into and in the light of the property disposition which it made, Mr Scott had remained liable to support her and she had retained her right to be supported by him. Although by the time of his death she had taken no action, either by way of negotiation or by seeking a Court order, to have him provide maintenance for her or the children, she had not thereby demonstrated an intention not to require him to provide such maintenance. She had explained why she had deferred taking action. She had acted on the advice of her solicitor, who had taken account of all the circumstances which existed at that time. There had been an intention to obtain a court order for maintenance at a time which she and her solicitor considered opportune. Mr Westbrook submitted also that Mr Scott's obligation to support the applicant had neither ceased to exist nor been diminished by reason of her being in receipt of the supporting parent's benefit or financial support from her parents. It was his submission that she was wholly or substantially dependent on Mr Scott at the time of his death.

35. Section 72 of the Family Law Act makes the obligation of one spouse to a marriage to maintain the other dependent on the latter being unable to support herself or himself adequately and provides that regard is to be had to any relevant matter referred to in section 75(2) in ascertaining whether or not she or he is able to support herself or himself. Section 75(2)(f) provided, in 1982 that a matter to be taken into account was "the eligibility of either party for a pension, allowance or benefit under any law of Australia or of a State or Territory or under any superannuation fund or scheme, or the rate of any such pension, allowance or benefit being paid to either party". The effect of the requirement in section 72 that regard be had to relevant matters referred to in section 75(2) has been considered by courts in a number of cases; their decisions have not all been entirely consistent with one another.

36. The first question which the courts had to consider was whether a spouse's liability to provide maintenance was to be ascertained by a process involving two stages and two decisions or by one involving only one process and one decision. Should the inability of a spouse to support herself or himself be the inability of a spouse to support herself or himself be ascertained before regard was had to the matters referred to in section 75(2), and then those matters be taken into account together with the financial means of the other spouse in deciding whether he or she was liable to provide maintenance? Or should the matters referred to in section 75(2) be taken into account in deciding whether or not the spouse was unable to support herself or himself?

37. In Wong v. Wong (1976) 2 FamLR 11,159 Carmichael J. in the Supreme Court of New South Wales decided that the fundamental test to be applied was whether or not the spouse was able to support herself or himself. He refused to accept that eligibility for a pension or benefit could make a spouse self-supporting. In his view the question of dependency had to be decided without taking into account any amount that might be received by way of such benefit or allowance. In reaching that decision he had regard to aspects of public policy which included, he considered, a principle that a spouse who had the capability of maintaining the other spouse should be required to do so before the support of the other spouse became a charge on public funds. By contrast, in Brady (1978) FLC 90-513, where the court took into account the other spouse's entitlement to a pension or benefit under the Social Security Act 1947 and decided not to order the spouse to pay a periodic sum for the maintenance of the other spouse, it approached its task by considering the effect of ordering the payment of any particular amount rather than by deciding at the outset whether or not a liability existed in the circumstances.

38. With respect, there is considerable logic and cogency in the reasoning of Carmichael J. in Wong v. Wong; the reasoning has been adopted by the Family Court in cases such as Kajewski (1978) FLC 90-472, F. v. F. (1982) FLC 91-214 and Bailey (1981) FLC 91-041, to which we refer in more detail below. While there can be no certainty about the approach which the court would have adopted if the applicant had applied for maintenance under section 74, we consider that the preponderance of judicial dicta indicates that the approach of the court in Wong would probably have been followed. In that event the applicant would not have been regarded as able to support herself simply because she was in receipt of a supporting parent's benefit under the Social Security Act 1947.

39. The second stage of the process of deciding whether Mr Scott was liable to maintain the applicant would then have required consideration of his financial means and the matters referred to in section 75(2). The judges who have been required to consider how regard is to be had to the matters referred to in section 75(2)(f) have been unable to agree on that question. To date, as far as we are aware, there has been no decision which is a binding precedent. Before the Family Law Act was enacted and came into force courts generally declined to take into account the pensions or benefits which the claimants were receiving under the Social Security Act 1947 and the effect on them of orders for the payment of maintenance, although there were a few cases in which they did so. Since the Act came into force two distinct lines of decisions can be detected.

40. The principal cases in the first line include Kajewski (supra), F v F (supra), Matthews (1980) FLC 90-887 and Bailey (supra). Broadly the view adopted in those cases has been that, if a spouse is unable to support herself or himself and the other spouse is able to pay without hardship the amount needed by her or him to support herself or himself, any amount received by her or him by way of pension or benefit under the Social Security Act 1947 is to be ignored. Thus, in F v F Fogarty J. held that Social Security benefits should play a part in the support of a spouse only to make good the amount by which the amount the other spouse could reasonably pay fell short of the amount needed by the spouse.

41. The other line of decisions includes Brady (supra) and Mehrtens (1977) FLC 90-288 and Todd (No 2) (1976) FLC 90-008 In those cases the court noted that payment of maintenance by the other spouse would reduce the amount of benefits received by the spouse under the Social Security Act 1947. It declined to make an order which would impose a burden on the spouse ordered to pay maintenance as no substantial gain would result to the spouse receiving it. In those cases the court appears not to have stated explicitly its reasons for taking the view that amounts which the former spouse could reasonably afford to pay without hardship should instead be paid out of public funds. However, commentators have suggested that a number of grounds exist supporting that view. They include the greater certainty which a spouse has of receiving benefits under the Social Security Act than she or he may have in many cases of receiving regularly and punctually amounts ordered to be paid by the other spouse by way of maintenance.

42. The commentators also suggest that there have been changes in the attitude of society towards responsibilities for meeting the needs of persons affected by the breakdown of marriages and that that may have resulted in acceptance of the concept of the obligation for support in such circumstances resting principally on society at large rather than on the other spouse. We note, however, that the decisions in Bailey and F & F are more recent than the decisions in the other line of cases (although still decided before or contemporaneously with Mr Scott's death). Also we doubt very much whether the attitudes of Australian society have yet changed to the extent suggested by the commentators. We have no doubt it is generally accepted that the community has an obligation to provide support where it is needed; but we consider that the view expressed by Fogarty J. in F & F that public funds should be provided to meet the needs of a spouse from a broken marriage only to the extent that the other spouse cannot reasonably be expected to meet those needs reflects more nearly the current attitude of Australian society. We have considered this matter at some length as, if at the time of Mr Scott's death, the applicant had not, because of the maintenance agreement, lost her right to be maintained by him, it is relevant to the assessment of the extent of her dependence on him.

43. We should, we consider, at this stage state also what we understand would have been the principles governing the assessment of the quantum of the maintenance which Mr Scott could reasonably have been expected to pay in respect of the applicant. That is the amount which the parties might reasonably have been expected to negotiate or the applicant to receive under a court order. The matter is undoubtedly complicated by the need to take into account the matters referred in section 75(2). However, subject to the effect of taking those matters into account, the basic formula for computing maintenance under the Family Law Act is generally recognized as the ascertainment of the need of one spouse for support and of the ability of the other spouse to provide it. The process that requires to be undertaken to resolve the equation is to calculate first the cost of the reasonable needs of the spouse requiring support and to deduct from that the total and notional income of that spouse. The resulting figure is what may be referred to as her or his net needs. Then it is necessary to calculate the cost of the other spouse's reasonable needs and to deduct that amount from his or her total actual and notional income. The resulting amount may be referred to as the surplus. Provision of maintenance will be ordered only if the first spouse has net needs for support and the other has surplus income. The court will not usually order the other spouse to pay more than the amount of the surplus income, even if the net needs exceed that amount. However, if the surplus income exceeds the net needs, the other spouse will usually be ordered to pay sufficient to meet those net needs.

44. We have already discussed some aspects of the requirement of section 72 of the Family Law, read with section 75(2)(f), that regard be had to the fact of the eligibility of a spouse for a pension or benefit under the Social Security Act 1947. In the process of calculation it may need to be borne in mind that, no matter what amount of maintenance is ordered to be paid, the spouse receiving it will in many instances receive also a Social Security pension or benefit, albeit at a rate lower than that at which she or he would receive it if maintenance were not paid. After the calculations of net needs and surplus had been made, therefore, in respect of the applicant and Mr Scott, it might well have been necessary to consider the fairness of any order based simply on those amounts; the court might well have considered also the respective amounts which the applicant and Mr Scott would each have available to spend each week if the order were made on that basis.

45. A matter which is relevant to the calculation of the needs of a spouse is that referred to in paragraph (c) of section 75(2), namely whether he or she has the care or control of a child of the marriage who has not attained the age of 18 years. The need of a spouse who is a homemaker for the children of the marriage will often be greater than the needs of a spouse who has to provide only for herself or himself. Her or his responsibilities for the children may also be relevant to the question whether or not she or he should take steps to obtain work. The Family Law Act envisages that each spouse has an obligation to support himself or herself by work if it is reasonable for him or her to do so. Another matter relevant to that consideration is the length of the duration of the marriage and the spouse's work history before and during the marriage. If the spouse who is prima facie liable to provide maintenance has other family responsibilities, those have to be taken into account in assessing his or her needs. Ultimately the assessment of the needs of both the spouses involves value judgments being made. So we reiterate that there can be no certainty what amount the court would have ordered Mr Scott to pay. Nevertheless, it is possible to make an informed guess as to what it would have been.

46. We shall defer for the moment consideration of the relevance of the registered maintenance order to the question of the applicant's dependence on Mr Scott and shall examine the question of dependence as though it had contained no provision that maintenance was not to be paid or sought. She had care and control of two children of the marriage whose ages at the time of Mr Scott's death were 14 and 11 years respectively; she had not engaged in regular employment for about 20 years; she was not qualified for any particular type of employment. There can be no doubt that, in terms of section 72 of the Family Law Act, she was unable to support herself adequately. The fact that, if she had received no other support whatsoever, her parents might have provided adequate support is not relevant; they had no legal obligation to do so and their financial means were, in any event, quite limited. We are concerned only with the question whether the applicant was able to support herself. In the circumstances of the present case we consider that the change made by Mr Scott to his will after the separation is of no significance in relation to the question of the applicant's dependence on him at the time of his death.

47. It is the respondent's case, however, that she had abandoned that right of support when she entered into the maintenance agreement and registered it under section 86 of the Family Law Act, or at least when she and Mr Scott acted on it. The fact that a maintenance agreement has been made and has been registered under section 86 does not oust the jurisdiction of the Family Court, under section 74 of the Family Law Act, to order one spouse to pay maintenance to the other (Calabrese v Miuccio (1984) FLC 91-548). The court can make an order overriding the terms of a maintenance agreement (Candlish v Pratt (1980) FLC 90-819). In deciding whether or not to do so, it will take into account the fact that the parties have acted on a maintenance agreement; but in Burgoyne v. Burgoyne (1978) 4 FamLR 204 the Full Court of the Family Court held that "whether or not an agreement has been put into effect and whether or not it has been enforced by order under section 88 of the Family Law Act, neither party is or can be precluded from seeking an order under section 74". After observing that in such proceedings the court would no doubt consider the maintenance agreement and the parties' intentions when they entered into it as demonstrated by it, the Full Court said that the matter would nevertheless be decided in accordance with the agreement only if it accorded with what the court would itself have ordered. In particular, it was stressed that the court would not consider only whether the terms of the agreement were proper.

48. We are satisfied, therefore, that the fact that the maintenance agreement was entered into, registered and acted on had not precluded the applicant from making an application to a court under section 74 of the Family Law Act for an order to be made for Mr Scott to provide maintenance for her. Although the agreement was within the definition of "maintenance agreement" in section 4(1) of the Family Law Act, it provided only for the division of the matrimonial property between the applicant and Mr Scott and contained express provision that she would not seek maintenance from him. We are satisfied that the property was divided in the proportions of approximately 60% to Mr Scott and 40% to her. As he had no dependants other than the applicant and the children of the marriage, the agreement undoubtedly did not accord with what a court would have decided if it had been required to make an order for the maintenance of the applicant and her children and to divide the matrimonial property (the latter being possible only after dissolution of the marriage had been commenced).

49. Nevertheless, the question whether the applicant was dependent on Mr Scott at the time of his death or had abandoned her right to be maintained by him must depend ultimately on her intention at that time. It is clear from her evidence and that of her solicitor, Mr Andrews, that she had acted throughout on his advice and that she intended to do whatever he advised her was in the best interests of herself and the children. It is, therefore, to Mr Andrews' evidence that we must look to ascertain why the maintenance agreement contained the provision that she would not seek maintenance and why no action had been taken up to the time of Mr Scott's death to pursue her right, and that of the children, to have Mr Scott provide maintenance. We are satisfied that Mr Andrews' strategy was as follows. The first objective was to obtain sole possession of the matrimonial home without delay. He considered that a maintenance agreement was required in order to achieve that. Mr Scott was a difficult person to deal with, at the best of times, as was made clear by the evidence of Mr Mackenzie; his behaviour towards the applicant and the children had become increasingly bad. He was not likely to be willing to discuss his obligations dispassionately.

50. Mr Andrews decided, therefore, that, in order to attain that first objective, it was necessary to accept a maintenance agreement with terms unduly favourable to Mr Scott. However, Mr Andrews knew that that would not preclude the applicant from pursuing her rights under the Family Law Act in respect of herself and the children once Mr Scott had left the matrimonial home and the property had been divided. He planned that the applicant should seek maintenance for the children as soon as the daughter attained the age of 12. He intended to attempt to try to negotiate it with Mr Scott's solicitors; if agreement could not be reached, he planned that the applicant should make application to the Family Court for an order for payment of maintenance for them.

51. He took account of Mr Scott's difficult personality. He expected that the applicant would commence proceedings in May 1983 for dissolution of the marriage; section 48 of the Family Law Act prevented such proceedings being commenced until 12 months after she and Mr Scott separated. That being so, he considered that it would be more advantageous to seek provision for maintenance by payment of a lump sum when the property rights of the applicant and Mr Scott were redefined, either by negotiation or by the court under section 79 of the Family Law Act at the time of the dissolution. In 1982 sub-section (3) of section 79 prevented the powers to alter property interests given to the Family Court by that section being exercised before proceedings for dissolution had been commenced. The award of lump sum maintenance would have accorded with the requirements of section 81 of the Family Law Act, which imposed on the court a duty to make orders, so far as was practicable, to determine finally the financial relations between the spouses and to avoid further proceedings between them. Mr Andrews did not intend that the applicant's right to be maintained by Mr Scott should be abandoned. We are satisfied that the applicant intended to commence proceedings for dissolution of the marriage as soon as she could and generally to act in accordance with Mr Andrews' advice. That being so, we are satisfied that she never intended to abandon her right to be supported by Mr Scott and that it was her intention to enforce that right in about May 1983. We find therefore, that at the time of his death she was dependent on him.

52. The remaining issue which we have to decide is concerned with the degree of the applicant's dependency on Mr Scott at the time of his death. That has to be determined by reference to the obligation imposed on Mr Scott by section 72 of the Family Law Act. Although at that time she was in receipt of a supporting parent's benefit, the primary obligation to support her rested on Mr Scott. She had no independent income whatsoever; she had paid him all the money which she had had in the bank when he transferred his share in the matrimonial home to her. In relation to Mr Scott's obligation to provide support for her and the children of the marriage, the financial assistance and gifts in kind provided by the applicant's parents are not to be taken into account; even during cohabitation he had not provided adequate support, although he had had the means to do so. The parents' support was given to make good that deficiency. After the separation, it was given to make good his total failure to provide support.

53. Mr Scott's earnings were approximately $15,100 per annum. He had received $23,000 in cash from the applicant under the maintenance agreement. He had no dependants, other than the applicant and the children; he had not entered into any de facto marriage relationship with any other person. As the matrimonial home was valued at $50,000, it is reasonable to assume that Mr Scott would have been able to buy a house or unit suitable for occupation by himself alone for not more than $40,000. If he had utilised the $23,000 towards the purchase of that house and therefore had no investment income, after payment of income tax he would have had a net income of something over $11,500 per annum. He had, as the result of the division of the matrimonial property, a nearly new motor car and basic furniture sufficient for himself. He would have had to repay the mortgage loan, pay the rates on the house or unit, provide food, clothing and other necessaries for himself and pay for gas and electricity, as well as the maintenance and running of the motor car. Having regard to the applicant's evidence of her costs of maintaining herself and her children in the matrimonial home after Mr Scott had left, we calculate that Mr Scott's needs were of the order of $7,000 per annum. That left a surplus of just over $4,500 available for the support of the applicant and the children of the marriage. Although it is impossible to state with certainty what order the court would have made for payment of maintenance for the applicant and the children of the marriage, it is, we consider, reasonable to proceed on the basis that the amount ordered would have been about $35 per week for the applicant and $25 per week for each of the children. The effect of sections 63(2) and 83AAE of the Social Security Act would then have been to reduce the supporting parent's benefit paid to the applicant to $76.35 per week by reason of the payment of the maintenance. That would have resulted in the applicant's total income from maintenance and the benefit being $161.35 per week, approximately $8,400 per annum. As she would have had to maintain herself and the children on that income, it could not have been said that it was unfairly large in comparison with the $7,000 which Mr Scott would have had available to meet only his own expenses.

54. Having regard to the amounts which the applicant had to pay to support her children and the part of the household expenses that can reasonably be attributable to them, $35 per week would certainly have been more than a trifling part of the total amount which the applicant would have had left to spend on her own needs. We are satisfied, therefore, that at the date of Mr Scott's death the applicant was substantially dependent on him and is, therefore, entitled under section 81 of the Superannuation Act to be granted a spouse's pension. Accordingly, the decision under review is set aside and the matter is remitted to the Commissioner for reconsideration in accordance with a direction that the applicant is so entitled.
