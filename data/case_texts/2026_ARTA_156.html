Halse and Commissioner of Taxation (Taxation) [2026] ARTA 156 (9 February 2026)

Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. Mr Halse (also referred to as the Applicant) is in dispute with the Commissioner of Taxation (Commissioner or Respondent) over the quantum of his capital gains tax (CGT) liability for the years of income ended 30 June 2021 and 30 June 2024 (Relevant Income Years).
  2. Mr Halse says certain expenditure incurred since August 1991 should be included in the third element of the cost base as non-capital costs of ownership of a CGT asset. The incorporation of this expenditure in the relevant CGT calculation would reduce the Applicant’s assessed taxation liability.
  3. The Commissioner says the expenses must be excluded as they relate to ownership of a CGT asset acquired prior to 21 August 1991.

BACKGROUND

  1. The critical facts are not in dispute and are taken primarily from the Respondent’s Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions.[1]
  2. On 20 October 1989, Mr Halse purchased a block of land located in Port Lincoln, South Australia (the Property) for $55,000.
  3. In early 2019, Mr Halse decided to subdivide the Property into two equal parcels (Parcel 1 and Parcel 2).
  4. On 18 June 2021, Mr Halse sold Parcel 2 for $310,000.
  5. On 25 August 2021, Mr Halse lodged his income tax return for the year of income ended 30 June 2021 (2021 Income Year). In the return, the Applicant reported a total capital gain of $250,836, a net capital gain of $125,418 and a total taxable income of $199,376.
  6. On 1 September 2021, a Notice of Assessment (2021 NOA) was issued to the Applicant. The tax on the taxable income of $199,376 was recorded as $60,386.20 with an amount payable of $48,836.70.
  7. On 7 May 2024, Mr Halse sold Parcel 1 for $400,000.
  8. On 12 September 2024, Mr Halse lodged his income tax return for the year of income ended 30 June 2024 (2024 Income Year). In the return, the Applicant reported a total capital gain of $358,556, a net capital gain of $178,781 and total taxable income of $269,114.
  9. On 19 September 2024, a Notice of Assessment (2024 NOA) was issued to the Applicant. The tax on the taxable income of $269,114 was recorded as $91,768.30, resulting in an amount payable of $77,205.55.
  10. On 10 February 2024, Mr Halse lodged an objection to the 2021 NOA and 2024 NOA together with an extension of time. In the objection document, the Applicant stated:
    I believe it is undeniably reasonable and appropriate in this case to include the third element costs of owning the CGT asset of a vacant block of land for the 30 year period between the time that the caveat was introduced on 20 August 1991 (which allows claiming of ongoing third element costs for land purchased after 20 August 1991) up until the sale of the land asset. These rates and taxes of course are required government payments as part of the ownership of the asset and I expect this was accepted when the caveat changes were made.
  11. The Applicant included his calculations which the objection. Those calculations sought to reduce the capital gain from $125,418 to $104,168 in the 2021 year, and $178,781 to $152,220 in the 2024 Year.
  12. On 20 March 2025, the Commissioner allowed the extension of time but disallowed the objection in full. He did so as the CGT asset was acquired prior to 21 August 1991 and therefore did not fall within the requirements of s 110-25 of the ITAA 97..
  13. On 9 May 2025, the Applicant lodged his application with the Tribunal seeking a review of the Respondent’s objection decision.

RELEVANT LAW

  1. The CGT provisions contained in Parts 3–1 and 3–3 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997(Cth) (ITAA 97) were introduced to replace the CGT provisions found in the former Part IIIA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth). The provisions in the ITAA 97 seek to prescribe a comprehensive regime for the taxation of capital gains.
  2. The aim of the CGT provisions is to tax as income, gains made on the disposal of assets. Generally speaking a taxpayer makes a capital gain from a CGT event if the taxpayer is entitled to receive capital amounts which exceed the taxpayer’s total costs associated with that event. Alternatively, a taxpayer incurs a capital loss if the taxpayer’s total costs associated with the CGT event exceeds the capital amounts the taxpayer is entitled to receive.[2]
  3. Capital amounts received from a CGT event are referred to as capital proceeds.[3] The total costs associated with a CGT event are generally referred to as the cost base for capital gains or reduced cost base for capital losses. [4]
  4. The amount of the net capital gain derived by a taxpayer for an income year is included in the assessable income of the taxpayer.[5] A net capital gain generally refers to the excess of the sum of the capital gains derived during a year over the capital losses (if any) incurred in a year, and reduced by the unapplied net capital losses from previous income years.[6] Accordingly, subject to special averaging rules, capital gains are taxable at the applicable marginal rate of tax of the taxpayer (or the corporate income tax rate in the case of companies).
  5. A common CGT event is A1, which happens when a taxpayer disposes of a CGT asset.[7] A CGT asset is defined as any kind of property or legal right.[8] A taxpayer is regarded as having disposed of a CGT asset if a change of ownership occurs from the taxpayer to another entity, whether because of some act, event or by operation of law.[9]
  6. In the event of CGT event A1, a taxpayer makes a capital gain if the capital proceeds from the event exceed the cost base of the CGT asset disposed of. A taxpayer makes a capital loss if those capital proceeds are less than the asset’s reduced cost base.
  7. The cost base of an asset is central to the calculation of the capital gain or loss arising under CGT event A1. Section 110-25 of the ITAA 97 sets out the five elements which make up the cost base. Section 110-25(4), which deals with the third element of the costs base, provides as follows:
    The third element is the costs of owning the *CGT asset you incurred (but only if you *acquired the asset after 20 August 1991). These costs include:

    (a) interest on money you borrowed to acquire the asset; and

    (b) costs of maintaining, repairing or insuring it; and

    (c) rates or land tax, if the asset is land; and

    (d) interest on money you borrowed to refinance the money you borrowed to acquire the asset; and

    (e) interest on money you borrowed to finance the capital expenditure you incurred to increase the asset’s value.

ISSUES

  1. The overarching issue is whether Mr Halse has discharged his onus under section 14ZZK of Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (TAA53) by proving the 2021 NOA and 2024 NOAs are excessive or otherwise incorrect, and what the assessments should have been.
  2. In the circumstances of this case, the Tribunal must determine whether the Applicant is entitled to include the rates and land taxes incurred in respect of the CGT asset (being the Property) in calculating the costs of owning the CGT asset under subsection 110-25(4) of the ITAA 97.

EVIDENCE AND SUBMISSIONS BEFORE THE TRIBUNAL

  1. The Tribunal had the benefit of the following documents and evidence:
    • The Tribunal documents; [10]
    • The Respondent’s Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions;
    • The response and evidence filed by the Applicant in September[11] and November 2025;[12]
    • The written outline of submissions filed by the Respondent;
    • The oral evidence of Mr Halse;
    • The documents tendered by Mr Halse at the hearing; and
    • The oral submissions of each party.

The Applicant’s position

  1. The material provided by Mr Halse included a chronology and history of living arrangements and property ownership from the mid 1960’s to the sale of Parcel 2, which allowed the Tribunal to understand the full context of the current application.
  2. Mr Halse explained that when he purchased the Property in 1989, his intention at the time was to eventually build a house on the block to live in. He went as far as having plans designed by a builder.
  3. As a result of changing personal circumstances, Mr Halse decided to subdivide the Property and sell Parcel 2. He retained Parcel 1 as a possible future home site. A few years later, when it became apparent he was not going to return to Port Lincoln to live, he decided to sell Parcel 1 so he could utilise the proceeds.
  4. Mr Halse repeated these matters in his oral evidence and reiterated the arguments in respect of the illogicality and unfairness of the provision relied on at the objections stage.

CONSIDERATION

  1. The sole issue concerns whether the Applicant is entitled to include rates and land taxes in calculating the costs of owning the CGT asset under subsection 110-25(4) of the ITAA 97 as part of the cost base. The parties disagree as to the correct statutory construction of this provision and application to the facts of this case.
  2. The text of s 110-25 (4) of the ITAA 97 explicitly states that the third element of the cost base is ‘...the costs of owning the CGT asset you incurred (but only if you *acquired the asset after 20 August 1991).[13] Statutory construction must begin with the text itself. The language employed in the text of the legislation is the surest guide to the legislative intention.[14] The natural and ordinary meaning of the provision is clear and unambiguous. The costs of owning the CGT asset will only form part of the costs base if the CGT asset was acquired after 20 August 1991.
  3. It is not in dispute Mr Halse acquired the Property for the purposes of s 109-5 of the ITAA 97 on 20 October 1989, and the subsequent subdivision into Parcel 1 and Parcel 2 did not vary or change this.[15] As the date of acquisition of the relevant CGT asset was before 21 August 1991, the Applicant is unable to satisfy the test in 110-25(4) of the ITAA 97.
  4. Mr Halse submitted that based on a purposive interpretation of the law, he should be entitled to claim the rates and taxes as part of the cost base notwithstanding the acquisition date limitation. In his view, the purpose of the provision is to ensure taxpayers are taxed on real economic gains and denying the inclusion of these expenses in the costs base is inconsistent with that objective. The subsection, insofar as it imposes an acquisition date restriction, is both unfair and invalid in operation. The provision produces an arbitrary outcome between taxpayers based solely on the acquisition date of the CGT asset, rather than on nature of the expenses incurred.
  5. A plain reading of 110-25(4) reflects a deliberate intention to only include the costs of owning a CGT asset in the cost base, to CGT assets acquired after 20 August 1991. The Applicant’s argument ascribes a statutory intention to the provision which nullifies the effect of a clearly expressed position.
  6. The Applicant encouraged the Tribunal to have regard to extrinsic materials to support the purposive interpretation. These, however, are only useful insofar as they assist in clarifying or fixing the meaning of statutory words. Here the statutory language is clear and unambiguous. The extrinsic material cannot alter or displace the meaning conveyed by the text of the provision.[16]
  7. The Tribunal’s function on review of a taxation objection decision is to determine, on the material before it, whether the Commissioner’s assessment is excessive or incorrect and what the assessment should be. Questions about the validity of the legislation or whether the law ought to operate differently as a matter of policy or fairness are matters for the Courts or Parliament. In undertaking a merits review, the Tribunal cannot ignore, disregard or recast statutory provisions by reference to its own view of what would be fair, reasonable or desirable of the legislative scheme.
  8. Mr Halse also raised that he did not intend to purchase the Property as an investment, or as he has expressed it, for capital gain purposes. This submission is misconceived. Had the Property been purchased for a profit-making purpose, the sale of Parcel 1 and Parcel 2 would still have attracted taxation consequences. The CGT provisions were specifically designed to tax, as statutory income, realised gains.
  9. Mr Halse also raised that the Property was purchased with a view to later becoming his primary residence. How this prevents the application of the CGT provisions was not fully explained but in any event the Property was a vacant block and remained so after subdivision and sale. It was never the primary residence of the Applicant for the purposes of the statutory exception. [17]
  10. The Tribunal acknowledges Mr Halse presented his case respectfully and in a considered way. The Tribunal also accepts the operation of the taxation law can, on occasion, present outcomes that may be perceived as unfair by individual taxpayers. Notwithstanding this, the Tribunal is required to apply the law as enacted to the facts before it.

CONCLUSION AND DECISION

  1. The orthodox interpretation and application of the law to the facts in this case means that Mr Halse is not entitled to include the expenses associated with the ownership of the CGT asset in the cost base for CGT purposes as the Property was acquired by him before 21 August 1991.
  2. The 2021 NOA and 2024 NOA are therefore not excessive or otherwise incorrect. The Respondent’s objection decision is affirmed.
