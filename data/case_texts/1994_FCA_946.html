Abraham Gilbert Saffron and Alb Ert Gilbert Saffron v Commissioner of Taxation [1994] FCA 946; (1994) 27 Atr 596 (3 March 1994)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

ABRAHAM GILBERT SAFFRON AND ALB ERT GILBERT SAFFRON v. COMMISSIONER OF

TAXATION

Nos. 485 and 487-491 of 1993

FED No. 75/94

Number of pages - 10

Income Tax

[1994] FCA 946; (1994) 27 ATR 596

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

DAVIES(1), GUMMOW(2) AND LEE(2) JJ

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - objection to amended assessments - onus on taxpayer to establish actual taxable income to prove assessment excessive - appeal from Administrative Appeals Tribunal - whether Tribunal erred in making findings of fact and in declining to make other findings of fact - whether case confined to a discrete issue - failure to call witness.

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 sub-s.44(1)

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 para.190(b)

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Dalco [1990] HCA 3; (1990) 168 CLR 614

Jones v. Dunkel [1959] HCA 8; (1959) 101 CLR 298

Krew v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 71 ATC 4213

Ma v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1992) 92 ATC 4373

HEARING

SYDNEY, 24 February 1994 3:3:1994

Counsel for the Applicant: F.P. Carnovale

Solicitors for the Applicant: Messrs P. Wise and Co.

Counsel for the Respondent: G.K. Downes QC

S.W. Gibb

Solicitors for the Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

1. The appeals be dismissed.

2. The applicant pay the respondent's costs to be taxed.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

DAVIES J: These are appeals from decisions of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal constituted by a Presidential Member, Mr Justice Beaumont. His Honour sat as a member of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal in reviewing seven amended assessments which were issued in 1987 and which included income from several businesses, including a restaurant called the Venus Room, in Mr Saffron's taxable income. His Honour also sat as a judge of this Court on appeals relating to six amended assessments which issued in 1984 and which included additional sums from the businesses in Mr Saffron's income. There is no appeal from his Honour's judgment in relation to the 1984 assessments.

2. In any review of an objection decision, it is necessary for the Court or the Administrative Appeals Tribunal to keep in mind that s.190(b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), now s.14ZZK(H)(i) and s.14ZZO(b)(i) of the Taxation Administration Act 1953(Cth), provides that the burden of proving that an assessment is excessive shall lie upon the taxpayer. In respect of this, Walsh J said in Krew v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 71 ATC 4213 at 4216:-

"I think it is clear that the onus of proof in relation to the

main issues of fact in the case is on the appellant.

Furthermore, it is not sufficient, in my opinion, for the

appellant to make it seem probable that the overall

discrepancy ... disclosed by the betterment statement is not

wholly correct. Each year must be considered separately and

it must be shown in respect of a particular year that the

challenged assessment was wrong. If it is not proved that the

whole of the amount of income which is in dispute has been

wrongly included as taxable income, then material must be

provided upon which the amount of income which should have

been excluded can be ascertained. What I have just stated is

subject to the qualification that in some cases it may be

enough for a taxpayer to show that the assessment has

proceeded upon a wrong basis or upon wrong principles: cf.

Henderson v. F.C. of T. (1970) 70 ATC 4016; 44 ALJR 115."

In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Dalco (1990) 168 CLR 615 at 624-5, Brennan J, with whom Mason CJ, Deane, Dawson, Gaudron and McHugh JJ expressed agreement, said:-

"The manner in which a taxpayer can discharge that burden

varies with the circumstances. If the Commissioner and a

taxpayer agree to confine an appeal to a specific point of law

or fact on which the amount of the assessment depends, it will

suffice for the taxpayer to show that he is entitled to

succeed on that point. Absent such a confining of the issues

for determination, the Commissioner is entitled to rely upon

any deficiency in proof of the excessiveness of the amount

assessed to uphold the assessment, though the taxpayer is

limited to the grounds of his objection. In Gauci v. Federal

Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 54; (1975) 135 CLR 81, at p 89, Mason

J said:

`The Act does not place any onus on the

Commissioner to show that the assessments were

correctly made. Nor is there any statutory

requirement that the assessments should be

sustained or supported by evidence. The

implication of such a requirement would be

inconsistent with s.190(b) for it is a consequence

of that provision that unless the appellant shows

by evidence that the assessment is incorrect, it

will prevail.'

That view, expressed in a dissenting judgment, now prevails:

Macmine Pty Ltd v. Commissioner of Taxation (1979) 53 ALJR

362, at pp 366, 371, 381; McCormack's Case (1979) 143 CLR,

at pp 303, 306, 323."

3. However, in proceedings by way of review of an objection decision, the assessment itself is not evidence of the facts upon which the assessment was based. The review authority, the Court or the Tribunal, must come to its own decision on the facts and the issues. Walsh J said in Krew's case at 4216:-

"I have a duty to reach my own conclusions on the questions of

fact which have to be decided and to give effect to those

conclusions. ... I must make my own decisions as to the

facts."

Although the taxpayer may be untruthful and a tax evader, that factor, of itself, is no reason not to undertake a calculation of income tax as contemplated by the Act, if such a calculation can be made. As Walsh J said in Krew's case at 4219, "A witness upon whose word one cannot rely may yet give evidence which is true in part." In that case, although Walsh J held that he could not accept the appellant himself as a reliable witness, he nevertheless found that several of the facts alleged by the taxpayer were established as a matter of probability and accordingly that four of the amended assessments before the Court should be set aside. Moreover, in the calculation of the taxable incomes of years past, exact proof may not be available. As Burchett J said in Ma v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1992) 92 ATC 4373 at 4379:-

"... the making of estimates upon inexact evidence, which is

so much a feature of both judicial and administrative

decision-making, cannot be uniquely excluded from appeals

against betterment assessments."

If, despite the general untruthfulness of a witness and the lack of records, the Court or the Tribunal nevertheless feels satisfied, as a matter of probability, as to the existence of a relevant fact or factor, effect must be given to that conclusion.

4. The error of law in his Honour's decision, as alleged by Mr F.P. Carnovale, counsel for Mr Saffron, raised these points. Mr Carnovale submitted that his Honour failed to make a finding as to whether certain sums, which were recorded in books of account relating to the Venus Room against the name "Jim", were paid to a Mr James McCartney Anderson and whether, if so, it was improbable that they or any parts of them were received by, or were income of, Mr Saffron. Mr Carnovale submitted that his Honour did not deal with this important issue raised before him, but instead relied too heavily on s.190(b).

5. To determine this issue, it is necessary to examine how the case was put to his Honour for resolution. In the hearing before the learned Judge, Mr Saffron gave evidence that he had not had any interest in the subject businesses, including the Venus Room, and that he had received no income from them. The case put for the Commissioner relied principally upon certain records which had come into the Commissioner's hands and the evidence of the two bookkeepers who had maintained them. Although there was no evidence as to how the assessments had been arrived at, counsel for the Commissioner put to the learned Judge a written explanation. For example, during one period, weekly payments to "Jim" of sums totalling $2,285 were recorded. These were alleged by the Commissioner to constitute payments of $360 and $225 (total $585) in respect of rent or rent equivalent, with the balance of $1,700 being divisible, $1,000 to Mr Saffron and $700 to Mr Anderson. There were indications in the records that such a division was appropriate. That the sums included in Mr Saffron's income could be derived arithmetically from the takings books in this manner was not challenged before his Honour. The Commissioner also relied upon the evidence and records of a bank manager who said that Mr Saffron had played an active part in the financial affairs of the Venus Room.

6. Mr Carnovale submitted that notations in the takings books, such as that of 27 June 1976 "owes Abe $2,000 owe Jim $700", were not evidence of profit sharing between Mr Saffron and Mr Anderson. Mr Carnovale submitted that the notations were not susceptible of indicating a division of income or profits. However, the notations were consistent with, indeed supportive of, the view which the Commissioner adopted. His Honour was entitled to take the material into account as he did.

7. Beaumont J held that entries in the books relied upon for the 1987 assessments, which recorded deductions from takings variously described as, eg., "Expenses AS" "AS", "Abe", were evidence that these amounts were paid to Mr Saffron. His Honour held that they had been so received. His Honour made no such finding in relation to sums recorded in the name "Jim" in the books relied upon for the 1984 assessments. His Honour understandably did not do so, for the sums were not recorded as having been paid to Mr Saffron. Mr Carnovale submitted that his Honour should have made an express finding as to whether the sums recorded against the name "Jim" had been paid to Mr Anderson and whether, if they had been so paid, they or any part of them were ever paid to or received by Mr Saffron. Mr Carnovale submitted that the Tribunal erred in law by failing to deal with an issue which arose on the evidence before it.

8. However, as Mr Saffron's evidence had been that he had no interest in the Venus Room and had received no income from it and secondly, as no challenge was made to the calculation made by the Commissioner on the basis I have outlined, it was sufficient for his Honour to hold, as he did, that the Commissioner had demonstrated that Mr Saffron had an interest in the Venus Room and in its profits. It is implicit in his Honour's reasons that he regarded the sums recorded in the name of "Jim" as including profits of the business in which Mr Saffron was entitled to share. In the circumstances, his Honour necessarily concluded that Mr Saffron derived the income as and when it was earned in the businesses, or at the latest when the weekly sums were paid out to "Jim". His Honour therefore held that Mr Saffron had not satisfied the burden of proof establishing that the assessments were excessive. I should perhaps add that the findings in the 1984 assessments, which were based on different records, were consistent with the results of the 1987 assessment. I see no error in his Honour's approach.

9. Mr Carnovale also challenged his Honour's decision on the ground that his Honour declined to draw any inference adverse to the Commissioner's case from the fact that Mr Anderson was not called as a witness. Mr Carnovale relied upon the principle enunciated in Jones v. Dunkel [1959] HCA 8; (1959) 101 CLR 298. However, the evidence before his Honour showed that Mr Anderson was unwilling to give evidence and, if forced to do so, could not be relied upon to give evidence of the truth. In this circumstance, it could not be expected that the Commissioner would call Mr Anderson. The assessments were not dependent upon any statement made by Mr Anderson. The Commissioner relied upon the takings books, the evidence of the bookkeepers and the evidence of the bank manager with whom Mr Saffron had had dealings in relation to the affairs of the Venus Room. There was no occasion for the Commissioner to call as a witness a person on whose probity he could not rely. See, eg. Pappas v. New World Oil Developments [1993] FCA 375; (1993) 117 ALR 304 at 313-4.

10. For these reasons, I would dismiss the appeal with costs.

GUMMOW AND LEE JJ

These "appeals" are made on a question of law under

sub-s.44(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 from a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal ("the Tribunal") constituted by a presidential member (Beaumont J). The Tribunal decided that objections lodged by the applicant ("Saffron") to amended assessments of tax issued by the respondent ("the Commissioner") in 1987 for years of income ended 30 June 1974 to 1979 be allowed in part but otherwise disallowed.

2. According to the notices of appeal the question of law on which the appeals are brought is whether the Tribunal erred in making certain findings of fact and in declining to make other findings of fact upon the material presented to the Tribunal by the parties.

3. The nature of the question of law sought to be determined in this Court by the appeals is not disclosed by that notice of appeal, however, in the course of his argument counsel for Saffron submitted that the Tribunal had erred in law by grounding its decision upon findings of fact made on issues that the parties had not put to the Tribunal for determination. In particular, it was said that the parties had participated in a hearing before the Tribunal on a single issue, namely the amount, if any, that Saffron had received from monies drawn by one James McCartney Anderson ("Anderson") from the earnings of several businesses managed by Anderson.

4. It was submitted that because the respective cases of the parties had been so confined the Tribunal was obliged to allow the objections and set aside the amended assessments if the Tribunal found that Saffron had received no such sum, or a lesser sum, than that included by the Commissioner in the taxable income of Saffron.

5. It is to be noted that this was not a case where the parties formally agreed to confine the point to be decided by the Tribunal to a discrete issue, the decision on the objection to the assessments to be dependent upon the outcome of the determination of the agreed issue.

6. In its reasons the Tribunal expressed the main issue for determination to be as follows:

"Did Mr. Saffron derive, but omit to declare in his

returns, income in the form of monies allegedly

received in cash, in the first instance, by James

McCartney Anderson? These monies were said to have

been shared between Mr. Anderson and Mr. Saffron, in

the proportion 60:40, and to have come from funds

generated by Mr. Saffron and Mr. Anderson operating

several nightclubs and restaurants."

7. The Tribunal had before it statements made by relevant parties, documents under the hand of Saffron or prepared as a result of instructions received from him directly or indirectly and books of account. Viva voce evidence was presented by several bookkeepers who had made material entries in the books of account.

8. Saffron gave evidence that he had no interest in the businesses managed by Anderson and did not receive from Anderson any part of the proceeds of those businesses paid to Anderson.

9. The Tribunal noted that Saffron's evidence conflicted with inferences of fact to be drawn from documents such as Business Name Registrations, bank signatory forms, bank diary entries and books of account. The Tribunal did not accept Saffron's version of events, considering his testimony to be seriously undermined by the contemporary documentation and the credible testimony of the bookkeepers. Accordingly, the Tribunal concluded that Saffron failed to establish that he had no interest in the businesses conducted by Anderson.

10. It followed from that finding that it was unnecessary for the Tribunal to determine the extent to which, if any, the drawings made by Anderson from the businesses were distributed to Saffron. Nor was it necessary for the Tribunal to determine the extent of the beneficial entitlements of Saffron and Anderson in the businesses managed by Anderson.

11. The case presented by Saffron to the Tribunal was that Saffron had no interest in the businesses and no entitlement to any part of the profits earned from the conduct of those businesses. Saffron did not seek to prove that if it were found that he had an interest in the businesses his entitlement to share in the profits of the businesses was a lesser entitlement than that which the Commissioner had determined to be the fact according to such information as he had before him.

12. It can be seen from the above that the question Saffron sought to raise on appeal, namely whether the Tribunal was obliged to determine the extent to which, if any, Saffron received any part of the monies drawn by Anderson from the earnings of one of the businesses, to wit The Venus Room, was not germane to the Tribunal's finding that Saffron and Anderson were the proprietors of the respective businesses and no question of law is revealed in the relevant grounds of appeal.

13. The amended assessments issued in 1987 continued to be subject to the provisions of para.190(b) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 notwithstanding the subsequent repeal of that section and the burden of proving that the amended assessments were excessive was cast upon Saffron.

14. As Toohey J stated in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Dalco [1990] HCA 3; (1990) 168 CLR 614 at p 634:

"For this taxpayer to demonstrate that in some

respects, indeed it may be in a number of respects,

the Commissioner erred in the way in which he

attributed income to the taxpayer or otherwise dealt

with the material available to him does not provide

that an assessment was excessive. It does not prove

that the taxable income of the taxpayer was less than

the amount of taxable income shown in any of the

assessments. It was necessary for the taxpayer to

make good the proposition that his income was less;

this he failed to do in respect of any of the

assessments."

and Deane J at pp.626-627:

"In a case where the only issue between a taxpayer and

the Commissioner is whether a particular item of

income which the Commissioner has treated as

assessable income of the taxpayer was derived by the

taxpayer or by someone else, the onus which the Act

imposes upon the taxpayer will commonly be discharged

if the taxpayer establishes, on the balance of

probabilities, that the relevant income was derived

by the other person. The present is not, however,

such a case. Even if it be accepted that the

respondent succeeded in proving that particular items

of income were primarily derived by one or other of

the companies associated with him, there remained in

issue the question whether some or all of the

relevant amounts had been subsequently derived by the

respondent as payments in the nature of income made

to him or on his behalf by that company. There also

remained in issue the question whether the respondent

had derived other undisclosed income. The

respondent's failure to discharge the onus which the

Act placed upon him in respect of those remaining

issues had the consequence that he failed to

establish that the Commissioner's assessments of his

assessable income were excessive."

15. Saffron failed to make out his case that he had no entitlement to profits of the businesses managed by Anderson and, therefore, failed to show that his taxable income, after including all assessable income derived directly and indirectly by Saffron from all sources, was less than the amount of taxable income shown in the amended assessments.

16. As a further ground of appeal counsel for Saffron sought to argue as a question of law that the Tribunal had erred in accepting the explanation submitted by the Commissioner to the Tribunal for failing to call Anderson as a witness in the Commissioner's case and further erred in declining to draw any inference favourable to Saffron by reason of the Commissioner's failure to produce Anderson as a witness.

17. Without expressing any view as to whether the principle referred to in Jones v. Dunkel [1959] HCA 8; (1959) 101 CLR 298 arose in the context of the hearing before the Tribunal, it is quite apparent that no question of law arises in this case.

18. The Tribunal received evidence on behalf of the Commissioner directed to an explanation as to why Anderson would not, or could not, be produced as a witness. The Tribunal accepted that evidence and was satisfied by the facts thereby established that an adequate explanation had been provided. The evidence received by the Tribunal was contained in an affidavit and in additional viva voce evidence from the deponent who was cross-examined for approximately one hour.

19. If the ground of appeal now relied upon by Saffron is to succeed it would be necessary for Saffron to show that no part of the testimony received by the Tribunal was capable of explaining why the Commissioner had not called Anderson. The evidence did provide a reasoned explanation. Thereafter it was a matter for the Tribunal as to whether the explanation, in its terms, was sufficient in the circumstances. It has not been shown that the Tribunal's conclusion in that regard involved an error of law.

20. Since preparing the above we have had the opportunity of perusing the reasons of Davies J with which we agree.

21. The appeals should be dismissed with costs.
