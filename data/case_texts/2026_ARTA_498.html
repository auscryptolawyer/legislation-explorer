Statement of Reasons



INTRODUCTION

  1. Mr Haines (Applicant) seeks review in the Tribunal of the Commissioner’s decision to disallow the objection to the Notice of Assessment in part, and to disallow the objection to the Notice of Assessment as to Penalty in full, for the 2024 income year (Income Year).
  2. Mr Haines says the income he earned from his business activities is less than the amount assessed by the Commissioner and he is entitled to claim as a deduction a number of expenses which he says he incurred during the Income Year in respect of those business activities. Further, he says the non-commercial loss rules do not apply in the circumstances of his case and he is not liable for an administrative penalty. The Commissioner disagrees.

PROCEDURAL HISTORY

  1. Mr Haines was employed as a Senior Manager with Ship Bob Australia Pty Ltd (ShipBob) between 1 July 2023 and 21 December 2023.
  2. On 25 July 2023 the company Unity Scents Pty Ltd was registered. The current directors are Mr Haines, [REDACTED] (appointed 12 November 2024) and [REDACTED] (appointed 3 June 2025). Mr [REDACTED] was also a director between 9 August 2024 and 12 November 2024. The current shareholders are One Percent Holdings Pty Ltd and Beoci Holdings Company Pty Ltd.
  3. On 29 April 2024, the company AI Army Pty Ltd (AI Army) was registered. The sole director and shareholder at the date of registration was Mr Haines. AI Army was deregistered on 4 June 2025.
  4. On 10 July 2024, the company Haines One Corp Pty Ltd was registered. Mr Haines is the sole director and the sole shareholder is One Percent Holdings Pty Ltd.
  5. On 5 August 2024, Mr Haines lodged his income tax return for the Income Year. In that document he listed his main occupation as ‘Director- Executive’. He reported receiving employment income of $162,724 which was comprised of wages from ShipBob totalling $95,832, allowances from ShipBob totalling $3,053 and $66,892 in compensation payments from Allianz Australia Workers Compensation (Victoria) Limited. He also claimed $98,865 in work related deductions.
  6. Mr Haines also reported business income of $2631 and a net loss of $128,261 after claiming business related expenses of $138,892.

  1. On 20 August 2025, the Commissioner advised the Applicant of his intention to conduct an audit of the tax return. As part of the audit process, Mr Haines provided evidence in support of his claim. The evidence included invoices, bank statements, Shopify sales information, a lease agreement, a spreadsheet which set out the claimed expenses and the rent and utilities calculations.

  1. The Commissioner finalised in August 2025. He determined that having regard to the information provided, Mr Haines was entitled to work related expenses of $257 and business related expenses of 35,960. Some of those deductions had been initially claimed as work related deductions but the audit team found that they were more properly characterised as business expenses.   1. The Commissioner also increased Mr Haines business income to $7607.00 which resulted in a net loss of $25,339. The Commissioner also determined that that loss ought to be deferred under the non-commercial loss rules that are contained in Division 35 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) (ITAA 97) and imposed an administrative penalty of 50% on the basis that the Applicant was reckless as to the operation of the taxation law.
  2. On 5 and 6 September 2024, Mr Haines objected to the Notice of Assessment and the Penalty Assessment. During the objection, the Applicant provided additional evidence to the Commissioner including receipts, a spreadsheet setting out the transactions, a document which provided an explanation of the expenses and a travel diary.
  3. The Commissioner finalised the objection on 6 November 2024. He decided that:

LEGISLATIVE PROVISIONS

The key provisions

  1. It is appropriate to begin by setting out the key provisions from the ITAA 97 and the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) (TAA 53) which are relevant to this case.

Income

  1. Section 6-5 of the ITAA97 relevantly provides:
    (1) Your assessable income includes income according to ordinary concepts, which is called ordinary income.

    (2) If you are an Australian resident, your assessable income includes the *ordinary income you *derived directly or indirectly from all sources, whether in or out of Australia, during the income year.

General deductions

  1. Section 8-1 of the ITAA 97 provides:
    General deductions

    (1) You can deduct from your assessable income any loss or outgoing to the extent that:

    (1) (a) it is incurred in gaining or producing your assessable income; or

    (1) (b) it is necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing your assessable income.

    (2) However, you cannot deduct a loss or outgoing under this section to the extent that:

    (a) It is a loss or outgoing of capital, or of a capital nature; or

    (b) It is a loss or outgoing of a private or domestic nature; or

    (c) It is incurred in relation to gaining or producing your *exempt income or *non-assessable non-exempt income; or

    (d) A provision of the Act prevents you from deducting it.

  2. The operative provision has a positive and a negative part which both need to be satisfied by the taxpayer. Not only must the loss or outgoing have the requisite connection to the production of income, it must not also be prohibited by one of the conditions in s 8-1(2).
  3. It is generally accepted that the term ‘incurred in gaining or producing assessable income’ in s 8-1(1)(a) of the ITAA 97 means incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable income. For an expense to be incurred in gaining or producing assessable income it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the expense should be in whatever is productive of that assessable income.[1]
  4. However, not just any casual connection is enough to satisfy the statutory test. Deductibility requires a more immediate connection. It is not sufficient that it is for the purpose of deriving income, it must be in the course of the activity itself.[2]
  5. When determining what is productive of an employee’s assessable income, consideration should be given to the scope of the income producing activities. This is a question of fact and degree and is not confined to the day-to-day activities performed. [3]
  6. The classic exposition of the word ‘necessarily incurred’ in the context of s 8-1(1)(b) of the ITAA, which refers to losses or outgoings relative to carrying on a business, is found in Ronpibon Tin NL v Federal Commissioner of Taxation[4] where Latham CJ, Rick., Dixon, McTiernan and Webb said:
    The word ‘necessary’ no doubt limits the operation of the alternative, but probably it is intended to mean not more than ‘clearly appropriate and adapted for’.
  7. An outgoing will therefore be necessarily incurred, if the expense was reasonably capable of being seen as desirable or appropriate from the point of view of the pursuit of the business ends of the business.[5]
  8. The second limb contained in s 8-1(2) of the ITAA 97, denies a deduction for a loss or outgoing that is of a private, domestic or capital nature. These terms are not defined in the legislation but it is generally accepted that they are those expenses which are of a personal or private capacity or relate to household or domestic affairs of the taxpayer. Capital or capital nature refers to items that are not regular or recurrent but those expenses expected to have an enduring or lasting benefit.[6]
  9. A deductibility of an expense is generally determined objectively, without reference to a taxpayers subjective motive or intention.[7]
  10. Where expenses are to be apportioned, there is no specific formular or test but rather what is a fair and reasonable apportionment in the circumstances of the particular case.[8]

Substantiation

  1. Division 900 of the ITAA 1997 sets out substantiation rules that apply to certain expenses that are incurred by an individual taxpayer. Those expenses must be substantiated.[9]
  2. Subdivision 900-B applies to ‘work expenses’ which are defined as a loss or outgoing incurred in producing your salary or wages.[10] Subsection 900-15(1) outlines the requirements that a taxpayer must satisfy for work expenses. The relevant requirements under subsection 900-B are that:
    (a) the expense claimed must be deductible under a provision of the income tax acts; and

    (b) the taxpayer must obtain and retain written evidence to support the expense.

  3. Written evidence[11] for goods or services purchased is a document from the supplier that shows the following:
    (a) name or business name of the supplier;

    (b) amount of the expense;

    (c) nature of the goods or services purchased; and

    (d) date the expense was incurred, and

    (e) date the document was produced.

  4. In circumstances where such a document has not been obtained, the Commissioner will accept other forms of evidence, in accordance with subsection 900-115(3):
    (a) if the document does not show the day the expense was incurred, you may use a bank statement or other reasonable, independent evidence that shows when it was paid;

    (b) if the document the supplier gave you does not specify the nature of the goods or services, you may write in the missing details yourself before you lodge your *income tax return for the income year.

  5. It is an established principle in the case law that a taxpayer who does not obtain or retain appropriate records of deductable expenditure faces an uphill battle to discharge their onus.[12]

Non-commercial losses

  1. The purpose of Division 35 of the ITAA 97 is to prevent individuals from non-commercial business activities being offset against other assessable income in the year the loss is incurred. The division does not apply to activities that do not constitute carrying on a business. The provision is not a complete bar to offsetting the losses, they are simply deferred.
  2. For an individual to be able to deduct business related outgoings in excess of their income from that business pursuant to Division 35 of the ITAA 1997, they must:
    (a) Have taxable income for the year of less than $250,000;3 and

    (b) The business activity has to satisfy the tests to establish that it is commercial in nature.

  3. As set out in section 35-30 of the ITAA 1997, an individual taxpayer cannot deduct outgoings for business activity in excess of the income derived from that business if:
    (a) the amount of assessable income from the business activity for the year; or

    (b) where the taxpayer started to carry on the business activity, or stopped carrying it on, during the year--a reasonable estimate of what would have been the amount of that assessable income if they had carried on that activity throughout the year;

is at least $20,000.

Administrative Penalties

  1. The law concerning administrative penalties is contained in Division 284 of Schedule 1 of the TAA 53. Section 284-75 provides as follows:
    284–75 Liability to penalty

    (1)You are liable to an administrative penalty if:

    (a)you make a statement to the Commissioner or to an entity that is exercising powers or performing functions under a *taxation law (other than the *Excise Acts); and

    (b)the statement is false or misleading in a material particular, whether because of things in it or omitted from it.

    Note: This section applies to a statement made by your agent as if it had been made by you: see section 284–25.

    ...

    Exceptions to subsections (1) and (4)

    (5)You are not liable to an administrative penalty under subsection (1) or (4) for a statement that is false or misleading in a material particular if you, and your *agent (if relevant), took reasonable care in connection with the making of the statement.

    (6)You are not liable to an administrative penalty under subsection (1) or (4) if:

    (a)you engage a *registered tax agent or BAS agent; and

    (b)you give the registered tax agent or BAS agent all relevant taxation information; and (c) the registered tax agent or BAS agent makes the statement; and

    (d)the false or misleading nature of the statement did not result from:

    (i)intentional disregard by the registered tax agent or BAS agent of a *taxation law (other than the *Excise Acts); or

    (ii)recklessness by the agent as to the operation of a taxation law (other than the Excise Acts).

    (7)If you wish to rely on subsection (6), you bear an evidential burden in relation to paragraph (6)(b).

    ...

  1. The Commissioner must give written notice to the taxpayer of the liability to pay the penalty and of the reasons why the taxpayer is liable to pay the penalty unless the Commissioner decides to remit all of the penalty[14] Otherwise, the Commissioner must make an assessment of an amount of an administrative penalty under Division 284 of the TAA 53.[15]
  2. Where an administrative penalty has been imposed, the Respondent may remit all or part of it[16] Although the discretion is framed broadly, the power to remit must be exercised for a proper purpose, in accordance with the objects of the TAA 53.[17]
  3. A taxpayer that is dissatisfied with such an assessment may object against it in the manner set out in Pt IVC of the TAA 53.

The Burden Of Proof

  1. Section 14ZZK of the TAA 53 provides:
    Grounds of objection and burden of proof

    On an application for review of a reviewable objection decision:

    (a) the Applicant is, unless the Tribunal orders otherwise, limited to the grounds stated in the taxation objection to which the decision relates; and

    (b)the Applicant has the burden of proving:

    (i) if the taxation decision concerned is an assessment—that the assessment is excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessment should have been; or

    (ii) in any other case—that the taxation decision concerned should not have been made or should have been made differently.

  2. In order to discharge the onus of proof, the Applicant must positively prove their actual taxable income and in doing so, show that the amount of money for which tax is levied by the assessment exceeds the actual liability. [18]
  3. It is for the taxpayer to establish any fact demonstrating the assessment to be excessive.[19] The Commissioner is not required to defend the assessment and is entitled to put the Applicant to proof of any aspect of his case.[20]
  4. The standard of proof that the taxpayer must meet is the civil standard, being the balance of probabilities. The decision maker, may in evaluating the evidence as a whole, be cautious of a taxpayer’s self -serving statements.[21] This does not necessarily mean that a self -serving statement should not be believed or accepted, but some other corroborative evidence would normally be required.[22]

ISSUES

  1. In a proceeding such as this where s 144ZZK (b)(i) of the Taxation Administration Act (Cth) (TAA 53) applies, the overarching issue is whether the assessment is incorrect or excessive and what the assessment should be. In the specific circumstances of this case, this requires the Applicant to establish
    (a) in respect of the Notice of Assessment:
    (i) he is entitled to claim expenses totalling $106,439.00 as deductions pursuant to s 8-1 of the ITAA 97 (Deduction Issue);

    (ii) his business losses should not be deferred under s 35-10 of the ITAA 97 (Non-commercial loss Issue); and

    (iii) the assessable income from business activities is $2,631 (Income Issue).

    (b) In respect of the Penalty Assessment

    (i) The penalty should not have been imposed at the level of recklessness; and/ or

    (ii) There is a basis upon which the penalty should be remitted in part or in full.

    (Penalty Issue)

MATERIAL BEFORE THE TRIBUNAL

  1. At the hearing, the Tribunal had the benefit of the following material:
    • The Tribunal documents;[23]
    • The Supplementary Tribunal documents;[24]
    • Witness statement of the Applicant with various attachments;[25]
    • The Statements of Facts, Issues and Contentions of the Applicant;[26]
    • The oral evidence of Mr Haines given at the hearing;
    • The Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions of the Respondent;
    • The Submissions filed by the Applicant which also included evidence he says was provided during the objection but did not form part of the Tribunal documents; and
    • The submissions filed on behalf of the Commissioner.
  2. Mr Haines represented himself throughout the audit, objection and review. He does not have any accounting or legal qualification and I have taken that circumstance into account in considering the way in which the Applicant's case was presented and the manner in which the evidence was given.
  3. In his witness statement, Mr Haines explained that the statement was made in support of his Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions and that he relied upon the documents provided with the affidavit. Those documents included a spreadsheet that identified the expenses claimed, invoices, bank statements, an explanation of the expenses and documents he had obtained as part of an FOI process.
  4. The Applicant clearly invested considerable time and effort in the preparation of his case for hearing. He addressed the Tribunal confidently and respectfully. In addition to his oral evidence in chief (which was directed in part by questions from the Tribunal), Mr Haines was cross-examined by the Commission for the best part of two days.
  5. Mr Haines explained to the Tribunal that he was employed as a Senior Partner Manager for ShipBob until December in the Relevant Year. ShipBob is a global e-commerce fulfilment and logistics platform. In this role he worked across a large portfolio of partners including Amazon, Shopify and Australia Post and engaged in business development and marketing activities related to e-commerce.
  6. He says that from time to time he was required to attend events or speaking engagements on behalf of ShipBob and this may involve travel. Mr Haines was provided a corporate credit card which could be used to pay for incidentals and employees could also apply to have their expenses reimbursed.
  7. The Applicant conceded that some of his expenses during his employment were reimbursed or met by using the corporate credit card. There were, however, some expenses that he met personally because the corporate credit card did not work or it was impractical to seek reimbursement.
  8. In addition to his employment with ShipBob, in late 2020 he decided to start his own business activities. The first of these was Unity Scents, a fragrance business established to develop and market roll-on perfumes. The business name and concept were registered in December 2022 but it didn't formally commence until February 2023. Initially the business was run under the Applicant’s personal Australian Business Number (ABN) as a sole trader. At this stage the focus was on setting up the manufacturing and putting in place the structures in of the trade, including establishing the website.
  9. Mr Haines initially gave evidence that the business activities were ‘transitioned’ into the company but not until 2025. This also explains why there are some documents in the name of the company in the Income Year. Unity Scents Pty Ltd did not record any income or deductions in its tax return for the Relevant Year. Even though there were no income producing activities attributable the company, he still had responsibilities as the director and was incurring expenses personally while it was in the process of transitioning over to the corporate structure.
  10. Business Activity Statements (BAS) were lodged by Unity Scents which reported no sales or expenditure, with the exception of one period. Mr Haines explained this was on the advice of his accountant who advised that he should cancel the GST registration given the turnover was going to be below $75,000. It was not clear whether the BAS were returned as ‘not required’ or as ‘nil’. When the Tribunal queried whether the BAS did not accurately reflect the activities of Unity Scents, Mr Haines reiterated the advice he received was that it was permissible in circumstances where he would not be claiming in credits.
  11. The Applicant also explained that Unity Scents also had a number of subsidiary activities that operated under the Unity Scents umbrella. These included Talking Ship and Haines One. These enterprises were concerned with promoting the personal image and brand of Mr Haines and involved speaking engagements, webinars and the like. Haines One transitioned into its own company upon the registration of Haines One Pty Ltd.
  12. Mr Haines is also the director of AI Army. The Applicant explained that unlike Unity Scents, it has only ever traded as a company. Its business was concerned with developing an AI application for e-commerce businesses.
  13. The Applicant gave evidence that his employment with ShipBob, work in developing Unity Scents, and company directorships are one continuous entrepreneurial income-producing undertaking. In this context many of the expenses claimed are relevant to multiple business ventures and income-generating activities. The expenses had a dual or overlapping character and were essential for his ShipBob employment as well as the Unity Scents and corporate ventures. He confirmed that no expenses relative to company activities have been reimbursed by those companies.
  14. Where an expenses was plainly private in nature the Applicant gave evidence it was not claimed. As an example, Mr Haines took two holidays in the Relevant Year, although incidental entrepreneurial work occurred during those trips, none of those expenses are claimed as part of the review.
  15. Mr Haines was cross examined on a number of expenses claimed in the proceedings. His evidence in respect of that cross examination can be summarised as follows:
    • He strenuously denied that he ever represented to the Commissioner that the travel expenses related to his employment with ShipBob. He has consistently maintained that the majority of the expenses were in respect of Unity Scents but there were a few occasions where he undertook travel for ShipBob and incurred expenses that have not been reimbursed.
    • The accommodation expenses for 31 January 2024 totalling $1063 were in respect of a trip to Canberra in order to speak with business advisors and mentors and to scope out potential salons and barber shops to stock Unity Scents products. The fact the travel occurred on the weekend was of no consequence.
    • Accommodation expenses incurred on 20 October 2023 totalling $2325 were intended to be used to film marketing material for Unity Scents products. Unfortunately, that didn’t eventuate due to unforeseen events. Mr Haines stayed the night in the penthouse but moved back into a hotel the following night.
    • In respect of two Uber receipts dated 18th of February 2024, those receipts suggest the Applicant took two trips to Melbourne airport on the same day. Mr Haines was unable to say exactly what the travel was in relation to but was confident it was either to attend a meeting or to catch a flight for work purposes. The travel did not coincide with any of the interstate trips referred to in the travel diary.
    • In response to a question directed to the number of Uber trips undertaken in the Relevant Year (227), Mr Haines told the Tribunal that he attended a lot of work related events. He denied that any of the Uber expenses were private in nature.
    • Mr Haines was questioned about the Uber expenses contained within the spreadsheet. He explained that the total amount of $5484 reflects all of the Uber transactions incurred on his account. Critically, he then applied a reasonable apportionment based on what he considered to be work related expenses to get to the final figure of $2000. He denied it was a guess and stated that he reviewed the trips on the app and from there he could determine which ones were work related.
    • Mr Haines agreed that those calculations were not placed into evidence before the Tribunal. In his view he is not required to keep substantiation of every trip made for business purposes. He is an entrepreneur of multiple business and the estimate is both reasonable and likely to be less than the actual amount. He also stated that in this case it was reasonable as he was required to produce the evidence at short notice to the Commissioner and during a particularly difficult period personally.
    • In respect of microphones, the Applicant stated they were to be used to create high quality marketing videos, online content and conduct virtual meetings for Unity Scents, AI Army and Haines One. He said videos in which the microphones are visible can be accessed from the Unity Scents Instagram and YouTube pages. Those videos were not in evidence.
    • An expense in respect of luxury bedding was also claimed as deductible expense. Mr Haines stated the sheets were purchased in order to research the shopping cart and shipping process. The sheets were not later returned because he thought they may make a good backdrop for product imagery. He gave evidence they were not used for personal use.
    • In respect of the Uber eats expenses, Mr Haines explained that he used the same methodology as the Uber travel expenses. He went into the app and determined the purchases that coincided with business trips.
    • Mr Haines was asked about self-education expenses identified as being for professional development totalling $319.99. The specific product was a deck of cards and each card contained a framework on how to tell a specific story. Mr Haines advised the Tribunal that storytelling and marketing is central to a brand and the purpose of the expense was to develop the most appropriate way to tell those stories to market products to different audiences. The products was intended to be used across all his business interests.
    • Mr Haines was taken to another transaction in the excel spreadsheet which was for ‘data science analytics an AI business in the real world’. The Tribunal was told it was an introduction course for AI agents and it was required by Mr Haines in his capacity as the director for AI Army but also his other business more generally.
    • When asked about the business structures relative to the Unity Scents business, Mr Haines conceded that Unity Scents Pty Ltd was registered on the 25th of July 2023. He said that even after the registration of the Company, some business activities were undertaken as a sole trader and expenses were claimed on that basis.
    • Mr Haines stated that after incorporation he undertook tasks in his capacity as a director of Unity Scents Pty Ltd and at the same time Unity Scents operated as a sole trader and he was permitted to do so. This also explains why some of the invoices appear to be billed to Unity Scents Pty Ltd.
    • In response to questions about the Shopify records, Mr Haines stated they were not originally issued to the Company. The details were changed as a result of the information in the system being updated to Unity Scents Pty Ltd. Notwithstanding the reference to the company the relationship was with him as a sole trader.
    • Mr Haines explained the redactions in the bank statements were to remove private expenditure. He agreed that some of the redactions were made to remove references to Pty Ltd in the account owners name (which was Unity Scents Pty Ltd). He says he did this on the advice of his accountants.
    • Mr Haines stated that the bank accounts were owned and operated by him and whether it had the company as the account owner was irrelevant. In answer to a questions from the Tribunal, Mr Haines accepted that the account was opened on behalf of the company but he insisted the transactions did not relate to that corporate entity but rather the sole trader. He also conceded the accountants advice was that given the account holder was the company, all the transactions would be attributed to the corporate entity by the ATO.
    • Mr Haines claimed around $11,000 in home office occupancy expenses. He advised the Tribunal business products were stored in lots of different areas of the home and that evidence of the work related footprint were provided to the Commissioner. In any event the penalty should be remitted as in substance there has been an unjust result.

CONSIDERATION OF ISSUES

Preliminary matters

  1. During cross-examination, there were several times where Mr Haines did not directly answer the question but made submissions on how he considered the law applied to the circumstances of his case. It was necessary for the Tribunal to direct the Applicant to answer the question asked on several occasions.
  2. Some of the Applicant’s evidence and submissions focused on the manner in which the Commissioner conducted the audit and objection process. Although I accept that those matters may be of significance to Mr Haines and have been a source of frustration, inconvenience or distress, they are not outlined or discussed in any detail in these reasons as they are extraneous to the Tribunal’s task.
  3. In cases of this nature, an Applicant is required to establish the relevant assessment is excessive or incorrect and what the assessment should be. There is no onus on the Commissioner to show that the assessments were reasonable or supported by evidence and the Tribunal does not need to be satisfied that the assessments represent the correct and preferable decision.[27]
  4. In a taxation review, the process by which the Commissioner made the assessment or objection decision is completely irrelevant to what the Tribunal must decide[28] Even if an Applicant can establish errors in the process or cast doubt on aspects of the Commissioner's reasoning, that does not discharge the statutory onus placed on the Applicant to prove their actual taxable income and that it is less than the amount assessed.[29]
  5. Discharging that burden requires the Applicant to do more than provide a tranche of documents such as bank statements, invoices or the like. It is incumbent upon the taxpayer to specifically identify the evidence relied upon and demonstrate how that evidence establishes is the factual matters necessary to show the assessments are incorrect.
  6. It is not the role of the Tribunal to identify or obtain additional evidence on behalf of a party or to reconstruct the parties case from materials that have not been tendered. Similarly, although the Commissioner has an obligation to assist the Tribunal to reach the correct and preferable decision, that obligation does not extend to identifying additional evidence that might assist the Applicant in discharging the statutory burden. So, while the proceedings have an inquisitorial feature it remains the responsibility of each party to identify and present the necessary evidence to establish their case.

The Deduction Issue

  1. In circumstances where the Applicant is required to establish that an expense was incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable income or was necessarily incurred in the production of business income, identifying the nature and scope of the income producing activities is of significance. It is by reference to the income producing activity that the requisite nexus of the expense is established by the taxpayer.
  2. Nexus requires more than a general or broad connection between an expense and an income producing activity. In the case of an employee, the expense must be productive of the work duties. Where the claim is said to arise in the capacity of a director, the relevant question is whether the expense is incurred in gaining or producing income derived in that capacity. Similarly, where the claim is said to arise from the carrying on of a business, the expense must be necessarily incurred in carrying out that business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income.
  3. It is common ground between the parties that Mr Haines was employed with ShipBob until December 2023. He worked from home on a fulltime basis and was responsible for managing clients in the Asia Pacific region. Mr Haines gave evidence that he was required to travel from time to time to attend events. On occasion expenses associated with those activities was not reimbursed.
  4. The nature and scope of the remaining activities of the Applicant is less clear. Mr Haines contends that during the Income Year he was engaged in several entrepreneurial ventures. These included:
    • Unity Scents, which was primarily directed to the development and distribution of perfume products;
    • AI Army Pty Ltd which was described as a technology venture developing artificial intelligence applications for e-commerce businesses; and
    • Haines One which was associated with personal branding an entrepreneurial promotion.
  5. A difficulty arises from the imprecision in the evidence in respect of the specific structure and nature of the relevant activities said to be conducted for each pursuit. The Applicant’s evidence was that the Unity Scents business initially operated as a sole trader and later transitioned over to the company. There is some material before the Tribunal which suggests that the Unity Scents business may also have operated as a partnership. No partnership agreement was produced or evidence directed to when it commenced or ceased.
  6. Mr Haines also suggested that after the establishment of the Unity Scents Pty Ltd some aspects of the broader Unity Scents venture were conducted through the sole trader enterprise while other aspects were undertaken through the corporate entity. The evidence however did not clearly establish the nature of those alleged divisions. In particular, the evidence did not specifically identify which activities were undertaken by the Applicant personally as a sole trader, which activities were undertaken by the corporate entity and when particular activities transitioned from one structure to another.
  7. While the Applicant asserted there was no overlap between the activities undertaken through the sole trader and the company, his evidence failed to identify with any precision the tasks or operation undertaken by each entity. In the absence of such evidence the Tribunal is unable to determine with any certainty which entity incurred the particular expense or derived the associated income.
  8. Apart from Mr Haines assertions, there was little contemporaneous evidence demonstrating that the business continued to operate as a sole trader enterprise after the establishment of Unity Scent Pty Ltd. Following this date, which was relatively early in the Income Year, the company held its own bank account and invoices were issued to it.
  9. Where business activities are conducted through a company, expenses incurred in carrying on that business are generally expenses of the company. The fact that a director may personally have paid for those expenses does not convert them into the director's personal expenses for taxation purposes. The Applicant also suggested that a number of the deductions were incurred by him personally because he was the person who met the expense. That approach reflects a misunderstanding of the legal meaning of when an expense is incurred for the purposes of the income tax legislation.
  10. Whether an expense has been incurred is not determined by the timing or who physically makes payment. Rather, the relevant question is who has become definitively committed to the liability. In other words the question is which entity is legally obliged to pay the expense. Private arrangements under which one person pays an expense on behalf of another, or is later reimbursed for it, do not alter the identity of the player taxpayer who properly incurred the expense.
  11. In respect of the non-perfume related aspects of Unity Scents (for example the activities of Talking Ship or Haines One before incorporation) the evidence before the Tribunal did not clearly establish the nature and scope of the income producing activities said to be undertaken through each of these enterprises. The Applicant in his evidence described the activities in broad terms such as ‘consulting’ or ‘promotion’. There was no evidence demonstrating how these business operated, exactly what services or goods were supplied, or how income was to be generated.
  12. Mr Haines stated some of the expenses were incurred in connection to his directorship of Unity Scents Pty Ltd, AI Army Pty Ltd and Haines One Pty Ltd In the case of AI Army, the company was incorporated only a couple of months before the end of the financial year, Haines One Pty Ltd was not incorporated until the following financial year. Any expenses that predate the Applicant’s appointment as a director, were incurred at a point to soon and would not be deductable in any event.
  13. Nor was there any evidence directed to the nature of scope of the director duties. Mr Haines did not derive director’s fees in the Income Year (or any later year). In those circumstances it's difficult to establish any expenses said to relate to those roles were incurred in gaining or producing assessable income.
  14. In evidence were a substantial number of invoices said to support the claimed deductions. I accept those documents indicate that certain expenses occurred but the existence of an invoice alone does not establish the expenditure was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income. In some instances, the nature of the expense may suggest a potential connection to the income producing activity. But for some invoices, further evidence may be required, especially if on their face they appear to be private in nature.
  15. There are also some invoices that are in the names of the companies or other individuals. Without more, the Tribunal cannot be satisfied they were incurred by the taxpayer or had the requisite connection to his income producing activities.
  16. Another difficulty with the Applicant’s case concerns the absence of independent evidence corroborating the connection between the claimed expenses and any income producing activity. Much of the Applicant’s evidence consisted of general assertions that particular expenses were incurred in connection with his various entrepreneurial ventures. However, those assertions were rarely supported by objective evidence identifying the specific activity to which the expense related.
  17. While the Tribunal accepts that the taxpayer's oral evidence may be relied upon inappropriate circumstances, the absence of corroborating material assumes particular significance where the claimed expenses are numerous and include items that may readily be private or domestic in nature.
  18. The manner in which the Applicant identified and quantified some of the expenses was also a source of difficulty. In several instances the Applicant adopted a broad apportionment approach. For example, in relation to the ride share and food expenses the Applicant reviewed his transaction history and estimated the overall business percentage. That approach does not satisfy the statutory test. The relevant question is not whether a proportion of the overall expenditure might relate to work activities in a general sense. Rather, the Applicant must establish the purpose for which particular expenses were incurred and that they were incurred in gaining or producing or necessarily incurred in the production of assessable income. Given this uncertainty the Tribunal cannot be satisfied that the apportionments adopted were fair or reasonable.
  19. The Applicant also took the view that it was unnecessary to allocate expenses with any precision between the different entities or activities with which he was involved because they form part of a broader entrepreneurial undertaking. That approach is problematic if aspects of the undertaking do not form part of his income producing activities.
  20. Mr Haines suggested that any specificity in allocating expenses between the different entities was unnecessary because there was no double dipping, and the tax consequence was immaterial. Although there may not have been duplicity in claims it is not the case that the tax consequences were immaterial. Claiming the expenses in his personal return would result in a refund, which would not have been the consequence had the expenses been claimed by the company.
  21. Having regard to the evidentiary difficulties outlined above, Mr Haines has not discharged the onus of demonstrating that the assessment is excessive. A number of the claimed expenses are discussed in greater detail below by way of illustration of the evidentiary issues, however it is unnecessary to consider every aspect of the claim in light of the deficiencies.
  22. Even if some minor expenses were established, this is insufficient in establishing the assessment as a whole was excessive. In this regard, a large number of deductions were allowed on the assumption that Unity Scents operated as a sole trader. For the reasons already explained, this may be hard to justify. Thus, even if some expenses were hypothetically allowable, the Applicant has not demonstrated that the total assessment is excessive and the assessment must stand

Non-commercial Losses Issue

  1. The Applicant contends that the losses arising from the Unity Scents activities should not be deferred under division 35 of the ITA a 1997. Relevantly, section 3510 provides that losses from business activity conducted by an individual will generally be deferred unless one of the statutory tests is satisfied. The Applicant says he has provided a reasonable estimate of assessable income for the Unity Scents business of at least $20,000 under s 35-30, such that s 35-10(2) (which operates to defer a net loss from business) ought not to apply.
  2. During the audit, the Applicant provided an estimate of assessable income in the amount of $37,624.40 but later revised that estimate to $67,740.75 at objection. In support of that calculation, Mr Haines explained it he had adopted a part year trading rationale of $31,572 (base annual projection extrapolating the May 2023 sales), plus $4,604.25 in sales adjustments (for events like Black Friday) and $31,564.50 in wholesale orders.
  3. This estimate is not accepted by the Tribunal for the following reasons:
    • The Applicants estimate cherry picked the only month in which sales were made;
    • any estimate of increased sales during peak periods had no proven reliable basis and not an appropriate estimate.
    • two invoices which the Applicant says are wholesale orders totalling $31,564.50 are issued by “Haines One Corp Pty Ltd” and would not be income of the Unity Scents business even if ultimately derived.

Penalty Issue

  1. There was a suggestion in the Applicants submissions about the application of the safe harbour provisions in subdivision 284-B of Schedule 1 of the TAA 53. Those provisions only have application where a statement is made by registered tax agent or BAS agent on behalf of the taxpayer. In this case Mr Haines lodged the relevant income tax return himself.
  2. Mr Haines says he filed the return based on the advice obtained from accountants and legal advisors. However, the Tribunal was not provided with the written advice said to have been given, the tax returns prepared by those advisors or any other evidence from the advisors themselves explaining the advice provided or the assumptions upon which it was based.
  3. The material that is before the Tribunal suggests that the advisors initially prepared tax returns on the basis that the activities were conducted by Unity Scents Pty Ltd. The evidence further suggests returns were not ultimately lodged in the form prepared by the advisors and Mr Haines lodged his own return reflecting a different approach. In those circumstances the Tribunal cannot determine the content of the advice said to have been provided or whether the Applicant in fact acted in accordance with that advice. Is not appropriate to infer the advice supported the position adopted by the Applicant as reflected in the return in this case.
  4. Mr Haines also have evidence that the Airwallex statements being in the name of the company may raise issues from the ATO.
  5. As can be gleaned from the table in s 284-90 to Schedule 1 of the TAA 53, recklessness suggests conduct that is more blameworthy than a failure to take reasonable care to comply with a taxation law but less culpable than an intentional disregard of a taxation law.
  6. The concept of recklessness was explained by the Federal Court as follows[30]:
    Recklessness in this context means to include in a tax statement material upon which the Act or regulations are to operate, knowing that there is a real, as opposed to fanciful, risk that the material may be incorrect, or be grossly indifferent as to whether or not the material is true and correct, and that a reasonable person in the position of the statement-maker would see there was a real risk that the Act and regulations may not operate correctly to lead to the assessment of the proper tax payable because of the content of the tax statement. So understood, the proscribed conduct is more than mere negligence and must amount to a gross carelessness.
  7. Recklessness is assessed objectively and involves conduct that demonstrates indifference to a rule risk that a taxation law may not operate correctly because of the contents of a tax statement. The concept has been described as involving conduct there's more blameworthy than a failure to take reasonable care less culpable than intentional disregard.
  8. I am satisfied that the imposition of the penalties was appropriate. The evidence indicates that the Applicant adopted a position regarding the deductibility of expenses without clearly identifying the income producing activity to which those expenses relate, the Applicant treated expenses in curd in connection with corporate entities as if they were his own personal expenses, the Applicant relied upon estimates of apportionment and altered the basis upon which the returns had initially been prepared.
  9. The Commissioner has a discretionary power to remit a penalty, including for a false or misleading statement. Whilst the discretion is framed without limitation, the exercise of that discretion is guided by Practice Statement Law Administration 2012/5 ‘Administration of the false or misleading statement penalty – where there is a shortfall amount’ (PSLA 2012/5).
  10. Consistent with the principles in PSLA 2012/ 5 remission in full or in part of a shortfall penalty arising from a false or misleading statement will generally only occur where the result is unjust having regard to all the circumstances.[31] There was no evidence before the Tribunal to suggest this was the case.

CONCLUSION AND DECISION

  1. the tribunal is satisfied that the Applicant has failed to discharge the burden imposed upon him in demonstrating that the assessments are excessive and what they should be because
    • There was significant uncertainty as to the nature and scope of the income producing activities said to have been undertaken by the Applicant including whether those activities were conducted in his personal capacity, by corporate entities or in the course of his directorship roles. There was limited corroborating evidence to establish that the expenses claimed were of an income producing character in this context and the inherent capital or personal nature of some of the expenses claimed and Issues with apportionment.
    • The Applicant could not establish by reference to credible evidence that non-commercial loss provisions should not apply;
    • The Applicant relied in disputing the Commissioner’s assessment of business income but did not positively show what it should be;
    • There was no evidence the Applicant relied on advice in the preparation of his tax return and the conduct was consistent with recklessness and not grounds of remission apply.
  2. On this basis the objection decision is affirmed.
