Commissioner of Taxation v Betro Harrison Constructions Pty Ltd [1978] FCA 32; (1978) 37 FLR 150 (18 August 1978)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION v. BETRO HARRISON CONSTRUCTIONS PTY. LTD. (1978) 37

FLR 150

Income Tax - Companies

COURT

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

GENERAL DIVISION

Bowen C.J.(1), Brennan(2) and Deane(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Bad debts - Allowable deductions - Scheme of arrangement - Whether scheme effected change in beneficial ownership of debts - Whether at time of writing off debts, they were debts owed to taxpayer - Companies Act, 1962-1966 (S.A.), s. 181 (2) - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth.), ss. 63 (1), 80.

Companies - Scheme of arrangement - Change of beneficial ownership of book debts - Companies Act, 1962-1966 (S.A.), s. 181 (2). The question before the court on appeal was whether amounts claimed were, when they were written off as bad debts, debts owed to the taxpayer.

The taxpayer, a building company, entered into a scheme of arrangement which provided for a moratorium on the payment of its debts but did not involve the composition of any of the claims. The taxpayer had debts owing to it by four companies ("the development companies") which were associated with it. Those companies had substantial losses and there was no prospect of the debts being recovered. After certain intervening events, including a new scheme of arrangement, the debts the subject of the appeal were written off by the taxpayer.

On 30th June, 1967, an agreement was entered into for the sale of all of the shares but one in the taxpayer, it being a term of the agreement that the purchaser would pay an amount to the taxpayer calculated by reference to the taxpayer's losses and the amount so received would be available for the taxpayer's creditors. The agreement was conditional upon creditors accepting the amount and proceeds of the realization of the taxpayer's assets in satisfaction of their claims. On 13th May, 1968, the previous scheme was terminated and a new scheme was approved by the Supreme Court of South Australia. A committee of management was appointed and it was agreed that the purchaser would lend an amount calculated by reference to the taxpayer's losses, to the committee to be held on behalf of the taxpayer and to be distributed to its creditors pro rata in full satisfaction and discharge of their claims. If the taxpayer was entitled to a deduction for the debts written off pursuant to s. 63 (1) of the Act, then the consequential losses would be of benefit to the purchaser as deductions under s. 80 (2) upon the purchaser recommencing the business. It was submitted by the commissioner that prior to the debts being written off, the beneficial ownership in the debts was assigned by the taxpayer to the committee of management to be held by them on trust for the scheme creditors. It was accepted that there has been no legal assignment of the debt.

Held: (1) On a proper construction of the scheme, there was not an assignment of the debts and therefore the taxpayer was entitled to claim a deduction under s. 63 (1) of the Act.

A.G.C. (Advances) Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 7; (1975), 132 CLR 175, followed.

(2) It is necessary to look to the instrument and surrounding circumstances to ascertain the existence of an intention to assign.

Smith v. Perpetual Trustee Co. Ltd. [1910] HCA 39; (1910), 11 CLR 148, and Morgan v. Lariviere (1875), LR 7 HL 423, referred to.

(3) No particular form of words is necessary to effect an assignment.

G. E. Crane Sales Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1971] HCA 75; (1971), 126 CLR 177, and A.G.C. (Advances) Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 7; (1975), 132 CLR 175, referred to.

(4) The word "moneys" in the expression "any other moneys to which the company was entitled on 30th June, 1967", could include debts, particularly debts which, as here, had fallen due and were immediately repayable. Perrin v. Morgan, (1943) AC 399, referred to. In the present context "moneys" only related to money paid to the committee of management that is the money which the taxpayer had on hand. The trade debts as distinct from their proceeds fell outside cl. 9 of the scheme of arrangement.

(5) The debts were not the proceeds of the realization of the taxpayer's assets, but were the assets themselves. The proceeds were the moneys received when the debts were discharged.

(6) A distinction may be drawn between an obligation to pay to another the proceeds of a debt when received and an equitable assignment to another of the debt before the proceeds are received.

Palette Shoes Pty. Ltd. v. Krohn (1937), 58 CLR 1, followed.

Palmer v. Culverwell, Brooks & Co. (1901), 85 LT 758; Norman v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1963] HCA 21; (1963), 109 CLR 9, and Shepherd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 70; (1965), 113 CLR 385, referred to.

Sydney, 1978, April 18, 19; August 12, 18. 18:8:1978

H. C. Williams Q.C. and A. V. Russell, for the appellant.

J. M. Wilkinson and (Miss) R. I. Cabulis, for the respondent.

ADA MOSHINSKY

DECISION

August 18.

The following written judgments were delivered.

BOWEN C.J. This is an appeal by leave from the judgment of the Supreme Court
of South Australia allowing the appeal by Betro Harrison Constructions Pty. Ltd., the taxpayer, from the Board of Review and amending the commissioner's assessment of income tax for the tax years ending 30th June, 1968, 1969 and 1970. The assessments were amended by treating as allowable deductions certain bad debts which had been written off pursuant to the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s. 63 (1), and making consequential variations to the assessments for the succeeding tax years by allowing deductions under s. 80. (at p152)

2. Much of the judgment of the Supreme Court is concerned with factual questions as to whether the amounts which had been written off as bad debts could be characterized as debts which had formerly been brought to account by the taxpayer as assessable income. The court is not concerned with those questions on this appeal. The commissioner's leave to appeal was granted on terms that the appeal should be on the following grounds, namely that: "(1) The taxpayer's claims for deductions for bad debts should be disallowed under s. 63 (1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 for the following reason, namely that at the time the alleged debts were written off as bad debts they were as a matter of law no longer debts owed to the taxpayer. (2) In calculating the taxpayer's taxable incomes of the income years ended on 30th June, 1969 and 1970, the commissioner has correctly disallowed the taxpayer's deductions for losses pursuant to s. 80 of the decision upon s. 63 (1) above-mentioned." No argument was addressed on the second ground for it is agreed that the allowance of deductions under s. 80 will depend upon and be consequential on the allowance or disallowance of the deductions under s. 63 (1). (at p152)

3. Section 63 (1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 is as follows:

"Debts which are bad debts and are written off as such during

the year of income, and -

(a) have been brought to account by the taxpayer as assessable

income of any year; or

(b) are in respect of money lent in the ordinary course of the

business of the lending of money by a taxpayer who carries on

that business,

shall be allowable deductions." (at p152)

4. To understand the commissioner's contention that the debts purported to be written off by the taxpayer were not debts owed to it, it is not necessary to delve far into the background of the case. On 30th June, 1967, the taxpayer, a building company, had a number of trade creditors with whom it had previously entered into a scheme of arrangement providing for a moratorium on the payment of its debts but not involving the composition of any of the claims. It had ceased to carry on active business in October 1966. The taxpayer in turn had debts owing to it by four companies known as development companies, with which it was associated in its activities as a builder. Those companies had substantial losses and there was no prospect of the debts being recovered. Those debts were, after certain intervening events including a new scheme of arrangement, written off by the taxpayer, and they are the subject of this appeal. (at p152)

5. I turn to those intervening events. On 30th June, 1967, a document entitled "Heads of Agreement" was entered into between the taxpayer and the development companies, therein called the "vendor companies", on the one hand and a company called St. George Finance Pty. Ltd. and two other companies, therein called the "purchasing companies" on the other. It was stated that the shareholders of the various vendor companies had, on that day, transferred their shares in the vendor companies to the purchasing companies or to companies or persons associated or connected with the purchasing companies. In the case of the taxpayer, all but one of its shares were acquired by St. George Finance Pty. Ltd. The one other share was acquired by a person associated with that company. The consideration for the purchase of the shares was that St. George Finance Pty. Ltd. would pay to the taxpayer "an amount equal to 14 cents in the dollar . . . in respect of so much of the income of that company as would have been assessable income of the company but for the losses incurred by the company in the relevant year of income". When paid, that money would be available to the taxpayer's creditors. The agreement was conditional on those creditors accepting that sum and the proceeds of the realization of the taxpayer's assets in satisfaction of their debts. Payment was to be by way of an interest free loan which, except in the event of the creditors refusing to accept the sums previously mentioned in satisfaction of their debt, was not to be repayable until seven years had expired. The payment was to be made into a solicitor's trust account to be distributed amongst the creditors. Paragraph 7 of the heads of agreement provided: "It is an inherent part of the whole agreement that the unsecured creditors of each of the vendor companies must accept in full and final satisfaction of the amounts owing to them the total of the following: (a) the net proceeds of the realization of all assets owned by each such company as at 30th June, 1967, and (b) the total of moneys to be paid by the appropriate purchasers pursuant to par. 6 hereof." (at p153)

6. On 13th May, 1968, the Supreme Court of South Australia approved a new scheme of compromise and arrangement and terminated the previous scheme. The new scheme provided for the appointment of a management committee to be trustees for the scheme creditors and to be comprised of nominees appointed by the creditors. St. George Finance Pty. Ltd., the "scheme purchaser", convenanted to lend the sum equivalent to 14 cents in the dollar in respect of so much of the income of the taxpayer as would have been assessable income but for the allowance as deductions of the taxpayer's losses. It was provided that this sum would be paid to the management committee "on behalf of the company", thereby meaning the taxpayer company. (at p153)

7. Clause 9 of the scheme is crucial to the determination of this appeal. It provides: "The moneys to be lent to the company by the scheme purchaser in accordance with cl. 6 hereof and the proceeds of the realization of the assets of the company held, owned or on hand at 30th June, 1967, and the balance of moneys in the company's bank account at 30th June, 1967, and any other moneys of the company to which the company was entitled as at 30th June, 1967, shall be paid to the management committee and applied by them as follows: . . . (e) to pay thereout fifthly the balance thereof among the scheme creditors of the company pro rata according to the amount of their debts due and owing in accordance with this scheme." The scheme provided that the creditors would accept the distribution in full satisfaction and discharge of their claims. (at p154)

8. As yet, because the allowability as deductions of the taxpayer's losses has not been finally determined, St. George Finance Pty. Ltd. has not paid any sum in respect thereof. (at p154)

9. The case concerns the debts owed by the development companies to the taxpayer. They are bad debts and as an asset of the taxpayer are worth nothing to the scheme creditors. They do, however, have an attraction to St. George Finance Pty. Ltd., for if the debts are allowable deductions under s. 63 (1), presumably they will be available to the taxpayer, all but one of whose shares had been transferred to St. George Finance Pty. Ltd., to be deducted as losses under s. 80 (2) upon its recommencing business. The price St. George Finance Pty. Ltd. pays for this benefit will be available for distribution to the scheme creditors. (at p154)

10. The commissioner contends that the bad debts are not allowable deductions under s. 63 (1), because prior to their being written off, the beneficial ownership in the debts was assigned by the taxpayer to the management committee to be held by them on trust for the scheme creditors. He says that if the taxpayer has only the bare legal estate in the debts, it cannot treat them as allowable deductions under s. 63 (1). He accepts that there has been no legal assignment of the debt. (at p154)

11. Where the property which is said to have been assigned is a chose in action assignable at law, and where as here the assignment is not made in accordance with the statutory requirements for a legal assignment of that chose, the assignment may be effective in equity if made for valuable consideration. In such a case, equity will regard a purported assignment as a contract to assign and the contemplated assignee will hold the equitable title to the property pursuant to the maxim that equity regards as done that which ought to be done. If the assignment is voluntary, it will be effective if the assignor has done all that according to the nature of the property is required to be done to transfer the property. (at p154)

12. Here the debts are said to have been assigned in equity to the management committee who hold the beneficial interest in the debts on trust for the scheme creditors. The consideration for the assignment is the creditors' composition of their debts, which is always regarded in equity as sufficient consideration to support an assignment. The beneficial ownership in the debts may be held by the management committee on trust for the scheme creditors, either because there is an agreement to assign the debts or there may be an expression of an intention to make an immediate disposition of the property, which though failing at law, will be upheld in equity as an agreement to assign. To effect an assignment in equity, although there must be a sufficient expression of an intention to make an immediate disposition, the form of words used is not material except in so far as it throws light on the assignor's intention. (at p155)

13. We were invited by counsel to find an intention to assign from a consideration of the construction of the scheme of arrangement in the light of the surrounding circumstances, and in particular, in the light of the heads of agreement of 30th June, 1967. If that is done I consider that the intention there expressed on the question of the assignment of the taxpayer's assets is caught up in the scheme itself. This can be seen by comparing par. 7 of the heads of agreement with cl. 9 of the scheme. (at p155)

14. However, I do not consider it necessary to go further than construing the scheme of arrangement itself. It is true that where the purported or alleged assignment is contained in an instrument one looks to the instrument and the surrounding circumstances to ascertain the existence of an intention to assign (Smith v. Perpetual Trustee Co. Ltd. [1910] HCA 39; (1910) 11 CLR 148 and Morgan v. Lariviere (1875) LR 7 HL 423, at p 431 ). But the weight to be attached to the extrinsic evidence outside the instrument will vary inversely with the formality of the instrument and the deliberateness with which it was cast. Further, the search is for an objective indication of that intent. In the present case the scheme of arrangement operates by force of the Companies Act, 1962-1966 (S.A.), and the order of the Supreme Court. Its effect depends not on the perceived intent of contracting parties, but on the proper construction of the scheme. It still remains true that no particular form of words is necessary to effect an assignment (see G. E. Crane Sales Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1971] HCA 75; (1971) 126 CLR 177 and A.G.C. (Advances) Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 7; (1975) 132 CLR 175 ). (at p155)

15. The scheme does not use the language of assignment, but the language of agreement. The money which is lent by the scheme purchaser, St. George Finance Pty. Ltd., is to be held by the management committee on behalf of the taxpayer. Those moneys, however, which later in cl. 9 are described as being lent to the company, together with other moneys to which I shall later refer, are to be paid to the management committee and applied by it in accordance with the provisions of the scheme. With respect to those moneys, therefore, although they never reach the hands of the taxpayer, they remain an asset beneficially owned by it until they are appropriated to the management committee. The taxpayer is under an obligation to pay those moneys to the management committee, thereby relinquishing its interest in them. (at p156)

16. Clause 9 of the scheme provides that four classes of property are to be "paid to" the management committee. One of these is the moneys lent by the scheme purchaser. The legal and equitable entitlement to those moneys and the parties' obligations in respect of them are spelt out with reasonable clarity. Counsel for the commissioner contends that the debts owed to the taxpayer by the development companies fall into one of the other classes, and that the operation of cl. 9 in respect of those debts brings about a different set of entitlements. (at p156)

17. The three other classes of property dealt with by cl. 9 are the proceeds of the realization of the taxpayer's assets held, owned or on hand at 30th June, 1967; the balance of moneys in the taxpayer's bank account on that date; and any other moneys to which the company was entitled at that date. Does a provision that those moneys shall be paid to the management committee effect an assignment to the committee of the debts owed to the taxpayer by the development companies? I think not. (at p156)

18. Are the debts the "proceeds of realization of the assets" owned by the taxpayer? In my view the debts, that is, the intangible choses in action, are not the proceeds of the realization of the taxpayer's assets, but the assets themselves. The proceeds are the moneys received when the debts are discharged. (at p156)

19. The other category of property referred to in cl. 9 into which the debts might fall is "any other moneys to which the company was entitled on 30th June, 1967". The meaning to be given to the word "moneys" when used in a document must depend on the context in which it is found. There are many cases where "moneys" would include debts, particularly debts which, as here, have fallen due and are immediately repayable (see generally Perrin v. Morgan (1943) AC 399 ). (at p156)

20. But what is the meaning of "moneys" in the present context? To my mind, cl. 9 is concerned with sums of money that the taxpayer has on hand. Thus, the moneys lent by the scheme purchaser, the proceeds of realization of assets and the balance of moneys in the company's bank account, are all sums that are on hand and can be transferred, or in the words of cl. 9 "paid to", the committee so that they are immediately available to be used as directed by pars. (a)-(e) of the clause. In the same way, as applied to debts, I consider the words "other moneys to which the company is entitled" bring in the proceeds of the debts when they have been collected and can be physically paid over. That might not be so with respect to all debts. I am leaving out moneys held by financial institutions at call, bills of exchange and the like. They may be capable of being dealt with in the manner described. However, I consider that trade debts, as distinct from their proceeds, fall outside cl. 9. (at p157)

21. Thus I consider cl. 9 embodies a provision by which the taxpayer is obliged to pay the proceeds of the debts to the management committee. It is obliged to hand over the moneys received from the development companies on the debts being discharged. If such moneys ever came into existence, then on their being identifiable in the hands of the taxpayer, they will be beneficially owned by the management committee as trustees for the scheme creditors. (at p157)

22. Does the imposition of an obligation to assign the proceeds of the debts operate as a present assignment of the debts? In many cases it will be proper to read a reference to the proceeds as a reference to the debt itself. If, for example, an assignor assigns "the $500 X owes me", there would normally be an assignment of the presently existing debt, not of a fund of money to be received. In Palmer v. Culverwell, Brooks & Co. (1901) 85 LT 758 , a case referred to in argument, a letter from F. to his solicitors directing them to take proceedings to recover a debt and to hold the proceeds at the disposal of Culverwell, Brooks & Co., a copy of the letter being sent to Culverwell, Brooks & Co., was held to constitute an equitable assignment of the proceeds (see generally Halsbury's Laws of England (4th ed.) vol. 6,p. 23, par. 38). (at p157)

23. In Norman v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1963] HCA 21; (1963) 109 CLR 9 , Windeyer J., in dealing with the question of the assignment of interest which had not accrued or fallen due but which might be expected to become payable on a debt, said: "But a contract to pay a sum of money on a future day, call it interest or what you will, calculable in amount according to conditions presently agreed, is in my view a presently existing chose in action. As between parties to a contract of money lent at interest the borrower is simply a debtor who must pay a sum or sums (called interest) that he has, for good consideration (the forbearance of the creditor) contracted to pay to his creditor at the time or times stipulated. Why should not the creditor before the date when this debt becomes due and payable, assign his right to receive payment on the due date? . . . What he assigns is not, it seems to me, a right to arise in the future but a present contractual right to be paid at a future date a sum of money, to be calculated in the agreed manner." (1963) 109 CLR, at pp 37-38 . (at p157)

24. On the other hand in the view of the majority what was there the subject matter of the assignment was not the present contractual right to interest, but the interest itself, which was future property and not susceptible to immediate voluntary assignment. The distinction is similar to that in the instant case (see also Shepherd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 70; (1965) 113 CLR 385 )). (at p158)

25. In my view on a proper construction of the scheme of arrangement one should not infer that there is an assignment of the debts. It is not necessary again to set out the terms of the scheme. It appears what is contemplated is that the taxpayer company is obliged to collect the debts and to hand over the moneys thus realized to the management committee to be applied in the required manner. It is not necessary for that purpose that the property in the debts be transferred to the management committee. In this aspect the scheme does not differ from that considered by the High Court in A.G.C. (Advances) Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 7; (1975) 132 CLR 175 ). Indeed, the scheme provides that the scheme purchaser will make a payment in respect of debts which have been written off as bad debts and which are available as allowable deductions. The scheme contemplates that no proceeds of those debts will be forthcoming. If the agreement to assign the proceeds of all the debts owing to the taxpayer were to take effect as an immediate assignment of the debts themselves it would be idle to speak of allowable deductions. The purpose of the scheme would be defeated. (at p158)

26. It follows that the commissioner's contention that the taxpayer was not entitled to claim deductions under s. 63 (1) because it was not the beneficial owner of the debts written off must fail. I would dismiss the appeal with costs. (at p158)

BRENNAN J. The circumstances in which this appeal arises and the grounds of appeal are set out in the reasons for judgment of Bowen C.J. which I have had the advantage of reading. I agree in the conclusion at which his Honour arrives. (at p158)

2. On 13th May, 1968, pursuant to s. 181 (2) of the Companies Act, 1962-1966 (S.A.), the respondent company secured the approval of the Supreme Court of South Australia for a scheme of compromise and arrangement, as the order described it, which the members and creditors of the company had approved on the preceding 23rd April. At that time, St. George Finance Pty. Ltd. was the sole beneficial owner of the shares and, according to the terms of the arrangement, it agreed to lend to the company a sum of money to be calculated according to a formula which depended upon the availability of tax losses to the company. That sum together with "the proceeds of the realization of the assets of the company held at 30th June, 1967, and the balance of moneys in the company's bank account at 30th June, 1967, and any other moneys of the company to which the company was entitled as at 30th June,1967," were to be distributed to the creditors of the company and accepted by them in full satisfaction and complete discharge of all their claims (cl. 12 of the scheme, as I shall call the scheme of compromise and arrangement). (at p159)

3. Immediately before the scheme took effect, the respondent company was owed some debts which were doubtful or bad. It has since written them off as bad debts and it sought to deduct from its assessable income the amounts so written off. (at p159)

4. The question in this appeal is whether the debts were deductible under s. 63 (1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. If the debts were beneficially owned by the company at the time when they were written off, then it was entitled to the deduction claimed (A.G.C. (Advances) Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1975] HCA 7; (1975) 132 CLR 175 ; but if the scheme effected an equitable assignment of the debts, so that the debts were no longer "moneys which the taxpayer is presently entitled to receive" at the relevant time, the claim to the deduction fails. (G.E. Crane Sales Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 126 CLR, at p 186 )). (at p159)

5. The debts written off were owing to it on 30th June, 1967. Accordingly the debts were assets of the kind referred to in cl. 9 of the scheme. Clause 9 provides: "The moneys to be lent to the company by the scheme purchaser in accordance with cl. 6 hereof and the proceeds of the realization of the assets of the company held, owned or on hand at 30th June, 1967, and the balance of moneys in the company's bank account at 30th June, 1967, and any other moneys of the company to which the company was entitled as at 30th June, 1967, shall be paid to the management committee and applied by them as follows: . . . (e) to pay thereout fifthly the balance thereof among the scheme creditors of the company pro rata according to the amount of their debts due and owing in accordance with this scheme." (at p159)

6. Although arrangements made pursuant to s. 181 of the Companies Act are not, or are not necessarily, contracts (Bridges v. Hershon (1968) 3 NSWR 47, at p 56 and In re Garner's Motors Ltd. (1937) 1 Ch 594 )), an obligation which binds the company, its members or its creditors will be enforced by equity in the same way as it would enforce a contractual obligation in like terms (see Crane's case (1971) 126 CLR, at pp 185, 189, 190 per Menzies J. and per Walsh J.). When an obligee who is bound by an arrangement gives good consideration, he may thereby acquire, if the terms of the arrangement are appropriate for the purpose, an equitable interest in property. (at p159)

7. The arrangement bound the company to pay the proceeds of the realization of the debts to the management committee, as trustee for the creditors. The company's obligation related to the proceeds, not to the debts; and it was an obligation which affected the ownership of the proceeds, but did not affect the ownership of the debts. It affected the ownership of the proceeds because it bound the company to pay the moneys actually received, when received, to the management committee - an obligation which equity would compel the company to fulfil (In re Irving; Ex parte Brett (1877) 7 Ch D 419, at p 422 ). Lord Truro in Rodick v. Gandell (1852) 1 De Gm & G 763, at pp 777, 778; [1852] EngR 909; 42 ER 749, at p 754 said that, "an agreement between a debtor and a creditor that the debt owing shall be paid out of a specific fund coming to the debtor, . . . will create a valid equitable charge upon such fund; in other words, will operate as an equitable assignment of the . . . fund . . .". An illustration of this principle is an agreement to pay an amount owing from payments as and when received (per Jordan C.J. in Perpetual Trustee Co. Ltd. v. Smith [1938] NSWStRp 56; (1938) 39 SR (NSW) 19, at p 38 ). Equally, equity would have compelled the company to pay over the proceeds of the debts if the company had declared itself to be a trustee of the debts, for that would have amounted to an assignment of the debts (per Menzies J. in Crane's case (1971) 126 CLR, at p 183 )), and the proceeds in the hands of the trustee would have been impressed with the same trust as that which affected the debts represented by the proceeds. But it does not follow that the entitlement of the management committee to the proceeds of the debts is consequential upon an assignment of those debts, rather than upon an assignment of the proceeds when received. (at p160)

8. A distinction may be drawn between an obligation to pay to another the proceeds of a debt, when received, and an equitable assignment to him of the debt before the proceeds are received. The distinction was drawn in Palette Shoes Pty. Ltd. v. Krohn (1937) 58 CLR 1, at pp 13, 34 by Latham C.J. and by McTiernan J., and it is a distinction which determines this appeal. In the former case, the obligor remains the owner of debt and the obligee acquires no equitable interest in the debt (Carey v. Palmer [1924] HCA 17; (1924) 34 CLR 380, at p 388 per Knox C.J., approved by the Privy Council in Palmer v. Carey (1926) AC 703, at p 707 )). In the latter case, the assignor parts with his ownership of the debt, and the assignee as owner is entitled to the proceeds when received. (at p160)

9. In the present case, the arrangement did not purport to assign to the committee of management any interest in the debts. The company thus remained the owner of the debts, and was entitled to deduct them from its assessable income when they were written off. The appeals should therefore be dismissed with costs to be taxed. (at p160)

DEANE J. The relevant facts and background circumstances in the context of which this appeal falls to be determined appear from the judgment of the Chief Judge which I have had the benefit of reading. The outstanding question is whether the debts which the respondent taxpayer ("the taxpayer") claims as deductions were, at the time they were purportedly written off, debts owing to the taxpayer. If they were, the taxpayer was entitled to deductions in respect of them pursuant to the provisions of s. 63 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. If they were not, they were not capable of being written off by the taxpayer within that section (see G.E. Crane Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 126 CLR, at p 187 and the cases there mentioned). I agree, for the reasons which he gives, with the conclusion of the Chief Judge that that question should be answered in favour of the taxpayer and that, notwithstanding the effect of the scheme of arrangement between the taxpayer and its creditors which had been sanctioned by the Supreme Court of South Australia on 13th May, 1968, the debts remained debts owing to the taxpayer which were capable of being, and were in fact, written off within the provisions of s. 63. (at p161)

2. Examination of the terms of the scheme of arrangement discloses that it did not produce either the effect that the taxpayer was a corporate shell or the result that neither the corporate entity nor the shareholders had any beneficial interest of any kind in corporate assets or activities. It was clearly envisaged that the taxpayer would, in the future, carry on business for the benefit of its shareholders. The effect of the scheme was that the creditors bound by it would have no direct or indirect interest in the receipts or profits of any such business. The management committee established by the scheme consisted of nominees of the taxpayer's shareholders as well as of the scheme creditors. The ordinary corporate organs of the taxpayer (the shareholders in general meeting and the board of directors) remained in existence and in operation. The functions of the management committee were expressly confined to the specific powers and duties allocated by the scheme and were, as regards entitlement to receive or deal with assets of the taxpayer, restricted to the "moneys or other amounts . . . held" at the date of commencement of the scheme (cl. 4), "the moneys to be lent to the company by the scheme purchaser" (cl. 9), "the proceeds of the realization of the assets . . . held, owned or on hand at 30th June, 1967," (cl. 9: italics added) and "the balance of moneys in the company's bank account . . . and any other moneys of the company to which the company was entitled as at 30th June, 1967," (cl. 9). I agree, for the reasons which he gives, with the Chief Judge's conclusion that the "moneys" mentioned in these phrases did not, in the context in which the word appears, include the relevant debts and that the scheme did not, in terms or by implication, involve a legal or equitable assignment of them. Indeed, it was the essence of the scheme that those debts remained owing to the taxpayer so that they could, in due course, be written off by the taxpayer. The effect of the scheme was that such debts remained owing to the taxpayer but that any "proceeds" of them received by the taxpayer while the scheme remained in operation would be received upon trust to be paid to the management committee to be applied in accordance with the provisions of the scheme. In these circumstances, the relevant debts were, at the time they were written off in the taxpayer's books, debts owing to the taxpayer which were capable of being written off for the purposes of s. 63 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (see A.G.C. (Advances) Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1975) 132 CLR, at pp 183-184, 190-191, 194-195 ). The appeal should be dismissed with costs. (at p162)

ORDER

Order accordingly.
