REASONS FOR DECISION



23 February 1995 Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)



  1. This is an application to review an interim determination made on 17 March 1993, pursuant to s 15L of an Act, then known as the Occupational Superannuation Standards Act 1987 (“the Act”).
  2. The respondent received a notice from Multinational Australia Superannuation Fund that an eligible termination payment of $847,372 had been paid to the applicant by the Fund. The Commissioner then determined that the payment was in excess of the reasonable benefit limits. Those limits (shortly) are expressed as a multiple of the member’s highest average salary received over any 3 consecutive financial years. The Fund calculated that sum by reference to the salaries paid to the applicant for the 1990, 1991 and 1992 financial years. During that time the applicant was a non resident and a non citizen, and the salary was paid outside Australia in non Australian currency. The Commissioner determined that salary of this nature did not fall within the definition of salary in regulation 4A(1) made under the Act.
  3. According to information provided by the Australian affiliate of the Multinational company the applicant (who is not an Australian citizen) resided in Australia for some time prior to 1983 while working for the organisation. No doubt it was during this period that he became a member of Multinational Australia Superannuation Fund (although the actual date of his membership is irrelevant to the present issues). From 1990 he was located in various cities in South East Asia, working for local operations of the Multinational company in those locations.
  4. During that period he was paid half of his salary in the United States and half in the city in which he was then located. Document T-9 gives details of the way in which he was taxed in relation to those salary packages. It is not relevant to examine them in detail, except to note that at no time during the relevant period was his salary subject to Australian tax.
  5. Nevertheless, contributions continued to be made to Multinational Australia Superannuation Fund for his benefit by Multinational company. Those contributions were calculated by reference to his total salary package, all of which was paid to him outside Australia in non Australian currency. If that salary is not to be taken into account in calculating his reasonable benefit limits, then the overwhelming majority of his eligible termination payment will be subject to Australian tax, other than upon a concessional basis. The question to be determined, therefore, is whether the Commissioner’s decision to treat non Australian salary as falling outside the definition of salary was correct.
  6. Regulations 4A(1) and (2) define salary in the following terms —

“reg 4A(1)

“salary” means salary, wages, commissions bonuses, fees, allowances or gratuities paid to a person during a financial year, and includes :

(a) other earnings (other than earnings on investments); and

(b) the amount that would be the value of a benefit to which section 57, 57A or 58 of the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 applies if that benefit were not an exempt benefit; and

(c) a payment made by a company by way of remuneration to a director of the company; and

(d) in the case of a person who is an Australian citizen, or a resident of Australia within the meaning of the Tax Act — any amounts paid to the person from a source outside Australia that would fall within this definition if they had been paid from a source in Australia;

but does not include :

(e) a distribution from a trust estate; or

(f) allowances to cover expenses incurred on behalf of the person’s employer or business; or

(g) any ETP; or

(h) a superannuation pension or an annuity; or

(i) a payment to which section 26AC or 26AD of the Tax Act applies.:

..............

4A (2)



For the purposes of the definition of “salary” in subregulation (1) :

(a) amounts to which paragraph (d) of that definition applies are to be converted into Australian currency at the rate that is the average of the exchange rates for the currency in which the foreign source amounts were paid at the start, and at the end, of the financial year in which the amounts were paid; and ...”
  1. Sections 26AC and 26AD refer to amounts received on termination of employment in lieu of annual leave and long service leave. ETP refers to an eligible termination payment .
  2. Until the hearing, the Commissioner’s principal argument was that the opening words of the definition should be read and construed as if the words “from a source in Australia” were in those opening lines. It was submitted that otherwise paragraph (d) of the definition would be superfluous.
  3. On the hearing before me, the primacy of that argument was displaced by a reliance by the Commissioner upon s 21(b) of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 which, it was submitted, should govern the interpretation of the definition. That paragraph is in the following terms —

“s21

In any Act, unless the contrary intention appears :

(a) ........

(b) references to localities jurisdictions and other matters and things shall be construed as references to such localities jurisdictions and other matters and things in and of the Commonwealth.”
  1. It was submitted that the effect of s 21(b) was to restrict all references in the definition of salary to moneys sourced in Australia. It is convenient to deal firstly with that submission.
  2. In my view the submission is not correct. The purpose of the territoriality paragraph is to re-state the common law concerning limitations of jurisdiction. In Barcelo v Electrolytic Zinc Company of Australasia Limited [1932] HCA 52; (1932) 48 CLR 391 at 424, Dixon J observed : “It is always to be understood and implied that the legislature of a country is not intending to deal with persons or matters over which, according to the comity of nations, the jurisdiction properly belongs to some other sovereign or state”. This was quoted with approval by Taylor J in Meyer Heine Pty Ltd v China Navigation Co Ltd [1966] HCA 11; 115 CLR 10 at 31, a decision to which I was referred by counsel for the Commissioner.
  3. Similar provisions exist in Acts Interpretation Acts of various States. The true nature of those territorial limitations has been explained in a number of decisions. In O’Connor v Healey (1967) 69 SR (NSW) 111, an employee was killed in New South Wales while journeying from his place of work in that State to his home in Victoria. It was argued that the effect of the provision in the NSW Interpretation Act meant that all matters in the case had to be related to New South Wales. This argument was rejected. Jacobs JA said at 114 —

“It seems to me that the intention of [the legislation] is to provide the natural limit of legislation, so that it applies in its subject matter to those situations which have a nexus with New South Wales.”

  1. Referring to this and other cases, the learned authors of Pearce and Geddes, Statutory Interpretation in Australia third edition, paragraph 6.25 observed —

“These decisions reflect a sensible approach to the application of the Interpretation Act. The provision is intended to make it unnecessary to identify places, jurisdictions and so on each time they are mentioned, but it should not be used by a person to limit the scope of an Act.”

  1. In my view, the necessary nexus exists so that one is not precluded from taking foreign salary into account in applying the definition of salary. The nexus is formed by the fact that the applicant is a member of an Australian fund, and payments were made to that fund in respect of his service for a number of years which were calculated by reference to his foreign salary. The limitations of the Acts Interpretation Act do not, in my view, exclude foreign salary when the words of the definition are so clear. In fact they could almost be said to indicate a contrary intention.
  2. The definition is comprehensive. The use of the word “includes” can only be for the purpose of putting beyond doubt items that might otherwise be thought not to have been included in the general opening words. The draftsperson seems to have been over cautious in some respects. It is hard to see, for example, why paragraph (c) was included. Clearly directors’ remuneration would fall within the opening words of the definition. It does not follow, therefore, that the existence of paragraph (c) means that the opening words should be construed as not including directors’ fees.
  3. Similarly the inclusion of paragraph (d) does not mean that the opening lines of the definition are to be read down to exclude salary from a source out of Australia. This was the principal argument upon which the Commissioner originally relied. Paragraph (d) ensures that there is included in the concept of salary as defined, amounts paid to an Australian citizen or an Australian resident outside Australia that would have fallen within the definition had they been paid from a source in Australia. The Commissioner argued that to interpret salary as including foreign sourced income of this kind would render paragraph (d) of the definition superfluous.
  4. In my view that result does not necessarily follow. An argument for a possible use of paragraph (d), submitted by counsel for the applicant, seems to me to illustrate the sort of situation that might have been intended to be covered by the paragraph. It was submitted that the paragraph was design to ensure that payments of income to Australian citizens and Australian residents, but not non residents, from sources out of Australia were included as part of their salary in calculating their highest average salary, even though the characterisation of such payments in the country of source may, or would, mean that they fell outside the definition. In other words, it was submitted that the purpose of the paragraph was ameliorative and was to give a benefit to Australian citizens in the circumstances outlined. For example, a payment which would be characterised as a bonus if paid in Australia may not be so characterised in the country of source. Nevertheless by the operation of this paragraph, an Australian citizen or an Australian resident can take advantage of it in building up his or her highest average salary and consequently his or her reasonable benefits limit.
  5. Furthermore, it seems to me there are positive reasons, as well, for adopting the view that salary includes foreign sourced salary. The opening words are comprehensive. There is no indication that the draftsperson of the regulations intended to exclude exempt income as such. Had he or she wished to do so, it would have been easy enough. That category of income is exempt from the definition of salary or wages in sub-section 221A(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act. I do not suggest that the meaning of salary in the regulation is in any way governed by the definition of that term in another Act. I quote it only to show that special treatment of foreign salary is something that one can assume is considered by draftpersons in appropriate circumstances.
  6. Neither the Act nor the regulations contain any reference, or any inference, that would suggest that the legislature intended to discriminate between members of superannuation funds who were resident and those who were non resident in relation to the concessional taxation of benefits paid to them from Australian funds. A far clearer expression of legislative intention to discriminate would be required before the plain words of the regulation were modified in such a drastic manner.
  7. I was referred to an information paper issued by the Commissioner and to the explanatory statement to the rules issued when the regulation was proclaimed. In my view, it is not necessary to resort to these extrinsic sources, which are, in any event, of very little assistance. The words of the regulation are unencumbered by any other limitation than appears in the language of that regulation. A plain reading of the words indicates that there is no reason why, in calculating the reasonable benefits limit of a member of an Australian fund, salary paid to him from a foreign source ought not to be taken into account, whether or not he is a citizen or resident of this country, provided that the foreign sourced salary was the basis upon which contributions to the Fund on his behalf were calculated.
  8. Paragraph (a) of regulation 4A(2), it seems to me, has no bearing upon the construction of regulation 4A(1). It merely provides a mechanism for conversion of currencies when paragraph (d) comes into operation. It certainly can not govern the way in which the regulation is to be interpreted.
  9. For these reasons, the decision under review is set aside and the matter is remitted to the respondent with the direction that salary paid to the applicant from sources outside Australia should be taken into account in calculating the applicant’s highest average salary.
