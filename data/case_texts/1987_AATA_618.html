NT85/4059 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 618; 87 ATC 653 (7 May 1987)

Last Updated: 17 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Taxation - allowable deduction s51(1) - home office/study -whether amounts have a sufficiently close nexus to the gaining of assessable income; Handley approach - whether claim of a domestic nature - whether income is isolated venture income - whether s52 operates as a code in those circumstances.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s 51(1)

Handley v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4165

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Forsyth 81 ATC 4151

Swinford v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 84 ATC 4803

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Werchon 82 ATC 4332

REASONS FOR DECISION

7 May 1987 Mr B.J. McMahon (Senior Member)

  1. In his income tax return for the year ended 30th June 1980, the applicant responded to the item: "Sale of property did you during the year of income, sell any real estate, stocks, shares or other property?" by ticking the "yes" box and stating: "shares and share options, see attached schedule".
  2. The attached schedule in relation to this item shows that the applicant bought 200 options over shares in Western Mining Corporation Limited on 29 April 1980, and sold them on 15 May 1980. He bought 1,000 options over shares in Broken Hill Proprietary Ltd on 13 December 1979, disposing of them on 31 January 1980, and a further 1,000 options over shares in the same company on 31 January 1980 which he disposed of on 24 April 1980. From the three purchases and sales, a gross profit of $879.65 was derived.
  3. Items 19 to 25 of the income tax return dealt with "deductions from investment and property income". The applicant claimed a deduction of $428.00 which was arrived at in the following way:
  4. Water rates of $274.53 and Council rates of $325.35 levied in respect of his home were set out. These amounted to $599.88. He then set out fire insurance and household insurance premiums in respect of the same premises amounting to $210.52. Plumbing repairs were shown as $75.25. In response to the item "interest on borrowed money used to produce investment and property income (show name and address of person etc to whom paid), he listed "AMP Society $700.00; Bank of NSW $1,432.00; United Permanent Building Society $2,080.00; total $4,212.00". He also included under "Other Deductions" an item "Purchase of Financial Newspapers $63.20."
  5. In response to the question "Was the property or any part of it used for private purposes during the year of income?" He marked the "yes" box. This probably reflects a misunderstanding of the meaning of the question. It is clearly meant to establish whether there was private use of the property from which income was derived. Nevertheless the applicant claimed as a deduction a proportion of the gross outgoings referred to above and explained in the schedule that his basis for calculation of that proportion that was applicable to private use, was with reference to the area of his house. He applied a fraction of 13 over 14 to the three major items to establish the "private use" content of the amount of deductions claimed, and claimed the balance as a deduction against Ms assessable income. In evidence given before this Tribunal it appeared that the 13 over 14 relates to the 13 rooms in his private house which he says were not used for purposes associated with gaining assessable income. His claim therefore principally related to the expenses associated with the remaining room which he arrived at by taking an aliquot proportion of the expenses referable to the whole property.

6. The claim was disallowed in full and the applicant objected to the respondent's decision stating as his grounds for objection:

"As income arising from share and option transactions have [sic] been accepted as income in the assessment, the cost of accommodation and money associated with gaining this income should be allowed as a deduction from such assessment."

7. When the objection was disallowed, the applicant requested that it be referred to this Tribunal's predecessor, a Board of Review. In his letter requesting such a reference, some variations appear from the grounds of his objection. As it transpired, I do not consider that they are material variations. As will later appear, the result of this application would have been the same even if the additional points contained in the reference letter had been incorporated in the objection. The objection was dated 28 October 1980, well before the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986 and the powers conferred by that Act upon this Tribunal to amend objections. In any event the applicant made no application to amend the terms of his objection, although the discrepancies were pointed out to him at the commencement of the hearing before this Tribunal. I will therefore look at the present application in the light of the terms of the original objection.

  1. The application before this Tribunal is to review the objection decision. Although reference was made on the hearing of this application to some proportion of interest paid to the Bank of NSW as part of the cost of acquisition of assets, there is no such specific claim in the income tax return or in the objection except in so far as interest paid to a number of organisations is claimed in gross and a proportion of one of the interest amounts is claimed by the applicant as a deduction.
  2. In short, the applicant claimed a proportion of the general outgoings in relation to his house being that proportion referable to the room he referred to as his study, a proportion of the interest paid in respect of his housing loan because of the way in which part of the monies from that loan were used to finance purchases as will later appear, and the cost of certain newspapers.
  3. The applicant at the time was employed by a large shipping agency as a financial and administrative executive in a full-time capacity. His evidence was that when he intended to begin his investment venture, he deemed it prudent to monitor the market progress of options, which were particularly volatile. As this could not be done during the day he could do it only at home in his study. He did not work in his study on this calling every night. There were of course only three transactions carried out in the year in question. He did, however, say that insofar as the room was used, it was used for investment decisions and that his course of investment "required a semi-permanent eye being kept on options".
  4. The room is on the ground floor of a 14 room house of 2 storeys. To reach it, it is necessary to go through the front door, then through the bedroom of the applicant and his wife. Alternatively, the room can be entered directly by a door from a side passage. The house was acquired about 1971. However, the study was not used until some years later, just prior to the commencement of the financial year in question. At that stage the applicant acquired a metal desk with filing cabinet capacity, which was there throughout the year in question. Also in place were shelves for books and financial records, an easy chair which he said was covered with papers and a small table with a sewing machine on it, together with cupboards that were associated with that machine. He agreed that his wife used the machine occasionally but that otherwise the room was not used except by him. He asserted that there was nowhere else in the house where investment decisions of the nature that he had in mind could be made, although in fact there were several sitting rooms and family rooms throughout the house.
  5. The options were bought through a stock broker. No advice was sought from him. The applicant said that he made his own investment decisions. The stock broker was simply instructed by telephone either to sell or to buy.

13, Although he had an Informal credit understanding with the broker (the amounts involved were relatively small), he did recall paying for the options eventually against contract notes. His own personal cheque account was always kept at a low level of credit. It was possible to draw against the housing loan which had been granted by the Bank of NSW. This he did from time to time to "top up" his cheque account whenever it was necessary to write a cheque as, in this case, in payment of a contract note for assets acquired. In so far as the interest on monies used to acquire income producing assets represents one aspect of his claim, this interest is represented by the amount that would have been charged by the bank on the additional amount overdrawn on the housing account. The applicant produced bank statements for the relevant period but was, however, unable to indicate where any one of these transactions took place. He relied entirely on his memory. The bank statements disclosed that the housing loan (and therefore, it was agreed, the house) was in the joint names of the applicant and his wife.

14. The outgoings in respect of the house were paid by the applicant alone, although the property was owned jointly. These outgoings included plumbing repairs. It was agreed that there were no plumbing works in the study. It was also agreed that the rates included an amount for garbage collection as well as for general rates and that normally one would not have expected the use of the study to result in collectible garbage. Amounts of interest were paid to the AMP Society, the Bank of NSW and the United Permanent Building Society, although it would appear from the evidence given before the Tribunal that only additional draw downs from the Bank of NSW were involved in the acquisition of income producing assets. The applicant would ask that a proportion of the three interest payments be taken into account as part of the home study expenses claim, but only some part of the Bank of NSW interest be taken into account for the options claim. The exact amount of interest paid to the Bank of NSW in relation to this part of the claim was not identified. It is not clear whether it forms part of one fourteenth of the total bank interest claimed or is some other amount.

  1. It seems to me that even if the claim were not as loosely framed as it is, even if the applicant was precise in his proof of sources of payment and amounts paid, even if an exact proportion could be arrived at of outgoings that might be referable to the gaining of assessable income, this claim must fail on many grounds.
  2. To succeed the applicant would have to base his claim upon section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act which is in the following terms:

"51(1) [Deductions for losses and outgoings] All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

17. There have been many cases dealing with claims for home study expenses. An examination of the analyses of the issues in those cases indicates that success in such a claim is extremely difficult to achieve. The leading cases are two decisions of the High Court, Handley v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4165 and Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Forsyth 81 ATC 4157. Whilst it is not true to say that as a matter of law deductions are no longer available, as a result of those cases, for that proportion of the rent or interest on mortgages paid by a taxpayer which relates to a home office, it is nevertheless always difficult to establish the essential character of a home office as a place which is not used for private or domestic purposes. A study normally is an integral part of a domestic establishment. As Mason J. said in Handley, one might as well claim in respect of a dining room because work is done at the dining room table. As with all claims under s51(1) it is necessary to characterise the expenditure to establish whether or not it falls within any of the exceptions. At page 4171, His Honour said:

"I do not agree that in determining the essential character of the expenditure' we should look only to the use to which the study is put, though use is obviously a matter of great important. It is necessary to look to the character of what is said to have been acquired by means of the expenditure, namely the study, and to its relationship to the home of which it forms part, in order to decide whether the expenditure falls within the exception."

The clear cut circumstances of almost exclusive and systematic use which enabled the taxpayer to succeed in Swinford v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 84 ATC 4803, are certainly not present in the circumstances of the present application.

18. The present applicant is engaged in full-time activity away from the home. The study in question is hardly used, except occasionally by the applicant's wife for sewing and occasionally by the applicant for the few transactions which were not, in any event, a major contributor to his income. There was no evidence 'of clients visiting the home study, of telephone calls or of any other normal business activity being conducted from that downstairs room. The only activity of which evidence was given was that of contemplation and of monitoring investments. The room and its use could in no way be compared with the self-contained doctor's surgery referred to in Handley.

19. The payments in any event would not have a sufficiently close nexus to the gaining of assessable income. As Wilson J. said in Handley:

"The payments for mortgage interests, rates and insurance premiums were of a kind which in the circumstances of this case cannot be apportioned between home and office expenses. They related to the building and/or land as a whole, and are not affected in any way at all by reason of the fact that the taxpayer performs professional work on the premises. They would remain the same whether or not he worked at home."

20. It will be noted that in the present case no claim is made for direct costs such as depreciation of furniture, cost of telephone calls or the like. The claim is a proportion of the general overheads attributable to the property of which the subject premises forms a small part. The claim in respect of these general overheads would fail even if it were to be viewed as a claim under section 51(1) on this ground alone.

  1. The claim could also be viewed as a claim for "domestic" expenditure as distinct from "private" expenditure which takes any claims outside the scope of the general deduction section. The plumbing costs for example can only be described as household costs, or domestic expenditure.
  2. A further complicating factor is that the property is in the joint names of the applicant and his wife, although the claims are made only for deductions from the applicant's assessable income. If in fact he paid outgoings in respect of a property owned by himself together with another party, then part of those payments must be treated as a benefit voluntarily conferred by him on an associated person. The character of the payment would not be such as to render it deductible in whole, even if the nature of the payment had the necessary characterisation.
  3. Finally, the applicant's claim must also fail because it is, in my view, not possible to view it as a claim falling within s51(1) or any other relevant deduction section.
  4. To be deductible under section 51(1) monies must represent either "all losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income" or "all lossess and outgoings to the extent to which they are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income".

25. It is clear on the evidence that the applicant was not carrying on a business. That word is defined in section 6 of the Act to include "any profession, trade, employment, vocational calling, hut does not include occupation as an employee". To come within the second limb of section 51, the applicant would ' have to show that his activity in buying and selling three parcels of options would amount to a business according to the ordinary usage principles applied in identifying a continuing business. Here there was no evidence of options or shares brought in as stock as one would expect to find in a share trading account. There was, for example, no opening or closing balance. If he cannot come within the second limb of s51(1), can he claim a deduction under the first limb? In my opinion he can not.

26. The income derived from the three transactions, while assessable, can be viewed as isolated venture income. As such it would be taxable under the provisions of the former s26(a) of the Act. The reasoning of a Full Bench of the Federal Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Werchon 82 ATC 4332, while not specific on the point, suggests that s51(1) has no application in such a situation. It seems to be assumed that in those circumstances s52(1) acts as a code, displacing the application of s51(1) (as do a number of other sections). Losses are available as deductions under s52 only if the requirements of the proviso to that section have been met. These consist principally of giving appropriate notice of disposition intention upon acquisition of the assets. There is no evidence of any such notice in the present application.

  1. Once this is established, the claim for financial newspapers must also fail. Whether or not the applicant derived private or domestic benefit from a perusal of the Financial Review during his holidays and of a publication of the Stock Exchange entitled "Personal Investor" on other isolated occasions, and whether or not any apportionment might be appropriate if a claim for these publications were allowable under section 51, the fact that the claim must be viewed as a deduction against isolated venture income means that there is no scope for deduction for the cost of any of these publications.
  2. Accordingly, the objection decision under review is affirmed.
