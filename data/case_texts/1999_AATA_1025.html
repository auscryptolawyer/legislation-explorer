DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION [1999] AATA 1025

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No ST1999/6

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION
)




Re
THE TAXPAYER

Applicant
And
COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal
Deputy President B.H. Burns

Mr D.J. Trowse (Member)


Date 20 December 1999

Place Adelaide

Decision
In accordance with s43 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the Tribunal affirms the decision to disallow in full the applicant's objection against assessments for the years of income ended 30 June 1990, 1991, 1992 and 1998. The Tribunal directs that the implementation of its decision, as far as it relates to the 1990 year of income, be deferred until the High Court hands down its judgment in the case of Commissioner of Taxation v Ryan.



................(Signed).................

Deputy President B.H. Burns



CATCHWORDS

TAXATION – partnership income – whether applicant member of a partnership – sham – possible termination of arrangement by breach of condition – bad debt – effect of indemnity given by another to pay applicant’s tax liability – decision affirmed

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 – ss6, 19, 63, 92

Lindley on Partnership, 12th edition

Scott v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (No. 2) (1966) 40 ALJR 265

Snook v London and West Riding Investments Ltd (1967) 2 QB 786

Rose v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1951] HCA 68; (1951) 84 CLR 118

Everett v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 77 ATC 4478



REASONS FOR DECISION



20 December 1999
Deputy President B.H. Burns

Mr D.J. Trowse (Member)


  1. This is an application by the applicant for review of a decision of the Commissioner of Taxation (“the Commissioner”) to disallow in full an objection dated 11 November 1998 against assessments issued by the Commissioner for the years of income ended 30 June 1990, 1991, 1992 and 1998.
  2. The substantive matter before the Tribunal is whether the applicant was, during the 1990, 1991 and 1992 years of income, a partner in a business which operated a country hotel from leasehold premises. Another issue is the applicant’s entitlement to claim a deduction for an amount of $73,060 which is said to represent a bad debt incurred in the 1998 year of income and, in this regard, it is noted that the amount claimed equates with the sum of the additional amounts of income included by the respondent in the applicant’s returns for the years previously mentioned. The remaining matter requiring the Tribunal’s determination is the unusual submission that the tax assessed to the applicant should be the responsibility of the applicant’s former spouse on the basis of an indemnity which he provided in terms of an Order Property Settlement made in the Family Court on 27 September 1993.
  3. In considering the circumstances pertaining to the 1990 year of income, the Tribunal noted that the adjustments made by the Commissioner to the 1990 return were reflected in a ‘notice of assessment’ which issued on 17 February 1997 and that, at some time previous, the applicant had received a refund cheque on the basis of her being non-taxable in that year. If it is established that the processes applied in issuing the refund cheque constitute the making of an assessment, the Commissioner concedes that the time lapse is sufficient to prevent the issuing of an amended assessment. A question of that same kind is currently before the High Court (Commissioner of Taxation v Ryan) and, accordingly, it was agreed as between the parties that the implementation of the Tribunal’s decision as it relates to the 1990 year of income be delayed until the High Court delivers its judgment.
  4. The Tribunal had before it a copy of the documents lodged pursuant to s37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, together with nine exhibits tendered on behalf of the applicant and six by the Commissioner. The applicant was represented by Mr B., who is an accountant and registered tax agent. The respondent was represented by one of his officers.
  5. In the course of the hearing, sworn evidence was given by the applicant, her former husband, her daughter, the daughter’s spouse and Mr G. who is an officer with the Insolvency and Trustee Service Australia. The Tribunal has some reservations on some of the information disclosed by the applicant and her former husband and, in areas of conflict, the evidence of the daughter and her husband is preferred.
  6. To better appreciate the factual recital, it seems advisable to briefly touch upon the thrust of the contentions put on behalf of the applicant. It was argued that the Commissioner had erred in sharing part of the net income of the hotel operation in favour of the applicant for the following reasons:
    • the arrangement regarding the conduct of the hotel business was a sham either from the start or from when conditions of the arrangement were breached;
    • breaches of some of the conditions contained in an agreement which stated a partnership intention terminated the arrangement;
    • it was incorrect as a principle of tax law to include as assessable income amounts that had not been received and are not likely to be received in the future.
  7. The following summary of events represents the Tribunal’s findings as to the relevant facts:
    • (a) On 27 July 1989, the applicant, her then husband, daughter and son-in-law entered into a memorandum of agreement (the agreement) to form a partnership of hoteliers whereby, inter alia, each partner was to contribute an equal amount of capital and was to share equally in all profits and losses.
    • (b) In about August 1989, the four signatories to the agreement purchased the leasehold of a country hotel for a consideration of $395,000.
    • (c) The acquisition of the leasehold was funded, in the main, by a loan from the National Bank. The greater part of the balance came from monies advanced from an associated family trust to the individual members of the family in such amounts as to ensure that all four were making an equal contribution towards the venture.
    • (d) The loan from the bank was in the names of the four parties to the agreement.
    • (e) The lease of the hotel was also in their joint names.
    • (f) The bank account used in connection with the hotel operations was opened and maintained in the names of the four. Each of them possessed the sole authority to draw cheques on that account.
    • (g) All four worked and resided at the hotel for the period up until February 1992. It was then that the applicant separated from her husband and moved to the city.
    • (h) The joint arrangement ceased in June 1993. From that point on and until the leasehold was sold, the business was owned and conducted by the former husband of the applicant.
    • (i) Partnership income tax returns were prepared and lodged for all of the years 1990 to 1993 inclusive. Those returns showed an equal distribution of profits and losses between the four.
    • (j) Profit and loss statements and balance sheets were prepared for all the years in question. Included in those statements were details of capital contributions by each of the four, their drawings and also their sharing in the profits and losses occurring in those years.
    • (k) The personal income tax returns lodged by the applicant for the years 1990 and 1991 recorded a share of profits from the alleged partnership. The 1992 personal return disclosed a share of a loss from that same source.
    • (l) As a result of divorce proceedings, an Order Property Settlement approved by the Family Court on 12 October 1993 was entered into whereby the husband agreed to indemnify the applicant for any tax liability arising from the hotel partnership.
    • (m) In July 1995, either the applicant or the son-in-law acting with her authority handed to Mr B cash books which revealed details of daily hotel takings for the period to 30 June 1992. After an examination of the cash books, which disclosed as understatement of $292,238 in the amount of income declared in the partnership returns, Mr B forwarded the books to the Commissioner with a request for re-assessment.
    • (n) The Commissioner obliged by issuing assessments in January 1997. In the case of the applicant, her amount of taxable incomes were increased thus:
      1. $27,075
      2. $23,510
      3. $22,475

TOTAL $73,060



The additional tax liability resulting from those increases was $34,118.43.



(o) The applicant’s former husband, on his own petition, was declared bankrupt on 4 August 1997. The administration of his bankrupt estate has yet to be finalised and no final dividend has been paid.
  1. After a consideration of all the evidence, the Tribunal makes the following additional findings. First, the Tribunal finds that the applicant played no role in the understatement nor did she participate in the ill-gotten gains. Secondly, and this relates to the extent of the applicant’s drawings against her alleged share of profits, the Tribunal finds that the need to withdraw funds for private purposes was diminished to some degree by the circumstance that the hotel provided for her accommodation and meals. Furthermore, the Tribunal finds that the applicant’s purported share of profits was applied towards the payment of contributions to a personal superannuation policy and also the annual tax commitment raised on her returns as lodged. Also, it is accepted that interest charged on monies advanced to the applicant by the family trust and used by her as a contribution towards the purchase of the hotel leasehold had been offset against her profit share.
  2. The relevant provisions of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (the Act), to the extent considered necessary, are set out below:
“6(1) “partnership” means an association of persons carrying on business as partners or in receipt of income jointly, but does not include a company;

...

Money credited, reinvested etc. to be deemed to be derived

19(1) Income or money shall be deemed to have been derived by a person although it is not actually paid over to him but is reinvested, accumulated, capitalised, carried to any reserve, sinking fund or insurance fund however designated, or otherwise dealt with on his behalf or as he directs.

...

Bad debts

63(1) Debts which are bad debts and are written off as such during the year of income, and:

(a) have been brought to account by the taxpayer as assessable income of any year; or
(b) are in respect of money lent in the ordinary course of the business of the lending of money by a taxpayer who carries on that business;
shall be allowable deductions.

...

Income and deductions or partners

92(1) The assessable income of a partner in a partnership shall include:
(a) so much of the individual interest of the partner in the net income of the partnership of the year of income as is attributable to a period when the partner was a resident; and
...”


  1. A partnership is a contract of a kind which does not require to be entered into with any particular formalities (see “Lindley on Partnership” 12th Edition p 121). In any analysis of the existence of a partnership contract, particular regard must be had to the intention of the parties. On that and associated issues, the Tribunal finds – that it was the intention of all four members of the family to enter into a partnership arrangement, that all four held a basic understanding of the arrangement being entered into and that their purpose in becoming involved was to share in the profits to be yielded from the hotel business. Those factors, together with the conduct exhibited in acquiring and running the business, leave the Tribunal in no doubt, and it so finds, that a partnership arrangement existed in the years of income 1990, 1991 and 1992, and that throughout those years, the applicant was a member of the partnership. Certainly, it seems that the banking of hotel receipts in the joint bank account brings the arrangement squarely within the extended taxation definition of partnership in s6(1) of the Act.
  2. The alternative submission that the partnership was terminated by the fraudulent behaviour of one of the partners is now considered. It is true that the partnership arrangement does contain a provision whereby the non-offending parties may, by notice in writing, determine the partnership and that, in those circumstances, the partners giving notice have the option of purchasing the share of the other. No such notice was forthcoming and thus the submission that the breach by itself terminated the arrangement is rejected.
  3. The Tribunal now turns to consider the applicant's contention on the question of sham. It is the Tribunal’s considered view that the contention lacks foundation. An understanding of the term “sham” is assisted by a consideration of what was said on that topic by Windeyer J in Scott v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (No. 2) (1966) 40 ALJR 265, and by Diplock LJ in Snook v London and West Riding Investments Ltd (1967) 2 QB 786. Windeyer J commented, at p279:
“... On the other hand, if the scheme, including the deed, was intended to be a mere façade behind which activities might be carried on which were not to be really directed to the stated purposes but to other ends, then the words of the deed should be disregarded. ... A disguise is a real thing: it may be an elaborate and carefully prepared thing; but it is nevertheless a disguise. The difficult and debatable philosophic questions of the meaning and relationship of reality, substance and form are for the purposes of our law generally resolved by asking did the parties who entered into the ostensible transaction mean it to be in truth their transaction, or did they mean it to be, and in fact use it as, merely a disguise, a façade, a sham, a false front – all these words have been metaphorically used – concealing their real transaction: ...”


In the case of Snook (supra), Diplock LJ had the following to say:

“... one thing, I think, is clear in legal principle, morality and the authorities ... that for acts or documents to be a “sham”, with whatever legal consequences follow from this, all the parties thereto must have a common intention that the acts or documents are not to create the legal rights and obligations which they give the appearance of creating.”


In the current matter, the Tribunal concludes that none of the required indicia of a sham mentioned in the above judgments were present at any stage of the arrangement entered into. On that basis, the Tribunal rejects any suggestion of sham.

  1. The fact that the applicant did not withdraw her full profit entitlement is of no consequence for income tax purposes. The applicant’s contention to the contrary overlooks the wording of ss19 and 92 of the Act and also the judicial opinion that the design of s92 is to catch the whole of a partner’s interest in any year of income rather than merely the amount of his drawings – see Rose v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1951] HCA 68; (1951) 84 CLR 118, at p124, and Everett v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 77 ATC 4478, at p 4481.
  2. The amount claimed as a bad debt in the 1997 year of income represents a one-quarter share of the additional amounts of income brought to account by the Commissioner in determining the net income of the partnership for the years 1990, 1991 and 1992. While it is accepted that the adjustment effectively increased the applicant’s interest in the partnership to the extent of $73,060, the Tribunal is unable to ascertain the value of the applicant’s equity in the partnership at the time of its dissolution. This situation arises because no allowance has been made for the overdrawn state of the applicant’s capital account as revealed in the last partnership balance sheet made available to the Tribunal. Notwithstanding that deficiency, the claim for bad debt is based on the premise that the amount of $73,060 was owing to the applicant by her former husband who is an undischarged bankrupt.
  3. An examination of the Order Property Settlement approved by the Family Court reveals the manner in which it was agreed that the family assets were to be shared between the applicant and her former husband. The Tribunal notes that, as part of this distribution, the Order specifies that the applicant’s interest in the hotel partnership was to vest in the former husband as from 1 July 1993. In the Tribunal’s view, this represents a disposal by the applicant of her interest in the partnership and thus the demand for a further $73,060 for this same interest has no substance. There is no debt and, accordingly, the claim for bad debt fails.
  4. In the event that the Tribunal has misunderstood the bad debt claim and that conversely, it is based on the additional tax liability raised against the applicant and which is covered by the indemnity given by the former husband, the Tribunal offers the following opinion. The amount of the additional tax liability is not a debt which has been brought to account as assessable income in any prior year and thus the requirements set out in s63(1) of the Act have not been met.
  5. Finally, there is the proposal that, in view of the indemnity given by the former husband, the respondent should be seeking payment of the outstanding tax from him rather than the applicant. The Tribunal opines that the liability to the Commissioner is personal to the applicant and is not effected by the arrangement agreed in the Order Property Settlement.
  6. For the reasons stated, the Tribunal affirms the Commissioner’s decision to disallow in full the applicant's objection against the years of income ended 30 June 1990, 1991, 1992 and 1998, and it directs that the implementation of this decision, as far as it relates to the 1990 year, be deferred until the High Court hands down its judgment in the case of Commissioner of Taxation v Ryan.

I certify that the 18 preceding paragraphs are a true copy of the reasons for the decision herein of Deputy President B.H. Burns and Mr D.J. Trowse (Member).
