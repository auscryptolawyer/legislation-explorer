Franke and Cochlear Pty Ltd and Commissioner of Patents and MXM Party Joined [1993] AATA 465 (8 April 1993)

Last Updated: 25 August 2008



Administrative Appeals Tribunal



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION



PATENTS - patent application - notice of opposition - opposition dismissed - review of - whether jurisdiction to review - whether registered patent attorney is entitled to oppose an application for a grant of patent in own name



STATUTORY INTERPRETATION - relationship between Patent Act 1952, - Patent Act 1990 and Patent Regulations 1991 - interpretation of sub-section 234(3) of the 1990 Act - whether regulations apply to opposition proceedings under 1952 Act - discussion of the purpose or object of the 1990 Act



JURISDICTION - whether jurisdiction to review a decision dismissing an opposition - whether dismissing of an opposition to a grant of patent is an ultimate decision or a procedural step

REASONS FOR DECISION



8 April 1993 Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)





1. An application for a standard patent was filed by M.X.M., a French corporation, on 4 April 1989. It was accepted and advertised on 21 November 1991 pursuant to the Patents Act 1990 ("the 1990 Act") which came into effect on 30 April 1991. It is common ground between the parties that because of the transitional provisions contained in s 234 of the 1990 Act, the statutory principles to be applied in considering the application for patent are those contained in the Patents Act 1952 ("the 1952 Act"). A major area of disagreement between the parties is whether regulations made under the 1990 Act govern the way in which the patent application is to be considered under the 1952 Act.



2. On 18 February 1992 a notice of opposition was served by Peter Noel Franke under s 59 of the 1952 Act. It was in the following terms —



"AUSTRALIA



Patents Act 1990



NOTICE OF OPPOSITION



I, Peter Noel Franke of care of Floor 4, Amory Gardens, 2 Cavill Avenue, Ashfield, New South Wales 2131, give notice that I oppose the grant of a patent in respect of patent application _ 617164 in the name of M.X.M., of "Les Mimosas" Qartier Croix-Rouge, 06600 - Antibes, France.



A copy of this notice is sent this day to the applicants [sic] address for service, care of F.B. Rice & Co., 28A Montague Street, Balmain, New South Wales 2041.



Our address for service is c/o WATERMARK PATENT & TRADEMARK ATTORNEYS, of Floor 4, Amory Gardens, 2 Cavill Avenue, Ashfield, New South Wales 2131, Australia



By our Patents Attorneys,

WATERMARK PATENT & TRADEMARK ATTORNEYS



(Signed) Peter Franke 18/2/92

.............................................. ............................... ..........

P.N. FRANKE (Date)

Registered Patent Attorney"



3. It will be noted that the notice was given by Mr Franke in his own name. It was agreed between the parties that Mr Franke is, and was at all relevant times, a registered patent attorney.



4. On 18 March 1992 the opponent, in accordance with regulation 5.4, served on the applicant a statement setting out the grounds of opposition and the particulars relating to the grounds. There was no dispute between the parties that these grounds reveal substantial reasons for opposing the grant of the patent. On 14 April 1992 M.X.M. invoked the provisions of regulation 5.5 made under the 1990 Act (hereafter referred to as the regulations) and requested the Commissioner to dismiss the opposition on the grounds that the "opponent" had not established locus standi in the notice of opposition or in the statement of grounds and particulars.



5. The request for dismissal was heard by a delegate of the Commissioner. The reasons for his decision summarised the arguments put before him and contain certain findings. The opposition was dismissed and this application is brought to review that decision. Cochlear Pty Ltd has been joined in the application before this Tribunal but no arguments were advanced on its behalf.



6. The first matter to be considered is whether this Tribunal has jurisdiction to hear the application. Mr Franke's principal submission was that the patent application having been filed on 4 April 1989, is to be determined in accordance with the Patents Act 1952. The Patents Act 1990 did not come into effect until 30 April 1991. The applicant argued that the transitional provisions of s 234 of the 1990 Act resulted in the patent application being dealt with under the old Act and any relevant regulations under that Act. Consequent upon the repeal of the 1952 Act, there are no longer any relevant regulations flowing from powers granted by that Act. Nevertheless the applicant argued that this Tribunal had jurisdiction under powers conferred by the 1990 Act and the regulations made under that Act because procedures under those regulations had been invoked by the Commissioner and because those regulations conferred jurisdiction. It followed that although he was mistaken, in the applicant's submission, the Commissioner had by his actions exercised powers which were therefore subject to review by this Tribunal.



7. I can not accept that jurisdiction can be conferred in these circumstances. It is true that this Tribunal has power to review a decision made invalidly (Re Gowing and Civil Aviation Authority [1990] AATA 56; 11 AAR 411). This power, however, does not extend to exercising jurisdiction where none is conferred by a valid and extant statute by existing regulations. This Tribunal has jurisdiction to hear the present application only if it was conferred by the 1990 Act and the regulations made under it, and only if that Act, and more particularly those regulations, were validly applied in the processes of dealing with the patent application.



8. Jurisdiction was conferred on this Tribunal by s 224 of the 1990 Act which is in the following terms —



"s224(1) Application may be made to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review of —



(a) a decision of the Commissioner under section 10, 17, 32, 33 or 66, subsection 103(2), section 113, subsection 137(3), paragraph 142(2)(b), subsection 150(2), 151(2), 152(2) or 152(3), or section 173, 215 or 223; or



(b) a decision of the Commissioner under Chapter 20 not to register a person who is a patent attorney; or



(c) a decision of the Director under subsection 147(2) or (3), section 149 or subsection 152(1).



(2) Where a person who makes a decision mentioned in subsection (1) gives the person or persons affected by the decision written notice of the making of the decision, the notice must include a statement to the effect that, subject to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, application may be made to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal for review of the decision to which the notice relates by or on behalf of the person or persons whose interests are affected by the decision.



(3) Failure to comply with subsection (2) in relation to a decision does not affect the validity of the decision.



(4) In this section —



"decision" has the same meaning as in the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975."



9. It will be noted that jurisdiction is selectively conferred depending upon the statutory basis of decisions sought to be reviewed. Unlike s 1317B of the Corporations Law, s 224 of the 1990 Act does not confer jurisdiction to review any decision made under the Act.



10. In the present case, application was made for the grant of a standard patent and notice of opposition was given to that application. In those circumstances the Commissioner is obliged to "decide the case in accordance with the regulations". The relevant sections are as follows —



"s 59 The Minister or any other person may, in accordance with the regulations, oppose the grant of a standard patent on one or more of the following grounds, but on no other ground —



(a) that the nominated person is not entitled to a grant of a patent for the invention;

(b) that the invention is not a patentable invention because it does not comply with paragraph 18(1)(a) or (b);

(c) that the specification filed in respect of the complete application does not comply with subsection 40(2) or (3).



s 60(1) Where the grant of a standard patent is opposed, the Commissioner must decide the case in accordance with the regulations.

(2) The Commissioner must give the applicant and the opponent a reasonable opportunity to be heard before deciding a case.



(3) The Commissioner may, in deciding a case, take into account any ground on which the grant of a standard patent may be opposed, whether relied upon by the opponent or not.



(4) The applicant, and any opponent, may appeal to the Federal Court against a decision of the Commissioner under this section."



11. A decision under s 60 is not reviewable by this Tribunal. A power of review is expressly conferred upon the Federal Court by sub-section 60(4). Nevertheless, it was submitted that the Tribunal had jurisdiction to deal with the present application because of the terms of relevant regulations. Although the Act does not govern the substantive rights of the parties in determining the ultimate fate of the patent application, it was submitted by the respondent that the regulations invested this Tribunal with appropriate jurisdiction.



12. Section 228 empowers the Governor-General to make regulations "not inconsistent with this Act ... prescribing matters necessary or convenient to be prescribed or carrying out or giving effect to this Act". Without limiting the generality of this power, sub-section (2) provides that it includes the power to make regulations ... "(m) providing for appeals against decisions of the Commissioner made under the regulations ..."



13. Regulation 22.26 made pursuant to this power is in the following terms —



"22.26(1) In this regulation —



"decision" has the same meaning as in the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975.



(2) Application may be made to the Tribunal for review of a decision of —



(a) the Commissioner under the following provisions —



(i) paragraph 3.24(1)(b) ("Commissioner may request samples and viability statement");



(ii) subregulation 3.25(2) ("request for Commissioner's certificate authorising release of sample of a micro-organism");



(iii) subregulation 5.5(3) ("dismissal of opposition");



(iv) subregulation 5.6(3) ("determination of opposition");



(v) subparagraph 6.2(1)(b)(ii) ("prescribed period: grant of standard patent");



(vi) subregulation 22.21(5) ("protection or compensation of certain persons"); or



(b) the Patent Attorneys Professional Standards Board under subregulation 20.21(7) ("authorisation to bring proceedings against a patent attorney"); or



(c) the Patent Attorneys Disciplinary Tribunal under subregulation 20.23(2), (4) or (5) ("decision of Disciplinary Tribunal").



(3) If the Commissioner gives a person affected by a decision referred to in subregulation (2) written notice of the making of the decision, the notice must include a statement to the effect that, subject to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, application may be made to the Tribunal for review of that decision by or on behalf of the person whose interests are affected by it.



(4) Failure to comply with subregulation (3) in relation to a decision does not affect the validity of the decision.



(5) If the Tribunal reviews a decision of the Commissioner under subsection 224(1) of the Act ("review of decisions") and the decision is not affirmed, the Commissioner must publish a notice of the decision of the Tribunal in the Official Journal."



14. The respondent contends that this Tribunal has jurisdiction to hear the present application — an appeal from the decision of the Commissioner under sub-regulation 5.5(3) — by virtue of the operation of sub-paragraph 22·26(2)(a)(iii).



15. Section 228 can not empower the making of regulations that are inconsistent with s 224. Thus a regulation purporting to give this Tribunal jurisdiction to review a decision under s 60 would, in my view, be invalid. I have come to the conclusion however that the decision before me is not one made pursuant to s 60 as it does not "decide the case". I take that phrase to mean a decision whether or not the patent applied for should be granted. What is before me is an application to review a decision to dismiss an opposition. It does not follow as a matter of logic that the dismissal of the opposition by Mr Franke will necessarily result in the grant of the patent. The Commissioner may take into account any ground upon which the grant of a standard patent may be opposed, whether relied upon by the opponent or not. He is bound to investigate the merits of the application even where no opposition exists. Dismissing an opposition therefore is not the same as deciding the case. It is a procedural step taken along the way before an ultimate decision under s 60 is made. Provided the 1990 regulations have application when dealing with the patent application and the notice of opposition, then I hold that this Tribunal has jurisdiction to determine the question of dismissal of opposition.



16. The jurisdiction conferred by regulations made pursuant to s 228 appears to be additional to the jurisdiction conferred by s 224. As subordinate legislation, however, such regulations can not survive if they contradict the express provisions of the Act. An amendment to paragraph 224(1)(a) would be desirable to make this clear beyond doubt. Conferment of jurisdiction in an indirect manner can only lead to confusion in the future.



17. Section 59 of the 1952 Act entitles a person by notice in writing to oppose the grant of a patent on one or more of the specified grounds provided that the opponent is "a person interested". On the hearing before the delegate, findings were made that Cochlear Pty Ltd had an interest which was sufficient to establish locus for a person filing notice of opposition, that a serious opposition was foreshadowed by Mr Franke's documents, that Mr Franke was acting following instructions from Cochlear Pty Ltd in filing the notice of opposition, although he had not disclosed that fact, and that Mr Franke did not have locus derived from his role merely as an agent acting under exclusive instructions on behalf of Cochlear Pty Ltd. None of these findings was challenged on the hearing before me. In order to establish his locus standi, Mr Franke no longer relied upon the undisclosed connection with his client or upon the evidence from his client that was tendered at the hearing before the delegate. On the locus standi question, he relied entirely upon his status as a registered patent attorney and upon the statutory privileges conferred upon a person holding that qualification.



18. Section 164 of the 1952 Act provided that —



"Where by this Act an application, notice or request is required or permitted to be made or signed by a person, that application, notice or request may be made or signed, on behalf of that person, by a person entitled under this Act to practise as a patent attorney."



19. The corresponding provision is worded in a slightly different way in s 213 of the 1990 Act —



"An application, notice or request required or permitted under this Act to be made or signed by a person may be made or signed, on behalf of that person, by a person entitled under this Act to practise as a patent attorney".



20. Mr Franke argued that the rights conferred upon him by these sections entitled him to oppose an application for a grant of patent in his own name without disclosing the identity of his client. The arguments put before the delegate (and adopted in these proceedings) were summarised in the reasons for decision in these words —



"He argued that each word must if possible be given a meaning, ie if a second word is included that word should be given a relevant and sensible meaning which is different from the first word.



Following these principles he argued that the provisions of section 213 were permissive and not there to restrict any rights, that they were not limited as being "subject to this Act" and thus applied in parallel with section 59.



He argued that the words "made" and "signed" used in the section must have different meanings: that "made" must mean something different or more than just signing a document, and would include, for example, making an application; that the section clearly suggests that an application, notice or request may be made by a patent attorney on behalf of a person.



Mr Franke continued by referring to dictionary definitions of the words "make" and "behalf". He sought leave to adduce copies of the relevant pages from the Macquarie Dictionary and Webster's New Twentieth Century Dictionary. Mr Schilling for the applicant agreed and leave was granted.



Mr Franke referred to the large number of meanings for "make", which made reference to dictionaries not very helpful, and contended that it should be given its ordinary meaning in the context: it must mean "make an application". Turning to "on behalf of" he referred to the Macquarie Dictionary in which "behalf" is defined as "side, interest, or aid.....favour or interest". He submitted that he had made the present notice of opposition on behalf of Cochlear Pty Ltd and section 213 accordingly allowed it to be a valid notice of opposition.



Mr Franke then made reference to some judicial guidance which could be gained in the context of the Trade Practices Act, in particular Section 82(2), where it is necessary to determine whether an act was done "on behalf of a company". He referred to Adelaide Petroleum NL v Poseidon Ltd (1990) 98 ALR 431; R v Toohey [1980] HCA 2; (1980) 28 ALR 27 asserting that these decisions, amongst others, state that the narrowest way these words can be interpreted is "under the explicit or implicit instructions of the company". He stated that in R v Toohey (supra) a broader view was taken: where there was no real trustee relationship merely a vague beneficial intention, this was deemed sufficient to establish that the acts were undertaken "on behalf of" the aboriginals of the Northern Territory. In the present case, he argued, even taking the narrowest interpretation, Mr Franke had been instructed to file the notice of opposition "on behalf of" Cochlear Pty Ltd, he did so and, in accordance with section 213, he contends the notice of opposition has been validly filed."



21. In my view this submission must fail. Section 213 of the 1990 Act (which is in chapter 22 under the heading "miscellaneous") is merely facultative. It permits a designated person to exercise certain rights of another person "on behalf of that person" without further proof of authority, provided the person exercising those rights is entitled to practise as a patent attorney. I do not consider that the use of the word "made" enlarges the ostensible authority of a patent attorney. Grammatically, the word is used so that it may be applied to "an application" just as "signed" is used so that it may be applied to "notice or request". The patent attorney has authority to deal with documents under the Act and regulations on behalf of his client. This grant of authority does not, in my view, enable him to make applications (or to give notices of opposition) in his own name, as if he were not acting on behalf of another person.



22. The delegate pointed out in his reasons another consideration which assists in construction of the section and which I endorse —



"Mr Franke also argued that Section 213 allowed an attorney to "make an application". By this I take him to mean filing an application for a patent in his own name but on behalf of another, whose name he need not reveal. In the 1990 Act, Section 29 allows "a person" to apply for a patent without defining any special qualifications for a person to be an applicant. In the 1952 Act, Section 34 makes specific provision for an "agent or attorney" to make an application for a patent. It would thus create a redundancy to interpret that special privilege to make application for a patent is given to a registered patent attorney by means of either Section 213 or Section 164 of the respective Acts."



23. In the hearing before me the principal argument relied upon by the applicant was that the regulations had no role to play in proceedings dealing with an application for a grant of patent made under the 1952 Act. There were previously regulations under that Act. They were repealed following the repeal of the Principal Act by s 230 of the 1990 Act, except to the extent that they were saved when dealing with applications advertised under the 1952 Act.



24. The applicant bases his argument principally upon sub-section 234(3) of the 1990 Act. The whole section is in the following terms —



"s234(1) Where, before the commencing day —



(a) a patent application and provisional specification had been lodged under the 1952 Act; and



(b) a complete specification had not been lodged under that Act in respect of the application; and



(c) the application had not been withdrawn;



then, subject to this Chapter and the regulations, this Act applies in relation to the application on and after that day as if it were a provisional application under this Act.



(2) Where, before the commencing day —



(a) a patent application had been lodged under the 1952 Act; and



(b) a complete specification, or a petty patent specification, had been lodged under that Act in respect of the application; and



(c) the application had not been withdrawn or finally dealt with;



then, subject to this Chapter and the regulations, this Act applies on and after that day —



(d) in relation to the application as if it were a complete application made under this Act; and



(e) in relation to the petty patent specification as if it were a complete specification filed under this Act in respect of the application.



(3) Chapter 5 of this Act does not apply in relation to an application mentioned in subsection (2), but Part V of the 1952 Act, as in force immediately before the commencing day, continues to apply in relation to such an application.



(4) Chapter 9 of this Act does not apply in relation to —



(a) an application mentioned in subsection (2); or



(b) a patent granted on such an application.



(5) Objection cannot be taken to —



(a) an application mentioned in subsection (2); or



(b) a patent granted on such an application;



and such a patent is not invalid, so far as the invention is claimed in any claim, on any ground that would not have been available against the application or patent, as the case may be, under the 1952 Act.



(6) A petty patent specification to which subsection (2) applies cannot be amended under this Act so as to include more than one claim.



25. Chapter 5 of the 1990 Act referred to in sub-section 234(3) consists of s 59 and s 60 which I have quoted above. "This Act" is defined in schedule 1 of the 1990 Act to include the regulations. The applicant argued therefore that there was no scope for the application of the summary procedures for dismissal of oppositions introduced by the regulations. It must follow from this submission that there would be no regulations governing applications lodged under the 1952 Act but dealt with procedurally under the 1990 Act. The applicant submitted that the question of locus should be decided upon the hearing of the principal application for a grant of patent when all the evidence is before the delegate. It was also submitted as the principal reason why the decision under review should be set aside, that the procedural remedies provided by the regulations and invoked by M.X.M. were not available in the present case, and that accordingly the delegate had no power to dismiss the opposition in the way he did.



26. The short point in the applicant's submission, therefore, is that sub-section 234(3) of the 1990 Act excludes the operation of the 1990 regulations throughout the passage of M.X.M.'s application to finality, that there are no other relevant regulations and that accordingly the matter should be dealt with in accordance with the provisions of the 1952 Act. There was provision in s 163 of the 1952 Act to determine questions of interest of parties lodging oppositions. It was not availed of in the present circumstances because it has been repealed and has no application where the application has been advertised under the 1990 Act.



27. If read literally, the words of the sub-section appear to support the applicant's submission. The respondent argued that they should not be read literally, that ambiguities are apparent on the face of the sub-section, that absurdities would result in the general application of the Act if the applicant's submission is correct, and that therefore one is entitled, pursuant to s 15AA and s 15AB(1)(b) of the Acts Interpretation Act to resort to extrinsic aids to assist in interpreting the sub-section.

28. The arrangement of the regulations mirrors the arrangement of the 1990 Act. Chapter 5 of the Act deals with oppositions. Chapter 5 of the regulations deals with the way in which notices of opposition are to be filed and dealt with. It requires the filing of a statement, setting out grounds and particulars. It provides a summary procedure available to an applicant to have the opposition dismissed, after a hearing confined to the particularised grounds of opposition. It provides for certain ancillary matters dealing with the time for giving evidence and the time for carrying out preparatory steps necessary before a dismissal of opposition hearing. The respondent says firstly that "this Act" in sub-section 234(3) does not mean "this Act including the regulations" because the word and figure "Chapter 5" are in the singular. Had the draftsman intended to exclude the application of the regulations, he would have used the word and figure "Chapters 5". In itself this is an ambiguity, argued the respondent, entitling one to turn to extrinsic evidence for aid in interpretation.



29. Further possible ambiguities were also referred to, where it was submitted on the face of the legislation that the draftsman intended to refer to the Act only and not to the Act as well as the regulations. The words "this Act" appear, for example, in ss 1, 2, 7 and 13 in contexts where this must be so. I have not been through the Act exhaustively to see whether there are other examples. However, it should be noted that there is at least one other section of the Act (s 154) where the words "this Act" are clearly intended to refer not only to the Act but also to the regulations. A consideration of the use of the words "this Act" in other sections of the 1990 Act however must be of limited assistance. It seems to me that the words appearing in sub-section 234(3) are those to be construed, in the context in which they appear, in order to determine whether the "contrary intention" referred to at the head of schedule 1 appears.



30. Section 15AA of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 provides that in the interpretation of a provision of an Act, a construction that would promote the purpose or object underlying the Act (whether that purpose or object is expressly stated in the Act or not) shall be preferred to a construction that would not promote the purpose or object. This section, enacted in 1981, is declaratory of principles that were regularly applied prior to its enactment.



31. In Cooper Brookes (Wollongong) Pty Ltd v The Commissioner of Taxation [1981] HCA 26; 147 CLR 297, decided on 5 June 1981, Mason and Wilson JJ said at page 320 —



"...Generally speaking, mere inconvenience of result in itself is not a ground for departing from the natural and ordinary sense of the language read in its context. But there are cases in which inconvenience of result or improbability of result assists the court in concluding that an alternative construction which is reasonably open is to be preferred to the literal meaning because the alternative interpretation more closely conforms to the legislative intent discernible from other provisions in the statute.



There is a similar problem with the related so-called "golden rule" of construction. There are statements of the rule which would confine the courts to the ordinary grammatical sense of the words used unless that produces an absurdity or inconsistency. It is to be noted that Dixon J. in Broken Hill South Ltd. v. Commissioner of Taxation (N.S.W.) [1937] HCA 4; (1937) 56 CLR 337, at p.371 observed that departure from the ordinary grammatical sense is not legitimate unless there is "some obscurity or some inconsistency", though it may be that "obscurity" was intended to be a reference to "absurdity". For the reason already given in the discussion of the literal rule, departure from the ordinary grammatical sense cannot be restricted to cases of absurdity and inconsistency.



In some cases in the past these rules of construction have been applied too rigidly. The fundamental object of statutory construction in every case is to ascertain the legislative intention by reference to the language of the instrument viewed as a whole. But in performing that task the courts look to the operation of the statute according to its terms and to legitimate aids to construction."



32. What then was the purpose or object underlying the Act? It is well known that it was introduced following proceedings of the Industrial Property Advisory Committee, whose report to the Minister for Science was headed "Patents, Innovation and Competition in Australia". After a consideration of that report, the Minister issued a statement dealing with each of the recommendations and had the statement published in the Official Journal of Patents Trademarks and Designs on 18 December 1986. The 1990 Act was introduced as a Bill in the House of Representatives on 1 June 1989. An explanatory memorandum accompanying the Bill set out the background in these words —



"1. This Bill replaces the present Patents Act 1952. There are several important aspects to the change.



2. The Bill implements the Government's response to the 1984 report of the Industrial Property Advisory Committee (IPAC), "Patents, Innovation and Competition in Australia". The Committee's recommendations and the Government's response were tabled in the House of Representatives on 28 November 1986 by the Hon Barry O Jones MP, then Minister for Science. Apart from improvements on a variety of administrative matters, ranging from examination of patent applications to enforcing patents in the courts, the Bill implements a number of policy measures. Notably, the Bill strengthens the standards of patentability for standard patents by testing inventions for novelty and inventive step against prior disclosures in recorded form anywhere in the world rather than just in Australia. Other policy changes were also outlined in the Government's response to IPAC's report.



3. As recommended by IPAC and accepted by the Government, the text of the Patents Act has been thoroughly redrafted and re-arranged to modernise its language and avoid unnecessary complexity. So far as possible, the Bill is confined to matters of principle, leaving procedural matters to be dealt with in the regulations.



4. The Bill has had the benefit of input from a working party, set up under the Government's response to one of IPAC's recommendations, comprising representatives of the Patent Office, the Law Council of Australia and the Institute of Patent Attorneys of Australia. The working party reviewed the legislation and made a number of suggestions for streamlining procedures and the wording of the Patents Act.



5. The Bill incorporates amendments to the Patents Act, relating to extensions of patent term, made by the Patents Amendment Act 1989.



6. The Bill derives from the Patents Bill 1989, which was introduced into the House of Representatives on 1 June 1989 but lapsed when the Parliament was prorogued prior to the March 1990 election. The present Bill takes into account public submissions made on the Patents Bill 1989. In particular, some omissions and drafting errors which appeared in the previous Bill have been corrected, and the present Bill includes transitional provisions."



33. As one would expect, the Government intended to exclude procedural matters from the legislation, leaving them to be dealt with in the regulations. It was never contemplated that the Act be allowed to operate without the benefit of a regulatory sub-structure. Both parties before me agreed that the vast majority of patent applications awaiting consideration are those lodged under the 1952 Act but advertised under the 1990 Act. Counsel for the respondent informed me that there were over 500 such applications. It is inconceivable that the legislature would have intended such applications to be dealt with without the benefit of the mechanics of regulations. There was no saving of s 177, the regulation making section of the 1952 Act. Section 228(2)(h) of the 1990 Act confers a regulation making power for the purpose of "making provision for and in relation to opposition proceedings". Sub-sections 228(2)(za) and 228(7) specifically authorise the making of regulations covering transitional and savings provision as a result of the repeal of the 1952 Act. No such regulations have been made in the case of oppositions, presumably because reliance is placed upon the specific regulations made pursuant to the specific grant of power.



34. Acts required by the 1990 Act to be done under a provision of that Act which were in fact done under a provision of the 1952 Act that corresponds to a provision of the 1990 Act, are taken to have been done under the relevant provisions of the 1990 Act (regulation 23.14). In the application of that regulation, sub-section 234(3) gives the result that the notice of opposition is lodged under the saved s 59 of the 1952 Act as it corresponds to s 59 of the 1990 Act. Therefore the notice is taken to have been lodged under the provision of the 1990 Act. As the operation of the regulations is not excluded by sub-section 234(3) (as I believe) then they must apply to the opposition proceedings.



35. In my view, the words "this Act" in sub-section 234(3) means the legislation alone. The extrinsic evidence, as well as internal evidence contained in the Act and regulations, indicate that the purpose or object underlying the Act would not be promoted if the words "this Act" were given the "dictionary" meaning attributable to the words in schedule 1. The considerations to which I have referred indicate that a contrary intention appears when those words are used in this sub-section.



36. The sentiments expressed by the Minister concerning the drafting of the Act may not have been reflected in its own terms. During the Second Reading Speech he said —



"The drafting style in the Bill breaks some new ground. The definitions in the back are in the form of a dictionary in a schedule, reducing enormously the amount of sheer verbiage to be waded through before getting to the central concepts. And there is a flow chart, right up front, which sets out most of the typical steps involved in getting and maintaining a patent. It explains some difficult procedures in graphic form. This is the first legislation known to me anywhere with these particular features. I am very grateful to the Office of Parliamentary Counsel and the Patents Office for the quality of their work. It is a splendid piece of drafting and a Bill that is notably easy to read.



................



The Bill is not merely a redraft, since it implements the Government's policy as outlined in our response to IPAC's report. We recognise, however, that the Bill does represent a thorough overhaul of the Patents Act with all its complexities. By disposing of some of the archaic language, and relegating procedural matters to the regulations, we hope that the Bill will be more accessible to the non-expert. But it would be remarkable if a redraft of this magnitude were perfect, despite all the care which has gone into its preparation. Some bits of the baby might have accidentally gone out along with the bath water...."



37. Nevertheless the considerations to which I have referred, compel a reading of the words "this Act" in sub-section 234(3) to mean the legislation only. It is to be hoped that some attention will be given to re-drafting the sub-section so that it does not, in future, become necessary to discern its true meaning from a consideration of external factors.



38. The decision under review is therefore affirmed.
