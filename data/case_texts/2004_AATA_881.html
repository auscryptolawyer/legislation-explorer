CATCHWORDS

Income tax – review of objection decisions – amended assessments following audit - applicant failed to declare periodic workers’ compensation payments received – whether applicant had borrowed money from his father at interest and loaned that money to an associated company at interest – whether interest income and expenditure properly returned – whether applicant entitled to claim as a deduction the value of a loan written off – whether tax shortfall penalties should be remitted – circumstances in which applicant completed his tax returns – applicant assisted by Australian Taxation Office employees in the preparation of the tax returns – findings that applicant had failed to discharge the onus on him to show that the amended assessments were excessive – objection decisions affirmed.

Taxation Administration Act 1953 ss 14ZS, 14ZQ, 14ZZ, 14ZZK.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss 19, 226H, 226J, 170A.



Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Inkster (1989) 20 ATR 1516



REASONS FOR DECISION



20 August 2004
M J Allen


  1. These proceedings concern the review of decisions made by the respondent to disallow objections made by the applicant, Mr Jamile Azim Abuothman, against assessments of income tax in the years ended 30 June 1993, 1994 and 1996. The reviewable decisions were made by a delegate of the respondent in June 2000 and the applications were made to the Tribunal in August 2000.
  2. Since April 2000 the applicant has been a person in respect of whom an administrator has been appointed by the Guardianship and Administration Board of Western Australia pursuant to the provisions of the Guardianship and Administration Act 1990 (WA).
  3. The matter was heard over a number of hearing days, most of which were attended by the applicant with his carer and his current administrator, Mr Haddad. Oral evidence was given by the applicant; Dr Kay; the applicant’s father, Mr Abusalim, (who gave evidence by telephone with the assistance of an interpreter in the Arabic language); and an officer of the Australian Taxation office (“ATO”), Mrs Matthews. The applicant was represented by an accountant, Mr Veder, and the respondent was represented by an ATO officer, Mr Maloney.
  4. The Tribunal received into evidence the documents filed under s 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (T1 - T41) and Exhibits A1 – A16 tendered by the applicant and R1 – R9 tendered by the respondent.

BACKGROUND

  1. The uncontested background evidence concerning the matter is that the applicant came to Australia from Palestine at the age of 23. He initially lived in Victoria and was involved in a business that failed in the late 1980s. In approximately October 1991 the applicant and his then wife commenced a fruit wholesaling business in partnership, trading under the name of “Jim and Son” (the partnership). In May 1992 the business was incorporated into a newly-incorporated company named Jamile & Son Pty Ltd (the company) of which the applicant and his wife were the only shareholders and directors. They were both also employees of the company. In October 1992 the applicant was injured in the course of that employment and, although attempts were made to continue the company’s business, by June 1993 the company had ceased to carry on its wholesaling business.
  2. Income tax returns for both the company and the applicant for the year ended 30 June 1993 (“the 1993 year”) were filed in June 1994. The applicant declared a taxable income of $5,400.
  3. The applicant’s income tax returns for the years ended 30 June 1994, 1995 and 1996 (referred to as “the 1994 year”, “the 1995 year” and “the 1996 year” respectively) were lodged in July 1996. For the 1994 year the applicant declared income from a money lending business of $43,918 and interest expenses of $34,602 for a net income from that business of $9,316. The applicant claimed tax losses from prior years of $16,682 as deductions for a net taxable loss of $7,366.
  4. For the 1995 year the applicant declared net business income of $8,657 and claimed prior year tax losses of $7,366 for a net taxable income of $1,291.
  5. For the 1996 year the applicant’s income tax return showed salary income of $67,198. The applicant also declared income from the money lending business of $43,918 from which were deducted interest expenses of $33,016 and bad debts written off of $155,220 for a net loss from the business of $144,318. The return declared a net taxable loss of $77,120.
  6. Officers of the ATO subsequently audited the applicant’s tax affairs and in April 1999 assessments were issued. No issues have arisen in respect of the 1992 year.
  7. For the 1993 year the applicant’s income was adjusted to include income of $38,391 from periodic workers compensation payments said to have been received by him in that year and to allow a deduction for carry forward losses of $27,496. After the addition of penalty and interest amounts, the amount payable by the applicant to the respondent was $4,854.37.
  8. In the 1994 year the applicant’s income was adjusted to include income of $37,421 periodic compensation payments and social security payments of $527. The money lending income and expenditure, and prior year losses were disallowed. After addition of penalties and interests the amount payable by the applicant was $14,853.65.
  9. For the 1995 year the applicant’s income was adjusted to exclude the business income returned and to disallow interest expenses and prior year losses claimed. The net effect was that the applicant received a nil tax advice for that year.
  10. For the 1996 year the applicant’s income was adjusted to exclude the business interest income and expenditure that was claimed. After the addition of penalties and interest the amount payable by the applicant was $28,991.28.
  11. In June 1999 objections were lodged on behalf of the applicant. For the 1993 and 1994 years the objections were against the inclusion of the periodic compensation amounts as assessable income of the applicant. For the 1995 and 1996 years the basis of the objection was that the objections made in respect of the 1993 and 1994 years, if successful, would affect the carried forward tax losses available to the applicant to offset against the taxable income in the 1996 year.
  12. In June 2000 the respondent disallowed the objections in respect of the 1993, 1994 and 1996 years. The applicant’s objection in respect of the 1995 year was considered to be not a valid objection as a nil tax advice is not an assessment and hence no right of objection arises.
  13. As noted above, the applicant’s application to this Tribunal for review of those decisions was made in August 2000. That application is made pursuant to Part IVC of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (“the Administration Act”). Reference will be made below to the scope of the Tribunal’s ability to review aspects of the respondent’s decisions regarding the imposition of penalties and interest in the years in question.
  14. By virtue of s 14ZZK(a) of the Administration Act the applicant is limited to the grounds stated in the taxation objection to which the decision relates, reference to which was made in [15] above, unless the Tribunal otherwise permits. On 26 April 2002 Senior Member Fayle ordered that the applicant’s grounds of objection in each of the 1993, 1994 and 1996 years be extended to include grounds that interest paid is an allowable deduction; that bad debts written off are allowable deductions; and that the penalties imposed are excessive and should be excised or reduced.
  15. In the event, when the matter came on for hearing the applicant abandoned the grounds of objection relating to prior business losses in the 1993 year because the respondent had in fact allowed as a deduction in the 1992 and 1993 years amounts of prior year losses. As a consequence, the issues for determination by the Tribunal in the proceedings were, for the 1993 and 1994 years, whether the applicant’s income included the amounts of periodic compensation payments referred to above, and whether the applicant should be allowed deductions in respect of interest expenses. For the 1996 year the issue was whether the applicant should be allowed a deduction for interest expense and what the impact would be if either or both of the 1993 and 1994 issues were found in favour of the applicant. For all years the issue of what penalties (if any) should be imposed was also to be determined.

Compensation Payments in 1993 and 1994

  1. It was not in dispute between the parties that the company maintained a compensation insurance policy or that the applicant made a claim for compensation payments following his injury in October 1992. Likewise, it was not in dispute that the company claimed against its policy (T5) or that the insurance company made payments during 1992, 1993 and 1994 to the company (T6). Document T18 is a letter from the insurance company to the ATO dated 23 January 1997, which was obtained during the course of the audit of the applicant’s taxation affairs. An attachment to the letter sets out the periodic compensation payments made by the insurance company to the company between 11 November 1992 and 25 May 1994 totalling $75,813.66, being $37,141.80 up until 29 June 1993 and the balance of $38,671.86 in the 1994 year. The letter also advised the ATO that the sum of $38,000 had been paid to the applicant by way of a lump sum settlement on 7 June 1994.
  2. What was in issue between the parties was whether or not the company paid the periodic compensation payments to the applicant. The ATO auditors decided that the payments received by the company should be regarded as assessable income of the applicant because they were satisfied that the applicant had in fact received the periodic payments from the company, even though the records were inadequate and it seemed that no tax instalment deductions had been made. The main factors that caused the ATO officers to arrive at that conclusion were as follows:

(a) The applicant had been appointed as the Managing Director of the company at a salary of $250 per week from 1 July 1992, increasing to $35,300 per annum from 1 October 1992. From 1 January 1993 the applicant’s salary was to increase to $65,000 per annum;

(b) An analysis of the cheque books and banking records of the company show that in the 1993 year approximately $11,750 had been transferred from the company accounts to the applicant’s bank accounts and the corresponding figure in the 1994 year was $23,550. Company cheques that appeared to be for the applicant’s salary in the 1993 year amounted to $37,041.95 and in the 1994 year cheques from the company banked to the applicant’s accounts plus insurance company cheques banked directly to the applicant’s accounts amounted to $31,560;

(c) The company’s accountant and company secretary informed the ATO auditors that he was sure that the applicant had received his compensation payments from the company in the relevant period. The auditors were also advised by insurance company representatives that the applicant would collect the compensation cheques each fortnight (T page 192);

(d) In July 1993 the applicant and his wife applied to their bank for a housing loan, declaring that the applicant’s salary was $1,250 per week or $65,000 per annum. The branch manager noted that the wholesale fruit business had ceased trading and the applicant and his wife were living “on a workers’ compensation payment which is being paid weekly ... a letter confirming the continuity of this payment dated 20 May 1993 is on file ... these payments are to continue indefinitely” (T page 17);

(e) On 14 July 1993 the applicant’s then solicitors wrote to the insurance company advising that his weekly payments of compensation were approximately one week late and that it would be appreciated if the payments could be made on time (T8);

(f) In January 1994 the applicant’s bank, at the request of the applicant, passed on to the applicant’s brother (who was then living in Thailand) a letter written by the applicant’s wife. That letter referred to the applicant’s injuries suffered in October 1992 and said that the applicant “has since been receiving workers’ compensation. The fact that he owns the company and is also an employee of the company means the insurance company will pay to the [company] account. Your brother then receives payment from [the company] (T9, pages 19 and 20);

(g) In April 1994 an accountant consulted by the applicant regarding the company’s affairs recorded (T page 40) that the applicant had a contract with the company for $65,000 per year but that tax had not been deducted from payments to the company. It was noted that the only activities of the company since October 1992 had been the receipt of weekly workers’ compensation payments of $1250 and the collection of trade debts.

(h) The company’s records showed wages paid to the applicant by the company in the 1993 year totalled $37,041.95 (T page 182). This amount includes wages paid from July 1992 until October 1992 (ie prior to the workers’ compensation commencement). In the 1994 year the amount paid from July 1993 until May 1994 totalled $31,560 (T page 183).

  1. Against the above evidence the applicant’s oral evidence was that he left most of the administration of the company’s affairs to his wife because at the time he was in a wheelchair and not mobile. He agreed that he may have collected a few compensation cheques from the insurance company but claimed that he had not received compensation payments from the company because the company was in serious financial difficulties and he wanted the company to use the compensation money to pay creditors rather than pay it to him. He said that at a late stage the company’s bank account was overdrawn and the bank would not honour cheques drawn by the company. Accordingly, the cheques from the insurance company had been deposited directly into his personal bank account and he had used the money to make payments to creditors on behalf of the company. He had relied on advice from his wife that that was an appropriate thing to do.
  2. It is apparent that the applicant was, in 1993 and 1994, experiencing some health problems. The letter from his bank to his brother of January 1994 referred to above mentions that the applicant had been admitted to hospital at that time “and appears to be experiencing problems”. The letter of July 1993 from his solicitors to the insurance company refers to the applicant having recently separated from his wife. The applicant’s evidence overall was given in a way that was at times confused. There was no direct evidence given of his medical state in 1993 and 1994. In my opinion the evidence in the case points to the applicant having received compensation payments from the company from October 1992 until May 1994.
  3. Periodic payments of workers’ compensation are intended to replace lost income, are of an income nature, and therefore assessable income: Federal Commissioner of Taxation vs Inkster (1989) 20ATR1516. It follows that, to the extent that the applicant received the compensation payments, it is assessable income – even if the applicant chose to use the money to make payments from what were then his own funds to creditors of the company. The applicant was entitled to receive his weekly salary from the company and receipt by the company of the compensation payments from the insurance company put the company in funds to discharge its obligation to him. Even if, as the applicant says, he chose not to receive his salary from the company because he wanted the company to use the money to discharge creditors, then the money would have been dealt with on his behalf or as he directed for the purposes of s 19 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (ITAA 1936) and therefore derived by him at that time. In my opinion, however, and I so find, the applicant did receive from the company on a regular basis the workers’ compensation payments that in turn had been received from the insurance company. He may have chosen to make some payments himself to the company creditors, but that does not in any way alter the character of the income receipts in his hands.
  4. By virtue of s 14ZZK(b) of the Administration Act the applicant has the burden of proving on the balance of probabilities that the assessment is excessive. I find that the applicant has not discharged that onus in relation to the receipt of compensation payments in 1993 and 1994.

Interest income and expense and the write off of a bad debt

  1. This issue concerns the respondent’s treatment of substantial amounts of interest income and expense claimed by the applicant in the 1994 to 1996 years and the deductibility of the write off of a bad debt in 1996.
  2. It will be recalled from [6] above that in his personal tax return for the 1993 year the applicant claimed as income only the amount of $5,400. He did not declare interest income or claim interest expense as a deduction. However, in the returns for the following three years the applicant claimed substantial amounts of interest income and expenditure and the write off of a bad debt in 1996. The basis of these claims was that the applicant had borrowed a substantial amount of money from his father (who lived overseas but who visited Australia in 1992) and was obliged to pay interest thereon and that, in turn, the applicant loaned that money to the company at a higher rate of interest – but which in the event was not paid. When the company was wound up in 1996 the debt from the company to the applicant was irrecoverable and hence was claimed as a bad debt on the basis that the applicant was in the business of money lending.
  3. In April 1994 the applicant consulted a firm of accountants for the purpose of having his personal income tax return for the 1993 year and the company’s financial statements and tax returns for that year prepared. I have referred at [21(g)] above to the note made by the accountant in relation to the applicant’s income from the company and the fact that no tax had been deducted. At page 41 of the T documents are other notes made by the accountant concerning the company, part of which is as follows:
“Additional transactions not included in cash book


(a) loan of $250,000 – cash from father


(b) loan of 35 ounces of gold – from father cashed for $19,425


(c) loan of US$52,000 – from father for payment of consulting fee to Saudi Arabia”


  1. On the basis of the information provided, the accountant prepared journal entries for the company recording as a loan from the father to the company the sum of $340,211 described as “expenses paid by father”. The accountant also prepared journal entries in relation to the applicant’s loan account with the company, the most significant of which were to transfer amounts shown in the applicant’s drawings account with the company to the loan account, the net effect of which was to record, as at 30 June 1993, that the applicant owed the sum of $85,623 to the company.
  2. The audit report prepared by ATO officers (T27) records that the reason for the audit was that, when lodging his returns for the 1994 year to the 1996 year during 1996, the applicant had “demanded that he be classified as a money lender and claimed that he had lent US$250,000 to [the company] at 13% interest in 1992, which was money he had borrowed from his father, with his uncle as guarantor, at 10% interest.” The report refers to the fact that the applicant had been audited on an earlier occasion in relation to an earlier year of income and had provided to the earlier auditor a letter (in the Arabic language) “... purportedly from his father, advising that he had sent him US$250,000 between 1987 and 1991 and asking for some return of this money. This letter was provided to evidence that he had enough money to live on during his time in Australia (in 1987) despite a non-taxable income in each year and an unsupported loss on a retail clothing business ... in the year ended June 1990.”
  3. The auditors concluded that the applicant was not in the business of money lending but were initially prepared to give him the benefit of the doubt and to include in his assessable income some interest income on the claimed loan for the company and to allow some deduction of interest expense. The auditors estimated the amounts that might be involved based on imperfect financial records of the company. However, the auditors subsequently relied on new information said to have been given to them by the applicant in a visit the applicant made to the ATO on 15 October 1998 in which he is said to have advised the auditors that his father did not require him to repay the loan or to pay interest on it. The auditors concluded that this new information indicated that the loan must have been made direct to the company by the father, the applicant was not entitled to interest from the company nor required to pay interest to his father, and any bad debt would not be deductible by the applicant. ( T documents page 197 and 198).
  4. The applicant gave oral evidence that his father had given him money in the late 1980’s as a gift when he first married in Australia. He had no obligation to repay that money. However, his father had visited Australia for four or five weeks in approximately May 1992 and had given him the amount of A$310,000 in cash. The money had not been banked. He and his wife had stored it at their house and used it to buy fruit from growers in cash transactions over the next three or four months. The nature of the industry was that fruit had to be paid for in cash when the fruit was collected. Transactions could vary in value from as low as $3-4,000 or to as high as $30-40,000.
  5. The applicant said that he was definitely under an obligation to pay interest to his father and the money that he used to pay for fruit on behalf of the company should have been recorded as a loan by him to the company. The accountant must have made a mistake when accounting for it in the manner described above.
  6. There are a number of other documents that bear upon the issue. Exhibit A1 is a statutory declaration made by the applicant’s father, Mr Abusalim in July 2001, which states that “I came to Australia on several occasions in 1990 (Vic) 1992 (WA) 1996 (WA) and 1998 (WA). I lent US$250,000 to my son (the applicant) at 10% pa interest payable annually. The loan of US$250,000 is not a gift.”
  7. The applicant tendered another document in the Arabic language with what he said was a translation into the English language, although the identity and credentials of the translator were not explained (Exhibit A11). The letter purports to be to the applicant from his uncle. The date of the letter is not revealed. The relevant part of the English language version of the letter is as follows:

“The reason behind writing this letter and sending it to you is the sum of ..... two hundred and fifty thousand dollars which I sent you in December 1992 and the interest charged is 10%. So far you did not repay the loan nor the interest charged. I hope that you will repay the loan and the interest as soon as possible since we are in desperate need for the money. As you are aware, this amount is not entirely mine but it’s from your father and sisters and now your sisters and father are demanding the money of me and in turn I am requesting the money from you. To your knowledge, we have no connections with the companies that you invested the money in. We gave you this money and you have to repay us the full amount as soon as possible in order for us to remain relatives and for us to continue to love each other and to avoid the need for mediators or to resort to legal confrontations. We gave you the money in good faith and without any conditions. Also, the money was transferred to you some through the banks and the rest came with your father when he came to visit you. ... as you are aware since 1987 until 1992 we have been sending you monies and we never request you to pay any of it back. However, I hope you will repay the loan of $250,000 that we gave you in December 1992.”

  1. The applicant also tendered Exhibit A4, which is a document in the Arabic language and what purported to be a translation of it. The document purports to be signed by the applicant’s father and records that the father “... authorised Mr Amjed Rashed Daass to settle and compromise the whole debt account between me and my son Jamile Abuothman only in Australia, where the debt amounting to Two Hundred and Fifty Thousand US Dollars (US$250,000) plus all interest at the rate of 10% per annum from 18 December 1992 which is payable on demand.” This agreement is completed between me and my son, Jamile and if he does not respond to the demanded payment, then I give Mr Amjed my full authority to represent me in all courts and Australian government departments only and by which I sign to release the debts and interest.”
  2. However, the respondent tendered into evidence Exhibit R1, which is another letter in the Arabic language that was provided by the applicant to the ATO auditors in 1996 and which was translated into English by the Department of Immigration and Multicultural Affairs. The translation reveals that it is a letter addressed to the applicant and dated 18 December 1992. The signature of the writer of the letter is not decipherable, but the letter is addressed to the applicant who is described as “our dear son”. The relevant paragraph of the letter is as follows:

“Our son Azem, we summarised what we sent you while you have been in Australia from the year 1987 until the year 1991 and the result was that we sent you ($250,000) two hundred and fifty thousand American dollars and you are saying that you opened a factory and that you have a business and we did not know about your business and did not receive from you anything from the capital that we sent to you. The funds as you know are from your father ..., your brother, sisters and uncle and we have not received from you anything and we have been sending you the monies with the travellers such as the Hajjawi and a person from Abu Dais and the Tarawenah who was travelling by air, one part was transferred to you through the Jordanian-Kuwaiti Bank and until now we do not know anything about your factory or business.”

  1. The applicant’s father gave oral evidence by telephone with the assistance of an interpreter. He said that he had visited Perth in approximately May 1992 and had brought with him an amount of A$340,000 in cash. He said that he had obtained this money by exchanging US currency in Bangkok. He said that he did not know what his son intended to do with the money but he was sure that it was a loan to his son and interest of 10% per annum was to be paid on it. He could not recall having ever previously loaned money to his son free of interest but he did confirm that he had previously visited Australia and had made gifts of money to his son. He did not remember exactly when that was or how much the amount was. He said that he could not recall having ever gone with his son to buy fruit and he did not recall ever paying money to fruit growers himself.
  2. Mr Abusalim said that Mr Daass was not a family member and he did not know exactly who he was. However, he did know that the money had been forwarded to his son through Mr Daass. In relation to Exhibit A1, Mr Abusalim said he recalled signing the declaration in 2001 at the Australian Embassy in Israel but he did not know what the document said because he does not read English and nobody translated the document for him before he signed it.
  3. Exhibit R4 are notes made by an ATO officer, Mr Beattie, who assisted the applicant complete his income tax returns in July 1996. Mr Beattie records that the applicant informed him that he had received a loan of US$250,000 from a person named Jucube at an interest rate of 10%. He informed the officer that there was a contract recording the loan and similarly there was a contract recording the terms of a loan that the applicant had made to the company.
  4. Finally, the ATO auditors recorded (T documents p 195) that the applicant had given them “a note” (which was given a reference of C3.14-15) which they described as undated but bearing the seal of the company and signed by the applicant and his wife. A copy of this document was not adduced into evidence by either party, but I note that a copy of it formed part of a bundle of documents filed at the Tribunal on 14 May 2002 by the applicant with a statement of the issues in dispute in the proceedings. The document is hand written and records that the company borrowed from the applicant the amounts of $8,000 in September 1992, $2,404.47 in October 1992 and $9,443.70 in January 1993. The document was obviously prepared some time after January 1993 but there is no other information to allow a more accurate assessment of when it was prepared.
  5. In the light of all the above evidence I am left in very considerable uncertainty as to precisely what transactions (if any) occurred involving the company and either the applicant or his father. There are no banking records that assist in identifying when the applicant’s father gave money to the applicant or to the company. It seems apparent that money was given to the applicant by his father in the late 1980’s when he was living in Melbourne but it is by no means clear whether additional money was given during 1992 – either as a gift or as a loan. I have no reason to doubt that the applicant instructed the accountant in April 1994 that monies had been received from his father. However, it is significant, in my opinion, that neither the applicant nor his father made any reference to the 19 ounces of gold that were said to have been given to the applicant and converted into cash. Similarly, there is no explanation offered as to why the document referred to in [41] above referred to three loans made by the applicant to the company in 1992 and 1993 but made no reference whatsoever to a much larger amount that the applicant said he had loaned to the company during 1992. Equally significant is that in his return for the 1993 year the applicant made no reference to interest income or expenditure, even though, on his evidence, the loan had been made to him by his father and on-lent to the company by him as early as May 1992 or, at the latest, December 1992.
  6. I note also that the ATO auditors attempted to reconstruct the records of purchases and sales of fruit by the company in the relevant period in an attempt to estimate the amounts that the company would have needed to obtain from some source and could only arrive at a figure of approximately $115,000.
  7. Taking into account the evidence set out above, and bearing in mind that the creation of the loan from the applicant’s father to the company occurred by way of a journal entry after the close of the 1993 year and that there are no other documentary records of the transactions, I find that the amount of $250,000 or $340,000 was not loaned to the company by either the applicant or his father. Once again, the onus is on the applicant to demonstrate on the balance of probabilities that the assessment made by the respondent is excessive. In relation to the manner in which the respondent dealt with the interest income and expenses and the write off of the resulting bad debt, in my opinion the applicant has failed to demonstrate error by the respondent or that the assessment issued is relation to those amounts was excessive. I have reached that conclusion without considering whether the applicant was in the business of moneylending because it is unnecessary to consider that issue – because of my finding that no loans were made. Apart from his evidence regarding loans to the company, which I do not accept, the applicant adduced no evidence to demonstrate that he was in that business and in that regard also failed to discharge the onus on him.

Penalities and interest

  1. For the 1993 and 1994 returns the respondent imposed late lodgement penalties of $137.46 and $1,710.23 respectively. The amounts were calculated at the rate of 10% of the tax properly payable for the relevant number of weeks by which the returns were late.
  2. The Tribunal’s jurisdiction to review objection decisions under s 14ZZ of the Administration Act is limited to a “reviewable objection decision” – a term that is relevantly defined in s 14ZQ to mean an objection decision that is not an “ineligible income tax remission decision”. That latter phrase is relevantly defined by s 14ZS(2) to include amounts payable that do not exceed a rate of 20% per year of the tax properly payable in respect of the year of income.
  3. As the rate of penalty imposed in 1993 and 1994 for late lodgement was calculated at a rate of 10% per annum the decisions are an ineligible income tax remission decision and not reviewable by this Tribunal.
  4. For the 1993 year a tax shortfall interest amount of $1,142.89 was imposed pursuant to s 170A of the ITAA 1936. Section 170A(13) (as it applied prior to 1999 amended assessments) relevantly provided that notification of an amount of interest payable is deemed not to be an assessment and hence an objection cannot be lodged to challenge the imposition of it, even if that notification was made by way of a notice of amended assessment generally. Accordingly, the Tribunal has no jurisdiction to review the imposition of the interest charged.
  5. The respondent acknowledged that the applicant was entitled to have the tax shortfall penalties that were imposed on him reviewed by the Tribunal. In 1993 that penalty was $1,191.34 and related to the applicant’s failure to return as income the compensation payments referred to above. The penalty was imposed at the rate of 50% pursuant to s 226H of ITAA 1936 on the basis that the shortfall was caused by the applicant’s recklessness with regard to the correct operation of that Act.
  6. The auditor considered that the applicant’s actions in not returning this income for the 1993 year were deliberate because the applicant had received salary and wages in previous years and had included them in his taxation return; he was a director of the company, which paid wages to its other employees and prepared group certificates for them – even though it appears his wife may have prepared them; he was receiving weekly compensation payments by way of salary replacement at the time of the preparation of the 1993 return; and he had been running two businesses prior to the preparation of his return for that year. However, the auditor took into account that the applicant may not have had a good command of the English language and taxation laws regarding compensation payments in 1994 and was prepared to give the applicant the benefit of the doubt and regard the omission of salary as due to recklessness rather than deliberate – in which case the penalty would have been 75% rather than 50%.
  7. It is appropriate at this point to refer to the evidence that is available concerning the applicant’s mental state in the period 1994 to 1996. As noted at [23] above, in January 1994 the applicant’s bank manager had advised his brother that the applicant had been admitted to hospital and was experiencing personal problems. Document T8 had recorded in July 1993 that the applicant had separated from his wife but by January 1994 his wife wrote to the applicant’s brother regarding a dispute that had arisen between the applicant and the insurance company and refers to the risk of the applicant’s house being sold unless money is received from the brother. In his oral evidence the applicant said that he was taking a number of drugs by way of medication and was not able to make rational decisions about his affairs at that time.
  8. Dr Kay is a psychiatrist and gave evidence that he first saw the applicant in July 1997 regarding his psychiatric state in connection with a compensation claim at that time. In 1997 he considered that the applicant’s condition, which he described as a eccentric personality disorder, had been long-standing but he could not express an opinion about what the applicant’s state might have been in the period from 1994 to 1996. In 1997 the applicant was taking three drugs by way of medication but he did not know how long the applicant had been taking them. He said that it was possible that the medication the applicant was taking had unpredictable consequences for him. The drugs would have had some effect on his alertness and his abilities generally, but Dr Kay thought that they would not have affected his abilities on a day-to-day basis. Dr Kay also said that in 1997 he thought that the applicant was competent in the English language and that any difficulties in communication at that time were caused by his personality problems.
  9. On the evidence before me I am unable to reach any firm conclusions about the applicant’s state of psychiatric health in 1994 when he completed his 1993 return. On what evidence is available from a later time it would seem that the applicant’s medication might have had some affect on him, but it is apparent from the instructions that the applicant gave to the accountant concerning the company in April 1994 that he was aware that the company had been receiving workers’ compensation payments from the insurance company and, in my opinion, those amounts were being passed on to the applicant on a weekly or fortnightly basis. It follows, in my opinion, that the applicant was aware of the income nature of the payments. He has already received the benefit of a reduction in the penalty from 75% to 50% and in my opinion there is no basis upon which any further remission of the penalty is appropriate.
  10. For the 1994 year and the 1996 year tax shortfall penalties were imposed of $3,370.67 and $5,798.25 respectively. For the 1994 year the penalties were imposed in respect of the failure to declare the compensation amounts received at the rate of 50% under s 226H for the same reasons as were considered in relation to the 1993 year. Penalties at the rate of 25% pursuant to s 226G of the ITAA 1996 were also imposed in respect of an amount of social security payments received and also in respect of the net adjustment that resulted from the excision of the interest income that the applicant had returned but to disallow the interest expense and bad debt deduction that the applicant had claimed.
  11. As the 1994, 1995 and 1996 returns were all lodged at the same time in 1996 it is relevant to consider the circumstances in which the applicant lodged those returns.
  12. The respondent concedes that the returns were in fact prepared by an ATO officer who agreed to prepare the returns in order to assist the applicant. Exhibit R4 contains a detailed note prepared at the time by the ATO officer concerned (Mr Beattie) about the history of his dealings with the applicant. The contents of R4 were not in substance disputed or contested by the applicant. According to R4 the applicant attended at the ATO office on 29 July 1996 in what he said was his lunchtime from work and sought assistance in the preparation of the tax returns - because he had received from the ATO a final notice requiring the filing of outstanding returns for 1994 and 1995 (Exhibit R3). The applicant told Mr Beattie that he had consulted a tax agent but did not wish to pay the agent the $1,200 that the agent apparently wished to charge him for the completion of the returns. The applicant did not disclose that he had received periodic compensation payments or social security payments in the 1994 year. He said he had not worked in 1994 or 1995, but did disclose the lump sum compensation payment he had received in 1994. Mr Beattie considered this to be a capital amount and not taxable income.
  13. The applicant sought advice about whether he could claim as deductions against income various expenditure on medical treatment and other personal items. He also told Mr Beattie that he had borrowed US$250,000 from a man named Jacube and had on-lent it to the company, which had subsequently been liquidated in June 1996 without paying the interest that was due to the applicant or repaying any of the principal.
  14. Mr Beattie consulted a more senior officer about how to deal with the interest income and expenditure and write off of the bad debt and it was at that point that it was identified that if the applicant was in the business of lending money and was accounting for income and deductions on an accruals basis rather than on a cash basis, then he would be able to claim a deduction for the interest income he had not received. This advice was passed on to the applicant by Mr Beattie with a strong recommendation that the applicant apply for a private binding ruling (“PBR”) about the issue because that would enable the applicant to rely on the advice given and would avoid the risk of penalties in the future for the making of an inaccurate return. The applicant told Mr Beattie that he was not prepared to apply for a PBR because he did not consider it necessary and he was confident that he was entitled to deal with the loan arrangements in the manner described.
  15. Later in the afternoon of 29 July the applicant telephoned Mr Beattie and said that he had again consulted his tax agent (who was named Trevor) who had confirmed that the applicant would be able to claim a capital loss for the value of the loan written off and that the interest that had not been paid by the company could be claimed as a deduction. Mr Beattie had a conversation with Trevor, pointing out that this treatment depended upon the applicant being in the business of lending money and Mr Beattie and Trevor agreed that it would be difficult on the facts to argue that the applicant was in the business of lending money.
  16. The applicant returned to the ATO office the next morning (30 July) and was again told by Mr Beattie that a PBR was the preferable way to proceed, but the applicant said that he did not wish to go that way because he was sure of his position. He said he was prepared to call in the media if his returns were not prepared this way. Mr Beattie again consulted more senior officers and again the difficulty of establishing that the applicant was in the business of lending money was identified. While Mr Beattie was consulting his seniors the applicant began to create a disturbance in the reception area as a result of which Mr Beattie and another officer took the applicant to an interview room. Exhibit R4 records Mr Beattie’s observation that the applicant “... appeared very distressed. His walk was stiff and tense and his voice conveyed an aggressive and angry tone.” He complained generally about the taxation system and about discrimination from the Department of Immigration, which he said was trying to deport him. The applicant referred to court proceedings that he had been involved in regarding the attempted deportation.
  17. There was then a discussion about whether the applicant was obliged to pay the Medicare levy when, because of his immigration status, he was not entitled to any Medicare benefits. He was told that there was a possibility of him being exempted from the Medicare levy and at this point he began hitting his head in anger and the second ATO officer took him outside to try to calm him down with a cigarette. The second officer subsequently reported to Mr Beattie that the applicant was too emotional at that time to discuss the technical aspects of his tax return and that other ATO officers were trying to calm him down with a cup of coffee.
  18. Eventually there was further discussion between Mr Beattie and the applicant regarding how to deal with the loan and the applicant was again recommended to seek a PBR, which was said to take between eight and twelve weeks. The applicant said that he could not wait this long and at this stage Mr Beattie agreed that he would prepare the applicant’s tax returns for the relevant years based on his instructions that he was in the business of lending money. Mr Beattie then calculated the various amounts of interest income and expense and the affects of the write-off of the debt.
  19. On the afternoon of 31 July 1996 the applicant returned to the ATO office to sign the returns that had been prepared. He told Mr Beattie that he was not well as the events of the last few days had made him feel quite angry and frustrated – which had caused him to grind his teeth and he had developed a tooth ache. He told Mr Beattie that he had been to the dentist that day and had had root canal treatment with an anaesthetic. Mr Beattie recorded in R4 that he thought that an anaesthetic might have affected the applicant’s judgement but he noted that the applicant walked in unassisted and appeared to be fully aware of his environment, “if perhaps less animated than the previous two days”. Mr Beattie told the applicant that he would go through the tax returns with him to make sure that they had been completed to his satisfaction. The applicant said
“... that he would not really know and that he trusted me to do it for him. I persisted and showed him the amendment requests and the tax returns. I read out each step in the tax return and explained them to [the applicant]. When I had finished he signed each one. I reminded him of the implications of the declaration on the back of the return and drew his attention to the wording of the declaration. I advised him that he needed to be able to have the relevant documents to substantiate the claims in the returns because, as he knew from his earlier experience, in the event of an audit our auditors could ask him to provide these documents. I advised him that if he did not he would face severe penalties. He told me that he knew that and that he had contract documents for the loan with [the company] and Jacube in a safe deposit box. I said that in particular he needed to be able to demonstrate that he was in the business of money lending as this was the point which many of his claims relied on. He said that he could and told me that he had spoken to a judge who had told him that being in business was a question of fact. He appeared confident in that assertion. He thanked me for my trouble and said that he had only done what he thought was right. He did not claim for anything that was excessive or extraordinary.”


  1. The evidence outlined above suggests to me that the applicant, although angry and perhaps upset about what he perceived to be the inequities of the taxation and immigration systems, was aware of what he was doing and the significance of it. Although it was ATO officers who identified the need for the applicant to be in the business of lending money in order to deal with the loan in a particular way, I am satisfied that the applicant grasped the significance of how the loan was to be dealt with and consulted a tax agent before and after first seeing Mr Beattie. I am satisfied that the applicant was aware that he could apply for a PBR that would remove any uncertainty about whether or not he could deal with the interest income and expense and the debt in the way that he wished.
  2. In the end, it was the applicant’s decision to proceed in the way that was eventually followed. Whether, in the circumstances, it was wise for the ATO officers to complete the applicant’s tax returns, I can well understand their reasons for being prepared to assist the applicant in that way. Mr Beattie recorded that he was “swayed” to assist because of the applicant’s disadvantages due to being from a non-English-speaking background and because the ATO had a strategy of trained volunteers helping disadvantaged person complete their tax returns. I do not believe that it can be said that the ATO officers somehow lulled the applicant into a false sense of security. Rather, I am satisfied that the applicant was informed explicitly about the need to be able to substantiate the loan arrangements in future should he be required to do so and of the desirability of obtaining a PBR. Despite the health problems that the applicant may have been experiencing at the time, I am satisfied that he was aware of what he was doing and the significance of it. In the circumstances the applicant has not, in my opinion, discharged the onus on him to demonstrate that the penalties that were imposed in respect of the 1994 and 1996 years were excessive.

CONCLUSION

  1. For the reasons set out above I have concluded that the applicant has failed to demonstrate that the amended assessments that were issued to him in relation to the compensation payments, the treatment of the interest income and expenditure and write off of the bad debt, and the shortfall penalties that were imposed, were excessive. Accordingly, I affirm the objection decisions made on 12 June 2000 in respect of the years ended 30 June 1993, 1994 and 1996.
