Secure Parking Pty Ltd and Commissioner for ACT Revenue [1991] AATA 587 (25 June 1991)

Last Updated: 7 July 2008



Stamp Duty - objection to assessment and late payment penalty - whether instrument relating to sublease not one subject to assessment of stamp duty -Mena House referred to



Words and phrases - "agreement for a lease"



Australian Capital Territory Stamp Duty Act 1969, s.4

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

Re: SECURE PARKING PTY LIMITED



Applicant



And: COMMISSIONER FOR ACT REVENUE

REASONS FOR DECISION



25 June 1991 Deputy President R.K. Todd



1. The applicant in this matter applied to the Tribunal for review of a decision disallowing an objection made by the applicant to the assessment of stamp duty ("SD") on an instrument relating to the sublease of Block 1, Section 68 Canberra City, and the imposition of late lodgement penalty thereon. The SD had been assessed under the Australian Capital Territory Stamp Duty Act 1969 (Cth) ("SDA") due to the operation of s.4 and Item 6 of Schedule 1 thereof.



1. The SDA is to be read with the Australian Capital Territory Taxation (Administration) Act 1969 (Cth) ("ACTTAA") which by virtue of s.47(1) thereof requires that the instrument be stamped or lodged for assessment within 30 days of execution. Late lodgement penalty is assessed in accordance with s.70(2) of the ACTTAA.



2. The provisions of the SDA relevant to this application are as follows:

"s.4 Subject to this Act, stamp duty is imposed on the instruments included in the classes of instruments specified in Schedule 1



Schedule 1

AMOUNT OF STAMP DUTY



Column 1 Column 2 Column 3

Item No. Class of Instruments Amount of SD



6 Lease of Land situated in the

Territory ..."



3. The provisions of the ACTTAA relevant to this application are as follows:



"s.4(1) In this Act, unless the contrary intention appears -



...

'execute', in relation to an instrument, means sign the instrument

and, if the instrument is under seal, sign and seal the instrument, and 'execution' has a corresponding meaning;



'lease' includes a sub-lease and an agreement for a lease or sub-

lease, but does not include -



(a) an attornment under a mortgage or contract of sale;



(b) a right granted by a company to a shareholder of the company, by virtue of his being such a shareholder, to occupy or use land owned or held under lease by the company; or



(c) an option to renew a lease;



'lessee' means a person to whom a lease is granted or agreed to be granted.



...



s.47(1) Within thirty days after the execution of a transfer of an estate in fee simple in land or of a Crown lease for a term exceeding five years, or of an agreement for such a transfer, being a transfer or agreement on which duty is imposed, the transferee or person to whom the estate or lease is to be so transferred shall lodge the instrument of transfer or the agreement with the Commissioner of Taxation for assessment.



Penalty: Twenty dollars. s.70(2) The last preceding sub-section does not prevent the amendment of an assessment after the expiration of the period referred to in that sub-section in order to give effect to a decision on an appeal or review under Part V, or the amendment of an assessment after the expiration of that period by way of reduction on an objection under that Part or pending such an appeal or review."



4. There was initial debate at the hearing as to whether the application was governed by ACT or Commonwealth legislation and accordingly as to the Tribunal in which it should be heard. Mr P. Walker, representing the respondent, submitted a tabulation (Ex.2) of the relevant legislation that established that Commonwealth legislation continued to apply and that the application for review was properly before the Administrative Appeals Tribunal of the Commonwealth.



5. The history of the matter is as follows:

(i) An instrument relating to the sublease of Block 1, Section 68 Canberra City, transferred from Nelseen Pty Ltd to Secure Parking Pty Ltd, was lodged for SD assessment with ACT Revenue on 21 September 1988. That instrument arose as a result of negotiations between those two parties whereby Nelseen Pty Ltd, which had acquired the right by tender from the Commonwealth to construct a parking station on the Crown land would sublease the land to Secure Parking Pty Ltd which would then operate the car-park. The parties entered into a deed of agreement which annexed a form of sublease. The execution of the instrument was considered by the Revenue Office to be 29 May 1987 so that the instrument was lodged 14 months and 23 days later than the period of 30 days for lodgement stipulated for by s.47(1) of the ACTTAA. The primary duty under the agreement was calculated on the basis of the projected rentals over the term of the lease. The Tribunal was also informed that the respondent was prepared to treat the sub-lease as a counterpart to the initial agreement.



(ii) An amended assessment for SD was issued on 23 March 1989 which imposed a late lodgement penalty of 100 per cent, with further late payment penalty to be imposed if payment was not received within 14 days.

(iii) Agreement was reached between the applicant and the respondent, after examination of the taxpayer's financial details, for the payment of the primary duty and for payment of the additional duty to be deferred until a decision was made concerning the applicant's objection. That objection was lodged on 4 May 1989. The grounds of the objection were that the assessment and penalty were made on erroneous grounds, as it was contended that the instrument lodged for assessment was not an agreement for a lease.



(iv) That objection against stamp duty was disallowed by a decision dated 22 December 1989 whereby it was stated that the instrument amounted to an agreement for a lease or sublease and was therefore dutiable under the relevant legislation. Late lodgement penalty was correctly imposed as a result.



(v) Application for review of that decision was made to this Tribunal on 25 January 1990.



6. The issue central to the application for review is the liability of the applicant to pay stamp duty upon the instrument to which it was a party ("the sub-lease"). This requires determination of the question whether the sub-lease is a lease subject to duty as defined by s.4 of the SDA. Only if this question is answered in the affirmative does the second issue arise, which is whether the Commissioner properly assessed the duty payable on that instrument and, if so, properly determined an amount by way of late lodgement penalty pursuant to s.70 of the ACTTAA.



7. The representatives of the parties agreed to confine their submissions to the issues of the application of prime duty and of late lodgement penalty. This review does not attempt to deal with the issues of whether the amount of prime duty was correctly assessed, or the issue of late payment penalty.



8. In addressing the primary issue whether the instrument executed by Nelseen Pty Ltd and Secure Parking Pty Ltd could be characterised as a lease subject to stamp duty, the representatives of the parties made the following submissions.

9. Mr Harvey, for the applicant, submitted that the instrument in question was no more than a deed, and was not an agreement for a lease subject to duty. In support of this submission he relied on the principle enunciated in Mena House v Commissioner of Stamp Duties (1964) 64 S.R. (NSW), a decision of Else-Mitchell J, that an agreement for lease will not pass a legal estate in the land but will pass an equitable interest whereby the lessee can seek specific performance to ensure that full title to the lease is acquired. Whether the document takes on the character of an agreement for lease potentially subject to an order for specific performance will depend on the terms of the agreement itself.



10. The agreement in question, Mr Harvey submitted, was not one which would, on its creation, have been amenable to an order for specific performance. The instrument amounted to a mere deed rather than a binding and enforceable contract due to the absence of consideration between the parties at the time of entering into the agreement. If there was any question of enforceability, it could only be said to be enforceable on the grounds that it was reduced to writing as a deed. The reason it was reduced to writing was the existence of the condition precedent contained in clause 2 of the deed. That condition precedent stated that -



"Subject to practical completion of the improvements or the issue of the Certificate of Fitness for Occupancy and Use, whichever is the sooner, the Lessor will grant to or procure the grant to and the Lessee will take a sublease of the premises for the term and subject to the covenants terms and conditions contained in the annexed sublease and subject as herein provided."



The fact that the lease was to be governed by the terms and conditions as set out in the annexure meant, it was said, that nothing could be enforced until that time had been reached. It was finally submitted by Mr Harvey that that enforceable equitable interest is the sine qua non to the operation of the SDA. Therefore the instrument was not one which was required to be lodged for stamp duty under the ACTTAA.



11. Evidence was led from Mr B.R. Matthews, Director of the applicant company, that he did not consider the deed to be a lease subject to duty, or that an enforceable interest had been created until the lease had been executed. Such parol evidence, perhaps adduced primarily in support of the applicant's case for a reduction in late lodgement penalty, is of course clearly inadmissible for the purpose of construing the written terms of the sub-lease.



12. Mr Walker submitted that the document was one that attracted duty. This submission was based on the claim that if the document can be characterised as an agreement for a lease in accordance with s.4 of the ACTTAA then in accordance with Schedule 1 Item 6 of the SDA, it was subject to stamp duty, and was required to be lodged within a certain time in accordance with s.47 of the ACTTAA.



13. Mr Walker supported his submission that the document amounted to an agreement for a lease by reference to the following factors:



(i) The parties intended to create an agreement for a lease, evidenced by: the name of the document; the terms of the agreement referring to lessor-lessee; granting of a sub-lease; rent and tenancy; the yielding up of property; assignment; tenant's fittings; exclusive possession; re-entry; and holding-over. These were all terms associated with a lease. The fact that the agreement did not specify a definite starting date was overcome by the specification of the collateral act in clause 2. In short the agreement was a commercial arrangement evidencing a clear intention to create a lease. It had been prepared by legal representatives with care and attention.



(ii) The agreement was made binding and enforceable by the consideration of the parties. This consideration was constituted by the mutual promises of the parties on the one hand to grant a lease, and on the other to take a lease and pay rent according to its terms.



(iii) The document was one executed within the meaning of s.4 of the ACTTAA as it was in writing, signed and sealed by both companies on 29 May 1987.

(iv) In addressing the issue of the availability of specific performance to enforce the agreement, it was submitted by Mr Walker that the document would still meet the description of an agreement for a lease even if it were not amenable to an order for specific performance. This was due to the fact that if there is a contract whereby each party promises to execute a particular lease it will meet the description of an agreement for a lease. Mr Harvey's response to this point in reply was that mutual promises do not create an equitable interest enforceable by either party.



(v) Mr Walker submitted that the condition precedent in no way derogated from the character of the document as an agreement for a lease. The clause went merely to the development of certain buildings and to the issue of a certificate of occupancy. It was a condition precedent which could be regarded as being only for the benefit of the lessee and was one which, on an application for an order for specific performance, might well be waived by the lessee. Mr Harvey's response to this point in reply was that this argument presupposed the existence of an obligation, whereas, in his submission, there was no obligation until such time as the condition precedent was satisfied.



(vi) As an alternative to the above argument regarding the availability of specific performance, it was put that the document nevertheless met the description of an agreement for a lease as a contract. It was enforceable as a contract due to the mutual promises of the parties, its execution in the prescribed manner, and the annexure of the document which was to constitute the lease. As a result the agreement did not lose its character as an agreement for a lease merely because of the existence of a condition precedent. The document must either always be an agreement for lease or not. It cannot change character from one day to the next due to the satisfaction of a condition precedent. Mr Harvey's response to this point in reply was that it is irrelevant whether the document amounts to a contract if it is not within the definition laid down by the SDA. This document, in his submission, did not amount to an agreement for a lease as it could not be specifically enforced until the parties entered into possession or there was a final certificate of occupancy in existence.



14. Having regard to these submissions, the true nature of the sub-lease must be determined, first by considering the document in its entirety. This broad approach to interpretation is supported by the legislation which simply states that provided that there exists "an agreement for a lease" SD will apply to it, regardless of its details.



15. Adopting this broad approach, the sub-lease appears to me to be unquestionably an agreement for a lease. On its face it is an agreement for a lease due to the use of terms necessary for its creation, as stated in paragraph 13(i) above. On a less superficial level the document clearly evinces the intention to create a binding agreement. Both parties clearly had too much depending on their mutual promises to make it otherwise. The document was thus executed in order to state clearly the mutual promises forming the consideration of a binding and enforceable agreement.



16. The submission by the applicant's representative that an agreement for a lease will not pass a legal estate in the land but only an equitable interest is irrelevant, as the legislation imposes duty on an agreement for a lease whether or not it passes an equitable interest. Thus, whether or not the agreement may be the subject of an order for specific performance is not a relevant consideration.



17. Turning to the specific relevant provisions of the sub-lease, the critical point is the interpretation of clause 2. The applicant's representative focussed upon this point as the one thing that might derogate from the fact that the document was on its face an agreement for a lease. Mr Harvey argued that clause 2 was a prerequisite to the enforceability of the agreement. Quite apart from the issue of enforceability that has already been dealt with above, this argument cannot in my opinion stand.



18. As a matter of interpretation, the essential feature of the clause is that it imposes a time frame on the coming into operation of the sub-lease. The fact that the grant of the lease was subject to the practical completion of the improvements, or the issue of the Certificate of Fitness for Occupancy and Use, goes without saying. Both parties had a strong interest in the granting or obtaining of the sub-lease. Obviously this could not occur until the building was in a state in which the applicant could use it for its intended purpose. As this time could not be stated with any certainty, clause 2 was inserted into the agreement for the purpose of imposing that time frame. It would not appear to be any reflection of the parties' desire to make the agreement unenforceable.



19. Thus on this approach also the document should be characterised as a binding contract supported by consideration, with clause 2 defining the time at which the sublease would come into operation. It is therefore a binding agreement for a lease, within the legislative definition, and subject to SD.



20. There remains the question of late lodgement penalty. At the relevant date there was no policy established in relation to the penalty provisions of the relevant legislation. Those provisions are however in familiar form, providing for a penalty of "double the difference". Thus, a 50 per cent remission as applied here amounted to an effective 100 per cent penalty. From September 1988 a policy has been determined by the ACT Administration - see the "Revenue Ruling" in Ex.3. The evidence was that this policy has been applied retrospectively. Such a policy in broad terms is one which finds itself in an area in which a review Tribunal should tread cautiously. True it is that the AAT must, as much as the primary administrator, approach every case on its merits, but there is a real demand for fairness and equity towards all taxpayers who find themselves, or who have got themselves, into this position.



21. The contentions put for the applicant here were (i) that there had been no opportunity to contend for a lower rate of primary duty; (ii) that it was believed that a full lease would be being executed and that the documents would merge; (iii) that the applicant had suffered financial losses; (iv) that initially the assessment had been made under incorrect legislation; and (v) that there was no a priori basis for applying a 200 per cent penalty and then abating it.



22. As to (i), that is an irrelevant consideration before this Tribunal, where the whole matter is reagitated. As to (iii), such losses appear to me to be irrelevant to the present issue. As to (iv) the Tribunal was informed that the respondent would be prepared to treat the sub-lease as a counterpart to the initial agreement and that under the ACT legislation a counterpart document of a document dutiable under the old legislation is not dutiable under the new legislation, as discussed in paragraph 4 above. As to (v) the legislation in my opinion makes it clear that there is indeed a prima facie presumption of a 200 per cent penalty.



23. The main point is that referred to as (ii) in paragraph 22 above. The difficulty with it, and it was the difficulty that influenced the decision-maker, is that the applicant's solicitor was advised of the problem very clearly by the solicitors for the lessor, Nelseen Pty Ltd by letter dated 3 June 1987 in which it was, inter alia, stated: "Could you please note that stamp duty is payable in the ACT within 30 days of the date of the Agreement and not two months as in New South Wales. Substantial penalties are incurred for late payment." Evidence was given (Tr.52) to the effect of this advice having been given. The Ruling referred to above spoke of a suggested penalty of 50 per cent of the tax assessed, and it was indicated that the 100 per cent penalty imposed in this case was because there had been taken into account "the circumstances surrounding the lodgement of the document", with specific reference being made to the letter of 3 June 1988 referred to above.



24. It is not for this Tribunal merely to decide whether the decision of the respondent to impose the 100 per cent penalty was reasonable. But looking at the matter on the basis of the evidence as a whole, and in particular in the light of the very clear advice given in the letter of 3 June 1988, referred to in paragraph 23 above, I find myself unable to come to any other conclusion than that the decision to remit the penalty to the extent of 50 per cent should be affirmed.



25. In the result the Tribunal must in my opinion, notwithstanding that it cannot help but entertain some sympathy for the applicant because of what turned out to be the economic non-viability of the whole project, for which the applicant blames government, affirm the decision under review.
