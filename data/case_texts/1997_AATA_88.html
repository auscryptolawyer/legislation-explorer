DIRECTIONS AND REASONS FOR DIRECTIONS NO 11710

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) N0 NT96/164

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re CONFIDENTIAL      

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



DIRECTIONS

Tribunal Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)

Date 19 March 1997

Place Sydney

Decision The Tribunal directs —

1 It has jurisdiction to hear and determine the subject application

2 The evidence to be tendered on the hearing is not limited to evidence of matters considered by the decision-maker.



(Sgd) BJ McMahon

..............................................

Deputy President



CATCHWORDS

PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE - jurisdiction - applicant sought private ruling for year ended 30/6/95 - document issued by respondent purporting to be private ruling - whether Tribunal has jurisdiction to review the objection decision - whether Tribunal’s power of review extends beyond facts before the Commissioner



Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 s 43

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ss 160L(1), 160L(2)

Taxation Administration Act 1953 ss 14ZAAA, 14ZAF, 14ZAH, 14ZAI, 14ZAJ, 14ZAM, 14ZAN(g), 14ZAR(1), 14ZAS, 14ZAZA(1), 14ZZ, 14ZZJ, 14ZZK, 15AB

Taxation Laws Amendment (Self Assessment) Act 1992 ss 4, 13



Comptroller-General of Customs v Akai Pty Limited & Others 20 AAR 10

     

REASONS FOR DIRECTIONS



19 March 1997 Deputy President BJ McMahon



  1. The substantive application before the Tribunal is for review of a decision of the respondent to disallow an objection dated 11 July 1995 against a notice of private ruling issued on 19 April 1995 in respect of the year ended 30 June 1995. Prior to the hearing, the respondent sought directions as to jurisdiction and, in the event of jurisdiction being found, as to the way in which the application was to be conducted.
  2. It is necessary to give some history in order to understand the issues. The following facts relating to the circumstances leading up to the application for the private ruling are taken from the respondent’s statement of findings on material questions of fact given pursuant to s 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the AAT Act”). Because I adopt these findings, it is not to be thought that they are this Tribunal’s findings. If the matter proceeds to a hearing, then of course findings of fact can be brought in only after a consideration of all the material to be put before the Tribunal. I re-state these alleged facts only in order to explain the issues.
  3. Between 31 August and 20 September 1984, the applicant entered into an agreement with his mother and his then wife to purchase a home unit from his uncle. The applicant says that he acquired not less than an 87/170th interest in the unit at this time, while his then wife acquired a 73/170th, and his mother a 10/170th. It is then contended that his then wife entered into an oral agreement with the applicant under which she would assign her interest in the purchase agreement to him in consideration of his repaying her share of the deposit and assuming full responsibility for the monthly instalments. This agreement was placed about September 1984. In September 1986 the applicant contends that he acquired, or otherwise regained control over the interest of his former wife following a divorce settlement. Title was actually transferred to the applicant on 27 January 1987.
  4. On 25 June 1987 he gave a mortgage over the unit in favour of his uncle to secure repayment of the outstanding balance of the purchase price. The debt was subsequently forgiven under the uncle’s will and the mortgage was discharged. This discharge was registered on 1 May 1990.
  5. Between 1979 and 1990 the applicant was employed on a series of individual contracts outside Australia. The property was used as his principal residence in Australia during his visits. There is an issue as to whether there should be an apportionment of any capital gains tax (CGT) found to be payable having regard to this contention.
  6. Because of changes in his circumstances, the applicant subsequently sold the unit. Contracts for sale were exchanged in August 1994. On 15 December 1994 an application was made for a private ruling.
  7. It was in the prescribed form and was accompanied by a good deal of information, prepared and presented by the applicant’s tax agent. Essentially, the applicant sought a ruling whether there was a liability to capital gains tax under s 160L(2) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 or whether that section did not apply because the subject property was acquired prior to 20 September 1985. A document headed “Full description of the facts” accompanied the application. Notwithstanding this, the Commissioner considered that further information was required. This was supplied on 31 January 1995 and again on 2 February 1995. In response to yet another request for further information, a third letter was sent on 3 March 1995 and a fourth on 14 March 1995.
  8. On 19 April 1995 the respondent issued a document which he described as a private ruling for the purposes of Part IVAA of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (the Administration Act) and sent it with a formal notice and covering letter to the applicant’s tax agent, together with a notice of the applicant’s rights in respect of that ruling.
  9. On 11 July 1995, the applicant objected against the ruling. This objection was disallowed and notice of that fact was sent on 29 February 1996. This was followed by the present application to this Tribunal.
  10. Prior to the hearing, the respondent raised, for the first time, a number of fundamental matters best dealt with prior to the substantive hearing.
  11. The first matter raised was that the document was not a ruling because it referred to an “arrangement” that began to be carried out prior to 1 July 1992 and therefore was not susceptible to a ruling under Part IVAA. Accordingly, it was said that the document issued by the Commissioner was a nullity and that there was no basis of jurisdiction upon which this Tribunal could proceed.
  12. The second matter raised by the Commissioner was that the ruling was invalid because the question posed by the tax agent to the Commissioner was neither answerable nor answered. This fact, it was submitted, went to the validity of the ruling and provided no basis upon which this Tribunal could embark upon a review.
  13. The third matter raised by the Commissioner was that if, contrary to the first two submissions, it was found that the Tribunal had jurisdiction, then upon the substantive hearing the Tribunal was confined to a consideration only of the factual material that had been put to the Commissioner. The function of the Tribunal was (it was submitted) merely to consider whether on the basis of the material before the decision-maker, the Tribunal would have come to the same decision.
  14. Part IVAA of the Administration Act was enacted by s 4 of the Taxation Laws Amendment (Self Assessment) Act 1992 (Act Number 101 of 1992). Section 13 of this Act specifically provides that a private ruling can not be made in relation to an arrangement that was begun to be carried out before 1 July 1992.
  15. Section 14ZAAA of the Administration Act defines “arrangement” to include —
    • (a) scheme, plan, action, proposal, course of action, course of conduct, transaction, agreement, understanding, promise or undertaking; or
    • (b) part of an arrangement.”

Arrangement is thus defined in very wide terms.

  1. In order to determine whether the applicant is entitled to obtain a private ruling, it is necessary to identify the arrangement to which the ruling relates and to ascertain if that arrangement is one that began to be carried out on or after 1 July 1992. The disposal of an asset, being a “transaction” or an “agreement”, is an arrangement in its own right for which there may be CGT consequences. Alternatively, the disposal may be part of an arrangement if, for example, the disposal formed part of a wider transaction. An arrangement in terms of s 14ZAAA may include an acquisition of an asset before 1 July 1992. It was submitted by the respondent that on the disposal or proposed disposal of any asset acquired before that date, a person did not have the right to apply under ss 14ZAF or 14ZAG for a private ruling about the application of a tax law or tax laws to the arrangement.
  2. Whether this submission is correct in the present case depends upon what is ultimately found to be the “arrangement”. If the subject of s 160L(1) is not merely the disposal of the asset in question but the acquisition ownership and disposal of the asset, then there may be some substance in the respondent’s submission. On the other hand, if the relevant part of the definition is a “part of an arrangement” and deals only with the disposal of the asset, the liability trigger, then the mere acquisition or ownership of the unit would have no implication for the way in which a tax law would apply to the applicant in the relevant year for the purpose of obtaining a private ruling.
  3. In my view it is not possible to determine this question on an interlocutory application. To decide what is the relevant arrangement will require evidence and findings of fact. For reasons to which I will later refer, I consider that the findings of fact may extend beyond factual matters put to the Commissioner in the request for a private ruling. To accept the Commissioner’s findings at this stage and to accept the conclusions drawn from those supposed facts as to the existence of a relevant arrangement would, in my view, preclude the applicant from the administrative review to which he is entitled.
  4. In any event, I would observe that the Commissioner’s argument would, if successful, indicate a significant anomaly in the working of the private ruling regime which was introduced on 30 June 1992. That system was brought in to underpin the “self assessment” system which commenced on 1 July 1992. The purpose and object of the self assessment scheme would be hindered if the meaning of arrangement were extended back in the manner sought to be supported by the Commissioner. Whether this will emerge after the substantive hearing, will depend upon the Tribunal’s decision as to the constituents of the arrangement in this particular case.
  5. The second matter raised by the applicant concerns the validity of the ruling. The questions originally posed sought confirmation as to the date of acquisition of the property, or alternatively, that the applicant was the beneficial owner of the property on and from 31 August 1984. During the later correspondence, the applicant’s taxation agent withdrew the original questions and substituted the following —
“The question now addressed to the Commissioner is to confirm that since all beneficial interests were acquired prior to 20 September 1985 no capital gains tax liabilities arise on the sale of the property in 1994.“


  1. The substituted question proceeded on the assumption that all beneficial interests had been acquired and then asked a question based upon that assumption. It was submitted on behalf of the Commissioner that he should not have been required to treat the question as a valid request for private ruling by reason of the operation of s 14ZAN(g) of the Administration Act.
  2. The fact is, however, that he complied with the application and issued a formal private ruling. In my view, it is now too late for the Commissioner to say that he should not have done what he did, having regard to the statutory provisions and to the reasons for them.
  3. Section 15AB of the Administration Act provides that the production of a notice of a private ruling, or a document signed by certain nominated officers purporting to be a copy of such notice is conclusive evidence of the proper making of the ruling or withdrawal. Such a notice was produced and tendered in evidence in these interlocutory proceedings. In my view, the section means what it says, and for good reasons. There must be certainty in the administration of the self assessment system. Applicants are given the opportunity of obtaining a ruling so that they may proceed to order their affairs and to discharge their taxation liabilities in accordance with that ruling. It might be said to be part of a “fast track” mechanism. If an applicant were obliged to consider the validity of each notice of ruling as it was issued, the system could hardly survive. Once the Commissioner has issued a formal ruling and given notice of it, then assuming that the information upon which it is stated to have been based is correct, a number of legal consequences flow. These legal consequences are all postulated on the basis that a ruling purporting to be a valid ruling, is a valid ruling. There can be no basis for a submission which says, in effect, that s 15AB validates rulings unless they are invalid rulings. The observations of the various judges in Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Richard Walter Pty Ltd 95 ATC 4067 dealt with assessments, which immediately raise liabilities, and which therefore are quite different in nature from rulings.
  4. The taxpayer gained no advantage from the purported invalidity of the ruling or from the inappropriateness of the questions asked. The situation was restated in accordance with the Commissioner’s understanding and an unfavourable ruling was issued. That is why the applicant seeks to have the ruling reviewed in this Tribunal. If he does not do so, he is left in some doubt as to whether the matters contained in the private ruling will have the force of law or not. He is entitled to certainty in the matter of his taxation obligations.
  5. I consider therefore that the Tribunal has jurisdiction to proceed to review the objection decision. The question then to be decided is how the review proceedings are to be conducted.
  6. Under s 14ZAF a person may apply to the Commissioner for a ruling on the way in which, in the Commissioner’s opinion, a tax law would apply to the person in respect of the year of income in relation to an arrangement. The year of income may be a past, present or future year of income (s 14ZAH) and the arrangement may be a past, present or proposed arrangement (s 14ZAI). An application for a private ruling must give such information relating to the ruling as is required by the Commissioner (s 14ZAJ). If the Commissioner considers that a private ruling can not be made without further information, it is mandatory for the Commissioner to request the applicant to give that information (s 14ZAM). A private ruling is made by the Commissioner preparing a written notice of it (s 14ZAR(1)) which must set out the matter ruled on and identify the person, tax law, year of income and arrangement to which the ruling relates (s 14ZAS).
  7. The scheme of the Act therefore anticipates that a person will provide information and that the Commissioner will make a ruling based on that information, identifying the specific matters required by the statute. Only if the information provided is insufficient must the Commissioner require the applicant to provide further information. Accordingly the Commissioner does not engage in any independent evidence gathering activity either from sources other than the applicant, or based upon information that comes to hand other than via the applicant. That at least is the position according to the statute. In the present circumstances it would seem (subject to any final findings of fact) that the Commissioner (no doubt in an endeavour to be helpful) undertook his own enquiries by, for example, arranging for title searches and copies of relevant public documents to be obtained.
  8. Where a taxpayer is dissatisfied with the private ruling, there is a right of objection (s 14ZAZA(1)). The applicant has the burden of proving in the Tribunal that “the taxation decision concerned should not have been made or should have been made differently” (s14ZZK(b)(iii)). Section 43 of the AAT Act confers upon the Tribunal all the powers and discretions of the Commissioner on an administrative review. Nevertheless it was submitted on behalf of the respondent that although the Tribunal is entitled to reconsider this decision for itself, and is empowered to exercise any discretion and may substitute its opinion for that of the Commissioner, it does so within the confines of the limitations imposed by Part IVAA. It was submitted that the Tribunal’s review function was to consider the information placed before the Commissioner and that the Tribunal was limited to those facts which were before the Commissioner in deciding the correctness or otherwise of the ruling. Whilst the respondent acknowledged the authoritative restatement of the Tribunal’s powers of review in Fletcher and Others v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 88 ATC 4834 at 4846, it was submitted that this general power of review was limited by the peculiar provisions of Part IVAA. As the Commissioner had no power to make a decision outside the facts placed before him so also, it was said, the Tribunal could not assume additional powers in re-exercising the Commissioner’s original powers .
  9. It was submitted that the role of the Tribunal was analogous to the role of courts in judicial review in the manner discussed in Payne v Federal Commission of Taxation 94 ATC 4191 and CTC Resources NL v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 94 ATC 4072. Cases cited by counsel for the respondent relating to true case stated reviews, do not seem to me to be of great assistance in resolving the present issue in view of the particular statutory provisions. Thus, Commissioner of Stamps (South Australia) v Telegraph Investment Co Pty Limited & Another [1995] HCA 44; 184 CLR 453, a decision of the High Court on appeal from the Supreme Court of South Australia dealing with the question whether an appeal is exclusively by way of case stated and dealing with the nature of a re-hearing, does nothing to illuminate the procedure to be adopted by this Tribunal when quite different legislation is under consideration.
  10. A right of objection against a ruling decision is conferred “in the manner set out in Part IVC and such a ruling is a taxation decision for the purposes of that Part” (s14ZAZA(1)). By s 14ZZ, a person dissatisfied with the Commissioner’s objection decision may, if the decision is both “a reviewable objection decision” and an “appealable decision”, either apply to this Tribunal for a review of the decision or appeal to the Federal Court against the decision. To my mind the choice is given for important reasons. The distinction between administrative and judicial review is now well understood. It would be pointless to give to an administrative reviewer the same power as a judicial reviewer even if the former could substitute its own decision. The statute recognises this distinction.
  11. Had it been intended that the power of review be reduced to a level below the general power of review normally exercised by this Tribunal, then an opportunity to spell that out was available when Part IVAA was introduced. There was already in place a framework of review powers in the Administration Act which had been transferred from the Income Tax Assessment Act only some four months earlier. Section 14ZZJ had put in place modifications to s 43 of the AAT Act in many respects. Had it been intended to reduce the general power of review of reviewable decisions relating to private rulings, then a further amendment to s 14ZZJ could easily have secured Parliament’s purpose.
  12. I do not see that the use of the words “should not have been made” or “should have been made differently” in any way import a different function from the traditional administrative approach to resolving the whole issue. To restrict the Tribunal to a role of reassessing material before the original decision-maker, is so contrary to the ordinary concepts of administrative review, that I would have expected clear and more positive language to effect this object.
  13. A similar situation was considered by Hill J in Comptroller-General of Customs v Akai Pty Limited & Others 20 AAR 10 at 20. He concluded that the procedural limitations which circumscribe the Comptroller’s decision-making powers under the Customs Act, were limitations which had to be observed by the Comptroller in making the original decision and the internal review decision, but were not themselves part of the definition of the decision itself. The Tribunal was not bound to observe the procedural limits on material to be considered which applied when the reviewable decision was arrived at.
  14. In this case the question asked and the ruling given, were in relatively informal terms. The ruling, however, is susceptible of a complete review having regard to all relevant evidence which the applicant will seek to tender on the hearing. As counsel for the applicant put it, both the question asked and the question answered, could be rephrased in purely technical terms as follows —
“In relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1995 did the assessable income of the applicant include under s 16OZO(1) a net capital gain which accrued to him by reason of a capital gain deemed to have accrued to him under s 16OZ in respect of his disposal of his interest in the unit or did such assessable income not include any net capital gain by reason of the non application of s 16OZ and s 16OZO to such disposal because the condition in paragraph 16OL(1)(b) was not fulfilled in relation to such disposal.”


  1. Such a question was adversely ruled upon by the Commissioner. Should his ruling have been made, or should it have been made differently, will be the questions to be determined upon the ultimate hearing of this application, in accordance with all relevant evidence to be furnished.
