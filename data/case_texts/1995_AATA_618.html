ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

MATTER No NT95/14

By M.D. ALLEN, Senior member

................. and COMMISSIONER FOR TAXATION

SYDNEY, FRIDAY, 29 SEPTEMBER 1995



MR ALLEN: In this matter the applicant has appealed against an assessment for tax assessed for the years ending 30 June 1985 to 1991 inclusive. The law is very clear. Section 14(zzk) of the Taxation Administration Act 1954 states:



On an application for review of a reviewable objection decision:



  1. the applicant is, unless the AAT orders otherwise, limited to the grounds stated in the taxation objection to which the decision relates and

  2. the applicant has the burden of proving that:

1) if the taxation decision concerned is an assessment other than a franking assessment, the assessment is excessive or



2) if the taxation decision concerned is a franking assessment the assessment is incorrect, or



3) in any other case, the taxation decision concerned should not have been made or should have been made differently.



In other words, unlike some other proceedings in this Tribunal, the applicant in these proceedings bears an onus of proof. Consistent with that burden of proof, one must take into account the credit of the applicant. Now, in this matter the applicant in a lengthy cross-examination, did admit unequivocally, that he will, in order to preserve his assets, not only lie himself, but he will also suborn others to lie on his behalf. In that regard, evidence was given of the letter from A of 2 May 1991, which was apparently made in order to obtain a bank loan. That bank loan by the B Bank at ................. and also the purchase of property at .......................... ................................ was purchased in the name of Taxpayer’s Alias. Now, the law is quite clear that a person may use any name that he so wishes. However, when an applicant has been shown to be purchasing property; opening bank accounts and it is not only this bank account, there was a further bank account

with the C Bank, which was opened in the name of the Taxpayer’s Alias and another Peer Gynt. One must query why this property and bank accounts are then purchased and opened in false names. The question of false name also is concerned in an entry in the applicant’s diary for the year 1991. That entry on the 15th day of May 1991, is a draft letter which was apparently to be sent out by the applicant or someone on his behalf and it reads:



Dear Sir,



This is to advise that I have no connection with Taxpayer’s Alias previously of Business X. I bought the business from them late last year and any - indecipherable - words to your company should be re-couped from Business Y and signed,Taxpayer’s Alias.



Now, that quite clearly is at least a draft of a deliberate falsehood to be perpetrated upon a creditor of the applicant. It also fits with the applicants business reputation, which quite clearly, from the affidavit of E and other material in the T-documents, was bad. The applicant is no stranger to the Consumer Complaints Tribunal. Consequently, I am left in the position, I am not prepared to accept the evidence of the applicant unless it is corroborated. The applicant arrived in Australia in 1978, having sold a house in Denmark for 1.3 million Danish Kroner, according to his evidence. That also may be doubted in that in the subpoenaed material, there is a letter from an estate agent in Denmark, addressed to the applicant, which shows that the agent assessed his commission on a sale price of 1 million Kroner only. I suggest it defied logic that an estate agent would deliberately underestimate the amount on which he could raise his commission. Also attached to that letter is an advertisement from a Danish paper which is obviously a notice of sale in which, allowing for my negligible knowledge of the Danish language, clearly indicates a sale price of 1,200,000 Kroner. The applicant also gave evidence he had sold other assets in Denmark, namely a car for 80,000 Kroner and a photography business for 300,000 Kroner. He stated that he had brought into Australia the equivalent in Australian Dollars $150,000 in cash, but 800,000 Kroner remained in Denmark in mortgage securities at a high rate of interest, some 26 per cent was mentioned. He stated that that returned to him an income in Australian Dollar terms of between $30,000 and $32,000 per annum. That evidence again can be contrasted with a document which became T-12 in these proceedings, which is a letter from a D Bank in ......, addressed to some real estate agents at...... in ......, which states:

On 11 December 1978, Taxpayer alias 1 has sold his house in ...... and the proceeds converted into cash amount to Danish Kroner 300,000, however the proceeds will not be paid in cash but will be available in the form of mortgage deeds and bonds and for that reason the securities will be deposited in......, but when this deal in real property has been finished and permission is obtained from F Bank, a bankers guarantee will be issued in favour of Australia and H Bank. The amount of the guarantee is expected to be Danish Kroner 50,000 per year, which is understood that twice a year we will transfer about Danish Kroner 25,000 to the bank.

There was evidence of exchange rates and clearly that amount of 50,000 Danish krone would not amount to $A30,000 to $A32,000 per year. In fairness to the applicant, I should mention there is a later letter in April 1982 from a firm of accountants which says:



Upon request I can hereby confirm as a State authorised public accountant that Taxpayer’s Alias’ yearly return on assets employed here in Denmark exceeds $A32,000.



That letter again as I say just does not add up with the previous letter from the bank. We know the applicant that he will procure others to make false statements on his behalf and consequently are not prepared to accept that letter from the accountants particularly where it contrasts to a letter signed by a manager and assistant manager of a bank in ....... One might expect that banking officers having regard to the reputation of their bank and their own position would not be a party to a fraud but even if they were one is left with the situation which of the two letters is the fraud - I cannot decide.



The applicant and his then wife whilst in Australia have purchased assets. Admittedly, some were the trading up of a house after work had been done but as any householder will know it even costs money to effect repairs to a house. The applicant also purchased a boat. The applicant’s evidence was that he basically lived a life of leisure upon the proceeds of his Danish investments and that photography business which was conducted with his wife under the trade or business name of Business X was no more than a hobby.



As stated earlier, I do not accept the applicant as a witness of credit and given his assets in this country I would have expected copies of properly authenticated bank records to have been produced by him evidencing his investments in Denmark and the transfer of monies to him. Given the lifestyle and assets of the

applicant and his then wife there must have been a source of income. Clearly, the photographic business was one such source and even if supplemented by monies from Denmark, I am not satisfied that the photographic business was no more than a hobby. All the indicia contained in the materials before me show that it was an activity of a regular source of employment and income and hence more than a mere hobby. All in all, taking into account all of the material that has been placed before me, I can say that I am satisfied the applicant has not fulfilled the burden of proof placed upon him in these proceedings and consequently the decision under review must be affirmed and that will be the decision of the Tribunal.
