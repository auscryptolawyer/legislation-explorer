REASONS FOR DECISION



30 May 1994 Mrs H.E. Hallowes, Senior Member



  1. On 10 September 1993 the applicant's solicitor lodged applications for review of objection decisions made on 8 April 1993 by the Deputy Commissioner of Taxation disallowing objections against amended assessments for the financial years ending 30 June 1984 to 1988 inclusive, together with an application pursuant to sub-section 29(7) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") for time to be extended until 10 September 1993 within which to lodge the applications. The grounds for the application to extend time were:

"(1) The impecuniosity of the Applicant has made it difficult to raise the $300 required to make the application for review particularly when until relatively recently a separate

application fee of $300 for each decision in dispute would have had to have been paid.



(2) The reason for the application for review is, in part, that the Deputy Commissioner's Part IVA determination, which was the subject of the objections fails to identify, or alternatively incorrectly identifies the scheme for the purposes of Part IVA of the Income Tax Assessment Act. Part IVA and in particular the definition of a 'scheme', has recently been the subject of a decision of the Full Court of the Federal Court in Peabody's case, which is currently on appeal to the High Court. The precise grounds for the applicant's appeal are, therefore, currently the subject of some uncertainty. However rather than delay the lodgement date any further it is proposed to now lodge the application.



(3) The Deputy Commissioner is not prejudiced by this Application as the officer dealing with the matter at the objection stage is well aware of the efforts made by the Applicant's advisors to convince that officer that the objections should have been either allowed in full or substantially allowed and has been on notice that an application would be lodged."



  1. On 13 September 1993 a Deputy Registrar of the Tribunal made an Order under subregulation 19(5) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Regulations ("the regulations") which came into effect on 4 May 1993 that only one fee was payable. Subregulation 19(5) provides:

"19(5) If 2 or more applications:

(a) relate to the same applicant; and

(b) may, in the opinion of the Registrar or a Deputy Registrar, be conveniently heard before the Tribunal at the same time;



the Registrar or a Deputy Registrar may order that only 1 fee is payable for those applications."



No application appears to have been made under subregulation 19(6) which provides so far as relevant:



"19(6) An application fee is not payable if:

(a) the person liable to pay the fee is granted legal aid, under a legal aid scheme or service established under Commonwealth, State or Territory law or approved by the Attorney-General, for the matter to which the application relates; or



. . .

(c) the Registrar or a Deputy Registrar, having regard to the income, day to day living expenses, liabilities and assets of the person liable to pay the fee, waives the fee on the ground that payment of the fee would impose financial hardship on the person.



. . ."



  1. On 24 September 1993 the respondent advised the Tribunal that the application to extend time was opposed because:

"(i) attempts to commence proceedings outside the prescribed period should, prima facie, not be entertained. The notices of objection decision for each year issued on 8 April 1993 to:



(a) the postal address for services of notices viz Marino & Belli who are registered tax agents;



(b) the solicitors, Sly & Weigall, who lodged the notices of objection and whose Tax Partner is Mr. John Young;



(ii) the attachment to the notice of objection decision clearly states under 1.(iv) that an appeal 'must be lodged with the A.A.T. within 60 days of the decision on objection being served'. This requirement is in accordance with the provisions of Section 14ZZC of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 and Section 29 of the Administrative Appeals Act 1975;



(iii) the applicant, who employs 2 professional taxation advisers and would have been in receipt of professional advice, rested on his rights by failing to lodge, or alternatively failing to request or have his professional advisers lodge, an application for review of the decision within the prescribed period;



(iv) the applicant's employer - employees and/or his advisers have, since the commencement of an audit of the employer group in January 1987, failed to provide full details and evidence in relation to the employer group thereby delaying finalisation of the audit and the issue on, 4 May 1990, of the notices of amended assessment;



(v) it would be unfair to other taxpayers in the employer group who, through their professional advisers, have settled their notices of objection on the same subject matter and paid the tax and penalties applicable;



(vi) the applicant has not paid any amount of the tax and penalties notified in the notices of amended assessment. The respondent would suffer prejudice if an extension of time were granted as it would affect recovery of tax proceedings;



(vii) the notices of objection were lodged on 6 July 1990 and attempts to settle them at an interview, on a basis similar to that offered to and accepted by taxpayers in (v), have involved the applicant, his solicitor, and officers of his tax agents, on two, three and two occasions respectively;



(viii) the applicant is relying on the decision in Peabody v FC of T 92 ATC 4585, which was handed down on 18 September 1992, and is subject to appeal by the Commissioner;



(ix) notwithstanding Peabody's decision, the substantial objection application has no real merit, the late application does not contain valid reasons for the late lodgment and is accordingly futile;



(x) the officer handling the applicant's objections was advised by the solicitor on 14 July 1993, i.e. after the 60 day limit had well and truly expired, that he had not heard from the applicant or his tax agent and that a late application might have to be made;



(xi) it would be unfair, as between the applicant and other persons in a like position, to grant the extension of time and could create a precedent;



(xii) there would be a wider prejudice to the public if an extension of time were granted."



  1. Section 14ZY of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 ("the Administration Act") provides so far as relevant to these applications that, if a taxation objection has been lodged with the Commissioner within 60 days the Commissioner must decide whether to allow it wholly or in part or disallow it ("the objection decision"). Section 14ZZ of the Administration Act provides that, if a person is dissatisfied with the Commissioner's objection decision the person may apply to the Tribunal for review of the decision. By section 14ZZC of the Administration Act, section 29 of the AAT Act is modified, sub-section 14ZZC(d) providing that an application to the Tribunal:

"14ZZC

. . .



(d) must be lodged with the Tribunal within 60 days after the person making the application is served with notice of the decision."



These amendments to the Administration Act came into effect on 1 March 1992 when Parts IVAB and IVB of the Administration Act were repealed and a new Part, Part IVC substituted.



  1. At the hearing the Tribunal had before it a number of documents lodged by both parties. The applicant was represented by Mr K. Oliver of Counsel. The respondent was represented by an officer of the Australian Taxation Office ("the ATO"). The officer had been involved in discussions with the solicitor representing not only this taxpayer but also other taxpayers with respect to objections to amended assessments arising out of an audit of a real estate organisation. Offers of settlement were made, which the officer advised the Tribunal were accepted by some other taxpayers. The officer appears to have been confused because the taxpayer "...had ideas of settling, but then the next thing they want to go on". The officer suggested there may be an "organised agenda" in these applications to continually delay the matter.

  2. The officer told the Tribunal of his recollection of a discussion which he said occurred between him and the solicitor. If an officer has been involved in a taxpayer's matter and wishes to give evidence to the Tribunal with respect to relevant issues the Tribunal considers it preferable that he not be both advocate and witness at the hearing, although many unrepresented applicants do give both oral evidence and made submissions to the Tribunal. The respondent in these proceedings has resources which should obviate the need for an advocate to give evidence particularly if there are contentious issues and a dispute as to what was said during negotiations between the parties in which the advocate was involved. After making statements from the bar table the officer of the ATO decided to give sworn evidence to the Tribunal.

  3. Mr Oliver advised the Tribunal that the applicant was seeking review of decisions of the respondent to disallow objections to notices of amended assessments which issued on 4 May 1990 with respect to the taxpayer's liability for income tax for the income years ending 30 June 1984 to 1988 inclusive. The issue in dispute between the parties is whether or not a distribution received by a trustee from trusts associated with a real estate organisation constitute personal exertion income of the applicant. The applicant also contends that, if in fact he derived income from his personal exertion over and above that already disclosed in his personal returns, he is entitled to claim deductions with respect to that personal exertion income. The amount of additional tax imposed is also of concern to the applicant in that he contends that he has in no way inhibited the respondent in the carrying out of any inquiries. Evidence was given to the Tribunal by the applicant's solicitor and the applicant.

  4. The applicant said that he joined a real estate organisation as a salesman in about 1980 before adopting a management role two years later. In 1985 a number of trustee companies were established with the assistance of accountants. It was the applicant's understanding that the trustee of his family's trust had purchased a 10 per cent interest in another company in the web of companies involved with the real estate organisation. What may have been purchased is a 10 per cent interest in a discretionary trust. In April 1988 other purchases were apparently made by two trustee companies from the real estate organisation. Those matters are the subject of proceedings in the Supreme Court of Victoria. Whether there are discrepancies between what is contended by the applicant in the Supreme Court proceedings and what the officer contends is being put in these proceedings remains to be clarified. The Tribunal, is unable to express an opinion on this point on the evidence before it.

  5. In May 1990 the applicant received amended assessments from the respondent with respect to his personal earnings for the financial years in issue before the Tribunal. In contending that his only income from personal exertion was his salary for managing operations within the real estate organisation rather than profits from sales, the applicant said that this was so:

"...because I hadn't acquired my full licence. Under the statutories of the Estate Agent Act I couldn't be an officer in effective control. Whilst I ran that business the licence was still held by..."



He thought that his licence had come through in 1989. The applicant said that he thought he would eventually get his reward from the organisation by way of profit share. It is interesting to note in his returns for the relevant years which were in evidence before the Tribunal that the applicant is described, apparently by his accountant, as a "Real Estate Salesman" when referring to his travelling allowance. Certainly his salary appears to have been minimal for a person with his responsibilities.



  1. The applicant told the Tribunal that it was always his intention to object to the amended assessments and he had discussions with his solicitor having received a letter from the solicitor indicating that the solicitor would be prepared to act for him and others in the organisation. He attended two interviews with the ATO officer in company with his accountant to try and resolve the situation.

  2. On receiving advice in April 1993 that his objections to the amended assessments had been rejected by the respondent the applicant said that he gave instructions that applications for review should be lodged with the Tribunal and one of the companies in the web provided a cheque to the accountant for forwarding to the solicitor to lodge the applications. The applicant was not aware of any time limits. He had further discussions with both his accountant and his solicitor but he did not become aware that the applications were out of time until "just before Christmas". His proof of evidence provided to the Tribunal in response to directions given by the Tribunal on 30 September 1993 was signed by the applicant on 29 October 1993 and the Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant's memory with respect to when he became aware that the applications had not been lodged is incorrect.

  3. The applicant's solicitor gave evidence that he first commenced to act for the applicant in 1990 and he lodged objections against amended assessments with the respondent on the applicant's behalf. He was then involved in protracted negotiations with the ATO officer on behalf of the applicant and a number of other taxpayers. A stalemate was reached in April 1993 and the objection decisions were made. He received a letter dated 21 April 1993 from the applicant's accountant advising him to lodge applications for the matters to be heard by the Tribunal. He decided to see "how things were panning out on the financial front" for the applicant. He told the Tribunal that he was aware that the applicant had significant financial problems:

"...[T]hat the filing fee for making applications to this tribunal were $300 per - per year, which would have meant $1500 just to get a foot in the door here. So that aspect, coupled with the - the view that I had taken about the possible outcome of Peabody's case, I thought well, although there is a 60-day time frame in which to lodge these appeals, I was also aware that the tribunal had a fairly quick turnaround time in getting cases up for hearing".



It should be noted that during the 60 day period allowed for the applications in these matters to be lodged within time, the Tribunal's regulations referred to in paragraph 2 above came into effect.



  1. The solicitor said that he wanted to "buy some time". A decision was made to mount a Federal Court appeal in a similar matter for another taxpayer which the solicitor thought would enable him to negotiate a settlement with respect to these applications. All matters in the solicitor's opinion involved the interpretation of Part IVA of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Tax Act”). He thought that he had alerted the officer on 29 July 1993 to the fact that the applicant would be "proceeding". This was after time had expired. The solicitor could not recall speaking to the applicant after receiving the notices with respect to the objection decisions. He discussed the matter with the applicant's accountant and had at least one telephone conversation with the applicant approximately two or three months after the objection decisions were furnished. He said:

"...I would have mentioned that Peabody's case was still yet to be determined, but that eventually we would have to get around to lodging the appeals."



  1. Under cross-examination the solicitor agreed that there was no reason for him not to have lodged the applications with the Tribunal with a request that the matter not be heard until Peabody's case was decided. In light of the contentions in the notices of objection against the amended assessments apparently drafted by the solicitor in July 1990, the distinction drawn by the solicitor between these applications and the appeal on behalf of another taxpayer to the Federal Court (transcript page 25) is very flimsy indeed. The solicitor agreed that it did not explain his delay.

  2. On 26 April 1993 the solicitor received a letter from the applicant's accountant stating:

"We enclose copies of letters concerning the Notices of Objection for the abovenamed and advise they request that you proceed with an application for the matter to be heard by the A.A.T.."



The solicitor received a cheque for the lodgement fee from the applicant's accountant on 29 July 1993. However, he did not lodge the applications until 10 September.



  1. The officer gave evidence that after receiving correspondence from the solicitor with respect to another taxpayer he had a telephone conversation with the solicitor on 14 July 1993. The solicitor advised him that he had not heard from the applicant but he supposed that he would have to lodge late applications for review with the Tribunal.

  2. There is only one reason why these applications were not lodged within time. The applicant’s solicitor failed to carry out the instructions given to him. The Tribunal is satisfied that the close working relationship between the solicitor and the officer with respect to a number of objections to assessments has led them both to make assumptions which should not have been made. Prescribed periods within which applications should be lodged are not to be ignored however, the attitude of Courts has changed and a solicitor's delay may itself constitute "special reasons" why time should be extended (see Hill J in A'Hearn v Comcare (1993) 18 AAR 22). The applicant has given an explanation of the delay although, there is no rule that an explanation is an essential pre-condition to the exercise of the discretion to extend time (see Comcare v A'Hearn [1993] FCA 498; (1993) 119 ALR 85).

  3. The solicitor's conduct is only one matter for the Tribunal to take into account in deciding whether to extend time. Other relevant factors should be considered. Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v Minister for Home Affairs and Environment (1984) 58 ALR 305 listed matters for guidance. Those matters have frequently been used as a guide by the Tribunal although they do not fetter the Tribunal's discretion under sub-section 29(7) of the AAT Act. The Tribunal gives no weight to the solicitor's contention in his grounds for extending time (paragraph 1 above) that the impecuniosity of the applicant made it difficult to raise the $300.00 required to lodge the applications. Within the 60 days allowed in these applications, the Tribunal's regulations 19(5) and (6) came into effect. It became likely that only one fee would be payable. If the first ground for the application to extend time had any substance, an application under regulation 19(6) could have been made by the solicitor on the applicant's behalf. The Tribunal was not impressed with these contentions. The $300.00 lodgement fee pales into insignificance when it is compared with the amount presently owed by the applicant to the respondent under the amended assessments. The Tribunal is concerned that taxpayers who comply with time requirements do not perceive that other taxpayers are advantaged in any way. That is not to say however that taxpayers should pay other than tax properly assessed under the Tax Act.

  4. The applicant contends that the Deputy Commissioner's Part IVA determination fails to identify or alternatively incorrectly identifies a scheme for the purpose of Part IVA of the the Tax Act and that the definition of a scheme has been the subject of appeal to the High Court from the decision of the Full Federal Court in Peabody v FC of T [1993] FCA 74; (1993) 112 ALR 247. In Peabody's case Hill J, with whom Ryan and Cooper JJ agreed, said at page 253 that it is obvious that the first step in making a determination under the provisions of section 177F of the Tax Act is the identification of the “scheme” to which the provisions of Part IVA of the Tax Act apply. It is critical to the powers under sub-section 177F(1) that a scheme exists to which Part IVA applies and that the taxpayer has obtained a “tax benefit“ in connection with that “scheme".

Hill J also said at page 256:



“It will be seen that s177C requires the making of an hypothesis as to what might reasonably be expected to have happened had the scheme identified not been entered into or carried out.”



  1. Whether income might reasonably be expected to have been included in the assessable income of this applicant if the scheme as contended by the respondent was not entered into may hinge on the High Court's decision in Peabody's case when it is handed down. In coming to that conclusion I have taken into account the cases to which I was referred by the officer, in particular, Case 36 (1993) ATC 402 and what Deputy President Gerber had to say with respect to the limits to what can be achieved by the use of a trust. That may be so, but investments made by a trust may impact on those limits. However, in considering the merits of these applications the Tribunal with respect, adopts what was said by Heerey J in Osborne v FC of T (1993) 26 ATR 463 at page 464:

"That brings me to the final question which Mr Vitale for the respondent urged against the granting of the extension; namely, that the appeal was without merit. I feel it quite impossible to say at this stage that this case, involving as it does an examination of a substantial body of authority at Privy Council and High Court level as well as other authorities, is without merit. There are cases that broadly speaking, are comparable to this type of case on s260 which have gone either way. The fate of this case will depend on a careful analysis of the evidence and the application to that evidence of the principles which are to be deduced from the cases. Nothing that has been put to me today persuades me that the case is not arguable."



As in Osborne's case the amount involved in this application is substantial although the Tribunal notes that the tax and penalty have not as yet been paid by the applicant.



  1. In exercising my discretion I have taken into account the fact that as yet no tax has been paid by the applicant, that is as I understand it, no tax has been paid with respect to the original assessments before the audit of the real estate organisation. The Tribunal also notes that the officer of the ATO has been attempting to bring these matters to finality for over three years. Offers of settlement have been made by the officer. It is reasonable to accept that, having given carriage of his affairs to his solicitor and his accountant, the applicant must to some extent carry the burden of their actions. He has had the advantage of his solicitor's expertise in taxation matters and his solicitor's good relationship with the ATO officer.

  2. It may have appeared to the respondent that the applicant was resting on his rights particularly when time continued to pass after 14 July 1993. It is hard to understand why the solicitor did not immediately lodge the applications for review when he received the instructions to do so or at least when he received the $300.00 from the accountant. The Full Court handed down its decision in Peabody's case on 8 March 1993. The effect of that decision on the applicant's circumstances needed to be considered and time allowed to see whether the High Court would grant leave to appeal. Those are relevant matters for this Tribunal's consideration. There also remains an issue as to what deductions the applicant may be entitled to if Part IVA of the Tax Act applies and whether additional tax should be imposed if the applicant has co-operated fully with investigators. Whether or not other taxpayers' in a like situation have negotiated a settlement with the respondent is not relevant to the rights of this taxpayer.

  3. Having considered these matters the Tribunal is satisfied in the circumstances of these applications that it is fair and equitable to extend the time for lodging the applications which, although not brief, is not so lengthy as to have dulled memories and in that sense prejudiced the respondent. Much of the evidence has been documented and I am satisfied that there are issues to be determined between the parties to which Peabody's case may be relevant. There was some indication given by the solicitor to the officer on 14 July 1993 that applications may be lodged.

I CERTIFY THAT THIS AND THE TWENTY-ONE (21) PRECEDING PAGES ARE A TRUE COPY OF THE DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION HEREIN OF MRS H.E. HALLOWES, SENIOR MEMBER
