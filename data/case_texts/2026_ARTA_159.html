ISSUES

  1. The ultimate issue for the Tribunal is to determine whether the Applicant has discharged his onus of proving the income tax assessment for the 2023 income year is excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessment should have been. [1]
  2. Determination of this issue requires consideration of whether the Applicant:
    (a) is entitled to a deduction under s 8-1 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) (ITAA 97) for:
    (i) work related car expenses totalling $11,130

    (ii) work related travel expenses totalling $3,047.84;

    (iii) work related expenses totalling $4,127.93; and

    (iv) interest totalling $15,407.

  3. In the circumstances of this case this will require the Tribunal to determine:
    (a) whether the expenses claimed were incurred by the Applicant in the Income Year;

    (b) whether the expenses incurred were in the course of gaining or producing assessable income;

    (c) whether the expensed incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable income were of a private or domestic nature, and if so the correct apportionment; and

    (d) whether the applicant has substantiated the deductions.

  4. In a proceeding such as this where s 144ZZK (b)(i) of the Taxation Administration Act (Cth) (TAA 53) applies, the correct or preferable decision to be made by the Tribunal will depend on whether the Applicant is able to discharge his burden of establishing on the balance of probabilities[2] that the character of the payments made by him are properly deductable and substantiated.
  5. It is for the taxpayer to establish any fact demonstrating the assessment to be excessive.[3] The Commissioner is not required to defend the assessment and is entitled to put the Applicant to proof of any aspect of his case.[4]
  6. It is a well-established principle that self- serving uncorroborated evidence or evidence of witnesses who have an interest in the outcome of litigation, needs to be approached critically.[5] However it is not obligatory for a taxpayer to call all material witnesses or produce all material documents and the Tribunal is entitled accept the evidence of the taxpayer alone if it finds that evidence to be truthful.[6]

THE LAW

  1. The deductions claimed by Mr Afshari are subject to the general deductions provision contained in s8-1 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) (ITAA 97). In respect of the car expenses, they are also subject to special provisions in Division 28 of the ITAA 97.

Section 8-1 ITAA 97

  1. Section 8-1 of the ITAA 97 provides:
    General deductions

    (1) You can deduct from your assessable income any loss or outgoing to the extent that:

    (1) (a) it is incurred in gaining or producing your assessable income; or

    (1) (b) it is necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing your assessable income.

    (2) However, you cannot deduct a loss or outgoing under this section to the extent that:

    (a) It is a loss or outgoing of capital, or of a capital nature; or

    (b) It is a loss or outgoing of a private or domestic nature; or

    (c) It is incurred in relation to gaining or producing your *exempt income or *non-assessable non-exempt income; or

    (d) A provision of the Act prevents you from deducting it.

  2. The operative provision has a positive and a negative part which both need to be satisfied by the taxpayer. Not only must the loss or outgoing have the requisite connection to the production of income, it must not also be prohibited by one of the conditions in s 8-1(2).
  3. The relevant element of the positive limb in this case is s 8-1(1)(a). It is generally accepted that the term ‘incurred in gaining or producing assessable income’ in s 8-1(1)(a) of the ITAA 97 means incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable income. For an expense to be incurred in gaining or producing assessable income it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the expense should be in whatever is productive of that assessable income.[7]
  4. However, not just any casual connection is enough to satisfy the statutory test. Deductibility requires a more immediate connection. It is not sufficient that it is for the purpose of deriving income, it must be in the course of the activity itself.[8]
  5. When determining what is productive of an employee’s assessable income, consideration should be given to the scope of the employee’s income producing activities. This is a question of fact and degree and is not confined to the day-to-day activities performed by the employee. It requires consideration of the employment contract along with the duties to be observed and the tasks to be performed by the employee which may extend to tasks beyond those contained in the employment contract. [9]
  6. The second limb contained in s 8-1(2) of the ITAA 97, denies a deduction for a loss or outgoing that is of a private, domestic or capital nature. These terms are not defined in the legislation but it is generally accepted that they are those expenses which are of a personal or private capacity or relate to household or domestic affairs of the taxpayer. Capital or capital nature refers to items that are not regular or recurrent but those expenses expected to have an enduring or lasting benefit.[10]
  7. A deductibility of an expense is generally determined objectively, without reference to a taxpayers subjective motive or intention.[11]

Car expenses

  1. It is generally accepted that travel between home and a place of employment is not deductible. This is because the expenditure was incurred before or after each income earning activity has been performed, and is therefore not incurred in gaining or producing assessable income as required by s 8-1(1)(a) of the ITAA 97.[12]
  2. A taxpayer may, however, be entitled to claim car expenses in particular circumstances, including for travel between workplaces. Section 28-12 of the ITAA 97 states:
28‑12 Car expenses

(1) If you owned or leased a *car, you can deduct for the car’s expenses an amount or amounts worked out using one of 2 methods.

Note 1: For particular types of cars taken on hire you cannot use one of the 2 methods: see section 28‑165.

Note 2: In certain circumstances the lessee of a luxury car is taken to be its owner (see subsection 242‑15(2)).

Note 3: In certain circumstances (for example, under a hire purchase agreement) the notional buyer of property is taken to be its owner (see subsection 240‑20(2)).

(2) You must use one of the 2 methods unless an exception applies. If you can’t use either of the methods, you can’t deduct anything for the *car expenses.

(emphasis added)

28‑13 Meaning of car expense

(1) A car expense is a loss or outgoing to do with a *car.

(2) In addition, any of the following is a car expense:

(a) a loss or outgoing to do with operating a *car;

(b) the decline in value of a car.

  1. The two methods available to taxpayers are the logbook method and the cents per kilometre method. The cents per kilometre method is only available for the first 5,000 business kilometres, calculated as number of business kilometres travelled by the rate allowable in that year. [13]
  2. Section 28-90 ITAA 97 requires that a taxpayer calculate their ‘business use percentage use’ for their car by identifying their ‘business kilometres’. Business kilometres is defined in s 28-90 ITAA97 and means the kilometres the car travelled to either:
(a) produce assessable income; or

(b) travel between workplaces.

  1. Section 28-100 defines the term ‘travel between workplaces’ which relevantly excludes travel to and from a taxpayer’s residence.[14]
  2. Section 28-140 ITAA 97 sets out the requirements for keeping odometer records. Importantly, a record must be made at or as soon as possible after the start or end of the period, or the end of the specified day as appropriate and each record must be made before a taxpayer lodges their income tax return.
  3. Division 40 of the ITAA 97 sets out how to claim an expense deduction for a depreciating asset which includes a motor vehicle. The motor vehicle starts to decline in value when it's first used[15] and the decline in value can be worked out using the diminishing value method or the prime cost method.[16]
  4. Where a taxpayer uses the logbook method, they must provide written evidence of all car expenses to substantiate them.[17]
  5. The business use percentage calculated from the relevant lookbook, is applied to the substantiated running costs of the relevant vehicle, including fuel servicing, registration, insurance and depreciation to work out the taxpayers claimable expense.

Home office - running expenses

  1. As a general rule, expenses associated with a taxpayer’s home are of a private or domestic nature and do not qualify as deductions for taxation purposes. An exception to this general rule is where part of the home is used for income producing activities as distinct from minimal tasks, such as occasionally checking emails or taking calls. Running expenses may take on a different character where taxpayers can establish that they have incurred additional expenditure on the running expenses as a result of their income producing activities.[18]
  2. In appropriate circumstances, taxpayers are entitled to a deduction for the expenditure actually incurred through their income producing activities which is additional to their private expenditure. Taxpayers may use either the fixed rate method or the actual costs method to calculate the claim.
  3. The fixed rate method allows an amount per work hour for additional running expenses, separate claims for expenses not covered by the fixed rate (such as the decline in value of depreciating assets) and doesn't require you to have a dedicated home office.
  4. The actual cost method allows a person to claim the actual expenses incurred a result of working from home. This requires either a record showing the number of hours worked from home, the additional running expenses you incurred while working from home (such as receipts, bills and other documents) and how you worked out the amount of your deduction.
  5. Where expenses are to be apportioned, there is no specific formular or test but rather what is a fair and reasonable apportionment in the circumstances of the particular case.[19]

Substantiation

  1. Division 900 of the ITAA 1997 sets out substantiation rules that apply to certain expenses that are incurred by an individual taxpayer. Those expenses must be substantiated.[20]
  2. Subdivision 900-B applies to ‘work expenses’ which are defined as a loss or outgoing incurred in producing your salary or wages.[21] Subsection 900-15(1) outlines the requirements that a taxpayer must satisfy for work expenses. The relevant requirements under subsection 900-B are that:
    (a) the expense claimed must be deductible under a provision of the income tax acts; and

    (b) the taxpayer must obtain and retain written evidence to support the expense.

  3. Written evidence[22] for goods or services purchased is a document from the supplier that shows the following:
    (a) name or business name of the supplier;

    (b) amount of the expense;

    (c) nature of the goods or services purchased; and

    (d) date the expense was incurred, and

    (e) date the document was produced.

  4. In circumstances where such a document has not been obtained, the Commissioner will accept other forms of evidence, in accordance with subsection 900-115(3):
    (a) if the document does not show the day the expense was incurred, you may use a bank statement or other reasonable, independent evidence that shows when it was paid;

    (b) if the document the supplier gave you does not specify the nature of the goods or services, you may write in the missing details yourself before you lodge your *income tax return for the income year.

  5. It is an established principle in the case law that a taxpayer who does not obtain or retain appropriate records of deductable expenditure faces an uphill battle to discharge their onus.[23]

The Applicant’s circumstances

  1. Mr Afshari is married with two school aged children. His elderly parents also reside with him in the family home. Unfortunately, in July 2022, Mr Afshari’s father suffered from a serious medical episode and required an extended period of hospitalisation including a stay in the intensive care unit. Mr Afshari advised the Tribunal that his father was not discharged until December 2022.[27]
  2. Mr Afshari was employed by GHD in the Income Year on a full-time basis as an engineer. His duties included technical engineering work, responding to technical queries, preparing reports and proposals, project management and supervision of junior staff. His role also required attendance at offsite locations.
  3. The Applicant ordinarily worked standard full-time hours. During the Income Year, Mr Afshari had an approved hybrid working arrangement under which he worked from home 2 days per week and attended the office on the remaining days. Mr Afshari gave evidence that he worked from home more frequently from July 2022 due to his extenuating personal circumstances. He was working from home full time between July and December 2022. Mr Afshari says he did not alter his formal work from home arrangement but sought approval informally as required from his manager. The extent of the additional work from home was disputed.

In fact, he had another book in which the information was recorded and from which he transcribed for the purpose of the compliance process.

Work related travel expenses

  1. The Applicant claimed deductions for taxi and ride share expenses. Mr Afshari initially claimed expenses relating to attendance at the corrosion conference, but later withdrew that claim.
  2. In respect of the taxi and uber fares, Mr Afshari told the Tribunal that these were in respect of work-related travel, but no further details including the purpose of the individual journeys was provided.
  3. The documentary evidence indicated that the taxi expenses were paid using cabcharge facilities. The ride share documentation did not include dates, times or destination of travel.
  4. Mr Afshari was cross-examined on the expenses claimed in respect of the corrosion conference, which included flights to an accommodation in Newcastle. It became apparent from that line of questioning and the documents put to him, which included publicly available reports of the organiser which confirmed Newcastle conference was postponed and subsequently rescheduled for the following income year in Perth. Further, there was an entry in the log book provided by Mr Afshari in which he recorded a site visit on one of the purported conference days.
  5. Mr Afshari was adamant he attended the conference in Newcastle during the cross examination. Before the hearing resumed on the second day he no longer sought to claim these expenses on the basis they were not incurred in the relevant income year. Although I do not make any adverse findings as to Mr Afshari’s credit in respect of this line of questioning, it does go to the accuracy and reliability off the records he maintained more generally.

Other work-related expenses

  1. Mr Afshari gave evidence that he converted a part of his home into an office area which is used exclusively for working from home. The office is semi-detached from the house and has its own point of entry, bathroom and kitchen facilities. Mr Afshari could not recall precisely when the renovations were completed, acknowledging it could have been part way through the income year. The renovations took place over a number of months and involved a number of different tradespeople. He explained the lock repairs related to the external entry to the home office. The ceiling repairs were also only in respect of the home office.
  2. Mr Afshari said the screen shots of the calendar accurately reflected the time spent working from home, which was significantly more than the two day per week described by GHD. He agreed that this record was not contemporaneous but created for the purposes of the audit.
  3. Mr Afshari provided detailed calculations in respect of the utilities claimed. Invoices or bank statements evidencing the payments were not provided.
  4. In respect of the mobile phone and internet usage, invoices were provided. Mr Afshari conceded in cross examination there was an element of personal use.

Miscellaneous matters

  1. In passing, the Tribunal was advised Mr Afshari also runs as his own engineering consulting business, which has also been the subject of an audit an objection decision of the Commissioner. No further detail as to the nature and extent of the business or the compliance process was provided.
  2. In any event, the Tribunal is only concerned with the expenses as the relate to his employment with GHD. In response to a question about record keeping it was not clear what, if any, measures were taken to ensure separation between business and employment activities.

CONSIDERATION OF ISSUES

Preliminary observations

  1. The Applicant represented himself in the proceedings and does not have formal legal or accounting qualifications. The Tribunal has had regard to this fact in assessing the manner in which the evidence was prepared and presented.
  2. Mr Afshari accepted in his evidence that some records were not prepared contemporaneously and were reconstructed from memory or other documents for the purposes of the audit, objection and the review process. The Applicant also accepted that some records contained errors.
  3. The Tribunal has assessed the evidence having regard to those concessions. While the absence of contemporaneous records does not automatically preclude acceptance of a taxpayers evidence, it may affect the weight to be given particularly where objective records are available.
  4. At various points in his evidence, Mr Afshari suggested the Tribunal could obtain confirmation of aspects of his evidence from third parties. It is not the role of the Tribunal to undertake investigative inquiries of that nature. The Applicant bears the onus of establishing that the assessment is excessive and what the correct assessment should have been.
  5. The Tribunal has considered the evidence as a whole, including documentary evidence, oral evidence and the surrounding objective circumstances.

Work related car expenses

  1. Mr Afshari seeks to claim work related car expenses comprised of:
    • $9800 in deprecation for the Lancer and the Prado;
    • $1238 of service and maintenance costs for the Lancer and the Prado;
    • $2,101.95 in fuel costs.
  2. The expenses have been calculated based on the business use percentage, which Mr Afshari says is 42.36% for the Lancer and 79.29% for the Prado based on the log book records.
  3. At audit and objection, the Commissioner did not accept the claims on the basis that the Applicant had failed to establish the travel was on work (rather than to work) and the log book did not comply with the legislative requirements.
  4. The Respondent’s Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions put the Applicant to proof on the nexus issue. However, in closing oral submissions, the Commissioner conceded nexus but maintained the expenses has not been substantiated or properly apportioned.
  5. Mr Afshari’s evidence was that he undertook some work from home before travelling to offsite locations. This was inconsistent with the response provided by GHD that there was no such requirement, although the employer concedes they did not have full visibility of what Mr Afshari actually did.
  6. The evidence of the specific circumstances of each of the 22 site visits is sparse in nature, but even in accepting the travel was on work, Mr Afshari has still failed to discharge his onus of proof. The Applicant was required to keep logbooks in order to make the logbook claim.[28] He is also required to keep odometer records for the relevant income year.[29] Critically the accuracy and reliability of the logbooks was disputed by the Respondent.
  7. Mr Afshari has produced multiple versions of the logbook through the audit and review process. He maintains that any changes have been to satisfy requests from the Commissioner and although some minor errors have been identified, the distance travelled has remained consistent.
  8. Mr Afshari did not provide receipts relating to all the service and maintenance costs.[30] Some of the receipts said to substantiate the fuel costs did not include fuel or included other unrelated expenses.[31] There is no evidence before the Tribunal to substantiate the loan repayments of the Prado.
  9. From the Tribunal’s review of the log books and the evidence of Mr Afshari, it is plain that;
    • none of the versions were contemporaneous and were reconstructed from a separate book maintained by Mr Afshari that has not been produced in evidence;
    • the logbooks entries were inconsistent with independent records before the Tribunal. For example, the odometer reading reflected in the service record for the Lancer was significantly higher than the odometer readings in the log book after that date;[32]
    • the log books also refer to travel inconsistent with other records provided by the Applicant; [33]
    • Mr Afshari claimed in his response at audit, the Lancer was damaged during a site visit on 20 November 2022 and included a photo of the damage. That trip is not recorded in the Lancer logbook.[3] It is recorded as a trip in the Prado log book, which in any event undermines the reliability of the records.
  10. I cannot be satisfied the logbooks are accurate, complete or contemporaneous. I find that the logbook records are reconstructed, inconsistent with objective records and not prepared contemporaneously. I find the log books do not comply with the legislative requirements.
  11. The log book method is only available when the statutory record keeping requirements are satisfied. In the absence of a complying logbook there is no basis upon which the Tribunal can determine the deductible business use percentage or the actual expenses incurred. Accordingly, as the Applicant's claims are calculated on that basis, he has failed to discharge his onus of proof and the deductions must be disallowed in full.

Work related travel expenses

  1. Mr Afshari seeks to claim work related travel expenses comprised as follows:
    • $362,05 in taxi fares; and
    • $690 in uber fares.
  2. Mr Afshari did not lead evidence clearly identifying which travel expenses had been reimbursed by his employer and which expenses had not. Further, the Applicant did not establish that the taxi expenses were incurred by him having regard to the apparent use of cab charge facilities. The ride share documentation did not include the date, time or destination of travel, which are generally available as part of these records.
  3. Regardless, Mr Afshari gave no explanation to the expenses outside the broad assertion that were incurred for work purposes. There was no evidence as to purpose of the travel and the relationship with the income producing activities.
  4. Accordingly, I'm not satisfied that the expense was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and has the necessary nexus s 8-1 of the ITAA 97. The Applicant has failed to discharge his onus of proof and the claim must be disallowed.

Self-education expenses

  1. Mr Afshari sought to claim as a deduction $140 in respect of an Iranian language class provided by the Iranian Association.
  2. The Applicant was employed as an engineer. No evidence was provided to demonstrate how the language course maintained or improved skills required for that role, or was otherwise sufficiently connected to the Applicant’s income producing activities. It is not enough that a course may generally improve some skills or knowledge.
  3. Accordingly, I'm not satisfied that the expense was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and has the necessary nexus s 8-1 of the ITAA 97. The Applicant has failed to discharge his onus of proof and the claim must be disallowed.

Other work related expenses

  1. Mr Afshari sought to claim other work related expenses comprised as follows:
    • $2826.41 in home office expenses relating to utilities;
    • $717.42 mobile phone expenses;
    • $960 internet expenses;
    • $2451.01 in relation to an air conditioner and repairs to a door lock and the office ceiling.
  2. There is no dispute that Mr Afshari worked from home in the Income Year and that there was a dedicated space set aside for this purpose. The Commissioner allowed a deduction based on the fixed rate method calculated by reference to the formal work from home agreement.
  3. Mr Afshari in this matter seeks a deduction based on his actual expenses. The Commissioner did not accept the expenses were incurred or the proposed apportionment of those expenses.
  4. Mr Afshari did not provide documentary evidence substantiating the utility expense is said to have been incurred. Rather, the Applicant provided calculations estimating the business use proportion of those expenses. In the absence of underlying invoices or other corroborating evidence, I'm not satisfied Mr Afshari has established that the claimed utility expenses were incurred.
  5. Even if I were to accept that the utility expenses were incurred, I'm not satisfied that the apportionment adopted is fair and reasonable. During the relevant, the household included the Applicant, his spouse, 2 school aged children and his elderly parents. In those circumstances, it is reasonable to infer that the utilities were used for ordinary household purposes by multiple occupants. The Applicant claimed approximately 70% of the relevant expenses as work related. Notwithstanding at the work area was said to be physically separate from the main residence, I'm not satisfied there is an evidentiary basis supporting that level of work related use. The Applicant has therefore not discharged his onus of establishing the extent of any deductible portion of the utility expenses.
  6. Mr Afshari provided screen shots of payments to Vodafone which I understand relate to the mobile phone expenses. The front page of the internet invoice from Telstra was also in evidence.
  7. Even if I accept the mobile phone and internet expenses were incurred, apportionment remains an issue. In circumstances where expenses have both a work related and private component, apportionment must be undertaken on a fair and reasonable basis. The Applicant did not provide call records, usage analysis or other evidence from which the Tribunal could determine the extent of work related and private use of the mobile phone. Mr Afshari accepted in his evidence there was an element of private use. In the absence of objective evidence supporting the apportionment, the Tribunal is not able to determine the exact extent of any deductible proportion.
  8. Similar considerations apply in relation to the internet expenses. While I accept that the internet was available for work related use, there is no reliable or independent evidence establishing the extent of work-related usage.
  9. I have considered the calendar material provided by the Applicant. Mr Afshari accepted that these records were reconstructed. Further, there were inconsistencies between this material and the information provided by the Applicant’s employer regarding the work from home arrangements. In those circumstances I placed limited weight on this material and I'm not satisfied that those records provide a reliable basis upon which to determine the extent of work-related internet usage. The Applicant resided with his spouse and two children. It is reasonable to infer that the internet was used for private household purposes. In the absence of evidence enabling a fair and reasonable apportionment, the Applicant has not discharged the owners of establishing the extent of any deductible amount.
  10. Mr Afshari also claimed deductions for repair and maintenance expenses, including repairs relating to a door lock and water leakage. The Applicant asserted the repairs related specifically to the home office area, as opposed to other parts of the residence. I'm not prepared to accept the uncorroborated statements of the Applicant. Therefore, Mr Afshari has not discharged the onus of establishing entitlement to deductions for those repair expenses.
  11. In relation to the air conditioning unit, I accept that the Applicant is entitled to a claim a decline in value only in accordance with the applicable provision. To that extent I'm satisfied that the deduction allowed by the Commissioner is appropriate and correct.

CONCLUSION AND DECISION

  1. The Applicant’s case failed, in the main, because he was unable to provide accurate, reliable and contemporaneous documents to substantiate his claims. In doing so, he failed to discharge his onus of proof as set out in s 14ZZK(b)(i) of the TAA.
  2. The Respondent’s objection decision is affirmed.
