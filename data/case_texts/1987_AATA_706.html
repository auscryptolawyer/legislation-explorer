QT86/1888 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 706; 87 ATC 891 (9 July 1987)

Last Updated: 23 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Taxation -. sale of shares at profit - whether purchase as investment, for sale at profit or as share dealing - evidence of. nature of transaction equivocal - whether onus of proof discharged.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 S26A

FC of T v Werchon (1982) 82 ATC 4332

Werchon v FC of T (1981)81 ATC 4670

DECISION AND REASONS

APPLICANT

And:

RESPONDENT

AAT Decision No 3642

Date: 9 July 1987

Place: Brisbane

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

DECISION

Before: Dr P Gerber, Senior Member

Date: 9 July 1987

Place: Brisbane

Pursuant to subsection 43(1)(a) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975, the Tribunal affirms the objection decision in relation to the assessment for the year ended 30 June 1981.

(sgnd.) P. GERBER

SENIOR MEMBER

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

APPLICANT

And:

RESPONDENT

REASONS FOR DECISION

Before: Dr P Gerber, Senior Member

Date: 9 July 1987

Place: Brisbane

In this case, the taxpayer is a medically qualified university academic who, in 1979, had saved some money which he thought should be invested, and, after studying the financial section of the Courier Mail and having previously attended in an investment study course, went into the market buying a number of shares. In the year now before me, the year ending 30 June 1981, two parcels of shares, one in Leonard Oil No Liability and the other in Woodside Petroleum Limited, were sold with other shares and realized a profit of $4840. Both parcels were acquired in October 1980.

2. The sale of these two parcels of shares was disclosed in the return, where it was stated that they were acquired as an investment. This assertion was repeated before me, when it was claimed - in the words of the accountant who prepared the return - that the shares were bought "as an investment and as a hedge against inflation". It is not without significance that in the same year, a loss of $10.20 was claimed on the sale of some mining shares which had been acquired in 1979 and held for more than 12 months.

  1. The taxpayer, who gave evidence, deposed that he had seen an aunt's savings whittled away by inflation and that he looked on his share purchases as an investment. He further deposed -evidence which I unhesitatingly accept - that the subject shares were sold in the 1981 tax year solely because he was keen to acquire a block of land on Stradbroke Island. However, against the whole of the evidence, this explanation is not so overwhelming as to completely erase a picture of systematic share dealing, which emerges from a perusal of prior and subsequent returns lodged on behalf of the taxpayer and which "taint" the impugned transactions.
  2. In any case which involves the application of an alleged capital gain, tribunals traditionally have made a wide sweep and comprehensive survey over the activities of a taxpayer who asserts that property was acquired for purposes of long-term investment or as a hedge against inflation. A perusal of the returns in the years in which share dealing took place indicate that in each of those years claims were made which are consistent only with the position that the taxpayer - in those years, at any rate - either was engaged in the business of share dealing, or alternatively, had acquired his shares with the intention of resale at a profit.
  3. Thus, in the 1980 tax year, a share transaction loss was claimed and allowed - admittedly a very small one - on the basis that the dealing which gave rise to the loss was one which entitled the taxpayer to the deduction. In the same year, the taxpayer engaged in a one-off futures dealing which likewise resulted in a loss. This loss, too, was claimed as a deduction and an objection raised when it was disallowed, a claim (and objection) likewise consistent only with the view that it was a trading transaction. As mentioned before, a small share transaction loss was again claimed in 1981.
  4. It must be recalled that at that time, as the law then stood, a loss on shares was not allowable automatically merely because the acquisition and sale which gave rise to it occurred within 12 months. In any event, the loss claimed in 1981 related to a dealing where the acquisition and sale extended beyond twelve months. A repetition of this pattern is revealed in the 1984 tax year, where, once again, losses on the sale of shares were claimed, and, the only transaction which resulted in a gain included as assessable income, again consistent only with the view that the taxpayer either was engaged in the business of trading in shares or had acquired them with the intention of resale at a profit.
  5. The accountant who prepared all the returns, gave evidence to the effect that it was her belief that a transaction which involved an acquisition and sale, all within 12 months, both brought the profit to tax and permitted the deduction for the loss. If this was her belief (and I have no reason to doubt it) it was not good law. It was a heresy briefly propounded by Tadgell J in Werchon v FC of T (81 ATC 4670), soon to be reversed on appeal (FC of T v Werchon (82 ATC 4332)). In any event, Werchon at first instance was issued well after the 1981 return now before me had been prepared. Post-1981, the taxpayer has continued buying and selling shares in a modest way - shares predominantly of a speculative kind.

8. I therefore am left at the end of the case in the somewhat quixotic position where the oral evidence does not clearly point in one direction or another; that is, whilst I accept that the shares in dispute were sold for the reasons deposed to, I have not been wholly persuaded that they were acquired as a long-term investment or as a hedge against inflation. At one point, it was suggested that these shares were to assist the taxpayer in his retirement. However, such an avowed purpose is somewhat "neutral" in that it points with equal force in two directions, that is, an intention to receive dividend income or to sell the shares at a profit. Against this background, I am entitled - indeed bound -to look at the whole picture in the round as it emerges from the various returns. When one examines the 1980 and 1984 returns, these were lodged - and could only have been lodged - on the basis that the taxpayer was either in the business of share trading, or else acquired his portfolio with the intention of selling his shares at a profit. There was no other basis on which the losses on sale could have been claimed. Turning to the 1981 tax year, when a substantial profit was made on the two parcels now in dispute, it was asserted for the first and only time that the shares were acquired as an investment. No attempt was made at the hearing to distinguish between the shares sold in 1981 and the rest of the taxpayer's share portfolio. In the absence of any countervailing evidence, it must be presumed that the intention with respect to each and every share acquisition was the same.

9. Against the whole of the background, I am satisfied (i) that all this taxpayer's share transactions fall squarely within the ambit of sec. 26(a) of the Tax Act and (ii) that this taxpayer's returns were lodged on the basis of providing the best fiscal result, rather than accurately reflecting the realities.

  1. In the circumstances, the taxpayer has not satisfied the onus of proof, which is placed on his shoulders, in persuading me that the Leonard and Woodside shares were bought as a long-term investment or as a hedge against inflation. The decision on objection therefore must be upheld.
  2. The one consolation for this taxpayer is that even a decision in his favour would give him small comfort, since, on a finding of an absence of a sec. 26(a) purpose, the Commissioner could - and no doubt would - issue an amended assessment for the 1984 tax year, disallowing losses which had been allowed on the assumption that the shares sold in that year had been acquired with the intention of resale at a profit.
