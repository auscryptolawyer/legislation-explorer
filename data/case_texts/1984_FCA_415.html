Re Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia v Jax Tyres Pty Limited [1984] FCA 415; 85 Atc 4001 / 5 FCR 257 (21 December 1984)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA

And: JAX TYRES PTY LIMITED

No. G369 of 1984

Sales Tax - Words and Phrases

85 ATC 4001 / 5 FCR 257

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Lockhart(1), Sheppard(2) and Wilcox(3) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Sales Tax - taxpayer engaged in retreading tyres - whether retreading manufacture or repair - whether retreaded tyre a different article from the old tyre - relevance of cost of old tyre and scale and complexity of taxpayer's operations.

Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 1) 1930, ss.3, 17, 17A and 18

Sales Tax - Whether the process of retreading tyres was "manufacture" for sales tax purposes or merely a "repair" or a "renovation" of the worn tyre - Whether the article emerging after retreading was "different" from its components - Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930 (Cth), ss 3(1), 17, 17A, 18(1)(c) - Motor Traffic Regulations 1935 (NSW), Sched F, par 82(d).

Words and Phrases - "Manufacture" - Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930 (Cth), ss 3(1), 17, 17A. Held (per curiam): (1) The process of retreading a tyre irrespective of which of the processes known as "recapping", "top capping", "remoulding" and "pre-curing" it involves, does not constitute "manufacture" for the purposes of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No 1) 1930 (Cth) (the Act) either according to the ordinary meaning of "manufacture" or the statutory extension of that meaning as contained in the definition section of the Act, s 3(1).

(2) That both "manufacture" and "production", as used in the definition of "manufacture" in s 3(1)(a) of the Act, involve the element of "making a different thing from that out of which it is made". The emerging article is "different" if it has a character or use distinctive from its components. On the facts here, although a retreaded tyre was plainly distinguishable from a new tyre, it was not a different thing from its ingredients. The original tyre did not become a component or ingredient of a new product; rather the tread rubber and other substances applied to the original tyre in the retreading process became components of the original tyre in a process involving (per Lockhart J) the repair or remodelling of the worn tyre and (per Sheppard J) involving if not the repair of, then the renovation of the worn tyre. In making such a determination, it was relevant that tyres are manufactured with an inherent capacity for retreading and that, while the area of a tyre that comes into contact with the road may be in need of renewal, the rest of the tyre remains sound and useful.

(3) That the factory process was on a "large and recurring scale" bearing the appearance of manufacture and that the proportion of the cost of the worn tyre to the market value of the retreaded tyre was not large, were relevant but not determining factors, the critical question always being whether on the facts what is made is a different thing from that out of which it is made.

McNicol v. Pinch (1906) 2 KB 352; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Jack Zinader Pty Ltd [1949] HCA 42; (1949) 78 CLR 336, referred to with approval.

Ready Mixed Concrete (WA) Pty Ltd v. Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) (1971) 45 ALJR 293; Irving v. Munro & Sons Ltd [1931] HCA 57; (1931) 46 CLR 279; Cohns Industries Pty Ltd v. Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1979) 37 FLR 508; Adams v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1948) 8 ATD 332; Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation (SA) v. Ellis & Clark Ltd [1934] HCA 54; (1934) 52 CLR 85, referred to.

ORDER

DECISION

The principal question raised by this case is whether the process of retreading a tyre produces something different and distinct from the worn tyre that was retreaded and, if it does, whether the process answers the description of "manufacture". The question arises in the context of the sales tax legislation of the Commonwealth, in particular the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 1) 1930 ("the Act").

Jax Tyres Pty. Limited, the respondent, commenced proceedings in the High Court of Australia seeking a declaration to the effect that the process of retreading tyres carried on by the respondent does not answer the description of the "manufacture" of goods by it and that retreaded tyres are not "goods manufactured in Australia", in each case for the purposes of the sales tax legislation of the Commonwealth including the Act. The High Court remitted the matter to this Court, and the proceeding was heard at first instance by Beaumont J. His Honour made orders declaring that:-

1. The operations carried on by the respondent whereby tyres that had previously been used in Australia are retreaded do not constitute the "manufacture" of goods by it for the purposes of the Act: and

2. The retreaded tyres resulting from the operations carried on by the respondent in retreading tyres that had been previously used in Australia are not goods "manufactured" in Australia for the purposes of the relevant legislation including the Act.

The Commissioner of Taxation ("the Commissioner") appealed to the Full Court of this Court from His Honour's judgment.

The facts are not in dispute. My narration of them is taken primarily from the reasons for judgment of the learned trial Judge. The respondent carries on business in premises it describes as a "retread factory" in a suburb of Sydney. Part of the respondent's business is retreading tyres for its customers and charging a fee for its services; in which case no property in the tyres passes to the respondent. About 90-95% of truck tyres and about 50% of passenger car tyres retreaded by the respondent are in this class. The respondent also purchases worn tyres, processes them and sells them as "retreads": this accounts for some 5% of truck tyres and about 50% of passenger car tyres retreaded by the respondent.

The processes of retreading employed by the respondent are known as "top capping", "full capping", "remoulding" and "precuring". "Top capping" involves the application of new tread rubber to the area of the casing of the tyre which normally comes into contact with the road. "Full capping" involves the new tread rubber being applied, not only to the area of the worn tyre casing which comes into contact with the road, but to the shoulder areas of the casing as well. In the process of "remoulding" the new rubber is extended beyong both the area of the casing which normally comes into contact with the road and the shoulder areas and proceeds down to the bead of the tyre. The area of the bead is that part of the tyre made of steel wires, wrapped or reinforced by ply cords and shaped to fit the rim of the wheel.

For each of these processes the respondent obtains worn tyres (for both passenger vehicles and trucks) from worn tyre casing dealers, tyre retailers and private owners - especially truck operators.

After the tyre is received at the respondent's factory it is inspected to ensure that it is suitable for retreading in accordance with the standards prescribed by Australian Standard Specification No. 1973-1976 in respect of "retreaded pneumatic passenger car tyres".

More than one State of Australia has enacted legislation (for example, the Motor Traffic Regulations 1935 (N.S.W.)) prohibiting the fitting of tyres to motor cars which have been retreaded unless the tyres comply with the prescribed Australian Standard Specification No. 1973-1976. This specification prescribes the minimum requirements for the retreading of pneumatic passenger car tyres and defines the performance and the dimensional and physical requirements for the retreaded tyres. Each retreaded tyre is required by the specification to be marked with the word "retread".

At the time of inspection, repairs to nail holes in the inside of the casing are made. If the casing of a tyre remains sound. it may be retreaded more than once. This happens frequently in the case of truck tyres but not often in the case of passenger car tyres.

In the retreading process the entire tread surface which is to be treated is uniformly buffed to remove all old and excess rubber and to prepare a clean and smooth surface. A smooth buff promotes proper adhesion of the tread rubber. Truck tyres and, in some cases, passenger car tyres are sprayed with a rubber solution to ensure adhesion of tread rubber to the buffed surface. The rubber is then applied by one of two methods. In the processes of "recapping", "top capping" and "remoulding" a machine is used to apply automatically extruded tread rubber to the casing. The rubber is extruded in the form of a continuous hot ribbon which is wound, in overlapping layers, around the circumference of the casing. The depth and contour of the tread shape is determined by an electronically controlled system. The process of "pre-curing" is employed by the respondent only for retreading truck tyres. In that process the tread rubber is purchased by the respondent already cured with the desired tread pattern moulded into it. A thin layer of unvulcanised rubber bonding is applied to the back of the pre-cured tread.

After the application of the new tread rubber the tyre is ready for curing. The curing process involves the application of heat and pressure to the tyre.

The rubber which is applied to the tread area of the tyre is significantly stronger than that forming or applied to the casing: since casing rubber does not come into contact with the road, it does not wear as quickly as the tread area so that it is possible to retread a tyre more than once. There are limits to the depth of tread which can be applied to a worn casing. During use a tyre will build up heat which is directly related to the depth of tread: the greater the tread depth the greater the concentration of heat, with consequent increase in the prospect of failure of the casing.

The trial Judge accepted evidence that, although retreaded, the original tyre does not lose its identity and that it is easy, even for the inexperienced, to distinguish between a new tyre and a retreaded tyre.

In the case of passenger tyres the average weight of rubber applied in the retreading process is approximately 3 kilogrammes. For a truck tyre the weight is in the range of 10 to 12 kilogrammes.

The price of a retreaded truck tyre is approximately $200 cheaper than the price of a new tyre. The retail price of a new cheap radial tyre for a passenger vehicle ranges from $45 to $50. The same type of tyre when retreaded is sold as a retread in the retail price range of $26 to $35. There is also a market for second hand or used tyres which have not been retreaded.

Section 17 of the Act provides that sales tax shall be levied and paid upon the sale value of goods manufactured in Australia by a taxpayer and sold by him. Section 17a deems a transaction by which one person manufactures goods for another out of that other's materials to be a sale. The section provides that where goods are manufactured for a person, wholly or in part out of materials supplied by him, the manufacturer of the goods shall, for the purposes of the Act, be deemed to have sold the goods to the first mentioned person, at the time of their delivery to him, for the amount charged to him by the manufacturer in respect of those goods. Paragraph 18(1)(c) makes the amount so charged the sale value of the goods for the purposes of the Act, subject to certain immaterial exceptions.

The word "manufacture" is defined by sub-s. 3(1) to include (a) "production" and (b) the combination of parts or ingredients whereby an article or substance is formed which is commercially distinct from those parts or ingredients, subject to a qualification that is not in point.

The word "goods" is defined by sub-s. 3(1) of the Act to include commodities but not to include (a) goods which have, either through a process of retailing or otherwise, gone into use or consumption in Australia; or (b) goods which are sold as second hand goods and are manufactured exclusively or principally from goods which either have, whether alone or as parts of other goods, gone into use or consumption in Australia; and in the opinion of the Commissioner, in their condition as parts of the goods so manufactured, retained their character as goods or parts of goods which have gone into use or consumption in Australia.

A person is deemed to have sold goods if, in the performance of any contract (not being a contract for the sale of goods) under which he has received, or is entitled to receive, valuable consideration, he supplies goods the property in which (whether as goods or in some other form) passes, under the terms of the contract, to some other person: sub-s. 3(4). Hence, where any person for valuable consideration repairs the goods of another person and, in the course of making those repairs, supplies goods which are used in, brought into or attached to the goods of that other person, the goods so supplied are deemed to be sold by the repairer to that other person.

The Commissioner submitted first, that the process of retreading tyres constitutes "manufacture" within the meaning of that term in sub-s. 3(1) of the Act; and second, that the retreaded tyres are "goods" within the meaning of that sub-section.

It was common ground in argument before us that, if the process of retreading tyres does not answer the description of "manufacture" according to the ordinary meaning of that expression, then the appellant must fail in the appeal. It was also common ground that the result of this appeal does not turn on any differences between the processes of "top capping", "full capping", "remoulding" and "precuring".

It is to the question whether the operations of retreading carried on by the appellant constitute "manufacture" that I now turn. The definition of the word "manufacture" in the Act is not exhaustive. When the word "includes" is used in a definition section it is generally used to enlarge the meaning of the word it defines. So far as its meaning is not defined in the relevant statute it must be given its ordinary meaning.

The primary meaning of the word "manufacture" when used as a verb is to make something by hand; but since the industrial revolution the word has come to mean manufacture by machinery, often on a large scale and with a division of labour. This accords with the dictionary definition. As Windeyer J. said in Readymixed Concrete (W.A.) Pty. Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 71 ATC 4107 at p 4109:

"All that can perhaps be said of the word manufacture is that its derivative and etymological sense no longer determines its meaning. No longer is it restricted to the handiwork of individual craftsmen."

To manufacture an article necessarily involves producing a different article from the articles, materials or ingredients from which it was made. As Darling J. said in McNicol v. Pinch (1906) 2 KB 352 at p 361:

"I think the essence of making or of manufacturing is that what is made shall be a different thing from that out of which it is made". That passage was approved and applied by Dixon J. in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Jack Zinader Pty. Limited [1949] HCA 42; (1949) 78 CLR 336 - the leading case in this field. Whether the article which results from the process of manufacturing is a different article from the constituents or ingredients from which it was made is a question of fact: the Jack Zinader Case.

"Production", which is included in the definition of "manufacture" (para. 3(1)(a)), is a word of wide import; but it still involves the element of producing something different from the materials from which it was made. It is not possible to formulate precise definitions of such general terms as "manufacture" and "production"; but they do not bear a restricted meaning. Whatever answers the description of "manufacture" or "production" of goods according to accepted usage of the English language is within the Act.

Paragraph (b) of the definition of "manufacture", namely, the combination of parts or ingredients whereby an article is formed which is commercially distinct from those parts or ingredients, was introduced into the Act by Act No. 39 of 1932 following the judgment of the High Court in Irving v. Munroe & Sons Limited [1931] HCA 57; (1931) 46 CLR 279. In that case the defendant was a distributing agent in New South Wales and Queensland of a well-known English make of motor cycles. The motor cycles were imported by the defendant from England in cases each of which contained all the parts necessary to complete a motor cycle with the exception of tyres and tubes. When the goods arrived at the defendant's works in Sydney the parts were removed from the particular case and reassembled, usually by a man with the aid of a spanner. Tyres and tubes were purchased locally and fitted to the motor cycles which were then ready for sale. It was held by the High Court that there was no activity which could be described as manufacture.

In my opinion, para. (b) of the definition applies only where there is a mere combination of parts or ingredients. This conclusion follows from the legislative history of the definition and the language of para. (b) itself, especially since the word "whereby" in para. (b) relates back to and is governed by the previous word "combination" in the whole expression "the combination of parts or ingredients whereby an article or substance is formed . . . " This was the conclusion reached by the Full Court of the Supreme Court of Victoria in Cohns Industries Pty. Limited v. Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1979) 24 A.L.R. 658; but the point was not central to the case and their Honours did not give reasons for the conclusion. See also Adams v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1948) 8 A.T.D. 332 per Williams J. at p. 335.

When considering the question whether particular goods are manufactured for the purposes of the Act, it must be remembered that the general policy of sales tax legislation in this country is to levy sales tax once and only once upon the last sale of goods by wholesale (that is, upon the sale to the retailer by the last wholesaler); to avoid double taxation upon goods which have, through the process of retailing, gone into use or consumption in Australia and are in this sense second hand goods and outside the scope of the legislation. It is the evident purpose of the Act that the retail price of goods shall not be increased by the incorporation in it of more than one amount of sales tax: see Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation (S.A.) v. Ellis & Clark Limited [1934] HCA 54; (1934) 52 C.L.R. 85 and Adams v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra).

I do not find it helpful to refer to the numerous authorities to which we were taken in argument beyond stating the principles relevant to this case for which some of them are authority and which I have already mentioned. Some of the cases concerned sales tax legislation of the Commonwealth; others involved sections of the Income Tax (Assessment) Act 1936 concerning investment allowances (eg. s. 62AA) - which raise similar, though not the same, questions. They all turn on their particular facts and present a wide range of businesses as diverse as restoring ineffective batteries (Adams v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra)); repairing electrical goods (Deputy Commissioner of Taxation S.A. v. Ellis & Clark Limited (supra)); cooking fish and chips (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Rochester [1934] HCA 17; (1934) 50 C.L.R. 225); making aerated waters (Cohns Industries Pty. Limited v. Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra)); taking and supplying photographs (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Riley [1935] HCA 47; (1935) 53 C.L.R. 69); making aggregate for use in the construction industry (Readymixed Concrete W.A. Pty. Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra)); making split peas (Case B56 1970 70 A.T.C. 267); cutting granite and freestone (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Stronach [1936] HCA 29; (1936) 55 C.L.R. 305); remodelling old fur garments (Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Jack Zinader Pty. Limited (supra)); processing scrap metal (M.P. Metals Pty. Limited v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1968] HCA 89; (1967) 117 C.L.R. 631); reporting judicial proceedings and producing transcripts (Adams v. Rau [1931] HCA 43; (1931) 46 C.L.R. 572). Retreading tyres may now be added to the list in Australia. It has already been considered by courts in the United States of America and Canada: Skinner v. U.S. (1934) R F.Supp. 999; Zook v. Perkins (1948) 195 P.R. 2d 962; State Ex. Rel. A.M.F. Inc. v. Spradling (1974) 518 S.W. 2d 58 (the U.S. cases); Biltrite Tyre Company v. The King (1937) 1 D.T.C. 360; The King v. Boultbee Limited (1938) 1 D.T.C. 443; Crown Tyre Service Limited v. The Queen (1983) 83 D.T.C. 5426 (the Canadian cases). The United States cases support the view that retreading tyres is not manufacturing; but the Canadian cases generally treat it as manufacturing.

The Commissioner argued that the process of retreading commenced with a worn tyre that has normally suffered treadwear to such an extent that the grooved depth is reduced to the point where the tyre is either unsafe or illegal or both. The worn tyre is bought for a price which is determined, not with reference to the residual rubber, but merely by reference to factors of market supply. The cost is small and varies in the case of passenger tyres from 50[ to $8 and in the case of truck tyres from $2 to $60 or $70. Extruded rubber applied in the process of "recapping", "top capping" or "remoulding" varies in cost - depending on whether the tyre is a passenger car tyre or truck tyre - from $5 to $28 and in the case of remoulding it is still higher. In the case of "precured" strips which are used for retreading truck tyres the cost of the bandag and bonding material is about $45. In addition to the cost of raw materials it is necessary to add the cost of items such as power, labour and general factory overhead costs. In the result, the proportion of cost borne by the worn tyre to the market price for the retreaded tyre, although variable, is not large. Other arguments advanced by the Commissioner in respect of other matters included the following:

(a) the capacity of the respondent's factory enables it to produce retreaded tyres at the rate of about 40 tyres per hour. What is involved is "production" on a large and recurring scale, an activity which bears the general description and appearance of manufacturing;

(b) The retreaded tyre receives a new tread which comprises the whole portion of that part of the tyre which comes into contact with the road;

(c) The new tread normally has a different pattern to that borne by the original tyre; and

(d) A retreaded tyre has its own discrete market which is different from that of a new tyre and a worn out or used tyre and it has its own prescribed standards and performance characteristics.

It was submitted on behalf of the Commissioner that, in these circumstances, the operations of the respondent answer the description of "manufacture" according to both the ordinary meaning of the word and its extended meaning in sub-s. 3(1).

In my opinion the activities in which the respondent engages resulting in a retreaded tyre (using that expression to encompass the processes of "top capping", "full capping", "remoulding" and "precuring") are not accurately described as the manufacture of anything. What the appellant does in reality is to put a new tread on an old tyre. It is not any old tyre that can be used. It must be one that after inspection and testing can receive a new tread. The Commissioner's case is superficially advanced by the circumstance that there is a market for retreaded tyres. But in reality what the respondent does is to use an existing worn tyre as the basis of its operation. The old tyre to be used is examined and any breaks are repaired. A buffer is used to remove any old or excess rubber and to produce a clean and smooth surface. A rubber solution is generally applied and then the rubber is itself applied and the tread inserted (in "precured" tyres the tread has already been inserted). The rebuilt tyre is then cured. When the operation is complete no new product or article has been produced.

A tyre is manufactured with an inherent capacity for retreading. Indeed, Toyo Australia Limited, a manufacturer of truck tyres, specifically contemplates that a retread may be carried out on tyres manufactured by it because, included in the warranty which it gives against defects in workmanship or materials, there is a provision that such warranty subsists for a period of three years "from the date of manufacture or up to the buffing stage of the second recap, whichever comes first, provided that the recap has been carried out by one of Toyo's own retread factories".

The manufacturer knows that once a tyre is fitted it must meet and continue to meet the safety standards which are prescribed by law and that once the tyre becomes bald it can continue to be used on public roads only if it is retreaded. A new tread has no identity or existence apart from the tyre. The original tyre has not become a component or part of a new product; rather the tread rubber and other substances have become components or parts of the tyre. There has not been the manufacture of a new article but the repair or remodelling of an old one. Retreading is not manufacturing as that word is generally understood or as the extended statutory definition provides. The essence of manufacturing implies a change from which a new and different article must emerge having a distinctive character or use. This is not what happens with a retreaded tyre. The fact that the taxpayer's activities appear to be conducted on a fairly large scale does not affect my conclusions in this case. It is a relevant, though by no means a decisive, consideration.

The trial Judge correctly concluded that the retreaded tyres in this case did not result from a process of manufacture. It is not necessary, therefore, for me to consider whether the retreaded tyres answer the description of "goods" for the purposes of sub-s. 3(1) of the Act.

I would dismiss the appeal with costs.

This is an appeal from a judgment of a single judge of this Court (Beaumont J.) delivered on 28 September 1984. The respondent successfully sought a declaration that its operations, whereby used tyres are retreaded, did not constitute the manufacture of goods by it for the purposes of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 1) 1930. His Honour also declared that the retreaded tyres, resulting from the operations of the respondent, were not goods manufactured in Australia for the purposes of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 1) nor the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 3) 1930.

The respondent carries on business in a factory in a Sydney suburb where it retreads tyres for passenger vehicles and trucks. Its operations involve a variety of processes. These include topcapping, fullcapping, remoulding, pre-curing and curing. The essential distinction in the first three of these processes is the extent to which new rubber is applied to the casing.

In the process known as topcapping the new tread rubber is applied only to that area of the casing normally in contact with the road. In fullcapping new tread rubber is applied, not only to the area of the casing normally in contact with the road, but also to the shoulder area of the tyre. The process of remoulding involves the removal of rubber where necessary and the application of new rubber extending from bead to bead. The bead is that part of the tyre made of steel wires, wrapped or reinforced by ply cords, that is shaped to fit the rim.

The retreading process involves a number of stages; a preparatory stage, the application of the new rubber and the curing. Used tyres are inspected to ensure that they are suitable for retreading. If a tyre is suitable, the surface of the tyre is buffed. All excess rubber and foreign materials are removed and a smooth surface is created.

Tread rubber can be applied to a prepared tyre by means of an orbitread machine or by a pre-curing process. His Honour described the application of tread rubber and the processes of pre-curing and curing thus:-

"In the processes of recapping, topcapping or remoulding, an orbitread machine is used to apply extruded tread rubber to the casing. The rubber is extruded in the form of a continuous hot ribbon which is wound, in overlapping layers, around the circumferences of the casing. In the process of pre-curing, which the plaintiff uses only for retreading truck tyres, the tread rubber purchased by the plaintiff is already cured with the desired tread pattern moulded into it. A thick layer of unvulcanised rubber bonding is applied to the back of the pre-cured tread. After the application of the new tread rubber, the tyre is ready for curing. The curing processes vary, but it will suffice to say that they involve the application of heat and pressure to the tyre. . .

In the case of a passenger tyre, the average weight of rubber applied in the retreading process is approximately three kilogrammes. For a truck tyre, the weight is in the range of ten to twelve kilogrammes."

The respondent cannot apply its processes to all used tyres. Sometimes the casings will not be in sufficiently satisfactory condition to enable the retreaded tyres to be made safe. In some States there is legislation dealing with this matter. Thus the Motor Traffic Regulations (N.S.W.) 1935, (Schedule F, para. 82(d)) provide that on or after 1 January 1980, no tyre which has been treated by the process known as retreading shall be fitted to any motor car (an omnibus excepted) unless such tyre complies with the provisions of Australian Standard AS 1973 - Retreaded Pneumatic Passenger Car Tyres. The range of tyre referred to in the specification is that covered by AS D31, New Pneumatic Passenger Car Tyres, and corresponding older tyres. It provides, amongst other things, for the condition of casings which may be retreaded. The acceptable limits of damage to a casing vary depending on whether the tyre is a bias ply, bias-belted or radial ply tyre. Likewise the acceptable limits of damage due to buffing vary according to the original nature of the tyre.

The performance requirements of retreaded tyres, under the specification, also differ according to the type of tyre, not the process of retreading. The retreading process does not alter the important distinctions between bias ply, bias-belted and radial tyres.

A retreaded tyre is plainly distinguishable from a new tyre. His Honour accepted evidence:-

"that it is easy, even for the inexperienced, to distinguish between a new tyre and a retreaded tyre."

Furthermore, the specifications require that each retreaded tyre be marked with the word "retread".

Strangely, there are no legislative provisions prescribing the standard of truck tyres (and, apparently, aeroplane tyres) which may be used for retreading. The respondent nevertheless applies care in selecting casings which it uses. Very similar considerations apply in relation to truck tyres as apply in the case of motor car tyres.

The respondent both retreads tyres for customers and sells retreaded tyres on the market. In some cases, customers supply tyres to which the respondent applies its process for a fee. In other cases the respondent purchases used tyres, retreads them and sells them.

Depending on the degree of wear, it is possible to retread a tyre more than once. One truck tyre manufacturer, Toyo Australia, contemplates the application of the retreading process to its new tyres in the warranty it issues. Indeed, in certain circumstances the warranty for the new tyre may extend to cover the tyre after it has been retreaded. The warranty is stated in the following terms:-

"Toyo Australia Limited warrants the TOYO radial truck tyre casing against defects in workmanship or materials for a period of three years (36 months) from the date of manufacture or up to the buffing stage of the second recap, whichever comes first, provided that the recap has been carried out by one of TOYO'S own retread factories."

Section 17 of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 1) provides that sales tax shall be levied and paid "upon the sale value of goods manufactured in Australia" sold by a taxpayer or treated by him as stock for sale by retail or applied to his own use. By s. 17A, where goods are manufactured for a person wholly or in part out of materials supplied by him, the manufacturer of the goods, whether he manufactures those goods himself or procures their manufacture by another person, shall be deemed to have sold the goods to the person for whom the goods were manufactured. The presence of s. 17A means that there is no relevant distinction between cases where the respondent retreads tyres for other persons and retreads them and itself sells them on the market.

Sub-section 3(1) defines the words "manufacture", "manufactured", "manufactures" and "manufacturer". I do not find it relevant to refer to the meaning of "manufacturer". The words "manufactured" and "manufactures" have meanings corresponding to that of "manufacture". "Manufacture" is not defined exhaustively, but so far as it is necessary to the present case, it includes:-

"(a) production;

(b) the combination of parts or ingredients whereby an article or
substance is formed which is commercially distinct from those parts or ingredients, except such combination (not being a combination whereby concrete, cement mortar, lime mortar or any similar preparation of a kind used in the construction, repair or maintenance of buildings or other structures is formed, or whereby any other prescribed article or substance is formed) as, in the opinion of the Commissioner, it is customary or reasonably practicable for users or consumers of those articles or substances to undertake;"

The test to be applied in determining whether or not an article is manufactured is that formulated by Darling J. in McNicol v. Pinch (1906) 2 K.B. 352 where his Lordship said (p. 361), "The essence of making or of manufacturing is that what is made shall be a different thing from that out of which it is made". That test has been adopted by the High Court; see Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Jack Zinader Pty Limited [1949] HCA 42; (1949) 78 C.L.R. 336 per Dixon J. (as he was) at p. 343. I refer also to the judgment of Williams J. who said (p. 350):- "Work which could be fairly described as a mere repair or modification of the goods would not affect their original character. But once the work done causes the goods to lose this character, they become goods within the meaning of the Act."

The application of these tests may sometimes be a difficult exercise. The question is one of fact and degree. An exercise in judgment is involved. Having given this matter consideration, I have reached the conclusion that the process here is one, if not of repair, then of renovation. It is not manufacture. The essence of retreading is that, while the area that comes into contact with the road may be in need of renewal, the rest of the tyre remains sound and useful. A suitable worn tyre is rendered fit for use by the application of a new tread.

In the course of his submissions, counsel for the appellant drew our attention to the very cheap prices at which worn tyres could be obtained by the respondent and others retreading tyres and to the sophistication and complexity of the respondent's very extensive operations. They are conducted in a building called a factory and involve the use of much automated equipment. The production rate is very high.

Some support for the view that those considerations are relevant to be taken into account may be thought to be found in the judgment of Dixon J. in the Zinader case. After concluding that the process in question in that case produced a different article, his Honour added (p. 345):-

"When that consideration is added to the fact that the actual work done and the procedure employed in producing the new, that is the distinct, article is characteristically a manufacturing process, it must follow that the 'goods' are 'manufactured' within the ordinary meaning of that term."

I can understand that some may think that the process here is characteristically a manufacturing process. It has the hallmarks of it. But that looks only to the operations which are carried on and the machinery and other equipment which is used. It seems to me clear from the way the matter was approached by Dixon J. that the fact that a process may characteristically be a manufacturing process is not the determining factor. The critical question is always that earlier stated by his Honour when adopting what was said by Darling J. in McNicol v. Pinch. What is made must be a different thing from that out of which it is made; otherwise there is no manufacture.

I would add that the answer to the question of whether or not there is a manufacture ought not to depend on the nature and extent of a particular retreader's operation. There is little evidence on the point, but one would think that there are probably retreading organizations in the community whose operations are far less extensive and sophisticated than those of the respondent. Liability to tax ought not to depend on such distinction. I see no indication in the Act that the legislature thought that it should.

The cost or price of the tyres before the retreading operation takes place is a relevant factor to be taken into account. But it cannot be determinative. Shoes, which need resoling are no doubt worthless to their owner. Resoling them makes them useful again. But it does not turn them into a different article.

It remains to say that I find nothing in para. (b) of the definition of "manufacture" earlier quoted which assists the appellant's case. In my opinion the appeal should be dismissed with costs.

I am of the view that the appeal ought to be dismissed. I agree with the reasons of both Lockhart and Sheppard JJ.
