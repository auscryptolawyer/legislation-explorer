Pj Harper Superannuation Fund and Insurance and Superannuation Commissioner [1997] AATA 286 (13 August 1997)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

PJ HARPER SUPERANNUATION FUND v. INSURANCE AND SUPERANNUATION

COMMISSIONER

No. N96/544

AAT No. 12113

Number of pages -

8

Superannuation



COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

D. CHAPPELL (Deputy President), J. BARBER (Member) and C. PRIME (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - compliance with occupational superannuation standards - sole-member fund - member borrowed from fund while facing financial difficulties - breached restrictions on loans to members and in-house assets test - discretion to treat as complying - whether special circumstances relating to fund in relevant income year

Occupational Superannuation Standards Act 1987 ss 5(2)(b), 7(1), 3, 13(1)

Occupational Superannuation Standards Regulations 1987 regs 16(1)(a), 16A(1), 16A(2), 16A(17)(a), 16(1)(c)

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s 26AAB(14)

Case Z27 (1992) 92 ATC 255

Re Beadle and Director-General of Social Security (1984) 6 ALD 1

Case 33/94 (1994) 94 ATC 306

Case 17/94 (1994) 94 ATC 198

HEARING

SYDNEY, 22 May 1997 (hearing), 13 August 1997 (decision)

13:8:1997

Applicant's Representative: Mr Peter Harper

Solicitor for the Respondent: Mr Dieter Dambiec

ORDER

The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

DECISION

D. CHAPPELL, J. BARBER and C. PRIME

BACKGROUND

1. This was an appeal from a decision of the respondent dated 29 March 1996, affirming an earlier decision of 28 November 1995 that the applicant did not satisfy the superannuation fund conditions for the 1991-92 year of income. The applicant was represented by its sole member, Mr Peter Harper, who also gave evidence. The respondent was represented by Mr Dieter Dambiec from the litigation section of the Insurance and Superannuation Commission (ISC).

2. The Tribunal had before it documents (T documents and supplementary (S) documents) lodged under s 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. The following exhibits were entered into evidence on behalf of the applicant:

A1 Mr Harper's hand-written notes from the first Tribunal preliminary conference - dated 19/6/96 A2 Copy of diary entry of Mr Harper relating to second Tribunal conference - dated 27/8/96 A3 Documents relating to applicant and MacDougall Group - statements and figures regarding units in trust from 1991; fax dated 8/3/95 A4 Macquarie Bank statement titled "fully drawn advance" - dated 1/3/91 A5 Trust deed for applicant A6 Letter to Mr Harper from Chase AMP - dated 4/12/90 A7 Deed of loan from Permanent Custodians Limited to Mr Harper - dated 5/4/91 A8 Deed appointing new trustee of applicant - dated 18/1/90 A9 Deed of amendment of applicant - dated 18/1/90 A10 Extracted page from Amendment Trust Deed and Rules - dated 16/5/95 A11 Bundle of documents regarding tender for controlling interest in Markwell Pacific - dated 17/10/90 A12 Bundle of correspondence regarding Lothbury and loan, beginning with draft letter dated 25/5/90 A13 Statement of liquidated claim - dated 23/6/94 A14 Hand-written notes prepared by applicant's accountant regarding consequences of breach - undated

The following exhibit was entered into evidence on behalf of the respondent:

R1 Five ISC circulars - Annual returns for super funds (1990-91, 1989-90), 1989 Super funds - return forms and instructions, Circulars No. 5 (22/12/87) and 18 (10/90)

ISSUES AND LEGISLATION

3. According to the decision under review, the applicant did not comply with the superannuation fund conditions during 1991-92. These conditions are referred to in the Occupational Superannuation Standards Act 1987 (OSS Act) and the Occupational Superannuation Standards Regulations 1987 (the Regulations). Although the bulk of the OSS Act was repealed with effect from 1 July 1994, it still applies here because of the earlier relevant income year (1991-92): see Occupational Superannuation Standards Amendment Act 1993 s 16. Subsection 5(2) of the OSS Act refers to the conditions which superannuation funds must satisfy, including those specified in s 5(2)(b) as the standards prescribed by the Regulations. Regulations prescribing standards applicable to the operation of superannuation funds are authorised by s 7(1) of the OSS Act.

4. A number of these regulations are relevant to the present case. Regulation 16 states:

16(1) For the purposes of subsection 7(1) of the Act, the following standards are prescribed in relation to the investment of assets of superannuation funds and borrowing by trustees: (a) subject to subregulation (2), money of a superannuation fund shall not be lent to any of the fund's members, either directly or by means of arrangements being entered into for lending money to the members in the exercise of a general power of investment of assets of the fund...

5. Regulation 16A defines the "in-house asset" standards and limits the level of loans that the trustees of a superannuation fund may make to an employer-sponsor, or associate of the employer-sponsor:

16A(1) ... "in-house asset", in relation to a superannuation fund, means an asset of the fund that is a loan to, or an investment in, an employer sponsor, or an associate of an employer sponsor, of the fund... (17) ... the following standards are prescribed in relation to the investment of the assets of superannuation funds other than public sector funds: (a) the cost of the in-house assets of a fund established after 11 March 1985 is not to exceed at any time during a year of income 10% of the cost of all the assets of the fund...

"Employer sponsor" is defined in OSS Act s 3; "associate" of an employer sponsor is explained in regulation 16A(2) and defined in s 26AAB(14) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.

6. Section 13 of the OSS Act gives the Tribunal discretion, in the following terms, to treat the applicant as satisfying the superannuation fund conditions:

13(1) Where, in relation to a fund in relation to a year of income of the fund: (a) the trustees of the fund... have given the Commissioner the return and certificates referred to in that subsection but the Commissioner is not satisfied that the fund satisfied the superannuation fund conditions; and (b) the trustees of the fund satisfy the Commissioner that, because of special circumstances that existed in relation to the fund during the year of income, it would be reasonable for the fund to be treated as if it had satisfied the superannuation fund conditions... the Commissioner shall give notice in writing to the trustees of the fund stating that the Commissioner is satisfied that the fund should be treated as if it had satisfied the superannuation fund conditions in relation to the year of income.

7. The applicant claims that "special circumstances" exist which make it reasonable for the Tribunal to treat it as satisfying the superannuation fund conditions. The respondent contends that there are no special circumstances to explain the breaches arising under regulations 16(1)(a) and 16A(17)(a) for the 1991-92 income year.

8. The ISC's guidelines for the exercise of the "special circumstances discretion" are contained at T27. They do not have statutory force and are not binding on a primary decision-maker, but are a useful reminder of relevant considerations: see Case Z27 (1992) 92 ATC 255. Paragraph 5 contains a non-exhaustive list of relevant factors:

(i) the particular standard breached; (ii) the degree of breach of the standards; (iii) the effect of the breach on the long-term retirement interests of the members... (iv) whether the trustee knew of the breach and took steps to rectify the breach during the relevant year of income; (v) claims made that the breach will be rectified during the relevant year of income; (vi) claims made that incorrect advice from the ISC was relied on... (viii) special practical difficulties faced by the trustee in seeking to comply; (ix) impossibility of complying with the standards; (x) ignorance or misunderstanding of the nature or effect of the standards, providing that the ignorance or misunderstanding was reasonable in all the circumstances.

9. Paragraph 9 of the guidelines lists claims which, on their own, will generally not constitute "special circumstances":

(a) Claims relating to retrospectivity of legislation (b) Claims relating to Ignorance of the Law (c) Claims that incorrect advice was received from professional advisers (d) Financial Hardship (e) Claims made during the relevant year that the breach will be rectified if the discretion is exercised favourably. (f) Claims made that due to the complexity of the superannuation legislation it has not been possible to implement it. (g) Claims made that to comply with a standard will impose hardship, inconvenience to the fund members...

Paragraph 10 lists circumstances which - depending on all the other relevant facts - may result in a conclusion that special circumstances do exist:

(a) Incorrect advice by the ISC (b) Misunderstood Press Announcements (c) Delayed advice by ISC (d) Insignificant Breach (e) Impossibility of compliance (f) Where the fund is to be wound up (g) Cases where the particular circumstances of the fund take it out of the ordinary kind of funds so that the Legislature probably did not intend the legislation to apply (h) Cases where the breach was unintentional and committed due to a mistake

FACTS

10. The following facts were not in dispute, and arise mainly from the T and S Documents:

- The applicant fund was established on 17 December 1986 (exhibit A5). It is a one-member fund, set up solely for the benefit of Mr Harper and his dependents and beneficiaries (T22).

- In 1991-92, the applicant was supported by an employer-sponsor company, PJ Harper & Associates Pty Ltd. The company was also the applicant's trustee at this time (see exhibit A8). The two directors and shareholders of the company were Mr Harper and his wife (see T22).

- Mr Harper became guarantor for a loan of $3,870,000 by FAI Insurances Ltd (FAI) on 13 December 1988 (see T10 and T11). The two companies to which the loan was made (Pro-Export Australia Pty Ltd and Management Mutual Investment Corporation Ltd) encountered financial difficulties and FAI eventually served a notice of demand on Mr Harper on 8 October 1990 for repayment of principal and interest on the loan (see T11).

- During 1991-92, a verbal loan agreement between Mr Harper and the trustee of the applicant (PJ Harper & Associates) resulted in a loan to Mr Harper of amounts totalling $46,175.95 (T22). The loan was restructured so that, by the end of 1991-92, it was the Harper Family Trust that owed the amount to the applicant (see S1: 7).

- The applicant admitted to the ISC in question 4(a)C of its 1991-92 annual return, a breach of regulation 16(1)(a) and the loan to members standard (T25: 117).

- The applicant's accountant and auditor (Mr Ovenden) stated (T22) that the loan to Mr Harper and the Family Trust arose because:

As stated in the 1992 Annual Return, Mr Harper became personally liable under a guarantee given in respect of third party loans and it was a case of necessity to pay as much of the liability as soon as possible in order to avoid the forced sale of his family's home. Since no third parties are involved in the operations of the fund there has been no loss to other persons.

- In the same letter, Mr Ovenden wrote that the highest value of the applicant's in-house assets during the 1991-92 year was $46,971.95. The applicant's total assets in 1991-92 were $48,223 (see T25: 119). The 10% limit specified in regulation 16A(17)(a) would therefore be approximately $4,822, making the applicant's in-house assets approximately 96% of the total cost of all assets. This figure is obviously well above 10% and demonstrates an admission of failure to comply with the requirements of the "in-house asset" standard of regulation 16A(17).

- Mr Harper was released from his guarantee to FAI in December 1995 (T11: 72). The loan from the applicant to Mr Harper was outstanding as at March 1996 (T5), and interest on the loan (charged from 1993: T22) had yet to be paid.

EVIDENCE AND SUBMISSIONS

11. Mr Harper, on behalf of the applicant, argued that there were a number of factors which demonstrated the existence of "special circumstances" in his case and made it reasonable for the fund to be treated as if it had satisfied the relevant conditions:

(a) Mr Harper and the applicant's trustee were not aware of the legislative restrictions on loans being made from the fund to its members (T22: 91).

(b) Mr Harper fully intended to repay the debt "as soon as possible" (T22: 91).

(c) If Mr Harper did not repay FAI, his commercial standing and professional reputation would have been adversely affected. The loan from the applicant prevented his personal bankruptcy. Bankruptcy would have led to his disqualification from the Australian Stock Exchange and company directorships (T6).

(d) The difficulties of obtaining money from other sources meant that the fund was his last source of assets. The only alternative to the loan was selling the family home, losing his job and becoming bankrupt. During 1991-92 - when pressure from FAI to repay money was intense - the combined gross taxable income of Mr and Mrs Harper was $37,186. From this they had to pay living expenses and $69,000 in interest on the debts (S3: 40).

(e) Mr Harper submitted a statutory declaration to the effect that the debts and stress of averting personal bankruptcy placed a great strain on his health and family life (see T6). He was forced to seek extended medical treatment. Mr Ovenden wrote in a letter to the ISC on Mr Harper's behalf (T11: 71):

During the period from 8 October, 1990 it was stressed repeatedly to Mr Harper by Landerer & Co (the solicitors for FAI) that they looked to Mr Harper for repayment and that the only factor stopping them from instituting bankruptcy proceedings against him was his continued co-operation in attempting to reduce the debt. While this pressure was being maintained on Mr Harper (which occupied a considerable portion of his time) he was also required to maintain regular employment in order to support his family. It is difficult to see how these circumstances would not be regarded on any reasonable analysis as "unusual, uncommon, exceptional or abnormal".

(f) As the applicant is a sole-member fund, there has been no losses or other effects on third parties (T22). The purpose of superannuation is providing for retirement. Mr Harper argued that if he did not borrow from the applicant he would have lost his home - his principal retirement asset.

(g) The amount borrowed ($46,176) was small in comparison to the overall demands of the guarantee with FAI.

(h) If the applicant was treated as not complying, the subsequent tax difficulties would disadvantage Mr Harper, the member of the fund.

(i) Mr Harper also argued at the hearing that ISC staff had given him the impression that his situation constituted "special circumstances". The ISC had also not met his requests for detailed information on what constituted "special circumstances".

12. Mr Dambiec addressed the respondent's submissions to the various points raised by Mr Harper. He stressed that s 13 requires that "special circumstances" relate specifically to the applicant fund in 1991-92.

(a) The trustee of the fund has a duty to inform itself of the regulatory requirements; ignorance is not a special circumstance. Further, Mr Harper had signed a deed of amendment to the trust deed in 1990 which prohibited loans to members (see exhibit A9).

(b) The intention to repay is not relevant because it is not a circumstance that existed in relation to the applicant in 1991-92.

(c) Mr Harper's professional standing and reputation are personal to him and neither "special" nor in relation to the applicant. The guarantee he entered into with FAI was part of the ordinary circumstances of daily business, and came with an expected risk.

(d) The difficulty of obtaining money from other sources does not relate to the applicant. The money borrowed from the fund was relatively small in comparison to the debt and would not have staved off bankruptcy.

(e) Mr Harper did not demonstrate any nexus between his health problems and the loan made in 1991-92.

(f) Sole member funds are common, so the lack of effect on third parties does not constitute a "special" or "unusual" circumstance.

(g) The respondent agreed that the amount borrowed from the applicant was relatively small in comparison to the debt to FAI, but it is the degree of breach relative to the fund (96% - compared to the statutory limit of 10% - of total assets) that is significant. The breach of conditions has also persisted long after the 1991-92 year because there was no evidence before the Tribunal that the money had been repaid.

(h) Any financial disadvantages to fund members from non-compliance are consequential and therefore not "special". Taxation consequences only arise after the relevant year in any case.

(i) The ISC had supplied him with its guidelines on the exercise of the discretion (T27). It would have been difficult to be more specific than this document without hearing detailed facts of the actual case.

CONSIDERATION

13. There are a number of factors to consider when contemplating the exercise of the "special circumstances" discretion under s 13 of the OSS Act: see Case Z27, above at 258. First, the language of s 13(1)(b) makes it clear that the trustee has the obligation of satisfying the Tribunal that the conditions of s 13 are satisfied. Second, special circumstances must exist "in relation to the fund during the year of income" - circumstances that occur in years before or after are ineligible to be taken into account. Third, as well as demonstrating that special circumstances exist, the trustee must show that those circumstances make it reasonable to treat the fund as if it had satisfied the superannuation fund conditions. Finally, as to the meaning of "special circumstances", the Tribunal in Case Z27 quoted Toohey J in Re Beadle and Director-General of Social Security (1984) 6 ALD 1 at 3: "circumstances that are unusual, uncommon or exceptional".

14. Case Z27 was appealed to the Federal Court, but the Tribunal's reasons for decision were not distinguished or subjected to adverse comment, and were also cited by Purvis J in Case 33/94 (1994) 94 ATC 306 at 312. In Case 17/94 (1994) 94 ATC 198, Deputy President McMahon commented at 202:

Although it must take on its meaning from the context in which it is found, it is clear from judicial authority that special circumstances can only be used to describe circumstances that have a particular quality of unusualness, sufficient to take the matter out of the ordinary course.

15. In its 1991-92 annual return, the applicant admitted that its loan breached the fund conditions of regulation 16(1)(a). Mr Harper did not dispute the respondent's finding that the fund's level of in-house assets during 1991-92 were in breach of the test in regulation 16A(17)(a). The Tribunal finds that both regulations were breached by the applicant. The only issue remaining is whether Mr Harper has demonstrated for the applicant the existence of special circumstances making it reasonable for the Tribunal to exercise discretion under s 13 of the OSS Act.

16. Of the points raised by Mr Harper, ignorance of the Regulations cannot amount to a special circumstance: Case Z27 and Case 17/94, above. The trustee has a duty to inform itself of the requirements of the OSS Act and Regulations. Information about regulations 16 and 16A had been included in a number of ISC circulars (exhibit R1) which would have been sent to the applicant. When the 1991-92 annual return was completed, the applicant had enough knowledge of the legislative requirements to know that they had been breached (see T25).

17. Mr Harper's good intentions of repaying the debt are not special circumstances either because they are not in relation to the 1991-92 income year. In Case 33/94, Purvis J also held that capacity to rectify a breach did not amount to a special circumstance.

18. The next three factors raised by Mr Harper - (c) to (e) - relate to his personal circumstances: the strain on his health, his medical treatment, the potential loss of his professional standing and reputation. Mr Harper's financial situation in 1991-92 arose because of a guarantee made in 1988. The Tribunal accepts that this was a business transaction of which Mr Harper must be taken to have known - and accepted - the risks of having to fulfil his obligations as guarantor. His inability to find alternative funding and subsequent financial difficulties are not beyond the usual course - they arose out of ordinary business dealings and transactions. The potential loss of directorships relates only to Mr Harper and not the applicant. Nor did Mr Harper demonstrate that the personal stress caused by the guarantee amounted to a circumstance that related to the fund in 1991-92.

19. Being a sole-member fund does not imply any circumstance sufficiently unusual to constitute a "special" circumstance: see Case 17/94, above. Mr Harper's loan may not have affected any third parties, but the amount borrowed represented 96% of the applicant's total assets (compared to a statutory limit of 10%). The extent of the breach - and the length of time that the money is owing - are relevant factors in the exercise of discretion under s 13 (see paragraph 8, above). The breach here was significant, and there was no evidence that it had been repaid. Taxation consequences for the member of the fund arise outside the 1991-92 year, and are not relevant. Finally, the ISC guidelines on s 13 are a sufficient source of information for Mr Harper, and were part of the T Documents (T27). Mr Harper asserted that ISC staff had led him to believe that his case met the grounds of exemption. While incorrect advice by the ISC is a relevant factor (see T27 and paragraph 9, above), Mr Harper's assertions were of a general nature and do not provide any evidence to suggest the existence of a special circumstance in his case.

20. The Regulations specifically state that all investments of any of the fund's assets shall be made on an arms length basis (see regulation 16(1)(c)). Mr Harper was effectively the applicant's trustee. He appears to have regarded it as merely another source of funds. Consequently, the transactions between trustee and beneficiary were not at arms length.

DECISION

21. The applicant has not discharged its onus of proving that special circumstances existed in relation to the fund in 1991-92. In the view of the Tribunal, no special circumstances were shown which would make it reasonable to exercise the discretion conferred on it by s 13 of the OSS Act. The matters raised by Mr Harper either did not relate to the fund or the relevant year, or could not be said to be unusual, uncommon, exceptional or abnormal enough to qualify as "special". Accordingly, the decision under review is affirmed.
