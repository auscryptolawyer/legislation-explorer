ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Allowable deductions - Interest - Moneys borrowed to replace moneys lost - Interest paid in relation to period after business ceased not deductible

Income Tax Assessment Act (1936) (Cth) - Sections 17, 48, 51

F C of T v Herald and Weekly Times [1932] HCA 56; (1932) 48 CLR 113

Inglis v F C of T (1980) ATC 4,001

Income Tax - Allowable deductions - Interest - Moneys borrowed to finance purchase of non-income-producing investments - Interest not allowable as a deduction

REASONS FOR DECISION

26 June 1987 Mr Peter M Roach (Senior Member)

In this reference the Applicant (W) is a married man with three children. A carpenter by trade, for several years he has been employed by a public authority. He has represented himself before the Tribunal in these proceedings. In doing so it is quite clear that he does not have the understanding of matters of financial recording needed to clearly present the history of his financial transactions. Similarly he lacks such a comprehension of the relevant principles of taxation law as would enable him to recognize the issues and direct evidence to them. Despite that, after some confusion, it has been possible to establish fairly clearly all that seems to be relevant.

2. Having said that I have no hesitation in accepting him as an honest, but not invariably accurate, witness as to what occurred. In relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1984 he claimed a deduction for interest in the sum of $4,212 when he had intended to claim a deduction for $3,825. Inadvertently he had taken up a figure relevant to the previous year of income. As this matter only came to his notice during the hearing all claims he had previously made based on the figure of $4,212 will be hereafter referred to as if they had been related to $3,825. Secondly, his sworn testimony was that he alone was the owner of certain properties let to tenants and the only person to derive rental income from them. Other indications are that he was probably in error as to that and that those properties were probably held in partnership. Nothing turns on that.

  1. Over the years, as he has endeavoured to provide for the welfare of his family, he has sought to supplement his salary income and, in particular, to use his resources in such a way as to overcome the effects of inflation. One such endeavour caused him to invest in the letting of residential properties. Another was to invest in futures trading. A third was to acquire a miscellany of other investments.
  2. His investments in futures trading commenced during the year ended 30 June 1981 and concluded in March 1983. The result for this Applicant was quite a disaster. In all he suffered trading losses as follows - year ended 1981, $11,403; year ended 1982, $11,460; and year ended 1983, $9,019. The losses suffered in the years of income ended 30 June 1982 and 1983 were claimed as deductions and allowed. As no claim was made in relation to the first year, no claim was allowed. As between the taxpayer and the Commissioner, the taxpayer has only himself to blame for that and, as he has long since allowed the three year period for exercise by the Commissioner of a discretion to amend to reduce tax to pass, nothing can now be done about the matter. He accepts that, and that the responsibility for that situation is his.
  3. Before his trading in futures came to an end, the Applicant recognised that he had depleted his investment resources substantially and he arranged to borrow $30,000 on first mortgage from a building society on the security of his previously unencumbered residence. It is clear from the evidence of the Applicant that he considered the entirety of the borrowing to be occasioned by, as well as related to, the quantum of his futures trading losses. Insofar as he recognises that he had already borne the expense of some of those losses, he viewed the mortgage as being in part to "replenish" his investment fund. However, it is also to be noted that the taking up of the mortgage came at a time when he was also effecting modifications to his family residence - something he might have been able to effect without borrowing had it not been for his substantial losses.
  4. Having borrowed the monies on 12 May 1982 he deposited $30,000 to the credit of an interest-bearing building society account, thereby bringing the credit balance up to $36,564. By 27 July 1982 that figure had been augmented by an interest credit of $141.93. By the same date, by a series of drawings, all available funds had been drawn on that account and, for the most part, transferred to a trading account. What happened thereafter can best be identified under three headings:
    • (a) Futures-related Payments;
    • (b) Other Investments; and
    • (c) The Residue.

7. Futures-related Payments: I accept that trading in futures continued up until 17 March 1983 and I also accept that, in all, $8,222 was paid to futures-brokers in the period from the taking up of the loan to the close of futures trading. I also accept that to the extent to which trading in those periods generated a loss, that loss was something which the Applicant was entitled to have taken into account in the determination of his taxable income as, upon the evidence, it was. The Applicant acknowledges that trading in futures had ceased on 17 March 1983 but claims that, as he has had to borrow at interest to meet the aggregate loss suffered over the entire period and, alternatively, to meet the loss of $8,222 in the latter period, he is entitled to claim an income tax deduction in relation to the interest so paid. As to that, in my view, the Applicant is in error. The liability to pay interest after trading ceased was not related to the generation of income by futures trading.

8. Other Investments: I am satisfied that, from as early as August 1980, it has been the practice of the Applicant to purchase valuable coins (such as $200 gold "proof" coins and other proof sets), "speculative" shares and precious stones and I find that, following the raising of the mortgage, $6,972 was so expended. The items so purchased are still held. Some have fallen in value and, unfortunately for the Applicant, have fallen substantially in value. His evidence was that his endeavours in that regard were to offset inflation but that ultimately he "might realise the hoped for increase in value by sale, but it does not go beyond that. (He said that he hoped for dividends from the shares, but I am not persuaded that the holding of the particular shares was likely to generate dividend-income.) I am positively satisfied that he is not and has not been engaged in any business of trading in such items, whether by buying and selling or otherwise and that, if his dealings will ever be tax significant, it will be because of some provision of the Act other than section 25 of the Income Tax Assessment Act. For the rest, insofar as the claim to a deduction is, as it must be, based upon the provisions of section 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act, I am not persuaded that such interest liability as has been incurred and which might be said to be related to the cost of holding the items so purchased was incurred "in the course of deriving assessable income".

  1. The Residue; For the rest I am not persuaded by the evidence how the residue of the money borrowed was applied, although it seems quite probable that it might have been by way of private or domestic expenditure, either in the way of contributions to the costs of renovations or in meeting the recurrent private expenses of family life. That being so I am not persuaded that any deduction is allowable.
  2. Summary: Having regard to the confusing presentation of the Applicant's evidence, I sought to test the conclusions I have expressed above by approaching the matter in another way. The Income Tax Assessment Act provides for income tax to be levied on a year by year basis (section 17). "In calculating the taxable income of the taxpayer, the total assessable income derived by him during the year of income shall be taken as a basis, and from it there shall be deducted all allowable deductions" (section 48). For this taxpayer in the year of income ended 30 June 1984 assessable income comprised a salary of $26,783; and an interest in partnership income of $162. In consequence his taxable income was to be the sum of those figures ($26,954) less all allowable deductions. So far as is relevant in these proceedings, that calls for a consideration only of section 51 of the Act. That provides by sub-section (1):

"All losses outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income."

  1. Since, for the reasons I have already stated, the Applicant was not during the relevant period carrying on any business, it is only necessary to consider the first of the positive limbs of sub-section (1) which would make deductible "all losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income ...". If the Applicant had been able to point, for example, to the derivation of interest from a building society in the year of income ended 30 June 1984, it would have been possible for him to have shown that some moneys borrowed under the mortgage could have been traced into that account. But no such assessable income was derived in the year of income ended 30 June 1984. As it was the outgoing by way of mortgage interest was not shown to be related to either of the only two forms of assessable income derived in that year, namely, salary and partnership income.
  2. That does not necessarily put an end to the matter because, at least in relation to an ongoing business, expenditure which follows the derivation of assessable income may be deductible even though it falls into a subsequent year of income (F C of T v Herald and Weekly Times [1932] HCA 56; (1932) 48 CLR 113). But it must be incurred "in the course of" deriving assessable income. As the decision of the Full Bench of the Federal Court of Australia in Inglis v F C of T (1980 ATC 4,001) illustrates, interest payable on a farm mortgage ceases to be deductible when the business of farming ceases. That principle constitutes for this Applicant an insuperable obstacle in relation to the claim related to futures losses. In relation to investments in coins and other precious commodities and the application of the residue there is no present or future derivation of assessable income to be pointed to. At most there is a possibility that, in the event of some future sale, it may be that it would be held that the "profit" on sale (a figure derived after taking into account all holding charges, including interest) will then be held to constitute assessable income.

13. As a final argument, the Applicant relied on section 51(2) of the Act which provides:-

"Expenditure incurred or deemed to have been incurred in the purchase of stock used by the taxpayer as training stock shall be deemed not to be an outgoing of capital or of a capital nature".

Section 6 provides:

(1) In this Act, unless the contrary intention appears -

"trading stock" includes anything produced, manufactured, acquired or purchased for purposes of manufacture, sale or exchange, and also includes livestock."

In my view, no portion of the moneys invested or lost or borrowed to replace moneys lost constituted "trading stock". However, not only did that money not constitute "trading stock", but to the extent to which investment funds - the so-called "trading stock" - had been lost in trading, deductions had already been claimed and to that extent allowed. Section 52(2) simply does not deal with the question of deductibility of interest, whether that interest be the cost of holding "trading stock" or otherwise.

14. I affirm the determination of the Commissioner upon the objection.
