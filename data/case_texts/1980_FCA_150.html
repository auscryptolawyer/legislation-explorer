Re Magna Alloys & Research Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1980] FCA 150; (1980) 49 FLR 183 (2 December 1980)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: MAGNA ALLOYS & RESEARCH PTY. LTD.

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF AUSTRALIA (1980) 49 FLR

183 No. G107-9 of 1978

Income Tax

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

Brennan(1) Deane(2) and Fisher(2) JJ.

CATCHWORDS

Income Tax - Deductibility of outgoings - Legal expenses in defence of directors and agents charged with criminal offences - Payments by corporation - "necessarily incurred in carrying on a business" - Criteria of deductibility - Characterization of outgoings - Taxpayer's purpose - Objective purpose - Relevance of motive - Deductibility of legal costs of criminal proceedings - Outgoings of a private or capital nature.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth.),s.51(1).

Income Tax - Allowable deductions - Legal expenses of taxpayer's directors and agents in defending criminal charges - Relation between charges and taxpayer's business activities - Payment of expenses by taxpayer - Whether capital or private expenditure - Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth), s. 51(1). Various criminal proceedings were brought against three of the taxpayer's directors, four of its agents, one of its associate companies and, for a time, the taxpayer itself. The charges related to the payment of secret commissions in the course of the taxpayer's business. The taxpayer expended some $280,000 in defending all the defendants, some of whom (not including the taxpayer) were convicted. The taxpayer sought a deduction for this amount under s. 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. There was evidence that the principal motive or purpose for the expenditure was to defend the three directors. The Commissioner disallowed the taxpayer's claim, the taxpayer appealed to the Supreme Court of New South Wales, and Sheppard J. disallowed its appeal. The taxpayer appealed.

Held, appeal allowed, that the taxpayer's motive in incurring the expenditure was not the critical criterion for deductibility under s. 51(1). The test in the present case was whether the expenditure was apt to serve the business purposes of the taxpayer. The conduct which was the subject of the charges involved the taxpayer's way of carrying on business.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Snowden & Willson Pty. Ltd. [1958] HCA 23; (1958), 99 CLR 431, applied.

The expenditure was not of a capital or private nature, therefore it was deductible.

DECISION

Three of the appellant's directors were charged, tried on indictment, and convicted on a charge of conspiracy in Victoria. Four of its agents were also charged and tried, and three were convicted on the same charge. The directors were fined and the agents were released on bonds: none was imprisoned. Two of the directors were charged under the Secret Commissions Prohibition Act 1919 in New South Wales, but the charges were not prosecuted. The charges all related to the marketing methods adopted to sell the appellant's products. The facts surrounding the prosecutions are stated in my brothers' judgment and, having had the advantage of reading that statement, I adopt it. The directors resolved that the appellant should pay all the defendants' legal costs. They were paid in the respective income years ended 30 June 1972, 1973 and 1974, and these appeals relate to the deductibility under s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 of the amounts so paid.

The appeals are brought from judgments of the Supreme Court of New South Wales. Sheppard J., then sitting in that Court, heard extensive evidence and focussed his attention upon "one substantial evidentiary issue" which he defined in this way:

"That is the question of what it was that really decided the taxpayer to incur the expenditure in question. Was it, as counsel for the taxpayer submitted, necessarily incurred in carrying on its business for the purpose of gaining or producing its assessable income. Or was the situation rather one in which the three directors, faced personally with serious criminal charges and the possibility of imprisonment, caused the expenditure to be incurred by the taxpayer for their own personal needs and those of the agents who were also charged."

His Honour found:

"I am unpersuaded that I should accept that the dominant, or even a principal reason, why the expenditure was incurred was the salvation of the company's business rather than the personal needs and requirements of the three directors. I have restricted that last statement to the needs of the three directors rather than all the accused because it seems to me that it was the personal need of the directors that the agents be defended; otherwise they may have pleaded guilty and given evidence against the directors."

and later his Honour said:

"The circumstances were such that there was no fund available to them to meet the costs except moneys in the control of the taxpayer. The moneys were consequently expended for this purpose. That is not to say that the three directors were not very much concerned about the future of the companies in the group. But the essential thing, whilst the charges were pending, was for them to look after themselves. . . . The problem arose and the directors without examining the matter in the detail which the affidavit suggest simply made a decision to pay the costs. The evidence, accordingly, does not persuade me, as I have said, that it was incurred for the purposes of the taxpayer, except in a most indirect way."

As I construe these findings, his Honour rejected the claim under the second limb of s.51(1) to deduct the appellant's expenditure on costs because the dominant and principal reason why the directors incurred the expenditure was to satisfy the personal need of the directors. His Honour was of the view that that finding denied to the expenditure the character of expenditure incurred in carrying on its business, although "in a most indirect way" the expenditure was incurred for the purposes of the taxpayer's business. The question thus raised by his Honour's judgment is whether the reason why the taxpayer incurred the expenditure determines whether the expenditure was incurred for the purposes of the taxpayer's business and thereby concludes the issue of its deductibility. Motive and purpose are not novel or alien concepts in ascertaining deductibility under s.51(1), though their meaning is not always clear, and "purpose" is susceptible of ambiguity. As Lord Wright said in Crofter Hand Woven Harris Tweed Co. v. Veitch (1942) A.C.435 at p.469 "(t)he words 'motive', 'object', 'purpose' are in application to practical matters difficult strictly to define or distinguish."

It will nevertheless aid an examination of principle if a meaning be given to these terms in the context of a discussion of s.51, not in definition of statutory terms but in identification of concepts to which reference has frequently been made in applying the section. Motive means (to adapt terms which his Honour used) the reason why a taxpayer decides to incur the expenditure. Purpose may be either a subjective purpose - the taxpayer's purpose - where it means the object which the taxpayer intends to achieve by incurring the expenditure; or it may be an objective purpose, meaning the object which the incurring of the expenditure is apt to achieve. Both motive and subjective purpose are states of mind and they are to be distinguished from objective purpose, which is an attribute of a transaction. An objective purpose is attributed to a transaction by reference to all the known circumstances; whereas subjective purpose and motive, being states of mind, are susceptible of proof not by inference alone but also by direct evidence, for a state of mind may be proved by the testimony of him whose state of mind is relevant to a fact in issue.

Though references to motive and purpose are to be found in cases arising under s.51, neither motive nor either kind of purpose is a criterion of deductibility. The statutory criteria are expressed in the two limbs of s.51: "incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income", and "necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income." The purpose mentioned in the second limb is not a purpose imported by the phrase "incurred in carrying on", but the purpose of the business in the carrying on of which the deductible expenditure is incurred (John Fairfax & Sons Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1959) 101 C.L.R.30 at p.49).

The first limb of s.51 has a longer statutory history than the second limb. The first was to be found in s.23(1)(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922, and the second was added in the 1936 consolidation. The leading exposition of the meaning of the two limbs is the judgment of the High Court in Ronpibon Tin N.L. and Tongkah Compound N.L. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1949) 78 C.L.R.47. As to the first limb, the Court said (at pp.56,57):

"For expenditure to form an allowable deduction as an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income it must be incidental and relevant to that end. The words 'incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income' mean in the course of gaining or producing such income. Their operation has been explained in cases decided under the provisions of the previous enactments: see particularly Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay's) Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation ((1935) 54 C.L.R.295 at pp. 303-304, 307, 309, 310) and W Nevill & Co. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation ((1937) 56 C.L.R., at pp.300,301,305-306,308).

Notwithstanding the differences in other respects in the present provision, the expression 'incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income' has been left unchanged and bears the same meaning. In brief substance, to come within the initial part of the sub-section it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income or, if none be produced, would be expected to produce assessable income."

A view was then tentatively expressed (and subsequently confirmed: Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Snowden & Willson Pty. Ltd. (1958) 99 C.L.R.431 at p.436) as to the difference in scope of the two limbs (at p.56):

"No doubt the expression 'in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing' lays down a test that is different from that implied by the words 'in gaining or producing.' But these latter words have a very wide operation and will cover almost all the ground occupied by the alternative. The words 'such income' mean 'income of that description or kind' and perhaps they should be understood to refer not to the assessable income of the accounting period but to assessable income generally. If they were so interpreted, they would cover a case where the business had not yet produced or had failed to produce assessable income and the alternative would then itself suffice to authorize the deduction of a loss made in a distinct business."

The requirement in the second limb that expenditure be incurred in carrying on a business parallels the requirement in the first limb that the expenditure be incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income. In A.G.C. (Advances) Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1975) 132 C.L.R.175, Mason J. said (at p.198):

"Thus in the Ronpibon Case ((1949) 78 C.L.R., at p.57) the Court stated that 'to come within the initial part of the subsection it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income'. So also it may be said that it is enough to satisfy the second part of the subsection that the occasion of the loss or outgoing is to be found in the carrying on of a business for the production of assessable income."

Whether the assessable income against which expenditure is sought to be deducted is produced in the carrying on of a business or in some undertaking which does not constitute the carrying on of a business, the same kinds of factors are material to deductibility.

In Ronpibon Tin, supra, the Court did not explain either limb in terms of motive or purpose. The phrases "in the course of", "incidental and relevant", and "the occasion of" appear. These phrases, though not synonyms for the statutory terms, import a connection between the incurring of expenditure on the one hand and the gaining or production of assessable income or the carrying on of a business on the other. In Lunney v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1958) 100 C.L.R. 478, Williams, Kitto and Taylor JJ said (at p.497) that the words "incidental and relevant" stated "an attribute without which an item of expenditure cannot be regarded as deductible under the section", and their Honours said that the cited phrases were "intended as a reference, not necessarily to the purpose for which an item of expenditure has been incurred, but, rather, to the essential character of the expenditure itself."

The emphasis placed upon the objectivity of the test of deductibility has run through the history of s.51, and particularly through the history of its first limb, though the importance placed in particular cases upon evidence of a taxpayer's state of mind has sometimes given the appearance of a statement of principle to what is no more than an observation upon evidentiary material.

The relevant history of the first limb commenced with its enactment in s.23(1)(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922 which provided:

"In calculating the taxable income of a taxpayer the total assessable income derived by the taxpayer from all sources in Australia shall be taken as a basis, and from it there shall be deducted -

(a) all losses and outgoings (not being in the nature of losses and outgoings of capital) including commission, discount, travelling expenses, interest and expenses actually incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income;"

The operation of s.23(1)(a) was modified, however, by s.25(e):

"A deduction shall not, in any case, be made in respect of any of the following matters:-

. . . . .

(e) money not wholly and exclusively laid out or expended for the production of assessable income."

To qualify for deduction under these provisions, it was necessary that expenditure should fall within s.23(1)(a) and outside s.25(e) (Herald & Weekly Times Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1932] HCA 56; (1932) 48 C.L.R. 113 at p.119). In Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 C.L.R. 295 at p.309, in a passage to which Dixon J. referred in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Finn (1961) 106 C.L.R.60 at p.66, his Honour compared the criteria expressed by s.23(1)(a) and s.25(e):

"The expression 'in gaining or producing' has the force of 'in the course of gaining or producing' and looks rather to the scope of the operations or activities and the relevance thereto of the expenditure than to purpose in itself. Purpose in itself may be the criterion expressed by the word 'for' which occurs in the correlative prohibition contained in sec.25(e)."

The distinction thus drawn between the criteria was one of emphasis. His Honour did not hold purpose to be irrelevant in determining under s.23(1)(a) whether expenditure was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income. But in cases decided under the 1922 Act, it was unnecessary to mention expressly that the circumstances required a reference to purpose in order to determine whether expenditure was incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, as purpose was necessarily referred to in determining the application of s.25(e). The relevance of purpose to both sections appears in the judgment of Gavan Duffy C.J. and Dixon J. in Herald & Weekly Times, supra at p.118, where their Honours took the critical prepositions from each section, and distinguished the relevant purpose from the taxpayer's immediate reason for making the payment:

"The question whether money is expended in and for the production of assessable income cannot be determined by considering only the immediate reason for making a payment and ignoring the purpose with which the liability was incurred." (emphasis added)

But when s.51 was introduced in 1936, "the principle expressed by the former s.25(e) (was) abandoned" (Ronpibon Tin, supra at p.55), and it became necessary to consider, according to the circumstances of each case, whether and to what extent reference to purpose is required or appropriate to determine deductibility under the first limb of s.51(1). Clearly enough, purpose in itself could not be the test, for the section confers deductibility upon losses involuntarily incurred where the loss is not attributable to the conduct of the taxpayer and the taxpayer is concerned to avoid incurring the loss which nevertheless befalls him, as in Charles Moore & Co. (W.A.) Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1956) 95 C.L.R.344 where the taxpayer was robbed of his takings and a deduction was allowed. In that case the Court said (at p.351): "Phrases like the foregoing or the phrase 'incidental and relevant' when used in relation to the allowability of losses as deductions do not refer to the frequency, expectedness of likelihood of their occurrence or the antecedent risk of their being incurred, but to their nature or character. What matters is their connection with the operations which more directly gain or produce the assessable income."

Though purpose is not the test of deductibility nor even a conception relevant to a loss involuntarily incurred, in cases where a connection between an outgoing and the taxpayer's undertaking or business is effected by the voluntary act of the taxpayer, the purpose of incurring that expenditure may constitute an element of its essential character, stamping it as expenditure of a business or income-earning kind.

Indeed, under a New Zealand provision similar to s.51, Lord Donovan and Viscount Dilhorne thought purpose in the objective sense to be the test of whether expenditure is "exclusively incurred in the production of assessable income" (I.R.C. v. Europa Oil (N.Z.) Ltd. (1971) A.C. 760, the first Europa case, at p.779). But their Lordships were in the minority and the judgment of the majority insisted that in cases of that kind - expenditure incurred in purchasing trading stock - the test of deductibility required "a rigorous and objective examination of the contractual arrangements under which the expenditure is made" in order to ascertain whether the taxpayer has "acquired some advantage, not identifiable as, or related to the production of, assessable income" (ibid., p.772).

The circumstances of the first Europa case required no reference to purpose in ascertaining the character of expenditure beyond the reference to the interlocking contractual arrangements made by Europa. In this and the later Europa case, expenditure was made pursuant to a contract for the purchase of trading stock, and reference to the legal rights thereby acquired sufficiently fixed the character of the expenditure. As Lord Diplock said, speaking for the majority in Europa Oil v. I.R.C. (1976) 1 W.L.R.464 - the second Europa case - at pp.471,472:

"Their Lordships. . . content themselves with emphasising that it is not the economic results sought to be obtained by making the expenditure that is determinative of whether the expenditure is deductible or not; it is the legal rights enforceable by the taxpayer that he acquired in return for making it."

Though the principle may be too widely stated to be applied to s.51(1) in every case (per Gibbs A.C.J. in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. South Australian Battery Makers Pty.Ltd. (1978) 140 C.L.R.645 at p.660), it is susceptible of application where the activities and operations by which assessable income is gained or produced or by which a business is carried on are not reasonably open to dispute, and where the relevant expenditure is incurred solely in acquiring an asset or a legal right under a contract or in discharging an antecedent legal liability. In cases of that kind, of which Cecil Bros. Pty.Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1964) 111 C.L.R.430 is an example, the connection between the asset or legal right acquired or the legal liability discharged on the one hand and the gaining or production of assessable income or the carrying on of a business on the other appears from "a rigorous and objective examination". But there are cases where expenditure is not incurred solely to acquire an asset or legal right under a contract or to discharge a legal obligation, and there are cases where there is dispute as to the scope and nature of activities and operations by which assessable income is or is intended to be gained or produced, or by which a business is carried on. In cases of these kinds, the Europa approach does not give adequate guidance to a court in finding whether the expenditure is deductible under s.51(1).

In the infinite variety of transactions to which the application of s.51(1) may be in question, there are many in which there are no contractual arrangements and many in which contractual arrangements are but part of the background in which the character of expenditure falls for consideration. As Gibbs A.C.J. observed in South Australian Battery Makers, supra at p.659:

"The words of the judgments in the Europa Cases, like those of any judgmennt, may be understood in the light of the issues which fell to be decided. Their Lordships could not have meant to suggest that in every case the character of an outgoing must be determined by having regard only to the contractual return for it. That would indeed have been inconsistent with the principle stated by Dixon J. in Hallstroms' Case ((1946) 72 C.L.R.634), and with cases too numerous to mention in which payments made 'voluntarily and on the grounds of commercial expendiency' (to use the words of Viscount Cave L.C. in British Insulated and Helsby Cables Ltd. v. Atherton ((1926) A.C.205, at p.212) have been held deductible as outgoings of a revenue kind although the taxpayer obtained no legally enforceable rights in return for them."

Characterization of expenditure by a contractual quid pro quo requires an analysis of the consideration passing to the taxpayer under the contract, though analysis restricted to the consideration passing to the taxpayer may prove misleading (cf. Cliffs International Inc. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1979) 79 ATC 4059). Where an advantage other than or beyond a contractual quid pro quo can be identified as an advantage for the taxpayer which the relevant expenditure is calculated to effect, an analysis of that advantage gives character to the expenditure (South Australian Battery Makers, supra at pp.659, 660, 662). It is necessary to ascertain in each case what expenditure is for, because a "bare payment of money is itself devoid of character", as Stephen J. said in Cliffs International Inc., supra at p.4071. When the question is whether expenditure has the character of capital of of a revenue payment, as in the two cases last cited, the advantage for which the expenditure was incurred must be identified and the manner in which it "is to be relied upon or enjoyed" must be considered (Sun Newspapers Ltd. and Associated Newspapers Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1938) 61 C.L.R.337 at p.363). The role of the advantage in the incomeearning undertaking requires examination. Similarly, when the question is whether expenditure is incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, the connection between the advantage for the taxpayer which the incurring of the relevant expenditure is calculated to effect and the taxpayer's income-earning undertaking or business must be considered. If the advantage can be sufficiently identified by reference to a contract, and the taxpayer's undertaking is known, the connection between the incurring of the expenditure and the undertaking is manifest, and it would be otiose to refer to the purpose of incurring the relevant expenditure. But purpose is relevant to describe an element of connection between expenditure and a taxpayer's undertaking or business in cases where a taxpayer incurs expenditure or agrees to incur expenditure without any antecedent obligation to do so or where the occasion of the expenditure (unlike the purchase of trading stock) is not manifestly to be found in whatever is productive of assessable income or in whatever would be expected to produce assessable income, or in the carrying on of a business.

In cases under the 1922 Act where payments were made to retiring directors or employees, purpose was critical to deductibility which depended upon the fulfilment of the condition expressed in s.23(1)(a) as well as upon the non-application of s.25(e). In W. Nevill & Co. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1937) 56 C.L.R.290, a payment was made in order to obtain the resignation of one of the taxpayer's two joint managing directors. Latham C.J. held that the expenditure fell within s.23(1)(a) and observed (at pp.300, 301): "The facts stated in this case show that the expenditure was actually incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income of the year in question. The expenditure was made for the purpose of increasing the efficiency of the company and therefore increasing its income producing capacity. It was not a capital expenditure, it was, in my opinion, an expenditure incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable income . . ."

When s.25(e) of the 1922 Act required a test of purpose to be applied, the test was objective. In Robert G. Nall Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1937) 57 C.L.R.695, Dixon J. applied a test of purpose which may be taken to give the meaning of purpose in the objective sense (at pp.711, 712):

"But, in matters of income tax, purpose is an elusive and indefinite criterion. The purpose of a payment when a deduction is claimed for it becomes an attribute of the transaction rather than a state of mind in some actual person.

. . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . .

. . . When it is said that gaining or producing assessable income must be the purpose of the expenditure if its deduction is to be allowed, no more can be meant than that the circumstances of the transaction must give it the complexion of money laid out in furtherance of a purpose of gaining income."

When purpose is used in this sense, it means little more than that the incurring of expenditure in the circumstances in which it is incurred is conducive to the gaining or producing of assessable income or to the carrying on of a business by the taxpayer. But in ascertaining whether this connection exists the character of the taxpayer's undertaking or business must be known; and in order to say what relationship to the undertaking or business is borne by the incurring of expenditure, it is necessary to identify what the expenditure is for. Thus, in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. The Midland Railway Co. of Western Australia Ltd. [1952] HCA 5; (1952) 85 C.L.R. 306, Dixon J., referring to a number of considerations which bore upon the deductibility of the payments there in question, said (at p.313):

"The second consideration is that what governs the issue is the business purposes for which the outgoing was incurred from the point of view of the taxpayer company. The controlling factors are those which arise from the character of the business or undertaking and the relation which the expenditure or the liability to make it bore to the carrying on of the business or the gaining of assessable income."

Once the "controlling factors" are ascertained, the business purposes for which an outgoing is incurred may be determined. In that step towards characterization, the taxpayer's state of mind has no part to play. His purpose or motive is not a controlling factor of the purpose to be attributed to the incurring of the expenditure. But in ascertaining the controlling factors, the taxpayer's state of mind may have a significant evidentiary role to play, and by leading to the ascertainment of the controlling factors, it may even be the determinative element in characterizing expenditure in a particular case. Nevertheless, the taxpayer's state of mind - whether intention, or purpose, or motive - is evidentiary only.

What the taxpayer has in mind may bear upon "the character of the business or undertaking" in showing the scope of his business or undertaking. The taxpayer is at liberty to determine for himself what the scope and nature of his business or undertaking shall be and how it shall be conducted, the Act having no effect upon those matters but taking "the result of the taxpayer's activities as it finds them" (per Williams J. in Tweddle v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1942] HCA 40; (1942) 7 ATD 186 at p.190). Fullagar J., referring to the operation of "necessarily" in the second limb of s.51(1), said in Snowden & Willson Pty. Ltd., supra (99 C.L.R. at p.444):

"It means for practical purposes that, within the limits of reasonable human conduct, the man who is carrying on the business must be the judge of what is 'necessary'."

And thus what the taxpayer says are the character and scope of his business or undertaking is evidence of what its character and scope are in fact.

Similarly, the taxpayer's state of mind may be relevant evidence to show what expenditure is for in cases where the Europa approach does not give adequate guidance. The principal class of cases of this kind comprise those where payments have been made to directors or employees of a taxpayer company on or by reason of their retirement. In Robert G. Nall Ltd., supra, Latham C.J. said at p.705:

". . . in a case where natural persons are concerned, the object which they naturally have in their minds may properly be taken into consideration in determining whether a particular expenditure is made for the production of assessable income. If, for example, (to take a simple case) B was an employee of A working for a salary and A made a gift to him which was not connected in any way with the business and which was shown to be made for purely personal reasons, those facts would prevent the view being taken that the expenditure was made for the purpose of producing assessable income."

In Maryborough Newspaper Co. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1929) 43 C.L.R.450, a newspaper editor and director of the taxpayer was paid a retiring allowance to facilitate his retirement in order to obtain the advantage of new and younger talent. Rich J. held the payment deductible, referring to the considerations which made the payment desirable from the business viewpoint, and to the motives of the directors (at p.454):

"In my opinion they were not actuated, in giving the allowance, by any other motive than by a desire to better the business of the Company, and thus earn more profits."

and at p.455:

"No doubt these considerations were not expressly canvassed by the directors, but they are instinctively felt by business men in such situations. At any rate I am satisfied that no other purpose than the production of more income prompted the agreement to pay the retiring allowance."

In Union Trustee Co. of Australia Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1935) 53 C.L.R.263, Dixon J. (at pp.270, 271) noted a test formulated by Rich J. in Maryborough Newspaper Co., supra, and said:

"Tried by that test, the claim to bring the payment within the deduction allowed as a result of secs. 23(1)(a) and 25(e) must fail. There is nothing to show that it was not a purely voluntary grant to Lecky made by Black out of proper feelings of gratitude for long service of a confidential nature. There is nothing to establish a connection between the payment and the production of income."

In Finn, supra, Dixon C.J. implied that a taxpayer's motive or purpose may be material when, in evaluating the conclusions which might be considered to govern the character of the expenditure there in question, he used the phrase (at p.67) "so far as motive or purpose is material". And in cases not falling under s.51, where the issue was the income character of a gift in the donee's hands, the donor's motive was regarded as relevant evidence, though the characterization test is objective (see Hayes v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1956) 96 C.L.R.47 at pp.55, 56).

However, the evidentiary effect of a taxpayer's state of mind is to be distinguished from the objective purpose of expenditure incurred. The distinction between the two is illustrated by the judgment of McTiernan J. in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Robinson & Mitchell Pty. Ltd. (1941) 64 C.L.R.612, another case of a retirement allowance, where his Honour found (at p.617):

"The only purpose indicated by these facts for which the sum of 660 pounds was paid, was to provide an allowance for its managing director upon his withdrawal from its management, in recognition of his services to the company.

The motive which the directors had in agreeing to the payment of the allowance cannot affect the purpose or the tendency of the payment. The evidence of their motive merely shows why they voted, perhaps reluctantly, for the payment of a retiring allowance of a larger amount than they thought was warranted by the financial position of the company."

In W. Nevill & Co. Ltd., supra, the taxpayer company agreed to pay a retirement allowance to one of two joint managing directors in order to procure his retirement, and thereby to save his future salary and to increase the efficiency of the taxpayer's business. The objects which the directors had in mind in incurring the expenditure threw light on the question of what the expenditure was for, and from that finding it was an easy step to show the connection of the expenditure with the operations by which the taxpayer gained its assessable income. Latham C.J. said at p.301:

"No expenditure, strictly and narrowly considered, in itself actually gains or produces income. It is an outgoing, not an incoming. Its character can be determined only in relation to the object which the person making the expenditure has in view. If the actual object is the conduct of the business on a profitable basis with that due regard to economy which is essential in any wellconducted business, then the expenditure (if not a capital expenditure) is an expenditure incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income."

His Honour's remarks are to be construed, of course, in the light of the circumstances to which they are directed. Though his Honour's judgment is open to a different construction, I do not take his reference to "the object which the person making the expenditure has in view" to be a test for the application of s.23(1)(a) or s.25(e) of the 1922 Act, or to be asserting that a taxpayer's object is more than relevant evidence in ascertaining the objective purpose to be attributed to the incurring of the expenditure. Indeed some few months after publishing this judgment, Latham C.J., in Robert G. Nall Ltd., supra, appears to have denied that a taxpayer's purpose in making expenditure was the test of purpose under s.25(e). Immediately following the passage from his judgment earlier cited, his Honour said (57 C.L.R., at p.705):

"The provision in question is directed to the relation between the expenditure of the taxpayer and the income produced in the course of the income-earning enterprise of the taxpayer. The section contemplates a test which may be applied objectively and independently of subjective states of mind, even though there may be room for differences of opinion in applying any objective criterion and though the application even of an objective criterion may be affected in the case of natural persons, who are capable of having states of mind, by consideration of those states of mind."

No doubt his Honour's observations were affected by a view that a corporation is incapable of forming a state of mind. It is not material to canvass that view here, but in the light of his Honour's observations in Robert G. Nall Ltd., supra, I do not read what he said in W. Nevill & Co. Ltd., supra, as saying more than that the taxpayer's state of mind is evidentiary of what expenditure is for and of the character and scope of the taxpayer's business or undertaking. Dixon J., in Robert G. Nall Ltd. referred to purpose in a context which shows that he was using the term in its objective sense. He said (at p.305) that under s.23(1)(a) of the 1922 Act expenditure

"must have been incurred in the course of gaining or producing the assessable income. It does not require that the purpose of the expenditure shall be the gaining or production of the income of that year. The condition the provision expresses is satisfied if the expenditure was made in the given year or accounting period and is incidental and relevant to the operations or activities regularly carried on for the production of income."

Given a sufficient identification of what the expenditure is for and the character and scope of the taxpayer's income-earning undertaking or business, the question whether expenditure is incurred for the purpose of carrying on a business or for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income does not depend upon the taxpayer's state of mind. The relationship between what the expenditure is for and the taxpayer's undertaking or business determines objectively the purpose of the expenditure. In cases to which a reference to purpose is required or appropriate, objective purpose will be found to be an element in determining whether expenditure is incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or in carrying on business. If the purpose of incurring expenditure is not the gaining or producing of assessable income or the carrying on of a business, the expenditure cannot be said to be "incidental and relevant" to gaining or producing assessable income or carrying on business; or to be incurred "in the course of gaining or producing" assessable income or of carrying on a business; nor can the undertaking or business be seen to be "the occasion of" the expenditure.

In the present case, the character and scope of the taxpayer's business is known without reference to its state of mind. Equally, it is objectively certain that the relevant expenditure was incurred to defray the legal costs of the directors and agents in the criminal proceedings brought against them. The connection between the legal services thus acquired and the taxpayer's business neither requires nor permits reference to the taxpayer's state of mind. The nature of that connection is to be found in the objective facts found by his Honour or not in dispute between the parties.

The conduct of the directors and employees which was impugned by the laying and prosecuting of the charges was conduct by which a part of the appellant's business was carried on. Allegations reflecting on the integrity of those conducting the appellant's business and their conduct in carrying on that business reflected on the appellant and its reputation. True enough, the allegations carried the risk of conviction and punishment only for those accused of offences. Apart from a time during the trial when the taxpayer was joined as a defendant on the indictment, the allegations did not carry the same risk to it, but the allegations nevertheless attacked its business methods. The interests of the taxpayer and of the accused were inseparable. The expenditure furnished a defence for both the accused and the appellant. If the learned trial judge had found that the reason why the expenditure on costs was incurred was the defence of the appellant's trading reputation, in my view the case would not have been distinguishable from Snowden & Willson Pty. Ltd., supra. In that case, expenditure was incurred to defray the cost of advertising and the professional fees of counsel and solicitors in representing the company and its officers before a Royal Commission, as well as the fees of valuers, a surveyor and accountants. Thus the expenditure in that case furnished a defence against the allegations there made not only for the company but also for the directors.

Dixon C.J., after referring to the instinctive desire to defend a trading reputation, adopted an objective approach to the deductibility of the expenditure under the second limb of s.51(1). He said (99 C.L.R., at pp.436, 437):

"The case does not appear so far as the element now under consideration goes to depend upon case law or upon anything but an understanding of what in fact and according to the ordinary conduct of affairs is incidental to the conduct of a business. The word 'necessarily' does, however, seem to me to require consideration. Clearly its operation is to place a qualification upon the degree of connexion between the expenditure and the carrying on of the business which might suffice in the absence of such a qualification. In The Commonwealth and The Post-Master-General v. Progress Advertising & Press Agency Co. Pty. Ltd. ((1910) 10 C.L.R.457) Higgins J. supplied an interpretation of 'necessary' as not meaning essentially necessary but as meaning appropriate, plainly adapted to the needs of a department carrying out an Act ((1910) 10 C.L.R., at p.469). That was in another connexion but the phrase was availed of by the Court in the Ronpibon Tin Case ((1949) 78 C.L.R., at p.56) as throwing light on the use of the word 'necessarily' in s.51(1). Clearly the expression is used in relation to business. Logical necessity is not a thing to be predicated of business expenditure. What is meant by the qualification is that the expenditure must be dictated by the business ends to which it is directed, those ends forming part of or being truly incidental to the business.

In the present case it appears to me that the taxpayer company could do nothing else but defend itself, if it was to sustain its business and continue carrying it on in anything like the same volume or according to the same plan. That seems to me to be enough."

Fullagar and Taylor JJ. were of the same opinion (see pp.444, 447, 448). In that case, it was assumed that the directors would instinctively wish to defend the company against attacks upon its business methods and reputation. But it makes no difference that, in the present case, the learned trial judge was unpersuaded that that was "the dominant, or even a principal reason, why the expenditure was incurred". The reason why the directors incurred the expenditure does not define the scope or nature of the taxpayer's business, nor does it reveal what the expenditure was for beyond assisting to show that what was sought and obtained was legal representation to defend the directors and employees. The directors' reasons do not show or tend to show that legal representation to defend the directors and employees was not connected with the carrying on of the business. Quite the contrary. The ends of the business required that an attempt be made to defend the impugned conduct, and the attempt was made. The necessary connection between the expenditure and the carrying on of the business thus appears.

It may be noted that Sheppard J. was at pains in his judgment to show that the interests of the companies and of the directors and agents who were charged were interwoven, though it was the protection of the personal interests of the directors and agents which provided the principal motive or reason for Magna's incurring of the expenditure.

In the circumstances of the present case, the expenditure bears the character of expenditure necessarily incurred in the carrying on of Magna's business. This conclusion is a conclusion of fact, but it does no violence to the findings of fact made by the learned trial judge, nor does it set aside any inference actually drawn by him. The character of the expenditure is not lost because the expenditure was apt to serve both the business purpose and the purpose of defending the directors, nor is that character lost because the principal or dominant reason for incurring the expenditure was to defend the directors.

It is said, however, that expenditure in connection with breaches of the law is in a separate category from other expenditure incurred in carrying on a business, and that the weight of judicial authority denies a deduction to a taxpayer who incurs expenditure with respect to a breach of the criminal law. The learned trial judge so held, finding that what was said by Rowlatt J. in Inland Revenue Commissioners v. Warnes & Co. (1919) 2 K.B. 444 and by the Court of Appeal in Inland Revenue Commissioners v. von Glehn (1920) 2 K.B.553, had application to the facts of the present case. It was submitted that these decisions were expressly approved by the High Court in Herald & Weekly Times, supra, and in Snowden & Willson Pty. Ltd., supra.

In the High Court, the majority of the judgments confines the operation of the English cases to the payment of penalties imposed as a punishment upon the taxpayer. And here, of course, there is no claim to deduct moneys paid as a penalty. In Herald & Weekly Times, supra, Gavan Duffy C.J. and Dixon J. said with reference to the English cases (at p.120):

"The penalty is imposed as a punishment of the offender considered as a responsible person owing obedience to the law. Its nature severs it from the expenses of trading. It is inflicted on the offender as a personal deterrent, and it is not incurred by him in his character of trader."

The cases were not cited in the other judgments. In Snowden & Willson Pty. Ltd., supra, the same principle was expressed by Dixon C.J. (at p.437):

"There is no analogy here to cases in which fines or penalties are incurred. There the character of the expenditure and the reasons why the law imposes a fine or penalty separate the expenditure from the conduct of the business. It is not to the point that the conduct penalised found its motive in business considerations. Nothing of the kind can be said of the expenditure now under consideration nor is any principle of public policy affected by allowing the deduction."

Fullagar J. (at p.446), with whom Williams J. agreed, was content to cite the passage in Herald & Weekly Times cited above, and Taylor J. alone referred to costs as well as penalties in his judgment (at p.452):

"The final observation which I wish to make is concerned with the argument that, as the expenditure was incurred in an endeavour to rebut the charges of fraud which were implicit in the allegations made by Mr. Oldfield, the company is not entitled to the deduction claimed. It was sought to support this argument by reference to cases concerned with legal costs and penalties incurred in criminal proceedings. It is, I think, sufficient to say that there is no analogy between the two classes of cases and, accordingly, that there is no substance in this submission. (See Inland Revenue Commissioners v. Warnes & Co. ((1919) 2 K.B.444); Commissioners of Inland Revenue v. von Glehn & Co. Ltd. ((1920) 2 K.B. 553); Minister of Finance v. Smith ((1927) A.C.193, at pp.197, 198)."

This passage furnishes slender support for the view that the principle with respect to the non-deductibility of criminal penalties extends to the costs incurred in defending the proceedings in which the penalty is imposed. It is worthy of note that, although there were legal costs in issue in Warnes & Co., supra (being costs ordered to be paid to a prosecutor) and in von Glehn, supra (costs of the defence) the non-deductibility of the costs in both cases was regarded by Wrottesley J. in Spofforth and Prince v. Golder (H.M. Inspector of Taxes) (1945) 26 T.C.310 at p.314, as turning not upon the principle with respect to deduction of penalties but upon an application of a wider principle expressed by Lord Davey in Strong v. Woodifield (1906) A.C. 448. The particular test which Wrottesley J. drew from Lord Davey's speech was "for the purpose of earning the profits". But this is not the test under the second limb of s.51(1) (John Fairfax & Sons, supra, per Menzies J. at p.50), and accordingly the English cases provide no authority to determine the deductibility of legal costs under the second limb of s.51(1). Snowden & Willson Pty. Ltd., supra, was decided under s.51(1), and it was held in that case that expenditure on costs calculated to effect the repelling of a public attack upon the taxpayer's methods of carrying on its business was deductible. In the present case, the expenditure was calculated to achieve the same results and also to vindicate the appellant's business methods other than those impugned by the prosecution. There is no reason why the deductibility of expenditure on costs in the present case should be affected by the circumstance that the attacks upon the appellant and its reputation led up to and were pursued in a criminal prosecution.

The respondent further relied upon the exceptions to s.51(1) submitting that the expenditure was of a private or of a capital nature. As Menzies J. said in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Hatchett (1971) 125 C.L.R.494 at p.498:

"It must be a rare case where an outgoing incurred in gaining assessable income is also an outgoing of a private nature. In most cases the categories would seem to be exclusive."

What his Honour said with respect to outgoings incurred in gaining assessable income may be said of outgoings necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income. Expenditure falling under the second limb of s.51(1) is incidental to the carrying on of a business and it must be a rare case where the same expenditure can at once possess that character and be an outgoing of a private nature. This is not such a case.

Nor was the expenditure an outgoing of a capital nature. The capital of the business was in no way increased by the expenditure incurred. True it is that the expenditure protected the reputation and goodwill of Manga's business, but the attack which was made arose out of the day to day selling activities of that business and it was the business purpose of vindicating the methods by which it was conducted that brings the expenditure within s.51(1). Though goodwill is a capital asset of a business it is frequently earned and maintained by the daily activities of those engaged in the business. The valuable if intangible asset of goodwill frequently grows out of activities the cost of which is a charge on revenue account (see Sun Newspapers, supra at pp. 360, 361). Expenditure incurred in attempting to vindicate the business methods of the taxpayer, overcoming the obstacle to its trading which had been raised by the prosecutions is properly to be regarded as a cost on revenue account.

I would allow the appeals. The judgments in the Supreme Court in respect of the income years ended 30 June 1972 and 30 June 1973 should be set aside and in lieu thereof it should be ordered that the appellant's assessable income for those years be reduced by the amounts held deductible in this appeal. The judgment of the Supreme Court in respect of the income year ended 30 June 1974 should be varied by ordering that in addition to the amount held deductible by the Supreme Court the amount held deductible in this appeal be deducted from the appellant's assessable income for that year. The appellant should have its costs both here and in the Supreme Court.

The appellant taxpayer, Magna Alloys & Research Pty Ltd(the taxpayer), claims a deduction from its assessable income for the years of income ended 30 June 1972, 1973 and 1974, of amounts expended by it for legal costs in respect of charges of conspiracy brought in Victoria against the taxpayer three directors and four of its agents, and in respect of a number of charges brought under the Secret Commissions Prohibition Act 1919 (NSW) in New South Wales against two of the three directors.

The taxpayer is the main trading company of a group of five companies. The leading figure in the establishment of the group was Mr Leon Donald Richardson who continues to be a substantial shareholder in the taxpayer. He was a director of each company from the time of its foundation until 9 July 1973 when he resigned. The other directors were Mr David James Bedford and Mr Thomas Edward Dundon.

The companies, which commenced their businesses in 1965, served the maintenance area of industry. They sold a number of different products, including welding electrodes, welding aides and the like, and provided readily available maintenance service equipment of a good standard for businesses using sophisticated machinery. The companies sought to convince industry that a quick repair to sophisticated machinery, even if the repair was expensive, was far more efficient and economical than the replacement of a broken part which involved the particular machinery being out of action for a longer period of time, with consequent financial loss.

The companies operated through independent agents. The agents were not employed but, for purposes of efficiency and uniformity, were closely controlled by training in the selling technique which each was to use. Uniformity in marketing was achieved by a technique, described as "robot-like", whereby the agent read material directly out of sales manuals with which the agent was furnished by the companies. The technique was designed to ensure that no overstatement of the properties and capabilities of the companies' products was made.

The companies prospered. The name "Magna" remained, however, relatively unestablished in the industrial maintenance field. A number of different advertising activities and public relations programmes were implemented. In late 1965, the group introduced an additional programme of promotion and advertising which was known as the M3 programme. In 1967, it was followed by the M4 programme.

Under both the M3 and the M4 programmes, the taxpayer promised to give and gave various gifts to customers who purchased certain quantities of its products. The sales manual, which was to be shown and read to the customers, made clear that the gift was intended for the company or organization which was the taxpayer's customer, and not for the particular employee with whom the agent was dealing.

The main difference between the M3 and M4 programmes was that the M4 programme introduced a "points" system. The customer, on purchasing a certain quantity of the taxpayer's products, was awarded points which could be accumulated and later redeemed for gifts. In addition to publishing a catalogue of its own products and their prices, the taxpayer published another standard catalogue which displayed the available gifts and contained and index setting out the number of points which had to be accumulated and redeemed in order to receive a particular gift. There was in evidence a document described as the Magna advertising programme and which was in fact the M4 programme. On the second page is printed: "This programme is designed only for your company" (meaning Magna's customer) "to use in motivating its employees - these awards are not to be taken home for personal use. Unless used this way, this programme does your company no good, nor does it do our company any good". The M4 programme was, however, implemented in a manner which did not accord with this statement of basic principle. The agents, in selling goods, offered the points under the M4 programme to the employees themselves and not to the companies which were the actual purchasers of the goods. The prosecution case, as it developed, was that not only were the three directors, Richardson, Dundon and Bedford, aware of this practice, but that they had designed the programme to be used in that way.

In 1970, The Age newspaper in Melbourne published a series of articles, alleging that the companies' agents were offering secret commissions to employees of existing and potential customers. It was said that the employees, including employees of government departments and public authorities, were induced to purchase goods in return for points to be credited to them personally, resulting in their receipt of various gifts or prizes pursuant to the advertising programme. According to Dundon, this campaign by The Age had a substantial effect upon the companies' businesses and caused a loss of some customers. Despite that effect, the businesses continued to operate profitably and sales figures continued to increase.

The allegations in The Age were followed by an investigation by the authorities in Victoria. After some 18 months, charges were successively brought against four agents and the three directors of the company. At the end of the committal proceedings, all were committed for trial before the County Court. Indictments were presented to the County Court alleging that the seven persons charged had been parties to a criminal conspiracy between themselves and with Cygnus Metallurgical Co Pty Ltd (another company in the group) and the taxpayer. The trial in the County Court extended over some 73 days. The names of the two companies remained as alleged co-conspirators until the close of the defence case when they were deleted from the indictment. Three of the four agents and the three directors were found guilty of criminal conspiracy. The jury was discharged without returning a verdict in relation to the fourth agent who had become ill during the trial. The three directors were each fined $2000. The three agents who had been found guilty were released on good behaviour bonds.

A considerable part of the case for the defence in the criminal proceedings was directed to showing that the taxpayer's products were of a high standard and that any illegal selling methods were unnecessary to the success of the taxpayer's business. According to Dundon, the manner in which the defence case was conducted helped restore customer confidence. The avoidance of any custodial sentences was seen by the directors as being plainly in the interests of the taxpayer's reputation and business efficiency and success.

The charges under the Secret Commissions Prohibition Act 1919 (NSW) related to activities on behalf of the taxpayer in New South Wales. On or about 19 June 1972 and 20 March 1973, summonses were issued respectively against Dundon and Richardson out of the Court of Petty Sessions at Sydney alleging breaches of ss 3 and 8 of that Act. The offences, the subject of those summonses, were alleged to have taken place between 19 June 1970 and 13 October 1970. After having been adjourned on numerous occasions, these summonses were withdrawn on 11 December 1973.

The taxpayer paid all the legal costs in relation to the charges brought against the accused. Specifically, these amounts consisted of the sum of $80,046 incurred in the year of income ended 30 June 1972, being the amount paid for the legal representation of the accused in the committal proceedings which were heard in Melbourne; the sums of $204,291 and $625 incurred in the year of income ended 30 June 1973, being respectively, the amount paid by the taxpayer for the costs of legal representation of the persons charged at the trial of charges of conspiracy which took place in the County Court of Victoria in Melbourne, and the amount incurred by way of legal costs in respect of the charges brought under the Secret Commission Prohibition Act 1919 (NSW) in New South Wales against Dundon and Richardson; and the sum of $800 incurred in the year of income ended 30 June 1974, being the amount paid in respect of representation of one of the three directors (Richardson) who had been arrested in Sydney and whom it was sought to extradite to Victoria.

The taxpayer sought a deduction in respect of each of these items of expenditure in the determination of its taxable income for the relevant years. The Commissioner disallowed such deduction and the taxpayer appealed to the Supreme Court of New South Wales. The Supreme Court(Sheppard J)dismissed the appeal in respect of all those amounts (Magna Alloys & Research Pty Ltd v FC of T (1978) 22 ALR 377; 9 ATR 188). From this judgment the present appeals were brought by the taxpayer. Before Sheppard J, there was also in dispute a further claimed deduction in respect of the tax year ended 30 June 1974. The Commissioner has not appealed against his Honour's decision in the taxpayer's favour as regards that further deduction.

The taxpayer sought to justify the claimed deductions on the ground that they were outgoings within s51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (the Act) which, so far as material, provides that all losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gainging or producing such income shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital or of a capital, private or domestic nature. The Commissioner disputed that the outgoings were either incurred in gaining or producing assessable income or were necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income. Alternatively, it was submitted on behalf of the Commissioner that the outgoings were, in any event, of a capital, private or domestic nature. Finally, the Commissioner submitted that the outgoings were not deductible by reason of their character as payment of costs incurred in the defence of criminal proceedings.

The taxpayer placed primary reliance on what we shall refer to as "the second limb" of s51(1), namely, that part of the subsection which refers to losses and outgoings necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purposes of gaining or producing assessable income. The words "for the purpose of gaining or producing such income" qualify "carrying on a business": see eg, Ronpibon Tin NL v FC of T; Tongkah Compound NL v FC of T [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at 56; [1949] HCA 15; 4 AITR 236 at 245; FC of T v Snowden & Willson Pty Ltd [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431 at 435-6, 444; 7 AITR 308 at 311, 317. It is plain that the business which the taxpayer carried on was, in the relevant sense, carried on for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income. Indeed, the Commissioner did not dispute that this was so. What was in dispute between the parties on this aspect of the case was whether the outgoings were necessarily incurred in carrying on that business.

The requirement that the claimed outgoing be "necessarily" incurred in carrying on the relevant business does not, in the context, mean that the outgoing must be either "unavoidable" or "essentially necessary". Nor does the word "necessarily" import a requisite of logical necessity. What is required is that the relevant expenditure be appropriate and adapted for the ends of the business carried on for the purpose of earning assessable income: see, Ronpibon Tin NL v FC of T; Tongkah Compound NL v FC of T, supra, 78 CLR at 55-56; 4 AITR at 245; FC of T v Snowden & Willson Pty Ltd, supra, 99 CLR at 444 and 447; 7 AITR at 317 and 320. For practical purposes and within the limits of reasonable human conduct, it is for the man who is carrying on the business to be the judge of what outgoings are necessarily to be incurred: FC of T v Snowden & Willson Pty Ltd, supra, at 444; 317. It is no part of the function of the Act or of those who administer it to dictate to taxpayers in what business they shall engage or how to run their business provitably or economically. "The Act must operate upon the result of a taxpayer's activities as it finds them": per Williams J, Tweddle v FC of T [1942] HCA 40; (1942) 7 ATD 186 at 190; [1942] HCA 40; 2 AITR 360 at 364; see also, Ronpibon Tin NL v FC of T; Tongkah Compound NL v FC of T, supra, 78 CLR at 56-7; 4 AITR 245; Cecil Bros Pty Ltd v FC of T [1964] HCA 82; (1962-1964) 111 CLR 430 at 434, 441; 8 AITR 523 at 525-6; 9 AITR 246 at 249; IR Comr (NZ) v Europa Oil (NZ) Ltd (No 1)(1971) AC 760 at 772; 1 ATR 737 at 750; FC of T v South Australian Battery Makers Pty Ltd [1978] HCA 32; (1978) 140 CLR 645 at 653-4; [1978] HCA 32; 21 ALR 59 at 65; [1978] HCA 32; 8 ATR 879 at 883.

The issues involved in the present appeal require the definition with some precision of the relevance of the subjective purpose or motive of a taxpayer in incurring an outgoing to the determination of the question whether the outgoing was, for the purposes of s 51(1), necessarily incurred in carrying on the taxpayer's business. For the taxpayer, it was argued that subjective purpose is irrelevant to the determination of that question and that whether an outgoing was necessarily so incurred falls to be determined by reference only to the objective nature or character of the outgoing and its connection with operations which are carried on for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income. The taxpayer called in aid, in support of this approach, statements in a number of authorities. In particular, reliance was placed upon what was said in the joint judgment of Williams, Kitto and Taylor JJ in Lunney v FC of T; Hayley v FC of T (1958) 100 CLR 478 at 498; 7 AITR 166 at 179, where, in a case involving expenditure upon fares between a taxpayer's home and his place of work, the view was expressed that whether expenditure is incurred in or in the course of gaining or producing assessable income depends upon considerations which are concerned with "the essential character of the expenditure itself" in a context where that concept had been formulated by their Honours by way of contrast to "the purpose for which an item of expenditure has been incurred" (ibid, at 497; 178).

It is clear from the authorities that there are circumstances in which the subjective purpose or motive of the taxpayer will be of little assistance in determining whether an outgoing was necessarily incurred in carrying on a business. Where, for example, the outgoing was incurred as a consequence of overriding contractual obligations attaching to particular subsequent actions (see, for example, Cliffs International v FC of T [1979] HCA 8; (1979) 24 ALR 57; 79 ATC 4059; [1979] HCA 8; 9 ATR 507) or where the outgoing is largely involuntary in the sense that it was not optional or discretionary (see, for example, Charles Moore & Co (WA) Pty Ltd v FC of T [1956] HCA 77; (1956) 95 CLR 344 at 351; [1956] HCA 77; 6 AITR 379 at 385 and Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay's) Ltd v FC of T(1935) [1935] HCA 81; 54 CLR 295 at 309), the nature of the outgoing will be prone to be determined by reference to objective considerations. The subjective purpose or motive of a taxpayer either in incurring an outgoing or in engaging in an activity which attracts liability to make the outgoing is not however irrelevant to the question whether the outgoing was necessarily incurred in carrying on the relevant business. In a case such as the present where the taxpayer is a company, the relevant purpose is the corporate purpose which may or may not correspond with the individual subjective purpose or motive of one or more of those comprising the relevant corporate organ or acting as agent on the taxpayer's behalf.

There are some statements in the authorities which support the view that, in a case where the outgoing is voluntary in the sense that it was volitionally incurred in the pursuit of a particular advantage or object, the taxpayer's subjective purpose or motive or assessment of appropriateness to the ends of the business is of critical importance to the decision whether the outgoing was necessarily incurred in carrying on the relevant business. Thus, for example, in W Nevill & Co Ltd v FC of T [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 CLR 290 at 301; [1937] HCA 9; 1 AITR 67 at 72, Latham CJ, in referring to the requirement in s23(1)(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1922, that a loss or outgoing be "actually incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income", stated that the character of an outgoing: ". . . can be determined only in relation to the object which the person making the expenditure has in view. If the actual object is the conduct of the business on a profitable basis with that due regard to economy which is essential in any well-conducted business, then the expenditure (if not a capital expenditure) is an expenditure incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income." In the same case (ibid at 308;77), McTiernan J referred to "the result aimed at by" the taxpayer. A similar emphasis on the taxpayer's purpose or motive or assessment of appropriateness is implicit in the many statements in the authorities to the effect that it is for the man carrying on the business to be the judge of what outgoings are necessarily incurred. Again, in FC of T v South Australian Battery Makers Pty Ltd [1978] HCA 32; (1978) 140 CLR 645 at 655;[1978] HCA 32; 21 ALR 59 at 65;[1978] HCA 32; 8 ATR 879 at 884, Gibbs ACJ (with whom Stephen and Aickin JJ agreed) mentioned the need to "decide what was the advantage sought by the taxpayer by making" the relevant payments and referred (ibid, at 660; 887) to the decisions in the two Europa Oil cases (1971) AC 760; 1 ATR 737 and (1976) 1 WLR 464; 5 ATR 744) as supporting the conclusion that "it is the advantage which the expenditure was intended to gain, directly or indirectly, for the taxpayer that is relevant in determining the character of the expenditure. . .":see also per Jacobs J, ibid, at 667-8; 893.

Where an outgoing which was not involuntary has actually achieved the purpose for which it was incurred or where the connection between an outgoing and the relevant business is direct and obvious, there will ordinarily be little practical point in distinguishing between characterization of the outgoing by reference to what is achieved and characterization in the light of the purposes and objects of those responsible for incurring it. Thus, in the ordinary case of a payment under a contract, the nature of the outgoing will commonly be determined by reference to the contractual quid pro quo. Cases where the outgoing does not achieve its intended purpose or where the connection with the business is indirect and remote demonstrate, however, the need to distinguish between the character of an outgoing determined merely by reference to objective factors and its character determined in the light of subjective purpose in any precise formulation of the ingredients of the second limb of s 51(1). The key to the role of the objective and subjective in such a formulation is, in the case of a voluntary outgoing, to be found in the statement of Fullagar J in FC of T v Snowden & Willson Pty Ltd, supra, 99 CLR at 444; 7 AITR at 317 to which reference has already been made, namely, that "within the limits of reasonable human conduct the man who is carrying on the business must be the judge of what is 'necessary'". The controlling factor is that, viewed objectively, the outgoing must, in the circumstances, be reasonably capable of being seen as desirable or appropriate from the point of view of the pursuit of the business ends of the business being carried on for the purpose of earning assessable income. Provided it comes within that wide ambit, it will, for the purposes of s 51(1), be necessarily incurred in carrying on that business if those responsible for carrying on the business so saw it. The statement of the relevant question in this composite form finds support in the judgement of Dixon J in FC of T v Midland Railway Co of Western Australia Ltd [1952] HCA 5; (1952) 85 CLR 306 at 313; 5 AITR 243 at 247 where his Honour said:" . . . what governs the issue is the business purposes for which the outgoing was incurred from the point of view of the taxpayer company. The controlling factors are those which arise from the character of the business or undertaking and the relation which the expenditure or the liability to make it bore to the carrying on of the business or the gaining of assessable income" (emphasis added).

It is implicit in what has been said that the distinction between immediate direct purpose or object and remote indirect purpose or object does not play an essential part in determining whether an outgoing is properly deductible pursuant to the second limb of s 51(1) of the Act. To the extent that the subjective element is relevant, what is important in the case of a voluntary outgoing is the identification of the advantage or advantages which the outgoing was intended to achieve on behalf of the taxpayer regardless of whether that advantage or those advantages were seen as the direct result of the outgoing or as indirectly flowing therefrom or of whether the pursuit of them should be seen as "purpose" or as "object" or as "motive". Business outgoings may be properly and necessarily incurred in pursuit of indirect and remote, as well as direct and immediate, advantages. The fact that the business advantage sought is indirect or remote will not of itself preclude the pursuit of that advantage from characterizing the outgoing as an outgoing necessarily incurred in carrying on the relevant business.

The outgoings in the present case were not involuntary. They achieved the immediate purpose of those responsible for incurring them. That purpose was to provide legal representation for directors and agents of the taxpayer in respect of criminal prosecutions arising out of their actions in their respective capacities as directors and agents. The actions in question were performed on behalf of the taxpayer in the course of carrying on the taxpayer's income-earning business and were plainly directed towards earning assessable income for the taxpayer. In this regard, the learned trial judge expressly found that the course of conduct upon which the directors and agents were engaged was not on their own behalf but on behalf of the taxpayer. "Their actions, and those of the agents, were" his Honour wrote, "the taxpayer's actions. In this sense the taxpayer was compliant and implicated in all that occurred".

The evidence led on behalf of the taxpayer before the Supreme Court was largely directed to identifying the motives of the directors in incurring the outgoings on behalf of the taxpayer. The contest appears to have been directed to the question whether the dominant motive of the directors in incurring the outgoings had been, as the taxpayer maintained, to protect and preserve the taxpayer's business or whether their dominant motive had been, as the Commissioner suggested, to protect their own interests in the criminal proceedings in which they were embroiled. The evidence made clear both that the directors were concerned with the taxpayer's position and interests and that both the motive of protecting their own position and the motive of protecting and preserving the taxpayer's business were present and entwined. In a context where the relevant issue was seen as determining dominant motive however, Sheppard J reached the conclusion that the directors' dominant motive was to protect their own position. His Honour found (22 ALR at 394-5;9 ATR at 203): -

" . . . The circumstances were such that there was no fund available to them to meet the costs except moneys in the control of the taxpayer. The moneys were consequently expended for this purpose. That is not to say that the three directors were not very much concerned about the future of the companies in the group. But the essential thing, whilst the charges were pending, was for them to look after themselves. . . . The problem arose and the directors without examining the matter in the detail which the affidavit suggests simply made a decision to pay the costs. The evidence, accordingly, does not persuade me, as I have said, that it was incurred for the purpose of the taxpayer, except in a most indirect way".

If the essential question were the identification of the dominant motive of the directors in incurring the relevant expenditure, the taxpayer would face a heavy task in attempting to persuade this court to interfere with the conclusion which his Honour reached on that question after seeing and hearing two of the three directors give their evidence. In our view however, the identification of the dominant motive of the directors in deciding to incur the expenditure was not the essential question which arose for determination. There is no necessary dichotomy between what can properly be regarded as incidental and relevant to the business ends of a business and that which advances the personal interests of those persons who are employed or otherwise involved in that business. In many cases, the legitimate ends of a business carried on by a company will encompass what is in the personal interests of the directors and employees of that company. The provision of medical facilities and workers' compensation and superannuation benefits are obvious examples. Where a business is carried on by a company, many outgoings may advance the personal interests of individual directors, employees or agents or, indeed, of their relatives or friends. It is not essential, for the purposes of s 51(1) of the Act, that such outgoings be shown to have been incurred with a dominant motive characterized by lack of self-interest or generosity before they can be said to have been necessarily incurred in carrying on the particular business: see Cecil Bros Pty Ltd v FC of T, supra; FC of T v Phillips (1978) 78 ATC 4361;[1978] FCA 28; 8 ATR 783. An outgoing can, in the relevant sense, be necessarily incurred in carrying on a business notwithstanding that it flows from a sense of moral obligation to those involved in the business. In particular, the fact that the needs of some directors and agents provided the occasion of an outgoing and that the resulting benefit to directors or agents constituted the dominant motive of a taxpayer for incurring it does not, of itself, preclude the outgoing from being necessarily incurred in carrying on the taxpayer's business for the purposes of s 51(1). Whether a voluntary outgoing was so incurred depends upon the answer to the composite question which we have indicated, namely, whether the outgoing was reasonably capable of being seen as desirable or appropriate from the point of view of the pursuit of the business ends of that business and, if so, whether those responsible for carrying on the business so saw it. That composite question was, as we read his Honour's judgment, left unanswered by him. We turn to the consideration of what, in the light of his Honour's findings, is the correct answer to it.

It is not necessary for the purposes of the present appeal to determine whether outgoings incurred in providing appropriate legal representation for an employee on criminal charges directly arising from his activities in the course of a business are, without more, reasonably capable of being seen as desirable or appropriate in the sense we have indicated. The circumstances in the present case plainly involved elements transcending the mere provision of legal representation for those whose activities, on behalf of the taxpayer, had subjected them to criminal prosecution. The taxpayer's own reputation was under public attack and throughout most of the actual criminal proceedings in the County Court of Victoria the taxpayer was named as a co-conspirator. The interests of the taxpayer were inextricably involved with those of its directors and agents and it was plainly in the taxpayer's own interests that the directors and agents be properly represented. In these circumstances, the incurring of expenditure in providing adequate representation for the directors and agents was reasonably capable of being regarded as desirable and appropriate from the point of view of the pursuit of the business ends of the taxpayer's business.

The learned trial judge rejected the evidence led on behalf of the taxpayer that the outgoings had been incurred in consequence of a detailed weighing of the advantages which the taxpayer could be expected to derive from them. The directors, his Honour said,"simply made a decision to pay the costs". We do not question that this was so. It was, no doubt, intuitive in the directors to take the view that it was appropriate for the taxpayer to defend itself and those associated with it in its business from the attacks made upon it and them. It was also unavoidable that the individual directors should have in the forefront of their minds the personal predicament in which their activities on behalf of the taxpayer had placed them. In the circumstances however, the predicament of the directors and of the agents which resulted from activities on behalf of the taxpayer provided the occasion upon which the question whether the outgoings should be incurred by the taxpayer arose for consideration. The needs of the directors and the agents were unavoidably in the forefront of the proper consideration of that question by the directors. Indeed, those needs, in the circumstances, provided not an argument against, but a consideration favouring, the payment by the taxpayer of the legal expenses of its directors and employees.

There was no suggestion and certainly no finding that the directors, in incurring the outgoings, acted otherwise than within their powers as such. It is apparent from the evidence that they saw their own interests and the interests of the taxpayer as interwoven and that, in acting intuitively to protect their own interests, they saw themselves as acting to protect and advance the interests of the taxpayer. The learned judge at first instance expressly found that the directors were very much concerned not only about their own position but also about the future of the taxpayer. In these circumstances, the fact that the directors may have been motivated by consideration of their own position neither negatives the conclusion that the outgoings were reasonably capable of being seen as desirable and appropriate from the point of view of the pursuit of the business ends of the taxpayer's business nor prevents the conclusion that they were incurred by the directors on behalf of the taxpayer for the purpose of gaining advantages which they saw as being desirable and appropriate in that sense. While the question involved is essentially one of fact, we consider that the following comments of Dixon CJ, in FC of T v Snowden & Willson Pty Ltd, supra, 99 CLR at 436; 7 AITR at 311-12, are apposite to the circumstances of the present case: -

". . . But, as it appears to me, the carrying on of the business of the nature described brought with it the attacks against which the taxpayer company sought to defend itself. The attacks touched its business nearly; they disparaged the methods by which it was conducted; they were calculated to deter intending or likely customers from dealing with it and to destroy the faith of existing customers in their current relations with the company. No doubt it would be instinctive in the business man or, perhaps, in any man, to defend himself and those associated with him in business against an attack of such a description on the manner in which they were pursuing their business activities. But the instinct is founded upon sound if intuitive conceptions of what must be done if they are not to suffer in their pursuit of custom and profit. Whether on the merits they were in a position to defend themselves successfully or whether, on the other hand, the attacks upon them lacked adequate foundation alike seem to me to be matters not to the point."

The overall position in the present case can, in our view, be accurately summarized by saying that the directors intuitively saw the outgoings as not only being required in their own interests but as being desirable and appropriate in the pursuit by the taxpayer of its business ends. As has been said, the outgoings were reasonably capable of being so regarded. It follows that, subject to any question of disqualification resulting from their nature, the relevant outgoings were, for the purpose of s 51(1), necessarily incurred by the taxpayer in carrying on its business. It becomes necessary to consider whether the outgoings were of a capital, private or domestic nature.

The considerations which led us to conclude that the outgoings were, for the purposes of s 51(1) of the Act, necessarily incurred in carrying on the taxpayer's business also lead, in the circumstances of the present matter, to the conclusion that they were not of a private or domestic nature. The expenditure was of company funds to provide legal representation for those associated with the taxpayer in respect of criminal charges arising from commercial activities on the taxpayer's behalf. The occasion of the outgoings arose from the taxpayer's commercial activities in the course of carrying on its business. The outgoings were capable of serving the business ends of that business and were seen by those responsible for incurring them as desirable and appropriate in the pursuit of those ends. Plainly, they were neither private nor domestic in nature.

The question whether the outgoings were of a capital nature is, in our view, answered in the negative by the reasoning of the majority of the High Court of Australia in FC of T v Snowden & Willson Pty Ltd, supra, 99 CLR at 437, 441-446, 448-452; 7 AITR at 312; 315-319; 320-323. Except in the most indirect way, the criminal proceedings imperilled neither the business nor the capital assets of the taxpayer. As the learned judge at first instance pointed out, the gross revenue of the taxpayer continued to increase despite the difficulties with which the taxpayer was faced. The criminal proceedings in respect of which the outgoings were incurred arose out of the day to day business activities of the taxpayer. The outgoings did not involve the acquisition of any enduring or tangible asset. They represented expenditure incurred in carrying on the taxpayer's business which should properly be seen as being of a revenue character.

Finally, it was argued on behalf of the Commissioner that no deduction could properly be allowed in respect of the outgoings by reason of their nature as the payment of legal costs incurred in the defence of criminal proceedings. This broad proposition flowed, so it was submitted, from the decision of Rowlatt J in IR Comrs v Warnes & Co Ltd(1919) 2 KB 444 and the decision of the Court of Appeal in IR Comrs v von Glehn and Co Ltd(1920) 2 KB 553 which were said to have been expressly approved by the High Court of Australia in Herald & Weekly Times Ltd v FC of T(1932) [1932] HCA 56; 48 CLR 113 and FC of T v Snowden & Willson Pty Ltd, supra.

Consideration of the above-mentioned four cases has quite failed to persuade us that they are authority for the existence of any overriding proposition precluding the allowance as deductions of the outgoings in the present case by reason of their nature as the payment of the legal costs of the defence of the directors and agents in criminal proceedings. In neither of the two English cases was separate consideration given to the question of deductibility of costs as distinct from the deductibility of the fine or penalty which was imposed in the relevant proceedings. Indeed, in two of the three judgments of the members of the Court of Appeal in IR Comrs v Warnes & Co Ltd, supra, there is no reference at all to the fact that costs were also involved. In neither of the cases in the High Court of Australia were costs of defending criminal proceedings involved. In the Herald & Weekly Times case, supra, the costs involved were the costs of defending defamation proceedings. They were held to be properly deductible. In the Snowden & Willson case, supra, the cost involved were the costs incurred in appearing before a Royal Commission. Again, they were held to be properly deductible.

It is true that there are to be found in each of the two cases in the High Court statements indicating acceptance of the proposition derived from the two English decisions to the effect that a payment of a fine or penalty will not properly constitute a deduction for income tax purposes: see Herald & Weekly Times Ltd v FC of T, supra, 48 CLR at 120 (per Gavan Duffy CJ, and Dixon J) and FC of T v Snowden & Willson Pty Ltd, supra, 99 CLR at 437; 7 AITR at 312 (per Dixon CJ) and 446; 319 (per Fullagar J). The apparent acceptance of that general proposition was explained in the following extract from the joing judgment of Gavan Duffy CJ, and Dixon J in the Herald & Weekly Times case, ibid: -

"The cases of IR Comrs v von Glehn and IR Comr v Warnes & Co, which decide that penalties imposed for breaches of the law committed in the course of exercising a trade cannot be deducted, are distinguishable for a somewhat similar reason. The penalty is imposed as a punishment of the offender considered as a responsible person owing obedience to the law. Its nature severs it from the expenses of trading. It is inflicted on the offender as a personal deterrent, and it is not incurred by him in his character of trader. Lord Sterndale MR in von Glehn's case said: 'It is perhaps a little difficult to put the distinction into very exact language, but there seems to me to be a difference between a commercial loss in trading and a penalty imposed upon a person or a company for a breach of the law which they have committed in that trading'."

It is somewhat difficult to understand how it can be maintained, as an unqualified proposition, that the nature of a penalty severs it from the expenses of trading. Recurrent penalties for parking infringements incurred by a delivery man and per diem penalties for unlawfully using premises for business or commercial purposes in contravention of zoning requirements are not, for example, logically severed from the expenses of trading. The same can be said of fines imposed for actually engaging in some unlawful activities, such as illegal bookmaking or soliciting, for the purpose of earning assessable income. If, when the matter directly arises for decision in the Australian courts, it is to be held that all fines and penalties are to be denied deductibility under the Act, it would seem preferable that it be on the basis of some perceived overriding consideration of public policy which precludes deductibility. Even in that event however, it would not necessarily follow that, as a matter of overriding principle, a deduction should be refused in respect of a taxpayer's costs of defending the proceedings in which the penalty was imposed upon him: see, generally, Minister of Nat Rev v L D Caulk Co of Canada Ltd (1954) Can TC 28; Rowland Paper Co Ltd v Minister of Nat Rev (1960) Can TC 158 and the discussion in Day, Tax Consequences of Illegal Trading Transactions (1971) British Tax Review 104.

Be this as it may, there is nothing at all in any of the cases to which reference has been made which precludes a deduction in respect of the payment of the legal costs incurred not in the defence of the taxpayer himself but in the defence of directors or employees of a taxpayer in criminal proceedings arising out of their activities on the taxpayer's behalf. Indeed, the only express consideration in the cases mentioned of the deductibility of payments by a taxpayer of such legal costs is to be found in the judgment of Webb J in the Snowden & Willson case, supra, 99 CLR at 441; 7 AITR at 315 where his Honour expressed the view that it was arguable that a company would, at least in some cases, be entitled to treat the payment of the costs of an employee in such circumstances as an outgoing deductible under s 51(1).

Nor, in our view, do considerations of public policy provide support for the Commissioner's submissions on this aspect of the appeal. Whatever may be the position as regards the payment of fines or penalties, there is no basis in public policy for any overriding principle of non-deductibility of legal costs incurred in the defence of directors, employees or agents on criminal charges arising out of activities on the taxpayer's behalf. To the contrary, there are sound arguments to support the view that, in the absence of any question of inducement to criminal activity, an outgoing which is designed to achieve the result that a citizen charged with a criminal offence has proper and adequate legal representation, is positively in the public interest. Plainly, such an outgoing cannot properly be regarded as being contrary to contemporary notions of public policy.

In the result, we would allow the appeal and order that the assessments be amended by allowing the relevant deductions. We would order that the Commissioner pay the taxpayer's costs both in the Supreme Court and in this court.
