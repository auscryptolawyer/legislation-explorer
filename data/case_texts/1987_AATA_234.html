NT85/808-809 and Commissioner of Taxation [1987] AATA 234; (1987) 18 ATR 3663; 87 ATC 787 (19 June 1987)

Last Updated: 23 July 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Taxation - assessable income - sale of real estate - whether sale made pursuant to an agreement entered into during the first 12 months of ownership, s.26AAA(3) - date of the contract outside the 12 month period - onus on applicant to discharge on the balance of probabilities that agreement does not exist.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss 26(a), 26AAA, 80B, 177A, 221YHAAA, 260

Taxation Administration Act 1953 s35(1)

Land Sales Act (NSW) 1964, s1C(5)(c)

Local Government Act (NSW) 1919 S.342AS

McAndrew v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1956] HCA 62; 98 CLR 263

Boyded Industries Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1958] AC 450

L. Porter and Co Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation, 77 ATC 4472

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Cooper Brookes (Wollongong) Pty Ltd 79 ATC 4398

South Australian Mining Co Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1950] HCA 16; 81 CLR 188

Tribunal : Mr B.J. McMahon (Senior Member)

Decision : The Tribunal, in relation to the year of income ended 30 June 1980, sets aside both the objection decisions under review and remits the matters to the respondent for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the unit referred to in the adjustment sheets ' accompanying the amended assessments under review was not sold until 14 March 1980, after the expiration of the period of 12 months from the date of purchase.

(gsd) .B.J. McMahon.

B.J. McMahon Senior Member

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No. NT85/808-809 TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )

Re:

Applicant

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent

REASONS FOR DECISION

19 June 1987 Mr BJ McMahon (Senior Member)

  1. This application concerns a review of two decisions to disallow objections to assessments of income tax against a husband and wife. The facts relating to both assessments and objections are identical. In these reasons I will refer only to the applicant husband. It may be taken that what I say in respect of his objection decision may also be assumed to be my reasons in respect of that of his wife.
  2. In his return of income for the year ended 30th June 1980 the applicant included a lengthy and detailed statement setting out reasons why he had sold certain real estate during the fiscal year.
  3. There is no contest that he purchased a home unit for investment purposes by contract dated 12 March 1979. The statement attached to his return set out the reasons for the purchase and his intentions to use the income from this long term investment for reasons which are not here material. It is accepted that the unit was not acquired for the purpose of profit making by sale, and that accordingly any profit arising from the sale of the unit would not, on that account, be taxable under the former section 26(a) of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act"), which applied at the relevant time.
  4. The statement disclosed that the applicant had sold the unit by contract dated 14 March 1980, that is to say two days after the expiration of 12 months from the date of the purchase contract. Following this disclosure a field audit was carried out and as a result of information gained during the course of that audit and certain conclusions which the respondent reached as a result of that information, an amended assessment was issued. The adjustment sheet accompanying that assessment simply stated:

"Add

As a result of the recent audit conducted into your income tax affairs, the following adjustments have been made:-

profit from sale of home unit included in assessable income $6,114.

5. The applicant's objection to this assessment was disallowed and the matter was referred at his request to a Board of Review and subsequently to this Tribunal. In the statement required to be furnished to the Board of Review in accordance with sub-regulation 35(1) under the Taxation Administration Act, the respondent stated (inter alia):

"As the taxpayer did not make a full and true disclosure of all the material facts necessary for his assessment for the year ended 30 June 1980 and as there had been an avoidance of tax, the issue of an amended assessment for that year was authorised in terms of section 170 of the Act.

The amount of $6,114 being the taxpayer's share of profit on the sale of home unit at [address of unit] was correctly included in the assessable income of the taxpayer for the year ended 30 June 1980 under section 26AAA of the Act."

6. At the relevant time the parts of section 26AAA of the Act upon which the respondent relied were as follows:

"26AAA. (1) For the purpose of this section-

(b) a reference to an agreement or option includes a reference to an agreement or option that is not enforceable by legal proceedings whether or not it was intended to be so enforceable;

(c) an arrangement or understanding, whether formal or informal and whether express or implied, shall be deemed to be an agreement;

(g) if land is sold to or purchased by a person in pursuance of a contract, the date on which the contract was made shall be deemed to have been the date on which the land was so sold or purchased.

(2) Where-

(a) a taxpayer has purchased property after 21 August 1973 and before the commencement of this section or purchases property after the commencement of this section; and

(b) the taxpayer has, whether before or after the commencement of this section, sold the property or an interest in the property before the expiration of the period of 12 months from the date on which he purchased the property,

then, subject to this section, the assessable income of the taxpayer includes any profit arising from the sale of the property or interest.

(3) If-

(a) a taxpayer sells property or an interest in property after the expiration of the period of

12 months from the date on which he purchased the property; and

(b) the sale is made in pursuance of an option granted, or an agreement entered into, during that period,

sub-section (2) has effect as if the sale had been made before the expiration of that period."

7. The onus lies upon the taxpayer to prove that he has made a full disclosure where such an allegation of non-disclosure has been made (McAndrew v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1956] HCA 62; 98 CLR 263). However, the respondent does bear at least an evidential burden on this question. As was said in Bovded Industries Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 87 ATC 4047 at 4056:

"The taxpayer's onus of proof in relation to section 170 does not require it to speculate as to every matter and specifically to anticipate every conceivable fact which the Commissioner may subsequently argue has not been disclosed by him. The Commissioner bears at least an evidential onus of raising the relevant issues to enable the taxpayer to deal with them in the discharge of its overall burden of proof pursuant to section 190."

8. Accordingly at the outset of the hearing of this application it was indicated by the respondent that he relied upon section 26AAA(3) of the Act. It was agreed that the date of the contract was outside the 12 month period and that the date of the contract was the relevant date for the purpose of sub-section (2). It was alleged, however, that the sale was made pursuant to an agreement entered into during the 12 month period. The extended meaning of agreement in sub-paragraphs (b) and (c) of section 26AAA(1) was relied upon to substantiate this allegation. At the conclusion of the hearing the respondent pointed to three specific areas of evidence to which reference will be made later in discharge of his "evidential onus" and as indicating the basis upon which he relied as prima facie evidence of the existence of an agreement.

9. Notwithstanding the allegation of failure to make a full and true disclosure, no "penalty" tax was imposed on the applicant. The question to be decided is simply whether on the facts as given in evidence, the unit was sold outside the 12 months period pursuant to an agreement made within that period.

  1. The applicant was employed as a bank officer. The unit had been financed by his bank's credit union. In March 1980 he decided to move to a country town and to resign from the bank. The credit union would not have continued to carry the finance after he ceased to be employed by the bank. Furthermore, the original purpose intended for the application of the income had disappeared with the move to the country town. These were factors in his decision to sell the unit.
  2. Up until November 1979 the applicant had satisfactory tenants in the unit. After they moved out he had difficulty in finding further tenants. There was tendered in evidence some correspondence he had with his managing agent instructing him on 3 December 1979 to seek further tenants, preferably on a 6 months lease at $50 per week. He gave evidence that by January when tenants still had not been found, he authorised the agent to reduce the rent to $45 per week. At that stage they were still unsuccessful in finding suitable tenants.
  3. In February 1980 the applicant and his wife visited the country town to attend the wedding of a daughter of a friend. This friend, a doctor, suggested that if they moved to the town in which he practiced, he could look after their aged and ill parents, who were then of some concern to them. The applicant gave evidence that they did not think too seriously about the idea until it came to their attention that an old home, such as they had always wanted, was coming up for sale by auction at the end of March. They thereupon decided to buy that house and move their entire establishment to the country town. This decision precipitated the proposed sale of the unit.
  4. The applicant gave evidence that he was in an executive position with his bank and was well aware of the general effect of section 26AAA of the Act. He was conscious that if he sold the unit within the 12 months period he could suffer adverse tax consequences. He gave evidence that when he returned from the wedding trip early in February 1980, he was so overwhelmed by the prospect of the move and his impending retirement from the bank that he found it hard to find a starting point. Nevertheless, he contacted the agent, principally to find out whether a tenant had been found. He gave evidence that he told the agent that he was thinking of moving to the country town if he could purchase the house he had in mind. He told the agent that if he did decide to move, then the unit would be up for sale. However, he emphasised to the agent many times that he was not prepared to enter into any commitment to sell until at least 12 March 1980. This emphasis is confirmed by correspondence that was tendered in evidence. I should say here that the applicant presented as an open, honest and frank witness whose evidence I have no hesitation in accepting.
  5. So far as the applicant can recall no agency agreement was signed. He accepted that he must have had at least one telephone conversation with the agent concerning price. He said that the conversation on this subject could be summarised by a statement that if the applicant decided to sell then the price that they discussed would be the price that he would accept.
  6. The agent must have moved quickly, as he soon accepted a token deposit of $100 from a proposed purchaser on the understanding that the balance of 10% of the sale price would be paid if and when contracts were exchanged. The applicant said that he would have preferred not to have sold until after the auction sale of the country property. However, the purchaser "wanted it and he wanted it quickly". Accordingly, he instructed his solicitor to draw up a contract but told him that he was not prepared to commit himself in any way prior to the expiry of the 12 months period. In fact, he retained the form of contract for some 10 days after he received it from his solicitor before returning it .duly signed. He said that the longer the proceedings were delayed the better he would like it. He did not consider that he was in any way bound to the purchaser. He said "if people want to put themselves in a precarious situation that is okay with me, but I did not in any way feel committed".
  7. He denied that at any time he agreed that he would sell after the 13th of March. He had no conversations whatever with the purchaser, and indeed did not meet him either before or after the sale. The contract did not authorise early possession. In accordance with his instructions, contracts were not exchanged until 14 March and settlement took place on 11 April 1980.
  8. Attached to the contract, which was tendered in evidence, were two certificates. The first was a certificate under section lC(5)(c) of the (NSW) Land Sales Act 1964. It was signed by the purchaser's solicitor and was dated 28/2/80 (within the 12 month period). It certified that the solicitor had explained the effect of the contract to the purchaser. before it was executed by him. From this certificate one may deduce therefore that the counterpart contract was signed by the purchaser on or prior to 28 February 1980, even though contracts were not exchanged until 14 March 1980.
  9. The second certificate was one issued pursuant to section 342AS of the (NSW) Local Government Act. It certified as to the zoning of the property. It was issued on 25 February 1980 to the applicant's solicitors. At the time of this transaction, the attachment of such a certificate to the contract was common. Those familiar with conveyancing procedure will recognise that an application for such a certificate would have been made by the vendor's solicitors very soon after receiving instructions to prepare a contract or even soon after receiving information of the possibility of a future contract. The certificate under the Land Sales Act was required to negate certain rights which a purchaser might otherwise have had as against the vendor. In my view, the certificate does no more than indicate that the contract was signed by the purchaser prior to (or more likely on) 28 February 1980.

19. The three pieces of evidence upon which the respondent relies to constitute the arrangement which he alleges, are firstly the fact that a deposit was paid, even though a mere token deposit of $100, secondly the existence of these two certificates which are dated prior to the date of the contract, 14 March 1980, and thirdly a letter from the applicant's solicitors to the agent dated 20 March 1980 confirming that contracts were exchanged on 14 March 1980 and enclosing a cheque for the balance of the deposit. From this evidence, and the oral evidence of the applicant, I am invited to draw an inference that there was "an arrangement or understanding whether formal or informal" amounting to an agreement to sell within the terms of section 26AAA.

20. What are the characteristics of an arrangement to which that section refers?

21. The terms are certainly wide but they are by no means of infinite width.

  1. They are similar to the terms of sub-sections (10) and (11) of section 80B dealing with special provisions relating to beneficial ownership of or rights attached to shares that are transferred when control of a "loss company" is sold. There are some decisions to which reference will be made which have considered the extent to which these words may be interpreted. The formula of words in section 26AAA, however, may be contrasted with other anti-avoidance provisions to be found elsewhere in the Act.
  2. Section 221YHAAA deals with provisional tax avoidance schemes. In sub-section (1) arrangement is defined to mean:

"(a) Any agreement, arrangement, understanding, promise or undertaking, whether express or implied, and whether or not enforceable, or intended to be enforceable, by legal proceedings; and

(b) any scheme, plan, proposal, action, course of action or course of conduct, whether unilateral or otherwise."

24. Such a definition goes much further than the formula used in section 26AAA. The latter may also be contrasted with the terms of the general anti-avoidance provision inserted in the Act in 1981. Prior to then, section 260 spoke of "a contract agreement or arrangement". In section 177A(1) replacing it, the draftsman appears to have abandoned the narrow reliance upon a statutory meaning of "agreement" and has substituted the notion of a "scheme". This is defined to mean in terms similar to but not identical with those quoted in paragraph 23:

"(a) Any agreement, arrangement, understanding, promise or undertaking, whether express or implied, and whether or not enforceable, or intended to be enforceable, by legal proceedings; and

(b) any scheme, plan, proposal, action, course of action or course of conduct."

  1. The merest gossamer would be sufficient to enmesh a taxpayer under either of these later definitions. It seems to me that section 26AAA calls for the demonstration of a much stronger web of circumstances. A discernible understanding based on clear evidence, is the sort of understanding referred to in this section and is contrasted with the understanding required under the other anti-avoidance provisions. The confinements and limitations of an arrangement or understanding alleged under section 26AAA and the demonstration of its existence must, in my view, be tighter than those sustainable and clearer than those proveable under the other sections to which I have referred.
  2. An arrangement normally must be between two or more persons (Newton and Others v Federal Commissioner of Taxation C1958D AC 450). This characteristic is specifically excluded by S221YHAAA but not by S177A(1). In my view it is a characteristic which must be present in any arrangement alleged under S26AAA. A unilateral Act cannot amount to an arrangement or understanding. For example, if the applicant were simply to put a 'For Sale' notice outside his property, this could not amount to an arrangement or understanding on its own. Furthermore, if the applicant were to say to a friend "I'll do you a favour. I'll sell you my property for $X", and the friend replied "well thank you, I'll think about that", this too could not be said to be an arrangement or understanding. There must be acts of more than one party to constitute even the beginning of an arrangement or understanding.

27. There must, in fact, be at least a common understanding between the parties to the arrangement (K Porter and Co Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 77 ATC 4472 at 4478). Without that common understanding, there would be absent another essential element requisite for the constitution of an arrangement, namely the element of mutuality. There must exist understandings (or so called moral obligations) undertaken by more than one party. There must be, to use the words of the High Court in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Lutovi Investments Pty Ltd 78 ATC 4708, an element of consensuality. The majority in that decision said at page 4712:

"In the context of sec.260 an arrangement is something less than a binding contract or agreement, something in the nature of an understanding which may not be enforceable at law (Newton v FC of T, at p7). A similar view has been taken of an arrangement falling within sec.80B(5)) (see K. Porter & Co Pty Ltd v FC of T, 74 ATC 4093, at p.4100; (1974) 1 NSWLR ALJR 298). It is, however, necessary that an arrangement should be consensual, and that there should be some adoption of it. But in our view it is not essential that the parties are committed to it or are bound to support it. An arrangement may be informal as well as unenforceable and the parties may be free to withdraw from it or to act inconsistently with it, notwithstanding their adoption of it."

  1. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Cooper Brookes ' (Wollongong) Pty Ltd 79 ATC 4398, the requirement was expressed to be a mutual commitment to a known course of conduct.
  2. The agreement or arrangement may be inferred from objective circumstances. It is not necessary, however, that the inference should be irresistible (Cooper- Brookes at page 4417). It must, however, be based upon evidence. The taxpayer carries the onus of proof. However, it seems to me that his onus is similar to that to be discharged in the case of an allegation of incomplete disclosure. He does not have to disprove every fact and circumstance that might possibly be alleged as forming part of an agreement or an arrangement. He is entitled to have the issue raised by the respondent through particulars of facts and circumstances alleged and relied upon. He must then discharge the onus on the balance of probabilities of disproving only those facts and circumstances said to constitute the agreement or arrangement.
  3. All of the decisions dealing with the meaning to be given to these anti-avoidance provisions have certain features in common. They deal with circumstances where an obvious attempt at avoidance or "minimisation" of taxation - such as the procedures involved in the sale of a loss company - are under consideration. The wide interpretation which the courts have been prepared to give to such formulae of words have all been in cases where openes3 and frankness have not been features of the arrangement or of the evidence. This leads me to an opinion that a characteristic element of an arrangement or understanding within the meaning of the statute, is an element of surreptitiousness -an ingredient which might be described as the wink and nod ingredient. Such elements often go hand in hand with attempts to deceive. They are often to be found in conjunction with attempts to establish a set of facts that are inconsistent with objective evidence such as contemporary documents. They are often to be found in connection with assessments arising as a result of an incomplete disclosure. Mendacity is a not uncommon characteristic, although one would have to say that it is not an essential characteristic of an agreement or arrangement.

31. In the light of these principles I now return to the evidence.

32. Firstly, it is clear that there was never at any time any contact between the applicant and the purchaser of the unit. If any common understanding or element of mutuality is to be alleged as existing between them, it can only be established through the conduct of the applicant's agent. The evidence establishes, to my satisfaction, that the agent was at no time authorised to commit the applicant to any proposed sale until such time as it suited him. He was not, through the actions of his agent, locked in to a "commitment to a course of conduct". The payment of a preliminary deposit by the purchaser indicates no more than that he is interested in negotiating. I take it as a well known fact that payment of a preliminary deposit in these circumstances in no way binds any party and is nothing more than a token of good faith as a preliminary to further negotiations. As to the certificates attached to the contract, neither of these would have been specifically or even generally authorised by the applicant (particularly that given by the purchaser's solicitor) and in any event do not, in my view, contribute in any way to the cobbling together of any set of circumstances which might be described as an understanding between the applicant and the purchaser. Similarly the letter from the solicitor upon which the respondent relies is, in my opinion, innocuous in that context.

33. There is, in short, in my view no evidence upon which one could draw an inference on the balance of probabilities that the applicant had committed himself to a course of conduct that would ultimately result in a formal sale at any time prior to the expiration of the 12 months period.

34. It also seems to me that there is a complete absence in this case of the "wink and nod ingredient". Far from failing to make a full and true disclosure the applicant went to considerable trouble to explain in great detail what he did with his real estate and why he did it. He fulfilled what Williams J. called "the duty of disclosing every fact which he knows or is capable of knowing material to a correct assessment" (South Australian Mining Co Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1950] HCA 16; 81 CLR 188. There was no attempt to deceive, nor was there any evidence of the slightest lack of co-operation between the applicant and the investigator, except that during a conversation they are said to have disagreed on the meaning of "agreement". It is significant that no penalty tax was - imposed on the applicant. This may well entitle one to assume that at any rate in the eyes of the Commissioner, there was no "culpability factor".

35. In short, the evidence leaves me satisfied that the unit was not sold until 14 March 1980, after the expiration of the period of 12 months from the date of purchase. I am further satisfied that it was not sold on that date pursuant to an agreement entered into during the 12 months period. The objection decisions are therefore set aside and both applications are referred hack to the respondent with directions that fresh amended assessments he issued in accordance with these reasons.
