ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income tax - deductions - depreciation - residence also display house - whether "plant"

Income tax - deductions - home office expenses

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936, ss51, 54

REASONS FOR DECISION

19 October 1987 Deputy President R. K. Todd

  1. The applicant seeks review of objection decisions disallowing claims to deduct amounts expended in relation to the applicant's home. Such amounts related to depreciation of the building, electricity, rates, insurance on the building and interest. Considering the obstacles in the way of the making of such claims the applicant made, in the rather unusual circumstances which are set out below, a surprisingly impressive case. In the end however the obstacles, I am persuaded, are insuperable.
  2. The applicant is an architect with an impressive record of achievement in relation to the design of solar efficient houses. There is nothing extreme or extravagant in his designs. Essentially, and at the risk of understating his principles, the applicant relies on the siting of the building so as to catch the north sun; on the use of structural materials and internal finishes (including for example closed curtain pelmets); and on certain particular design features, to achieve maximum utilisation of sunlight to heat the house. Perhaps the most illustrative feature of one of his designs, and one which is to the fore in his own house, is the use of an upper clerestory window. In winter his design permits sun to enter through this window so as to heat an internal brick wall so as to store heat therein, while in summer the window can be opened to produce a cooling effect at night. It is unnecessary to go into further detail. It is enough to say that I found the applicant's claims for his design principles to be impressive in their practicality and in their apparent cost effectiveness.
  3. What the applicant essentially contends is that his own residence, in which he and his family lives, is a business entity which displays his skills to potential clients and to persons otherwise interested in the techniques which he employs and who for that purpose resort to it to inspect it. His family are, he says, used to being disturbed by the use of their house as a "display". In the case of some of the claims, deductibility is claimed however not only on the basis of the "display" character of the home, but also on the fact that the applicant carries on his architectural practice from the building. I have no difficulty in accepting the evidence given by the applicant as to how he uses the house. The difficulty arises in relation to whether the claims made are capable in law of being sustained, or whether the applicant can, on the facts, establish claims greater in quantum than those that have already been allowed.
  4. There are three years of income involved, being the fiscal years 1983, 1984 and 1985. In each the applicant claimed for depreciation of the building constituting his residence and the place from which he carried on his practice. As put at the hearing, this claim appeared to run counter to long established understandings of the position as to depreciability of buildings in Australian income tax law. But in deference to the arguments of Mr Ceramidas, who appeared for the applicant, I have given careful consideration to the English authorities to which he referred me. It was necessary for his argument that the building in question be capable of being found to be "plant" within the meaning of s54 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the ITAA"). In this behalf Mr. Ceramidas referred to: Yarmouth v. France (1887) 19 QBD 647; J Lyons and Co, Ltd v The Attorney-General [1944] 1 All ER 477; Hinton (Inspector of Taxes) v. Maden and Ireland Ltd. 38 TC 391; Jarrold (H.M. Inspector of Taxes) v John Good & Sons, Ltd. 40 T.C. 681 (in which Pearson LJ. said that the question was "whether the partitioning is part of the premises in which the business is carried on or part of the plant with which the business is carried on"); IRC v Barclay, Curie & Co. Ltd. [1969] 1 All ER 732; Dixon (Inspector of Taxes) v. R. & H. Hall Ltd. 49 TC 524; and Cole Bros. Ltd. v. Phillips (Inspector of Taxes) [1982] 2 All ER 247). Probably the most significant of these decisions from the point of view of the case put for the applicant was IRC v Barclay, Curie & Co Ltd , where a dry dock was held to be "plant".

5. Decisions of Australian courts are of course decisive, and here reference should be made principally to Australian Gas Light Co v Valuer-General (1940) 40 SR(NSW) 126, where Jordan CJ. said that "'Plant' is a very general expression which in certain contexts may be capable of including permanently fixed things such as buildings ..."; Moreton Central Sugar Mill Co Ltd v FC of T (1967) 116 CLR 151; Broken Hill Proprietary Co Ltd v FC of T [1968] HCA 16; (1967-1969) 120 CLR 240; and Wangaratta Woollen Mills Ltd v FC of T [1969] HCA 39; (1969) 119 CLR 1. The high watermark of the Australian authorities, from the point of view of the applicant's case, is the last mentioned of these cases, where McTiernan J held that a dye house in which the taxpayer company carried on a business of dyeing and spinning worsted yarn was "plant" and therefore depreciable. Yet the terms of McTiernan J's judgment clearly acknowledge the "setting" test which was expressed by Kitto J. in the Moreton Central Sugar Mill Case as follows: ..."the word (plant) includes every chattel or fixture which is kept for use in the carrying on of the mining operations, not being (in the case of a building) merely in the nature of a general setting in which a part of these operations are carried on" (120 CLR at 247). In the Wangaratta Woollen Mills Case McTiernan J. said: "I am of the opinion that the plaintiff's dye house is 'in the nature of a tool' in the trade and does 'play a part' itself in the manufacturing process. It is much more than a convenient setting for the plaintiff's operations". The decision of this Tribunal (Mr. P.M. Roach (Senior Member)) in Case T102 86 ATC 1178 in which a specially designed swimming pool used for teaching scuba-diving was held not to be "plant" applied these principles.

  1. I am completely satisfied from the authorities mentioned that it is quite impossible to find that the applicant's home, the residence of himself and his family, is "plant" within s.54 of the ITAA. While it is clear that the applicant carries on his practice from the house, and that he has clients coming there to do business with him and/or to inspect the house, the structure of the house remains to be characterised as the general setting within which his operations are carried on. The claims for depreciation under s.54 of the ITAA must therefore fail.
  2. The s51 claims in respect of electricity, rates, insurance and interest are in a different case. The applicant conducts his business from the house, and his position can broadly be compared with that of the taxpayer in Swinford v FC of T 84 ATC 4803, where the taxpayer was a self-employed television script-writer with no other place of business, rather than with the position of the barrister taxpayer in Handley v FC of T. 81 ATC 4165. The difficulty here is with proof and quantification of the claims, and, having regard to the decision in Handley's Case, with whether certain of the claims are deductible at all. I take the claims in groups, having regard to the way in which similar claims have been dealt with in the cases, but first observing that what the Commissioner did in broad terms was to allow ten per cent of the expenditure incurred. The Commissioner's stance was that in some instances too great an amount had been allowed, especially having regard to the fact that the house was owned jointly by the applicant and his wife. It is also to be observed that

(i) the house was designed as a four bedroom house;

(ii) that the applicant used one bedroom as a study but had to vacate this when another child was born, requiring him to work in the dining-room;

(iii) that while the applicant made a good deal of the fact that the house was opened to tours, having regard to its previously mentioned "display" character, only ten such visits in the three years were proved, and only one client visitation per week on average was proved.

8. The claims relating to interest, rates and insurance;

(a) In view of what was said in Handley's Case, in particular by Wilson J, I do not consider that these amounts were deductible at all. There, at 81 ATC 4176 Wilson J said:

"The room used as a study does not cease to be part of the taxpayer's home merely because as a matter of convenience he uses it for professional purposes for 20 hours per week during 45 weeks of the year. It is true that in choosing for purchase in 1969 this particular residence as a home for himself and his family the taxpayer was influenced by the fact that there was in it a room which he considered to be suitable for use by him as a study. But it remained essentially part of his home. The payments for mortgage interests, rates and insurance premiums were of a kind which in the circumstances of this case cannot be apportioned between home and office expenses. They related to the building and/or land as a whole, and are not affected in any way at all by reason of the fact that the taxpayer performs professional work on the premises. They would remain the same whether or not he worked at home.

In view of the foregoing considerations, and for the reasons I have given in Forsyth, I conclude that no part of the outgoings possess the requisite character of outgoings incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, nor are they necessarily incurred in carrying on the taxpayer's profession. In any event, I believe that they must be regarded as outgoings of a capital, private or domestic nature, thus coming within the exception to sec.51."

(b) It is true that in the present case the applicant did all his work at home, whereas Handley, a barrister, used his study at home for only some twenty hours per week. But the question is one of quality not quantity. What was being emphasised in Handley was the nature and quality of payments made in respect of a home by way of interest, rates and insurance. In Swinford, Hunt J. found that rental payments had a different character and were deductible, but while, as I have said, the nature of the use of the present applicant's house was more like that in Swinford than that in Handley, the payments that he made cannot be equated in terms of their character to those held deductible in Swinford. The distinction between rent and interest may sound a fine one, but it is not one which this Tribunal, in view of the authority existing in this area, is at liberty to disturb.

9. The claims relating to power and heating

In my opinion, the taxpayer was entitled to a deduction * under this heading. Such a claim was allowed in F.C. of T. v. Faichney 72 ATC 4245. But insufficient evidence was placed before me to enable me to find that any greater sum should be allowed under this heading than has already been allowed by the Commissioner.

10. It follows that while I entertained a good deal of sympathy for what was an attractively presented case, I am unable to do anything but affirm the objection decisions under review.
