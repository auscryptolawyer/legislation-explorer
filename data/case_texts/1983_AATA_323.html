Re Neil Trevor Sewter and Commissioner of Police [1983] AATA 323 (26 September 1983)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: NEIL TREVOR SEWTER

And: COMMISSIONER OF POLICE

No. A83/30

Gun licence

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

Mr Ewart Smith (Senior Member)

CATCHWORDS

gun licence _ objection by member of Australian Police Force to grant of licence _ absence of specific criteria in relation to discretion to grant licence _ whether applicant responsible person despite conviction _ discrepancies in evidence of applicant and Police _ presentation of applicant before Tribunal _ need for positive evidence of responsibility of an applicant for a gun licence

Law reform

Gun Licence Ordinance 1937 of Australian Capital Territory

Re Hollands and Commissioner of Police (1981) 3 ALN N78 (Note 46) referred to

HEARING

CANBERRA

26:9:1983

ORDER

1. The Tribunal sets aside the decision of Constable G.J. Williamson, a member of the Police Force authorized in writing for the purpose by the Commissioner of Police, certifying that he objects to the grant of a gun licence to the applicant, and in substitution for that decision the Tribunal decides that no such certification should be made.

2. The Tribunal recommends that the Registrar of Gun Licences grant the gun licence the subject of these proceedings.

DECISION

1. The applicant has sought review by this Tribunal of the decision refusing to grant him a gun licence under the Gun Licence Ordinance 1937 of the Australian Capital Territory ("the Ordinance").

2. The applicant applied to the Registrar of Gun Licences on 17 December 1982 for a gun licence in respect of a 12 gauge pump action shot gun, Model 500. That appliccation was, as is required by s.6 of the Ordinance, referred to the Commissioner of Police. A member of the Australian Federal Police authorized to do so (Constable Gordon James Williamson) having certified that he objected to the grant of the licence to the applicant, the Registrar, in accordance with s.6(2) of the Ordinance, did not grant the licence but, by letter dated 22 February 1983, informed the applicant of the objection and that s.6(2) of the Ordinance prevented him granting the licence.

3. In form, the applicant's application for review seeks review of the refusal of the Commissioner of Police to grant him the licence applied for. However, in view of the provisions of s.6(2) and s.7P(2) of the Ordinance, I think the application should be treated as an application to review the decision of the Commissioner, made through an authorized member of the Police Force, certifying that he objects to the grant of the licence.

Once such a certification is received by the Registrar of Gun Licences, the latter is required by the Ordinance not to grant the licence, but to notify the applicant in writing of the objection. That, as appears above, is what was done in this case. The practical effect of the objection, of course, is to deny the applicant the grant of the licence unless this Tribunal, in pursuance of s.7P of the Ordinance, sets aside the decision of the Commissioner or the member of the Police Force certifying that he objects to the grant of the licence.

4. The Tribunal received in evidence without objection the documents lodged in pursuance of s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Exhibits T1 to T10) and heard oral evidence from the applicant, from Constable G.J. Williamson, and Constables S.J. Corrigan and D. O'Connell (who reported the offence of 25 March 1982 of which the applicant was convicted). It also received in evidence an undated letter from Mr J.W. Goggin of Bredbo, stating that the applicant had been given permission to shoot on his properties on various occasions since October 1982, and a character reference dated 4 July 1983 from a neighbour of the applicant, Mr H.G. Fitzgerald, a retired Air Force officer. A supplementary statement dated 18 July 1983 by Constable Williamson concerning other gun licences the applicant had held was also received in evidence.

5. It is convenient here to set out the basic provisions of the Ordinance relating to the licensing of guns owned by individuals and other provisions of the Ordinance relevant to the present proceedings. These are contained in sections 6, 7P, 5 and 23:

'6(1) Subject to this section, the Registrar may, as he thinks fit, in pursuance of applications made in accordance with Form A in the Schedule _

(a) grant a gun licence to any person who satisfies him that he is not less than sixteen years of age; and

(b) grant a pistol licence to any person who satisfies him that he is not less than eighteen years of age,

and renew any such licence.

(2) The Registrar shall submit to the Commissioner of Police all applications for licences under this section, and, if the Commissioner or a member of the Police Force thereto authorized in writing by him certifies that he objects to the grant of a licence to any applicant, the Registrar shall not grant a licence to that applicant but shall forward to that applicant a notice in writing informing him of the objection.

(3) A gun licence may be granted in respect of a particular gun or a particular class of gun specified in the licence.

(4) A pistol licence may be granted in respect of a particular pistol or a particular class of pistol specified in the licence.

(5) Except where a gun licence or pistol licence is granted in respect of a particular class of gun or pistol, no such licence shall be granted in respect of or relate to more than one gun or pistol.

(6) For the purposes of this Ordinance, a person shall be deemed to be a licensee under a gun or pistol licence in respect only of the gun or pistol or class of gun or pistol for which his licence is granted.

(7) The fee payable for the grant or renewal of a gun licence is $2.

....'

'7P(1) Application may be made to the Tribunal for review of a decision of the Registrar _

(a) refusing to grant or renew a gun licence, a pistol licence or a gun dealer's licence;

(b) refusing to approve a pistol club for the purposes of this Ordinance;

(c) cancelling the approval of a body corporate or pistol club for the purposes of this Ordinance;

(d) refusing to approve a person for the purposes of this Ordinance; or

(e) cancelling the approval of a person for the purposes of this Ordinance.

(2) Application may be made to the Tribunal for a review of a decision of the Commissioner of Police or a member of the Police Force _

(a) certifying that he objects to the grant of a licence; or

(b) recommending that a licence should not be renewed.'

'5(1) Any person, not being the licensee under a gun licence or a pistol licence, who has in his possession any gun or pistol, as the case may be, shall be guilty of an offence.

Penalty: $100 or imprisonment for three months.

(2) Any person, not being the licensee under a gun licence or a pistol licence, who uses or carries any gun or pistol, as the case may be, between the hours of seven ante-meridiem and seven post-meridiem shall be guilty of an offence.

Penalty: $100 or imprisonment for six months.

(3) Any person, not being the licensee under a gun licence or a pistol licence, who uses or carries any gun or pistol, as the case may be, between the hours of seven post-meridiem and seven ante-meridiem shall be guilty of an offence.

Penalty: $500 or imprisonment for six months, or both.'

'23. Any person who discharges any gun or pistol in or near a street or public place unless thereto authorized by the Minister or, in the case of a member of the Police Force, by the Commissioner of Police or on private property without the consent previously obtained of the owner or occupier of such property shall be guilty of an offence.

Penalty: Twenty dollars.'

6. The applicant is an 18 year old apprentice electrician, employed by the ACT Electricity Authority. The essence of the case against granting him a gun licence was set out in the s.37 statement (Exhibit T2). The signatory to that statement, Constable Williamson, concluded that the applicant was not a person who could be trusted with the possession of firearms without prejudicing the public safety or the peace and that he 'was a person who had demonstrated little regard for the requirement of the Gun Licence Ordinance 1937'.

7. The facts on which Constable Williamson formed his conclusion were detailed in paragraphs (a) to (d) of that statement. I set these out as they are crucial to a consideration of the applicant's case:

'(a) On the 25th day of March 1982 the applicant was interviewed by Constable S.J. CORRIGAN relative to an offence of discharging a firearm in a public place;

(b) On the 19th day of August 1982 the applicant was convicted of the abovementioned offence;

(c) On the 31st day of August 1982, the applicant admitted to Constable BLACKA that he had shot bullets into his own car to impress others by subsequently claiming to have been shot at by a farmer;

(d) On the 24th day of December 1982 the applicant admitted to me that he had

(i) intended shooting on properties near Bredbo but had not obtained permission from any particular property owner in the area;

(ii) been convicted and fined on 19 August 1982 relative to discharging a firearm in a public place; and

(iii)shot holes in his motor vehicle so as to be able to tell others at a hotel that a farmer had chased and shot at him after having caught him spot lighting.'

8. So far as paragraphs (a), (b) and (d)(ii) are concerned, the applicant denied that he had been convicted of the offence stated. And indeed he had not. It was conceded both by Mr M.B. Edwards (for the respondent), and by Constable Williamson in answer to questions put to him by the Tribunal, that the offence of which the applicant was convicted was that provided for by s.5(2), namely, not being the licensee under a gun licence, using or carrying a gun between the hours of 7 a.m. and 7 p.m. Paragraphs (c) and (d)(iii) relate to the same occurrence to which I shall return below. Paragraph (d)(i) suggests that the applicant had formed the intention to shoot on properties without permission, but this was denied by the applicant in his evidence and his evidence is supported by the letter from Mr Coggin referred to in paragraph 4 above.

9. The applicant, early in the proceedings, stated that the account of the shooting of the holes in the mudguard of his own car, as set out in Constable Williamson's record of interview with him on 24 December 1982 (Exhibit T9), was not correct. In his evidence, the applicant said he had answered that he had put the bullet holes in his car when he was with his mate from Cootamundra, not when he was at Coonabarrabran, as stated in the statement, and that he had put the time when this occurred as about the same time as he was caught (i.e. for the offence of which he was subsequently convicted), not, as stated in Constable Williamson's statement, about the same time as he went to Court. The detailed account the applicant gave on oath of his shooting the holes earlier on the day the offence of which he was convicted occurred, and the phonetic similarity between his explanations and the account recorded by Constable Williamson, persuade me to accept the applicant's evidence on this point. It should be noted that Constable Williamson's 'question and answer' statement (Exhibit T9) was compiled some four months after the interview, though it is to be acknowledged that he stated in evidence that it was based on the record he made only some four days after the interview with the applicant. While accepting that the shooting of the holes in his car was a foolish thing, the applicant made two points that tend to diminish the seriousness of the incident. First, he said that the car was an 'old bomb', much battered and dented _ he was, it seems, not in truth lessening to any significant extent such value as the car may have had. He said he was careful to ensure that only the mudguard, and not the motor, was affected by the shots. Secondly, he said the shots into the mudguard were fired from close range, when the car was off the road and in a situation where there was no danger to any person (the only persons there were his friend and himself). It was done, he said, during daylight.

10. Neither of the constables who pulled up and questioned the applicant, and later charged him with the offence of which he was convicted, noticed any bullet holes in the mudguard. But they conceded that it was dark and they had no reason to be looking for any damage to the car. No doubt was, in my view, really cast by their evidence on the applicant's account of the shooting of the holes.

11. The Ordinance does not indicate the criteria to be applied in considering applications by an individual for a gun licence. Indeed, from Constable Williamson's evidence it appears that a person who has attained the minimum age (16 years) and has no police record will ordinarily be granted a licence on payment of the prescribed fee, without further enquiry as to the character of the applicant. Constable Williamson observed in his evidence that the Ordinance did not require an applicant to provide character references. It appears to me, however wise or unwise that policy may be, that the Ordinance does not exhibit any intention that the grant of a gun licence is to be a privilege, reserved to a select few carefully 'screened', responsible persons who establish a sound reason for wishing to possess a firearm. It is in this context that the applicant's application must be considered.

12. I should say at once that the approach that Constable Williamson adopted in considering the application, in the absence of specified criteria, seems to me to be sound enough. He looked to the question whether the applicant could be trusted with the possession of firearms without prejudicing the public safety or peace. Demonstrated irresponsible or improper use of firearms was a relevant factor in considering whether an applicant should be licensed to possess and use firearms. However, on a full consideration of all the evidence in this case and with the benefit of hearing the applicant's evidence given on oath at some length and observing the demeanour and attitude he demonstrated during the hearing, I have concluded that I should set aside the certificate objecting to the grant of the licence he sought.

13. In so concluding, I have accepted the applicant's evidence that he shot the holes in his own car on the same day as he committed the offence in the Tharwa area of which he was convicted. As I have mentioned above, it is likely that the disparity between his evidence on this point and Constable Williamson's statement is explicable on phonetic grounds; I make it clear that I have no doubt that Constable Williamson's statement was made in good faith and in the belief that it was accurate. I formed the impression from Constable Williamson's evidence that if the shooting of the holes in the car had in fact occurred on the day the applicant claimed it to have occurred, he would probably not have objected to the licence sought, but given a warning to the applicant to behave responsibly in future. I was impressed by the applicant's attitude before the Tribunal. He appeared for himself and, although clearly enough not a man of letters, conducted himself with credit and propriety. He impressed as a young man genuinely interested in the sport of shooting. He disclosed neither rancour nor resentment at his conviction for the Tharwa incident, and freely admitted that he had behaved foolishly in shooting the holes in his car and that he had presented poorly and shabbily dressed to Constable Williamson when interviewed in December 1982. Addressing the Tribunal, he said he had learnt his lesson, now knew a great deal more about the shooting laws of the Territory (he had purchased a copy of the Gun Licence Ordinance and put the view that copies should be made more readily available for persons seeking gun licences) and 'guaranteed' that he would observe the laws in future. In his case, I think we have indeed witnessed the getting of wisdom.

14. The decision under review should, in view of the foregoing, be set aside and the applicant's application for a gun licence should now be considered by the Registrar on the basis that there is no objection to it. I indicate my clear view that the licence should be granted.

15. Before concluding, I think I should observe that the evidence in this case demonstrates the desirability of the inclusion in the Ordinance of specific criteria to be applied by the Registrar or the Commissioner of Police in considering applications for gun or pistol licences. This matter was adverted to by Mr A.N. Hall (Senior Member, now Deputy President) in Re Hollands and Commissioner of Police (1981) 3 ALN N78 (Note 46). He commented, inter alia, as follows:

'(iv) Finally, as the (Gun Licence) Ordinance specifies criteria by reference to which other forms of licences or approvals may be granted (cf ss.7F(2) and 10(3)), and as the requirement that an applicant for a gun or pistol licence must be a 'fit and proper person' to hold such a licence appears, quite properly in my view, to be fundamental to the administration of the gun and pistol licensing provisions, it may be more satisfactory if that requirement, and any other appropriate requirements (cf s.7F(2)), were specified in the Ordinance rather than being left to implication.'

I would respectfully adopt those comments. If the control of firearms by individuals is to be left as it is under the Ordinance, it would seem that only a person who has a recorded offence or is notoriously an associate of criminals is likely to be challenged on an application for a gun licence however unfitted he may be to possess firearms. The question whether there should be a requirement on an applicant for a licence to demonstrate some positive evidence of fitness to possess the gun in respect of which the licence is sought _ if only in the form of character references from responsible persons in the community _ is a matter to which the policy Department might with profit direct its attention.
