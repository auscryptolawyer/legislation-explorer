DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No VT97/252

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re KEITH JAMES McCORKELL

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



Decision No 13136



DECISION



Tribunal Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member

Date 28 July 1998

Place Melbourne

Decision The decision under review is set aside and in its stead the Tribunal allows in full the objections lodged against amended assessment of income tax based on income of the years ended 30 June 1991, 1992, 1993 and 1994 inclusive.



......(Sgd) B. H. Pascoe......

Senior Member



INCOME TAX - sale of land by subdivision - land previously used as orchard - whether carrying on a business of subdividing and selling land - whether profit assessable income

Income Tax Assessment Act 1986

Stevenson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation as ATC 4476

Case W89 89 ATC 538

Statham v Federal Commission of Taxation (1988) 20 ATR 228

Casimaty v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1997) 37 ATR 358

Scottish Australian Mining Co Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1980) [1950] HCA 16; 81 CLR 188

REASONS FOR DECISION

28 July 1998 Senior Member B. H. Pascoe



  1. This is an application for review of a decision of the respondent to disallow objections against amended assessments of income tax for the years ended 30 June 1991 to 1994 inclusive. The amended assessments were issued to include profit on sale of land and impose additional tax for incorrect returns. The amounts involved were:-
Additional Income
Year ended 30 June 1991
$ 50,849
Year ended 30 June 1992
$ 55,106
Year ended 30 June 1993
$152,442
Year ended 30 June 1994
$ 16,042
  1. At the hearing the applicant was represented by Mr T Murphy of Counsel and the respondent by an officer of the respondent. Evidence was given by the applicant, Mr McCorkell, a consulting engineer and surveyor, Mr Muir, and two real estate agents, Mr Kiss and Mr Reed. The respondent called two officers of the respondent, one who made the decision on the objection and the other who was responsible for the audit which led to the amended assessment.
  2. The basic facts surrounding the sale of land were not in dispute. The property in question consisted of approximately 15 hectares on the outskirts of Shepparton. The property was divided by a drainage channel with approximately 4 hectares on the western side and the balance on the eastern side of the channel. Since some time before 1917 when the applicant was born it had been used as a commercial orchard. Mr McCorkell had lived on the property since birth and after working full time with his father after 1931, inherited the property on his father’s death in 1960 and continued the business as an orchardist. On 30 August, 1984 a permit was issued for subdivision of part of the 4 hectare lot into 40 housing lots. On 25 September 1990 a further permit was issued for a further 17 housing lots as a subdivision of the balance of the 4 hectares. On completion of the subdivision a total of 37 allotments were sold being 11 in the year ended 30 June 1990, 5 in the 1991 year, 7 in the 1992 year, 13 in the 1993 year and one in the 1994 year. The profit of these latter four years was included in the amended assessments issued on 2 April 1996. The calculation of the profit was based on the value of the land at the commencement of the subdivision plus the costs of the subdivision divided by the number of allotments and was not in dispute. What was in dispute was whether the profit on the sales was assessable on the basis that Mr McCorkell was carrying on a business with the intention of profit.
  3. Mr McCorkell gave evidence that the land was rezoned as future residential zone in the early 1980s and, at that time he began to think of selling the land as he contemplated retirement and had no family to continue the fruit growing business. In addition a growing number of residential properties were abutting his land with growing concerns and complaints about spraying the orchard which was, in some instances, within 15 metres of homes. He said that, during the 1980’s he was approached by developers offering to purchase the land but rejected the offers as being too low. He stated that, in 1982, he met with a representative of a firm of surveyors and engineers who were supervising an adjoining subdivision and arranging a common boundary fence and discussed the possibility of subdividing his land. He was told that it would be difficult to sewer the land and took the matter no further. He said that in late 1987, he met with Mr Muir, a consulting engineer and surveyor, who advised that the land could be sewered and who prepared a draft plan of subdivision. Mr McCorkell was then introduced to a consultant, Mr Wilkinson, who made applications on his behalf to the council during 1988. He said that the association with Mr Wilkinson was terminated in May 1989 after a dispute about fees. He then met with Mr Kiss and Mr Reed and accepted their offer to act as joint estate agents to sell the land. Mr McCorkell said that the only contractual arrangements which he entered into were with the surveyor and engineer who subcontracted the work on the subdivision to contractors and with the joint estate agents who recommended sale prices and dealt with all potential purchasers. He could recollect only referring three people to the agents and those were his nephew and the nephew’s two sons who were interested in purchasing allotments. He maintained that he did not personally discuss sales of land with any prospective purchasers, had no involvement with advertising other than contributing to advertising costs on two occasions and accepted the advice of the surveyors and estate agents. Mr McCorkell said that he obtained a short term loan from the Bank to supplement his own funds to pay for the first stage of the subdivision and repaid the loan from the first sales of land. He said that a separate bank account, titled “development account” was opened at the suggestion of the bank manager to keep the cash and sale proceeds separate from his orchard business. He stated that on advice from Mr Muir, the subdivision was done in two stages for practical and financial reasons, particularly to avoid borrowings which would be required to subdivide the entire 4 hectares at once.
  4. Mr McCorkell accepted that he wrote to the State Rivers and Water Supply Commission in 1976 to enquire about possible work on or relocation of the drainage channel which bisected his property. In that letter he indicated an intention of developing the land for housing allotments. However, Mr McCorkell said that, at that time, his expectation was a sale to a developer if the appropriate rezoning and planning permit could be obtained. He said that the drain had been a long standing concern for many property owners affected by the drainage channel. He agreed that several letters were exchanged in the intervening years regarding the possible relocation of the drain and the grant of planning permits but maintained that the major part of these were done on his behalf by consultants. Mr McCorkell said that he ceased carrying on the orchard business in 1991 and leased the land east of the drainage channel where spraying was not a problem to a neighbour who continued the orchard activities.
  5. Mr Muir gave evidence that he was first contacted by Mr McCorkell in November 1987 to advise and draw up plans of subdivision. He said that the role of his firm was to design and plan the subdivision, obtain permits and tender out the construction. He said that bills for all construction work were sent to his office, approved and then forwarded to Mr McCorkell for payment. He was not aware of any direct contact by Mr McCorkell with a sub-contractor. Mr Muir said that the subdivision was a standard subdivision with no work which was non-essential to the normal requirement of roads and services. He said that there was no formal contract with Mr McCorkell who gave him a free reign to plan and control the subdivision. He was not aware of any direct contact by Mr McCorkell with the Council and that any documents signed by him were prepared by the firm of surveyors and engineers for signature. Mr Muir was not aware of planning applications prior to his involvement.
  6. Both Mr Kiss and Mr Reed gave similar evidence. They said that they were appointed by Mr McCorkell as joint agents and, together, developed the sale strategy and advertising. All of their recommendations on pricing etc. were accepted by Mr McCorkell and they alone dealt with prospective purchasers. Both recalled Mr McCorkell referring relatives to them who purchased blocks at full price and full rates of commission were paid. They confirmed that Mr McCorkell gave no directions on marketing advertising or sale prices but left these matters to the agents and accepted their recommendations. Apart from referring the relatives the only other direct involvement of Mr McCorkell they could recall was that he built an advertising board from scrap timber which the agents had sign written at their expense.
  7. The evidence of the appeals and advisings officer of the respondent was primarily related to the obtaining of documents from various authorities and an inspection of the property in September/October 1997. The audit officer of the respondent gave evidence of inspecting the property, interviewing Mr McCorkell and obtaining copies of documents from him and from the Council. Apart from expressing a view that the activities of Mr McCorkell went beyond a mere realisation of a capital asset and amounted to the carrying of a business of developing and selling land, the evidence of these two officers added little to the factual material before the hearing.
  8. For the applicant, it was submitted that the activities in the relevant years amounted to no more than were necessary to realise his asset to its best advantage and did not constitute the carrying on of any business. It was said that, apart from some earlier simple approaches as an owner to investigate the possibility of disposing of land by subdivision, Mr McCorkell had no active involvement in the subdivision and sale of his land. He had no office, letterhead or business organisation, had no direct dealings with contractors or potential purchasers, did not advertise himself, no substantial sums were borrowed and simply arranged for others to do what was necessary to prepare the land for sale and sell it.
  9. For the respondent it was submitted that the activities of Mr McCorkell amounted to the carrying on of a business of subdividing and selling land. It was said that his personal involvement in seeking rezoning and planning permits, commencing in 1976, and the extensive street construction and provision of services went beyond a mere realisation of a capital asset. It was argued that the use of professionals was appropriate to provide skills not possessed by Mr McCorkell but his attendance at meetings, making personal applications, building a sale board etc. were not indications of a passive role. It was not clear why it was argued for the respondent that Mr McCorkell’s acceptance of a recommendation by the estate agents and his solicitor for restrictive covenants to be placed on the allotments was an indication of a business activity.
  10. The respondent relied heavily on the decision in Stevenson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 91 ATC 4476. In that case the taxpayer owned and worked a farm which had been in his family since 1904. In the early 1970’s he decided to sell most of his farm and retain a few acres for his own use. He was granted permission to sell residential blocks of the land subject to conditions requiring substantial expenditure on the provision of water and sewerage reticulation before any blocks could be sold. However, he could not find a developer who would pay his asking price and late in 1976 he decided to fulfil the conditions himself and sell the land in subdivided blocks. The subdivision was carried out in stages from 1977 and more than 180 out of 220 subdivided blocks were sold between 1980 and 1986. In dismissing the appeal from a decision of this Tribunal (Case W89, 89 ATC 538) which found that the profit on sale was assessable, Jenkinson J in the Federal Court concluded that the Tribunal had made no error of law in its finding that the activities of the taxpayer amounted to the carrying on of a business of subdividing and selling land. In the Tribunal decision, Deputy President Thompson said (at pp 545-546):
"31. As to the taxpayer’s personal involvement with the subdivision of the land, he was the sole decision-maker in respect of all matters of consequence in relation to it, he obtained professional advice but, except for the submission of the original planning application early in 1975, he chose for himself and directed the course of action to be followed. Thus at all times he fixed the price to be asked for the land. He sought and obtained finance and subsequently, having been refused further finance by that lender, he personally sought and obtained it from another source. He controlled the marketing of blocks after subdivision, he dealt personally with many prospective purchasers when they came to inspect the land. On many occasions over the years he dealt directly with the X Shire Council and its officers about the subdivision of the land and their requirements in relation to it. He similarly dealt personally with the State Rivers and Water Supply Commission; he personally proposed to it the cheaper way of building the sea wall and convinced it to accept his proposal. In addition to his management role at times in the course of the development he did work on the land himself to save the cost of employing a labourer. For instance, he personally cut and cleared the bullrushes growing in the lake adjacent to the land."

In concluding that the profit was assessable, the Tribunal said (at p552):

"60. In coming to the conclusion which I have reached I have taken into account in the taxpayer’s favour the fact that he has never subdivided, developed and sold any land which was not part of his farm. I regard as of neutral effect that his intention in subdividing and developing the part of his land adjacent to the lake was to enhance its value so that, in his words, he could get ‘the top dollar’ for it. What has caused me to decide that his activities extended beyond what can be accepted as being directed to and constituting the mere realisation of a capital asset, albeit with its value enhanced, is their extent. In particular I regard as significant the degree of his personal involvement in the planning, in the negotiations with the Shire Council and the State Rivers and Water Supply Commission, in obtaining finance, in the employment of contractors, in the marketing of the blocks and in their actual sale. The subdivision and development was substantial. The land has been subdivided into over 180 small blocks. The development has turned farmland which had been unserved by water supply or sewerage and without a made road into fully serviced residential blocks with a sealed road and drainage. The taxpayer not only obtained finance but he risked it."

Not surprisingly, Mr Murphy sought to distinguish the facts in this case from those in Stevenson (supra) and relied on the decisions in Statham v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1988) 20 ATR 228 and Casimaty v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1977) 37 ATR 358. The first of these latter cases was a decision of the Full Federal Court on appeal from a decision of this Tribunal and the second a decision of Ryan J on appeal from a decision of the respondent. In both cases the Court considered that the sale by subdivision of farming land constituted a mere realisation of the asset and not proceeds of a business. In Statham (supra) the Court said (at pp 235-236):

" The questions which the tribunal had to determine were whether the subdivision of the land in question amounted, on the one hand, merely to the advantageous and enterprising realisation of a capital asset or, on the other hand, to a business of land development carried on by the owners (s 25(1)) or to an undertaking or scheme in which an essential element was the purpose of profit-making (s 26(a)).

We agree with the submission made on behalf of the applicants that the way in which the subdivision and sale of the land progressed was simple and had few of the hall marks of a business enterprise. The following matters are significant:
(a) the owners were at first content to sell the land as one parcel, but were unable to do so;

(b) no moneys were borrowed by them, although a guarantee was provided to the Kingaroy Shire Council by way of bank guarantee;

(c) only very limited clearing and earthworks were involved;

(d) the owners relied upon the Kingaroy Shire Council to itself carry out roadworks, kerbing, electricity and sewerage works which were required to be done;

(e) the owners did not erect buildings on the land; not even, for example, a site-office;

(f) they had no business organisation, no manager, no office, no secretary, and no letterhead;

(g) Dr Bickerton maintained his medical practice;

(h) the owners did not advertise the land for sale;

(i) apart from the Kingaroy Shire Council’s activities, the owners did not engage any contractors, although they did obtain some professional advice;

(j) the books kept in relation to the sales of land were kept by Mrs Bickerton; and

(k) the land was sold simply by listing it with local real estate agents.
The matters which have been listed above strongly suggest to us that the owners were not conducting a business or engaging in a profit-making undertaking or scheme."

Also in that case the Court was of the view that reported cases had well established that the mere realisation of an asset as a profit does not necessarily render the profit taxable. It considered that the statement of principle of Williams T in Scottish Australian Mining Co Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1950] HCA 16; (1950) 81 CLR 188 (at p 195) had been approved in a number of later decisions. His Honour had said:

" The facts would, in my opinion, have to be very strong indeed before a court could be induced to hold that a company which had not purchased or otherwise acquired land for the purpose of profit-making by sale was engaged in the business of selling land and not merely realizing it when all that the company had done was to take the necessary steps to realize the land to the best advantage, especially land which had been acquired and used for a different purpose which it was no longer businesslike to carry out."
  1. In Casimaty (supra), the taxpayer acquired land by gift from his father in 1975. To alleviate financial hardships and deteriorating health the taxpayer sold approximately two thirds of the property by eight subdivisions between 1975 and 1995. Ryan J found in favour of the taxpayer. After considering many reported decisions including Stevenson’s case his Honour said (at p373):
"An examination of the reasoning in Stevenson’s case confirms that whether the subdivisional activity is sufficiently extensive and systematic to amount to the conduct of a business is, as Lockhart J observed in Crow’s case a question of fact. Jenkinson J was not required to resolve that question of fact for himself. He was concerned only to ensure that the tribunal had not committed any error of law in arriving at the conclusion which it did. His Honour did not distil from the authorities a principle of law that a subdivision involving a hundred or more lots, the construction of roads and the reticulation of water to each lot could never amount to a mere realization of a capital asset. Any such principle would run counter to the views expressed by all but one member of the High Court in FCT v Williams [1972] HCA 31; (1972) 127 CLR 226; 3 ATR 283; 72 ATC 4188 where Gibbs J observed (at CLR 249; ATR 291; ATC 4194-5):

An owner of land who holds it until the price of land has risen and then subdivides and sells it is not thereby engaging in an adventure in the nature of trade, or carrying out a profit-making scheme. The situation is not altered by the fact that the landowner seeks and acts upon the advice of an expert as to the best method of subdivision and sale or by the fact that he carries out work such as grading, levelling, road building and the provision of reticulation for water and power to enable the land to be sold to its best advantage. The proceeds resulting from the mere realization of a capital asset are not income either in accordance with ordinary concepts or within the second limb of s 26(a), even though the realization is carried out in an enterprising way so as to secure the best price.

..."
  1. In my view the facts of this case are very similar to those in Statham’s case. I accept the evidence of Mr McCorkell and the other witnesses called for the applicant that he had no direct involvement in the planning and contracting work for the subdivision or in selling the blocks. Using the matters which were found significant by the Court in Statham, it is appropriate to set out my findings in a similar format.
    • (a) Mr McCorkell had initially contemplated selling the land as one parcel but had not been able to attract a satisfactory offer;
    • (b) a small amount of money was borrowed for a short period but he was not prepared to risk funds for the complete subdivision at the outset. A guarantee was provided by Mr McCorkell’s bank to the Shepparton Water Board in connection with the provision of water and sewerage reticulation in the normal form required by that authority;
    • (c) the works carried out in developing the subdivisions were no more than what was necessary to secure the approval of the authorities and enhance the presentation of the individual allotments for sale. In the words of Mr Muir “It was no different to any other residential subdivision, and a fairly straightforward one at that.”
    • (d) Mr McCorkell relied upon the surveyors and engineers represented by Mr Muir to carry out the work required. That firm engaged the contractors and approved their accounts for payment by Mr McCorkell. He had no direct contractual relationship with any contractor.
    • (e) Mr McCorkell had no site office or building on the land and no direct contact with contractors or potential purchasers.
    • (f) Mr McCorkell had no business organisation, office or letterhead.
    • (g) He continued as an orchardist until 1991 when he retired and leased the balance of the property as an orchard.
    • (h) He had no involvement in advertising or promoting the sale of the land. The only evidence of any connection with advertising was the building by him of a board on which advertising prepared by the agents was placed and a contribution to the costs of advertising designed and arranged by the agents.
    • (i) Mr McCorkell obtained professional advice from surveyors, his solicitor a consultant and estate agents but in all cases relied on their advice and appointed them to carry out the required functions.
    • (j) A separate bank account was maintained to which proceeds of sale were deposited directly by the estate agents or the solicitor. No evidence of any more formal records or accounts was provided.
    • (k) The land was sold simply by placing it in the hands of two local real estate agents who recommended and had accepted prices. All negotiations for sale were conducted by the agents. Mr McCorkell had no direct contact with potential buyers other than three relations who were sent by him to the estate agents to discuss possible purchase.

The respondent saw as significant the involvement of Mr McCorkell in seeking planning permits and indicating an intention to develop the land for subdivision from 1976. It was also seen as relevant that, in May 1989, he requested an opinion from the respondent as to the potential taxation implications of subdividing and selling the land. In the letter he indicated possible options of financing the subdivision including taking a partner or borrowing. I am unable to see that either of these events can be seen as evidence of a business activity. Having reached a decision that the future conduct of an orchard on the subject land was likely to be difficult as a result of encroaching housing estates and advancing years, it is reasonable that Mr McCorkell would consider options available to him to realise the property to its best advantage. The seeking of permits was necessarily undertaken to enable sale of the land. It may well have been that, if Mr McCorkell had chosen an option involving partners or substantial borrowings, there may have been some indicia of business activities. But he did not. In my view he adopted a relatively passive role in the mere realisation by the most advantageous means of an asset which he had when he decided to abandon the intention of continuing to use it as an orchard.

  1. I find that the applicant was not carrying on a business of subdividing and selling land in the relevant years. The proceeds of sale were of a capital nature and neither those proceeds or the profit on sale constituted assessable income. It follows that the decision under review should be set aside.
