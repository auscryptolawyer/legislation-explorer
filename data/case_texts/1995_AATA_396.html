CATCHWORDS

TAXATION Sales Tax exemption - soft serve yoghurt - whether exempt as food for human consumption (frozen yoghurt) or assessable as confectionery, an ingredient for confectionery, or as ice-cream substitute - ordinary and trade meaning of “yoghurt” considered

REASONS FOR DECISION

22 December 1995 Deputy President T E Barnett

Associate Professor S D Hotop (Senior Member)

Associate Professor R D Fayle (Senior Member)



  1. The applicant is an importer and seller of a frozen product manufactured in the United States of America, marketed as HHF Soft Serve Yoghurt” [“SSY”].
  2. The respondent has assessed sales tax on the wholesale sale price of SSY, sold by the applicant to retailers, who then on-sell the product to the public, after partly thawing and stirring it to aerate. SSY is sold retail at around 4 degrees Celsius.
  3. In sum, the issue before the Tribunal is whether the wholesale sale of SSY is exempt from sales tax.
  4. The applicant was represented by Mr R Sceales, solicitor, and the respondent, by Mr S Bhojani, of counsel.
  5. The documents filed pursuant to s37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 were before the Tribunal and the following affidavits were tendered in evidence:

- by Mr Fred M Di Cosola, exhibit A1; sworn on 25 August 1995

- by Mr Michael P Jackson, exhibit R1; sworn on 24 August 1995

- by Dr Nigel A Thomas, exhibit R2; sworn on 15 August 1995

- by Mr Callum Elder, exhibit R3; sworn on 31 July 1995

- by Dr Roger Macbean, exhibit R4; sworn on 31 July 1995

- by Mr Norman A Reynolds, exhibit R5; sworn on 31 July 1995; and

- by Ms Julie Coates, exhibit R6; sworn on 2 August 1995.

None of the witnesses were called to give oral evidence.

  1. Because wholesale sales of SSY had been made by the applicant between 1 December 1989 and 31 January 1993 the respondent issued two assessments of sales tax on 27 April 1995. The first, pursuant to s10(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No.6) 1930, related to sales from 1 December 1989 to 31 December 1992 [“the first assessment”], and the second, pursuant to s10(1) of the Sales Tax Assessment Act 1992 (“the 1922 Act”) related to sales from 1 January 1993 to 31 January 1993 [“the second assessment”].
  2. The Tribunal understands that the first assessment is based on a contention by the respondent that although SSY is a food for human consumption, which would be exempt from sales tax under Item 23 of Schedule 1 to the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Act 1935 (“the 1935 Act”), it is not exempt because it falls within one of the exceptions to that Item, namely, it is either an “ingredient of confectionery” (clause 23(d)) or “goods being ... ice-cream substitutes, frozen confectionery (other than frozen yoghurt) ..., or similar goods ...” (clause 23(h)).
  3. The Tribunal understands that the second assessment is based on a similar contention, namely, that SSY does not qualify for exemption from sales tax under the 1992 Act, because it falls outside the general exemption of food items as specified in Chapter 7 of Schedule 1 to that Act, being within the specific exclusions from exemption as specified in s.14 of that Act. Those exclusions include “confectionery, or goods of a kind marketed principally as ingredients for confectionery;” (s.14(b)), and “ice-cream goods” (s.14(f)). The terms “confectionery” and “ice-cream goods” are relevantly defined in s.3(2) of the 1992 Act as follows:

“(3)2 In this Act, unless the contrary intention appears:

...

‘confectionery’ includes:

(a) goods marketed as confectionery or consisting principally of confectionery;

...

‘ice-cream goods’ means the following goods, or any similar goods:



(a) ice-cream, ... and ice-cream substitutes;

(b) frozen confectionery (other than frozen yoghurt);

...”.

  1. The applicant contends that SSY is food for human consumption (which is exempt) and that it is not confectionery, frozen confectionery, ice-cream or an ice-cream substitute or an ingredient for confectionery (which are taxable) and, further, because it is frozen yoghurt, it is excluded from frozen confectionery (which is taxable).
  2. The evidence will be discussed shortly. The parties agree that when SSY is sold by the applicant, (which sale gives rise to an assessable dealing under the legislation), it is in a frozen state and it is the retailer who partly thaws, stirs and aerates SSY in preparation for sale to its customers at about 4 degrees Celsius. Nothing further is added to SSY by the retailer, although it may be sold with other products. See the evidence of Ms Julie Coates (paragraph 31 below). It is also accepted by the parties that there is no issue as to the classification of SSY as a “food for human consumption”. The Tribunal accepts these agreed facts as both reasonable and appropriate. The only issue before the Tribunal is the classification of SSY. If it is classified as confectionery, an ingredient of confectionery, frozen confectionery (not being frozen yoghurt) on the one hand or, alternatively, an ice-cream or ice-cream substitute, on the other, then it is not an exempt food for human consumption. As will be discussed below the key issue will be whether SSY is yoghurt or frozen yoghurt at the time of retail sale.

THE EVIDENCE OF MR DI COSOLA

  1. Mr Di Cosola is a resident of the United States of America and at the time of the hearing was the Vice President-Operations of HHF, a Division of GPFH Inc. It is that company which exports the SSY to the applicant.
  2. Mr Di Cosola holds a bachelor’s degree in Science and graduate certificates in Food Science and is a certified microbiologist in the State of California and a member of the Institute of Food Technologists. He also serves on the Board of Directors of the International Ice Cream Association. His affidavit, among other things, describes the process used to manufacture SSY to the point where it is exported in a frozen state to the applicant.
  3. Many aspects of Mr Di Cosola’s affidavit were the subject of objection by the respondent on the grounds that his evidence of what he regards as a proper ordinary meaning of a word, and what he contends is an appropriate construction of legislation and his expert evidence on issues where the underlying factual basis for his conclusion, opinion or assertion has not been clearly stated, are inadmissible. The respondent referred the Tribunal to several cases in this respect. It was submitted that whilst the Tribunal is not bound by the rules of evidence (s33(1)(c) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975) that does not mean that the Tribunal shall not apply those rules in appropriate circumstances and, in any event, regard must be given to principles underlying rules of evidence, Re Kevin and Minister for Capital Territory (1979) 2 ALD 238 at 241-243 citing passages from Brennan J’s decision in Re Pochi and Minister for Immigration and Ethnic Affairs [1979] AATA 64; (1979) 2 ALD 33 at 41.
  4. The Tribunal, mindful of its obligation to determine the facts and inform itself on any matter in such manner as it thinks appropriate, accepted the whole of Mr Di Cosola’s affidavit of 25 August 1995 in evidence but has taken into account only those matters of a technical, scientific or factual nature which are reproduced in the passages quoted below.

“6 METHOD OF MANUFACTURE OF SSY

6.1 SSY is manufactured by means of a two phase production system, which involves the independent processing and later mixing of two distinctly different solutions, comprising a ‘dairy phase’ and a ‘sweetener phase’.

6.2 After these ‘phases’ are separately processed, they are joined together in one tank, and then blended together via a high intensity sheering process. The finished product is poured into cartons, which are then frozen for shipment to customers.

6.3 THE DAIRY PHASE

6.3.1 The dairy phase relates to the processing of non-fat milk, fortified with condensed skim milk to a relatively high milk serum solids level. This milk solution is homogenised and pasteurised at a high temperature, and held at temperature via a high temperature short time pasteurisation process.

6.3.2 After pasteurisation, the milk solution is cooled to 110oF. It is then inoculated with a bulk starter culture medium which contains L. bulgaricus, S. thermophilus and acidophilus.

6.3.3 The resulting solution is fermented for up to 6 hours achieving, in the process, a titratable acidity of 1.1% to 1.2% lactic acid, with a corresponding pH of 4.4 to 4.5. At this point in the process, the milk solution is fully gelled to a firm custard-like consistency. It is cooled to 35-40oF and held in the tank for later processing.

6.3.4 [Parts omitted] When ripening yoghurt to a titratable acidity (TA) of 1.2%, this acid level would have a corresponding pH in a range of 4.45 to 4.65.

6.3.5 [Omitted]

6.3.6 Immediately following the fermentation stage of the milk ingredients, the product is completely curdled in the form of a thick set gel. At this point in the process, the titratable acidity of the product is such that it would have a pH equivalent in the range of 4.45 to 4.65, and is not even pourable.

6.3.7 [Omitted]

6.3.8 [Parts omitted] L. acidophilus ... is adversely affected by lower pH levels such as 4.5, and thrives at pH levels about 5.

6.3.9 [Parts omitted] ... many ... [non fruit] flavours act as buffers which tend to raise the pH of the finished blended product. The addition of alkalised cocoa powder to yoghurt partially neutralises the lactic acid. It does not, however, adversely affect the cell count or viability of the yoghurt organisms.

6.3.10 [Parts omitted] The addition of increased amounts of sugar, or pH neutral flavouring agents will tend to raise the pH of the finished product.

6.3.11-12 [Omitted]

6.4 THE SWEETENER PHASE

6.4.1 The sweetener phase consists of mixing and processing of liquid sugar, liquid corn syrups, malto-dextrin, vegetable stabilisers/emulsifiers and, sometimes, cocoa powders.

(The Tribunal sought further evidence in this respect and in relation to what is the measure of the “relatively high level” referred to in 6.5.2 below. However, due to no fault of Mr Sceales this was not forthcoming.)

6.4.2 This solution is pasteurised and homogenised, and is immediately cooled to 35-40oF.

6.5 COMBINATION OF DAIRY PHASE AND SWEETENER PHASE

6.5.1 The two cold phases are then pumped together into the same tank. The gelled dairy phase and cold viscous sweetener phase are agitated under high speed until homogenised.

6.5.2 [Part omitted] This mix contains a relatively high level of low pH sweeteners and other ingredients, which results in the combined phases having a finished pH of about 5.7 to 5.8.

6.5.3 The cold, thoroughly blended and homogenised solution is then pumped through an in-line APV hydrosheer homogenising valve at high PSI. The sheering process breaks down any remaining curd or precipitated milk protein, resulting in a thin viscosity liquid mix which can appropriately function in a soft serve freezer and soft serve dispenser. This product is then packed into cartons and is frozen for shipment to customers.

6.5.4 The finished product, before freezing, is no longer in a curdled state because the dairy phase has been blended with liquid sugars and various flavour compounds and then homogenised and vigorously agitated, as I have described. The addition of these other ingredients to the hard gelled dairy phased portion results in a finished pH of between 5.8 to 6.0. This is because SSY is diluted with liquid sugars, alkalised cocoa powders and other flavour ingredients.

6.5.5 - 7.2 [Omitted]

7.3 [Part omitted] The product of the dairy phase and the sweetener phase is, ..., no longer coagulated, as a result of the sheering and mixing process. It does, however, contain viable and abundant organisms. L. bulgaricus, S. thermophilus and L. acidophilus are collectively present at levels in excess of 25 000 000 per gram. ...

7.4 - 7.6 [Omitted]

7.7 At the time of retail sale, SSY, is thawed, and is aerated in a mixing machine, so that the product is dispensed as a soft serve [product]. At the point of sale after aeration and thawing the product is not dissimilar from the more widely known soft serve ice-creams, but it is ... [distinguishable] because of the high level of viable and abundant organisms present in it. The product was designed to taste like ice-cream, so there might be a tendency to mistake the product for ice-cream from a consumer orientated, non-technical perspective.”

  1. Apart from paragraph 8.2.5, which repeats an earlier fact that during the dairy phase the fermentation process lowers the pH level to 4.5, the balance of Mr Di Cosola’s affidavit was not taken into account by the Tribunal being either unhelpful expressions of opinion, or statements which the parties agree are irrelevant.
  2. Attached to the affidavit is a summary of pH and titratable acidity levels of the 52 SSY products. The pH levels range from 4.82 to 6.29 (with a mean of 5.74 and a standard deviation of 0.413).
  3. The technical and scientific descriptions reproduced above from Mr Di Cosola’s affidavit were not challenged by the respondent or its witnesses and are accepted as factual by the Tribunal.
  4. Also attached to Mr Di Cosola’s affidavit is a copy of The Australian National Food Authority’s “Standard H8 - Yoghurt and Yoghurt Products”, first published in the Commonwealth of Australia Gazette No. P27 on 27 August 1987, and subsequently amended twelve times, the most recent of which was published in Gazette No.P4 of 20 February 1992. The Tribunal accepts this is an Australian standard, setting criteria for a product marketed for sale and consumption in Australia as “yoghurt” or “yogurt”, those being synonymous terms. Relevant parts of that Standard are reproduced below:

“ ...

(2) (a) Yoghurt, reduced fat yoghurt and skim milk yoghurt are the coagulated products prepared by the lactic acid fermentation of preparations of, subject to paragraph (aa), milk or milk products either pasteurised or treated by any other time and temperature combination of equal or greater lethal effect on bacteria, through the action of Streptococcus thermophilus, Lactobacillus bulgaricus or other suitable lactic acid bacteria in such a manner as to ensure the culture micro-organisms used in the fermentation are present in a viable form in each product so prepared.

(aa) Yoghurt, reduced fat yoghurt or skim milk yoghurt may be made in whole or in part from milk other than that of a cow.

(b) Yoghurt, reduced fat yoghurt and skim milk yoghurt -



(i) shall contain -
  • (A) in the case of yoghurt, not less that 30 g/kg of milk fat;
  • (B) in the case of reduced fat yoghurt, not more than 20 g/kg nor less than 5 g/kg of milk fat;
  • (C) in the case of skim milk yoghurt, less than 5 g/kg;
  • (D) not less than 85 g/kg of milk solids non-fat;


(ii) shall have a pH value not greater than 4.5;

(iii) may contain -

  • (A) sugars;

(AA) polydextrose in total proportions of not more than 50 g/kg;

(B) modifying agents specified in Groups I and VI set out in Standard A10 in total proportion of not more than 10 g/kg;

(C) gelatine;

(D) honey within the meaning of Standard K2.


(iv) when examined by the methods prescribed by clause (11) of this Standard, shall comply with the following microbiological standard -
  • (A) coliform count shall not exceed 10 coliforms per gram;
  • (B) yeast count shall not exceed 100 per gram;
  • (C) mould count shall not exceed 100 per gram.”

19. Clause (3) of the Standard deals with the requirements for fruit yoghurt, vegetable yoghurt and nut yoghurt. These must be prepared in the same manner and from the same ingredients as specified in paragraph 2(a) above except that fruit or vegetables or nuts may be added such that the quantities of those ingredients do not fall below specified ratios of grams per kilogram. That clause, in subparagraph (b)(ii) specifies that the yoghurt shall also have a pH value not greater than 4.5. Clause (7) of the Standard deals with flavoured yoghurt and specifies that it must be prepared in the same way as set out in paragraph (2)(a) above, except that flavours, fruit extracts or infusions, vegetable extracts or infusions or nut extracts or infusions or a mixture of two or all of them, are also added. Subparagraph (7)(b)(ii) provides:



“(b) Flavoured yoghurt, flavoured reduced fat yoghurt and flavoured skim milk yoghurt -

(i) may contain -
  • (A) colouring;
  • (B) other foods;


(ii) shall, in all other respects, comply with the standard for yoghurt, reduced fat yoghurt or, as the case may be, skim milk yoghurt prescribed by clause (2) of this Standard.

EVIDENCE OF MR M P JACKSON

  1. Mr Jackson is the Principal Food Scientist in the Environmental Health Branch of the Western Australian Health Department and, at the time of swearing his affidavit, was the Acting Director of that Branch. He deposed that between 1953 and 1991 the National Health and Medical Research Council’s [“NHMRC” ] Food Standards Committee formulated food standards after consultation with Commonwealth and State Government representatives, industry and consumer groups and scientific organisations. If approved by the NHMRC, those Standards would be recommended to the various States and Territories for adoption.
  2. Between 1972 and 1982 Mr Jackson was employed by the Commonwealth Health Department in Canberra, initially as Secretary, and later as Deputy Chairman, of the NHMRC’s Food Standards Committee and in the course of his duties he chaired a number of working parties established by the Committee, including a working party established to review the Standard for Yoghurt (“the Yoghurt Working Party”).
  3. Mr Jackson deposed that he attended a meeting of the NHMRC’s Food Standards Committee in August 1975. At the time the NHMRC Standard for yoghurt defined it as “the clotted products made from pasteurised preparations of milk products by the agency or organisms of the types lactobacillus bulgaricus or lactobacillus acidophilus and Streptococcus thermophilus”. That standard contained no reference to pH levels but, in Mr Jackson’s opinion, “its reference to ‘clotted products’ imports that coagulation has occurred, which can only happen when the pH level of the product has decreased to within a range of about 4.2 - 4.5.” The Committee accepted a recommendation of the Food Microbiology Sub-committee that: “A limitation for pH of 4.5 should be included into the Standard for Yoghurt as yoghurt is produced by the acid fermentation until the pH is about 4.2, and by setting an upper limit of pH 4.5 ensures that the acidity of the food is sufficiently high so that the growth of pathogenic micro-organisms is inhibited”. The Committee accordingly recommended to the NHMRC that the Standard for Yoghurt be amended by the insertion of an upper limit of pH 4.5.
  4. This recommendation was accepted and as noted above, was eventually reflected in clause (2) of Standard H8.
  5. Mr Jackson’s affidavit also refers to the operations of the Yoghurt Working Party which was established in 1981 to conduct a complete review of the Stand and relating to yoghurt and milk products. At that time there was in progress an expansion of the types of yoghurt products coming onto the market, including products fruit and nuts, and low fat and reduced fat yoghurts. After a process involving extensive consultation, the submission of a draft revised standard for comment by interested bodies, and the approval of the States and Territories, a Revised Standard for yoghurt and yoghurt products was approved by the NHMRC in October 1982 and was subsequently incorporated into the Food Standards Code and was given legislative force in the States and Territories. The Revised Standard formed the basis of the Australian National Food Authority’s “Standard H8-Yoghurt and Yoghurt Products” referred to in paragraph 18 above and subsequent amendments of Standard H8 have not affected the essential provisions relating to yoghurt contained in the Revised Standard and incorporated in Standard H8.
  6. Mr Jackson’s affidavit also provides a history of the developments which led to the creating of the National Food Authority [“NFA”] which replaced the NHMRC in 1991 as the body responsible for developing and recommending the adoption of Food Standards by the States and the Territories. Mr Jackson, who is presently a member of the NFA, refers in his affidavit to an application in June 1992 by Lactos Pty Ltd of Burnie, Tasmania to vary Standard H8 to accommodate a frozen soft-serve product having a pH of 5.6 to 6.1. That application was refused by the NFA. The NFA was of opinion that this frozen soft serve product did not comply with either the Australian or international definition of yoghurt, a critical criterion of which is a pH level close to 4.5. Mr Jackson goes on to express the opinion that, essentially for this reason, none of the 52 SSY products whose pH levels range from 4.82 to 6.29 falls within the meaning of yoghurt as defined by the Food Standards Code which applies in Australia.

EVIDENCE OF DR N A THOMAS

  1. Dr Thomas is the General Manager of the Marketing and Technology divisions of Peters and Brownes Group of companies which manufacture and distribute several brands of frozen and chilled foods and fruit juice and dairy products, including yoghurt. His affidavit sets out his understanding of the process by which yoghurt is manufactured. These steps are set out in paragraph 6 of the affidavit as follows:


“6. Manufacture of yoghurt generally proceeds by the following method:

(1) Raw milk is standardised, that is, undergoes a process by which the chemistry of milk is adjusted to satisfy legal requirements and precise recipe formulations.
  • (2) The milk is homogenised - the process by which milk fat globules are subjected to pressure treatments to ensure all globules are small and uniform.
  • (3) Stabilisers may be added, to provide viscosity and ‘mouth feel’ - typically these include gelatin, pectin, vegetable gums or starch.
  • (4) The product is pasteurised - involving holding it at a certain temperature (generally 72oC or higher) for a period prior to cooling, which results in all disease-causing bacteria being destroyed.
  • (5) The milk is inoculated with cultures (bacterial organisms) such as streptococcus thermophilus and lactobacillus bulgaricus which results in coagulation.

The Coagulation Process

(6) During the period following inoculation, when the product is kept at a prescribed temperature (usually 42o - 45oC) for a certain time, the culture bacteria grow and produce acid and elicit fermentation of lactose (naturally present in milk) to lactic acid. The effect of the fermentation is to lower the pH level of the product from approximately 6.5 for raw milk to a level in the vicinity of 4.2 to 4.6, when the distinctive ‘gelling’ effect and sour taste result. The product is then cooled to hold the activity of the bacteria and continued acid production, with the pH level dropping by a further one or two points. If required, flavours and fruit additives are added when the yoghurt is cooled to 15oC. The product is then packed and transferred to cold storage.”
  1. Other aspects of Dr Thomas’ evidence relate to his opinion on technical usage of the term “yoghurt” in the dairy products industry and his understanding of aspects of the Australian National Food Authority’s Standard H8. He explained what he understood by the terms “ice-cream” and “ice-cream substitute” as used in the industry. In that regard, Mr Bhojani conceded that SSY is not “ice-cream”, but submitted that it is an “ice-cream substitute”. At paragraphs 12, 13 and 14 of his affidavit, Dr Thomas stated:


“(12) A product marketed in Australia as yoghurt is required by Standard H8 (and, therefore, consistent with the dairy product industry definition) to have a pH of 4.5 or less after all fruits, flavours and additives have been combined with the yoghurt itself, and whether that yoghurt is sold in its natural (thawed) state or frozen.

(13) The pH of some fruit blends that are incorporated into yoghurt are very acidic, and could influence the pH of the overall yoghurt mixture by decreasing it.



(14) Conversely, melon flavoured sauces and chocolate flavoured sauces are less acidic than yoghurt, and if added in high enough concentrations could increase the pH level. Flavour extracts (essences) that are sometimes used in yoghurts have a neutral effect, and in any event are used in minuscule quantities.”

EVIDENCE OF MR C ELDER

  1. Mr Elder deposed that he is Research and Development Manager of National Dairies Limited, which he described as “the largest dairy manufacturing group in Australia”. His duties include responsibility for product development and technical support including research on dairy products and overseeing compliance with regulatory and statutory requirements for products manufactured by the group. The Tribunal accepts Mr Elder’s technical qualifications and experience as qualifying him as an expert technical witness in matters pertaining to yoghurt production in Australia. Paragraph 8 of his affidavit states:

“‘Yoghurt’ is the word used within the dairy products industry to describe the cultured milk product formed by fermentation brought about by live bacteria strains of lactobacillus bulgaricus, streptococcus lactis or other suitable lactic acid bacteria. Yoghurt, with its typical characteristics of abundant micro-organisms, thicker consistency and texture and sour taste, is not formed in the manufacturing process until the product exhibits a pH level of 4.5 or lower. It is possible to artificially acidify a mixture, and by this means bring its pH down to the 4.5 level or below. However, the product would not be ‘yoghurt’ because the result would not have been caused by fermentation brought about by the bacteria cultures. The end product may contain some thickeners and have been flavoured or had fruit, nuts or vegetables added. The product must also comply with Standard H8 of the Food Standards Code sanctioned by the National Food Authority.”

  1. Mr Elder describes the process by which yoghurt is manufactured. This description accords in essence with that of Messrs. Di Cosola, Jackson and Thomas, namely heating milk to approximately 80oC past the point of pasteurisation, cooling it to approximately 40oC, introducing yoghurt cultures, allowing fermentation and coagulation to occur until “strike point” where the pH level is around 4.3, resulting in an end product with a pH level in the range of 4.1 to 4.2, but never in excess of 4.5.

EVIDENCE OF DR R MACBEAN

  1. Dr Macbean deposed that he is Group Technical and Planning Manager of QUF Industries Limited, a manufacturer of fresh dairy products including two brands of yoghurt. His responsibilities include research and development, quality assurance, project engineering, supply, distribution and logistics planning for dairy products. His qualifications and experience place him in good stead to assist the Tribunal with relevant evidence of a technical nature. He deposed that the technical meaning of “Yoghurt” in the dairy products industry is that set out in Standard H8 of the Australian Food Standards Code on “Yoghurt and Yoghurt Products” and that the requirement of a maximum pH level of 4.5 applies to frozen yoghurt in the same way as it applies to unfrozen yoghurt.

EVIDENCE OF MR N REYNOLDS

  1. Mr Reynolds deposed that he is the Technical Services Manager of Australian Co-operative Foods Ltd, which markets three brands of yoghurt, and that he has been involved with the technical aspects of yoghurt manufacture for approximately 20 years, and with the manufacture of frozen yoghurt for the last 5 years. In essence his evidence in relation to the dairy products industry definition of “yoghurt” is consistent with that of Dr Macbean, being governed by Standard H8. He also deposed that, apart from the allowance of the use of emulsifiers in relation to frozen yogurt, Standard H8 applies equally to yoghurt as a refrigerated product or as a large frozen product.

EVIDENCE OF MS J COATES

  1. Ms Coates deposed that she is an Appeals and Review Officer of the respondent and that she attended three of the applicant’s retail outlets. She described the products offered for sale which include SSY in its partially thawed and aerated state, served in various ways - in cones, some with toppings, sundae style, with waffles, in milkshakes and fruit “smoothies” and so on. The advertising and promotion brochures (attached to Ex. R6) are headed with the words “The World’s Best Yogurt Served Here” and state therein “Less than half the calories and one-fourth the fat of premium ice-cream”. A colour photograph on the front of the brochures accepted in evidence shows the product as a whipped up whitish creamy product similar in appearance to that served by, and well known as, “Mr Whippy”, a soft-serve ice-cream style product.

FINDINGS OF FACT

  1. The evidence of Mr Di Cosola, particularly that set out in paragraphs 6.3.1 to 6.3.3 of his affidavit, is consistent with the descriptions of the process to manufacture “yoghurt” made by Dr Thomas at paragraph 6 of his affidavit, and Mr Elder at paragraphs 14 and 15, and reflected upon at paragraph 8, of his affidavit. The Tribunal finds that the manufacturing process employed by the applicant produces a product which is recognised within the Australian dairy products industry as yoghurt, when the “dairy phase”, the term used by Mr Di Cosola, is completed insofar as its pH level is 4.5 or below.
  2. The evidence of all the technical witnesses for the respondent - Dr Thomas, Mr Jackson, Mr Elder, Dr Macbean and Mr Reynolds - points to a conclusion that the industry and trade standard for a product to be marketed in Australia as “yoghurt” must conform with pH levels as set out in Standard H8, being not greater than 4.5, (paragraph 3(b)(ii) of the June 1992 published Standard).

Is SSY “frozen yoghurt” within the meaning of that term in the 1935 and 1992 Acts?

  1. The evidence is that at the time of the assessable dealing, when the product is sold wholesale for retail sale, it is frozen: but is it yoghurt?

Relevant definitions in the New Shorter Oxford English Dictionary are:

yoghurt n. Also, yogurt ... A semi-solid, somewhat sour foodstuff, now often fruit flavoured, made from milk curdled by the addition of certain bacteria; a carton of this substance.

curd n. ... 1 The coagulated substance formed (naturally or artificially) y the action of acids or rennet on milk, and made into cheese or eaten as food.

curdle v.t. & i. The act or product of curdling; a curd.

coagulate v.t. & i. Change from a fluid to a more or less solid state, esp. by chemical reaction;

The Macquarie Dictionary defines these terms as:

yoghurt = yogurt n. a prepared food of custard-like consistency, sometimes sweetened or flavoured, made from milk that has been curdled by the action of enzymes or other cultures.

curd n. 1. a substance consisting of casein, etc., obtained from milk by coagulation, used for making into cheese or eaten as food.

curdle v.t. 1. to change into curd.

coagulate v.i. 1. to change from a fluid into a thickened mass; curdle; congeal.

  1. There are clearly two meanings attributable to the word “yoghurt”. The ordinary or vernacular meaning, as above, and the technical or industry meaning provided in the National Food Authority Standard H8, which governs the labeling and ingredients of a product to be marketed in Australia as yoghurt, including frozen yoghurt. Standard H8 begins, in sub-clause (2) with what might be described as a technical description:

(2)(a) Yoghurt, reduced fat yoghurt and skim milk yoghurt are the coagulated products prepared by the lactic acid fermentation of preparations of, subject to paragraph (aa), milk or milk products either pasteurised or treated by any other time and temperature combination of equal or greater lethal effect on bacteria, through the action of Streptococcus thermophilus, Lactobacillus bulgaricus or other suitable lactic acid bacteria in such a manner as to ensure the culture micro-organisms used in the fermentation are present in a viable form in each product so prepared.

(aa) Yoghurt, reduced fat yoghurt or skim milk yoghurt may be made in whole or in part from milk other than that of a cow.

  1. Except to the extent that the above definition is couched in more technical phrases, in essence, there is no discernible difference between that part of the Standard’s definition of yoghurt and the ordinary usage definition. The Tribunal finds that SSY, to the extent that its pH level is not more than 4.5 at the completion of the “dairy phase” of production, as described by Mr Di Cosola, meets both the ordinary or vernacular definition and the sub-clause (2)(a) of the Standard’s definition of “yoghurt”.
  2. The Tribunal also finds that because SSY, at the point of the assessable dealing, has a pH factor in excess of 4.5, it does not meet the specific condition set out in Standard H8, at sub-paragraph (2)(b)(ii), to be marketed as “yoghurt” or “frozen yoghurt” in Australia.
  3. The Tribunal accepts that SSY’s pH is above 4.5 because certain additives combined with the product after the dairy phase cause its alkalinity to increase (para. 6.5.4 and 6.5.5 of Ex. A1). pH level is a measure of relative acidity and it is acidity which gives yoghurt its distinctly sour taste. The term is defined in the Macquarie Dictionary as:

1981 definition “pH n. A measure of acidity or alkalinity, as of soil, water, etc., on a scale, running from 1 (extreme acidity) to 14 (extreme alkalinity), numerically equal to the negative logarithm of the concentration of the hydrogen ion in gram atoms per litre. Thus pH of 5 indicates a concentration of 10-5 gram atoms of hydrogen ions in one litre.”

1991 definition “pH n. A measure of acidity, defined as the negative logarithm of the effective hydrogen ion concentration in moles per litre, usually used for aqueous solutions. Pure water has a pH of 7, acidic solutions have lower pH values and alkaline solutions have higher values.”

  1. Bearing in mind that the pH scale is logarithmic to the base 10, then the difference between a pH of 4 and 5 is a ten fold increment in the gram atoms of hydrogen per litre. So the difference between a pH of 4 and 6 is a 100 fold increment and between 4 and 7, is a 1,000 fold increase. The Websters Dictionary, Tabor House , New York, 1973, in its definition of ‘pH’, which for all intents and purposes is the same as the Macquarie, adds that a pH of 7 is neutral, that is, neither acidic nor alkaline. So a pH below 7 is still acidic.
  2. The addition of sweeteners counters the lower pH sour taste giving SSY a flavour which is akin to soft serve ice-cream as distinct from the sour, but palatable, taste of yoghurt. The evidence is that the addition of the sweeteners does not affect the viable abundant organisms of L. bulgaricus, S. thermophilus and L. acidophilus which distinguish yoghurt products from other milk based products such as ice-cream. See para. 7.3 of Ex. A1 and refer to para. (2)(a) of Standard H8 definition above. SSY, albeit having a pH in excess of 4.5, is a product derived from yoghurt. Whether it is itself of the genus of “yoghurt” as that word is used in, and for the purpose of, the 1935 and 1992 Acts must be considered in the light of case law dealing with ordinary and special meanings of words.
  3. Deputy Commissioner of Taxation v Zest Manufacturing Company Pty Ltd [1949] HCA 61; (1949) 79 CLR 166 is a case stated to the Full High Court concerning a classification of a product for the purposes of the Item 6(4) of the First Schedule to the 1935 Act. The matter for determination was whether fish were livestock in the context of that particular exemption provision. Rich J said, at pp.171-172:

“The context in which the word is set lifts the question out of the quagmire of dictionary meanings. ‘One of the main objects of every dictionary of the English language is to give an adequate and comprehensive definition of every word contained in it, which involves setting forth all the different meanings which can properly be given to the particular word. The Court, on the other hand, in determining what is the true meaning of a particular word used in an instrument which it has to construe, has to ascertain in what sense the parties to that instrument have used the word: all the help the Court can derive from dictionaries in such a case is, in case of doubt, to ascertain that the meaning which it comes to the conclusion ought to be attributed to the word is one which may properly be given to it’ (Mills v Cannon Brewing Co. Ltd. (1920) 2 Ch. 38, at pp.44, 45).”

  1. In the instant case there is a distinction between the trade or technical definition of “yoghurt” [as found in Standard H8] and its dictionary definition. The latter, of course, would not be expected to make any reference to the sort of technical specifics as are found in clause (2)(b) of Standard H8. The passage cited above is support for the Tribunal to consider the legislative context of the exclusion from the exemption for food for human consumption.
  2. The Federal Court (French, Hill and Whitlam JJ) in Diethelm Manufacturing Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation [1993] FCA 437; (1993) 44 FCR 450, provides a guide in this respect. That case concerned the classification of chairs for the purpose of Item 1(a) of the Third Schedule to the 1935 Act. French J, at p.457, made these observations:

“As a general proposition the taxpayer claiming the benefit of an exemption from the imposition of a tax has the burden of proving the facts necessary to fall within that exemption: Cuming Smith & Co Pty Ltd v Melbourne Harbour Trust Commissioners [1905] HCA 27; (1905) 2 CLR 735 at 742; Jackett v Deputy Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1932] SASR 405 at 407. On the other hand an exemption which exists for the purpose of encouraging, rewarding or protecting some class of activity is not to be given a narrow application.”

  1. Amendments to the 1935 Act in 1985 served to subject to tax a range of food items for human consumption previously exempted, being biscuits, ice-creams and similar goods and snack foods. The Explanatory Memorandum to the Sales Tax (Exemptions and Classifications) Amendment Bill 1985, said in this regard:

“Ice-cream and ice-cream like products such as flavoured iceblocks, frozen confectionery and ice-cream substitutes will also bear tax at the 10% rate.”

  1. That, of itself, is unhelpful in the present matter because the Bill, in deleting from exemption ice-cream, ice-cream products, gelatin, frozen confectionery and flavoured ice blocks, left frozen yoghurt as an exempt item. His Honour continued at p.457:

“The construction of a revenue statute requires at the outset a consideration of whether or not its words are used in their natural and ordinary meaning, or in some commercial sense, or perhaps, between those poles, some extension or limitation of the ordinary or commercial meaning. Revenue laws directed to commerce frequently employ the descriptions and adopt the meanings in use among those in the relevant trade: Herbert Adams Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) [1932] HCA 27; (1932) 47 CLR 222 at 227 (Dixon J). On the other hand, the inclination to adopt commercial construction must not be taken to exclude the possibility that the words of a relevant statute may bear their ordinary and natural meaning: D & R Henderson (Mfg) Pty Ltd v Forbes (Collector of Customs (NSW)) (1974) 48 ALJR 132 and see generally Collector of Customs v Bell Basic Industries Ltd [1988] FCA 371; (1988) 20 FCR 146 at 156-158.”

  1. Another pertinent statement is that of O’Connor J in Markell v Wollaston [1906] HCA 91; (1906) 4 CLR 141 at 106:

“The general rule of interpretation is that words used in a Statute must be taken to have been used in their ordinary meaning. If it is contended that there is a commercial or other special meaning of the word, there must be evidence before the Court on which it can come to the conclusion that the word is so used, and then it is for the Court to determine whether the legislature has used the word in its ordinary signification or in the special sense.”

  1. In the present case the phrase “frozen yoghurt” is used along with such words and phrases as “confectionery”, “ice-cream”, “ice-cream substitutes”, “frozen confectionery”, “flavoured ice blocks” etc, none of which conveys a technical or trade meaning. However, the evidence is that the term has a particular technical and trade meaning relevant to the market for yoghurt products in Australia, which meaning is a refinement of the ordinary or vernacular meaning.
  2. French J in Diethelm Manufacturing Pty Ltd, rather helpfully in the present context, makes the following observations at p.464:

“Generalisations about the construction of such statutory classifications is to be undertaken with caution. Decisions on one such classification may have only limited application to another, albeit they share similar features. While purposive construction is desirable, the purpose to be served by a particular exemption or rebate may be beyond discovery. The efforts of special interest groups or perceptions of political imperatives at the time the classification was enacted may all be reflected in the definition of the particular category or goods in question. Subject to those caveats, some broad general principles emerge from the cases:

  1. Classifications of goods attracting exemptions or beneficial rates should be liberally construed unless the text or context requires a narrow construction.
    1. Revenue laws addressed to commercial people frequently but not always classify goods in terms known to those in the relevant trade or industry.
    2. The question whether the words of a statutory classification are to be interpreted according to their ordinary natural meaning or a special commercial meaning or some extension of limitation or either is a threshold question of law.
    3. Words or phrases not accorded a special meaning should be construed according to popular usage.
    4. Generally speaking where a statutory classification adopts the form of a generic class description followed by specific classes linked to it by the word “namely” or some synonym, it is intended that the specific classes will exhaust the generic class.
  2. The statutory classification of goods to define exemptions or particular liabilities under revenue laws requires the determination, by reference to objective criteria, of the “essential character” of such goods. ...”
  1. The Tribunal makes the following observations in relation to the above “general principles” of French J. It finds that the purpose behind the exclusion of “frozen yoghurt” is not apparent and is not reflected in the legislation. If, by reference to general principle i), the Tribunal is to apply a liberal construction to the term then SSY could be classified as “yoghurt” and hence frozen yoghurt. However, since the law addresses a particular trade or industry, namely the dairy products industry in Australia, then adopting general principle ii) above, SSY fails the test and is not “yoghurt”. Regarding general principle iii), the Tribunal merely notes that the distinction as to whether a trade or industry meaning should be given preference over the ordinary meaning is central to this decision. However, general principle iv) would lead the Tribunal to adopt the special meaning attributed by the dairy products industry and documented in Standard H8, which governs the marketing of yoghurt products in Australia. General principle v) has no application in this instance since there is but one class, “frozen yoghurt”, which is an exception from frozen confectionery. In regard to general principle vi), the essential character of SSY, has to be determined at the point of the assessable dealing - not by reference to the market in which it will eventually be retailed: see Hill J’s judgment in this case at p.470 where he observes that:

“Once it is appreciated that the question for issue is concerned with the kind of goods in question, rather than the actual goods, it is clear that evidence such as was given in the case as to the market into which the actual goods are sold will be of little relevance.” (original emphasis)

  1. However, even without reference to the evidence of Ms J Coates, which strictly speaking is of no assistance to the Tribunal in this context, the evidence is that SSY, whilst not “ice-cream”, is, at the point of assessable dealing, the sole ingredient for an ice-cream substitute. On this basis alone, general principle 6 points to a determination that the product SSY, unless within the trade or industry specifics of “frozen yoghurt” should not be excluded from the class of “ice-cream substitute” or “frozen confectionery”. This obtains because the Tribunal finds that the essential character of SSY, at the point of assessable sale, is of the kind of goods describable as the ingredient of an “ice-cream substitutes” or “frozen confectionery”. This view finds support in what Hill J (with whom Whitlam J agreed) said in the same case (at p.470):

“Because sales tax is a tax ordinarily imposed upon the last wholesale sale in the course of the flow of commerce between manufacture (or importation) and consumption, it may be said that generally the task of classification to determine whether particular goods fall within a particular Item is one which looks at the essential character of the goods themselves rather than the purpose of the purchaser or the proposed destination of the goods in the mind of the manufacturer or importer: Deputy Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) v Lincoln Industrial Cleaners Pty Ltd [1972] 2 NSWLR 499.”

  1. At the point of assessable sale, SSY is an homogenised and stabilised frozen mixture of yoghurt and sweeteners having an essential character of the ingredient for an ice-cream substitute or frozen confectionery rather than the normally marketed curd like product of yoghurt. It does not conform with the yoghurt industry standard insofar as its pH exceeds 4.5. These facts, in the light of the above statement, lead the Tribunal to the view that SSY is not yoghurt or frozen yoghurt for the purposes of the 1935 and 1992 Acts but is an ice-cream substitute or frozen confectionery.

DECISION

  1. For the above reasons the objection decisions under review are affirmed.
