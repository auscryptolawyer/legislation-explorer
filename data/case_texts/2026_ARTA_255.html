Farley and Commissioner of Taxation (Taxation) [2026] ARTA 255 (23 February 2026)

Applicant: Jason Kenneth Farley

Respondent: Commissioner of Taxation

Tribunal Number: 2024/4696

Tribunal: General Member M Burch

Place: Adelaide

Date: 23 February 2026

Decision: The Tribunal affirms the decision under review.

......................[SGD]..................................................

General Member M Burch

Catchwords

TAXATION – income tax – employee – deduction for work-related expenditure – whether expenses incurred in gaining or producing assessable income – expenses incurred functionally too soon – objection decision affirmed

Legislation

Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) ss 8-1, 900-30

Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) s 14ZZK

Cases

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Payne [2001] HCA 3

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Maddalena (1971) 2 ATR 541

Secondary Materials

Australian Taxation Office website, Pilots – income and work-related deductions, Pilot expenses G-O (at 19 February 2026)

Taxation Ruling TR 95/19W – Income tax: airline industry employees – allowances, reimbursements and work-related deductions (withdrawn)

Statement of Reasons

  1. The Applicant, Mr. Jason Farley, an airplane pilot, claimed deductions for the income year ended 30 June 2022 (2022 Income Year) for expenditure he incurred on medical expenses that were required, in order for him to obtain medical clearance for his pilot’s license after it was suspended. Mr. Farley contends that the medical expenses in dispute are deductible as work-related expenses[1] with respect to his job as a pilot that commenced in March 2022 after he regained his pilot’s license. He seeks a review of the Respondent’s objection decision dated 17 May 2024 (Objection Decision) that, in relevant part, disallowed the claimed deductions on the basis that they were private in nature.[2] The Respondent contends in these proceedings that the relevant medical expenses are not deductible to Mr. Farley because they were not incurred in gaining his accessible income; in the alternative, the Respondent contends that the relevant medical expenses are not deductible because they are private in nature.[3]
  2. Mr. Farley as the taxpayer has the burden of proving, on the balance of probabilities, that the Commissioner’s assessment was ‘excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessment should have been’.[4]
  3. The evidence in this case is contained in the Joint Hearing Book (Hearing Book) provided by the parties pursuant to paragraph [4] of the Tribunal’s Direction dated 7 July 2025; the Respondent’s Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions (Respondent’s SFIC); and the Applicant’s evidence given at the hearing held on 30 January 2026 (Hearing).

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

  1. Mr. Farley was in the process of securing employment as a pilot when his medical clearance for a pilot’s license was suspended.
  2. On 9 June 2021, Mr. Farley was sent an employment contract to work for Bravo Airlines Pty Ltd (a subsidiary of Alliance Airlines Pty Ltd) (Alliance) with a starting date of 28 June 2021.[5]
  3. Before the employment contract was executed, Mr. Farley informed Alliance via email on 24 June 2021 that his “Australian medical” had been suspended.[6]
  4. On 1 July 2021, the Civil Aviation Safety Authority (CASA) sent Mr. Farley a notice of suspension of his medical certificates (which are among the requirements to be licensed as a pilot by CASA), citing “physiological complications” that Mr. Farley had suffered.[7] This notice, along with letters from CASA dated 29 July 2021[8] and 1 December 2021,[9] set out a series of steps that Mr. Farley needed to take in order to regain the medical certificates that were a prerequisite to him holding a license to work as a pilot. These steps included regularly presenting to a Designated Aviation Medical Examiner and a variety of other doctors; submitting to and providing the results of specified tests; and completing specified treatment.
  5. Mr. Farley incurred expenses relating to the above between July 2021 and May 2022; it is these expenses that are the subject of this decision.[10] The relevant medical expenses have been accounted for variously throughout the proceedings in this case.[11] The Tribunal considers that the relevant medical expenses at issue in this case are as listed in Schedule 1 of the Respondent’s SFIC.[12]
  6. It is unclear from the evidence exactly when Mr. Farley’s medical certificates were regained and pilot’s license issued, but on 9 February 2022, he was offered a different employment contract to work as a pilot for Alliance, which was consummated the next day, and which had a commencement date of 14 March 2022 (Employment Contract).[13]
  7. On 3 July 2023, Mr. Farley lodged his tax return for the 2022 Income Year and in it he reported $44,643 of gross salary and wages from his job as a pilot and claimed a deduction of $22,942 at item D5 for “other work related expenses”.[14]
  8. On 6 October 2023, the Respondent issued its audit decision (Audit Decision)[15] and on 19 October 2023 its notice of assessment (Assessment),[16] in which it denied Mr. Farley’s deductions for the relevant medical expenses claimed in the 2022 Income Year on the basis that they were private in nature.
  9. On 14 February 2024, Mr. Farley sent his Objection, a letter (with supporting documents) to the Respondent objecting to the Audit Decision and Assessment, in which he stated that the relevant medical expenses ‘were required to regain and maintain my pilots medical and enable me to resume my primary employment as a pilot’.[17]
  10. On 16 May 2024, the Respondent emailed Mr. Farley a detailed explanation for why it was disallowing his claimed deductions for the relevant medical expenses[18] and issued its formal Objection Decision the next day.[19]
  11. Mr. Farley filed his Application for Review on 3 July 2024.[20]
  12. In giving evidence at the Hearing, Mr. Farley generally referred to the information and contentions in his previously submitted documents and provided brief comments reiterating and emphasising that the relevant medical expenses he incurred were solely related to his job as a pilot. As in the Respondent’s SFIC, the Respondent contended that the expenditure was not incurred in the course of the Applicant earning his income, and was at any rate private in nature.

Is the relevant medical expenditure deductible under s 8-1 ITAA 1997?

  1. The substantive issue in this case is whether the Applicant can deduct the relevant medical expenses under s 8-1 of ITAA 1997. To be deductible under that provision, expenditure must satisfy the “positive limb” (s 8-1(1)) and also not get caught by the “negative limb” (s 8-1(2)). The positive limb requires that, for expenditure to be deductible, it must be incurred in the gaining of one’s income or in the course of one carrying on a business. The negative limb requires that the expenditure not be capital or private in nature, that it not be related to earning income that is exempt, and that its deducibility is not prevented elsewhere in the ITAA 1997.
  2. The relevant rule for the purposes of this decision is s 8-1(1)(a), which provides that a taxpayer can deduct from their assessable income ‘any loss or outgoing to the extent that [i]t is incurred in gaining or producing [the taxpayer’s] assessable income’ (italics added). For the reasons given below, the Tribunal finds that the positive limb of s 8-1 is not satisfied; it therefor need not and does not address the question of whether the relevant medical expenditure was private in nature.
  3. Under s 8-1(1)(a), a causal connection between the expenditure incurred and the earning of income is necessary but not sufficient for deductibility; it is not enough that the purpose of an expenditure is to put the taxpayer into position to earn income, the expenditure must be incurred in the course of the income-earning activity itself. As the High Court explained, ‘It has long been established that ‘incurred in gaining or producing’ is to be understood as meaning incurred ‘in the course of’ gaining or producing’.[21]
  4. A leading authority on the question at hand, Maddalena,[22] involved a taxpayer who was both an electrician and a professional rugby league player. While he was a member of a rugby league club in Wollongong (Wests), he incurred travel and legal expenses when he sought (and got) a new employment contract with a club in Sydney (Newtown). The Court held that the taxpayer’s expenditure to obtain an employment contract was not deductible because it did not have the requisite connection to the earning of income; it was incurred before, not in the course of, deriving income. Menzies, J, considered, by way of illustration, the taxpayer’s earnings as an electrician:
    Had the taxpayer claimed as a deduction the expenses of changing from one job to another as an employee electrician his outlay would not have been an allowable deduction. The expenditure would have been incurred in getting, not in doing, work as an employee. It would come at a point too soon to be properly regarded as incurred in gaining assessable income.[23]
  5. The parties both addressed the applicability of a tax ruling that has since been withdrawn.[24] Though it is no longer in force, it is worth noting that the ruling applied to current, rather than prospective, employees, and included the following illustrative examples:
    Licenses: A deduction is allowable for the cost of renewing licences held by an airline employee in respect of his or her employment. A deduction is not allowable for the cost of obtaining the initial licence.

    Medical examinations for licence renewal: A deduction is allowable for the expenses associated with medical examinations for the renewal of relevant licences. These expenses include the cost of the travel to and from the medical practitioner.

  6. The parties both also addressed the Australian Tax Office (ATO) website page regarding the deductibility of pilot expenses, which includes the following illustrative guidance:
    Medical examinations

    You can claim a deduction for medical examinations that you must take to fulfil your health assessment requirements associated with the renewal of your work-related licences.

    For example, you can claim your regular aviation and medical appointments and examinations required by the Civil Aviation Safety Authority. This includes the cost of the travel to and from the medical practitioner.

    You can't claim a deduction if you undertake a pre-employment medical examination, even if you must have it as a condition of employment.[25]

  7. With regard to the above examples, Mr. Farley’s relevant medical expenditure is akin to the “initial license” and the “pre-employment medical examination” that are required to get a job, rather than the expenditure related to “renewals” of existing licenses for an existing job.
  8. The evidence clearly establishes, and the Tribunal accepts, that Mr. Farley’s employment as a pilot commenced on 14 March 2022. The Employment Contract provides that the employment commencement date was 14 March 2022,[26] and a variety of communications confirm that date.[27] Mr. Farley also confirmed at the Hearing that he started his employment as a pilot on 14 March 2022.
  9. The Tribunal considers that the relevant medical expenses incurred by Mr. Farley merely put him in a position to undertake employment as a pilot, and as such are not deductible under s 8-1(1)(a) ITAA 1997. The expenses were incurred to get work, not in the course of doing work. As such, they were incurred functionally “too soon”. The relevant test is not entirely temporal (thus the modifier “functionally”), and so even the relevant medical expenses that were incurred after Mr. Farley’s employment commencement date were incurred in getting the employment and were not incurred in the course of earning assessable income.

CONCLUSION

  1. As the relevant medical expenses are not deductible, the Applicant has not discharged his burden of proving that the assessment in question was excessive or otherwise incorrect. Thus, the reviewable Objection Decision is affirmed.
