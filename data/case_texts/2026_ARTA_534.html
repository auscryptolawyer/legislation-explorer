Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. The Applicant seeks review of a decision of a delegate of the Commissioner of Taxation (Respondent/Commissioner) made on 19 February 2025 (Internal Review Decision) to confirm an earlier decision to disqualify the applicant from being a responsible officer of a corporate trustee of a superannuation fund under the Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Act 1993 (Cth) (SISA).
  2. The Internal Review Decision related to a decision of the respondent’s delegate made 30 October 2024 (October Decision). The October Decision was made under section 126A(2) of the Act because the delegate was satisfied “that the corporate trustee of one or more superannuation entities has contravened the SISA on one or more occasions, and at the time of the contraventions [the applicant was] a responsible officer of the corporate trustee and the seriousness of the contraventions provides grounds for disqualifying [the applicant].”[1]
  3. Inter-alia, the Internal Review Decision recorded the following matters (citations omitted):
    Investment strategy

    11. You contended that you have fully adhered to the investment strategy approved by the ATO during the SMSF pre-registration audit. However, the investment agreement you provided does not align with this strategy. The investment strategy did not include investing in a related overseas construction company.

    12. After careful consideration, we have determined that the investment evidence you have submitted does not carry sufficient weight to support your claim. Therefore, it does not displace the Commissioner's view that illegal early access of the super benefits has occurred.

    Non-lodgement of annual returns

    13. You failed to lodge annual returns for the first three years of your SMSF operation, despite receiving reminders and being granted two deferrals. Your contention that the audit officer did not mention the lodgement requirements in their email does lessen your level of accountability and does not justify the non-lodgement contravention. By the time the audit commenced, you were aware of the two overdue returns.

    14. Lodging the first return on time is particularly important as it sets a positive precedent for future compliance and demonstrates that the trustees are committed to meeting their obligations.

    15. Your contention that your accountant retained the SMSF documentation and was uncooperative does not hold, as it is ultimately your responsibility, as a director of the corporate trustee, to ensure timely lodgement of the annual returns. You did not take necessary actions to resolve disagreements with your accountant in a timely manner.

    16. Furthermore, by failing to meet your lodgement obligations you delayed the Commissioner becoming aware of your other contraventions and demonstrated that you pose risk to future compliance.

    17. The fact that SMSF had no investment income does not affect the income included in your personal income tax returns. The ATO did not fabricate the income. If a member of a self-managed superannuation fund (SMSF) receives a superannuation benefit from a superannuation fund outside of prescribed payment standards, that amount is considered illegally accessed, and must be included in their assessable income.

    18. A payment is treated as being to super benefit to a member when it is made to another person or entity at their direction or benefits them.

    19. The money transferred to your overseas construction company is considered illegally accessed and therefore, must be included in your personal income tax returns.

    20. The fact that your construction company owns a high-value property is irrelevant, as we cannot accept the money withdrawn from the SMSF as valid investments.

    21. Accordingly, we are affirming the disqualification decision.

    22. You must still lodge your outstanding annual returns as part of winding up your SMSF.

  4. In forming the above views, the Respondent said it had “used” ss 34, 35D, 62 and 65 of the SISA. However, the reasons of the delegate did not expressly identify how these sections were used. Nor were there the findings in respect of non-compliance with each of these sections and the reasons. Therefore, save in respect of the non-lodgement of returns as required by section 35D.
  5. The Internal Review Decision was received by the Applicant on 28 February 2025. The applicant filed his application for review in the Tribunal on 17 March 2025. (Review Application). In the section entitled “Reasons for the application”, the applicant said:
    The ATO have responded with 21 points (attached). The first 10 points are procedural.

    The box is too small say the summary is:

  6. I did explain to Sultan Huq (ATO) EXACTLY what I would like to do. This was in writing, AND an interview.
  7. In hindsight, it appears that the evidence from Ms Huq was incorrect. However, I relied on what I was told by her.
    Regarding non-lodgement;

    I am dealing with accountants who are not doing their job properly, as per my auditor, Johan Kaufmann. The ATO is saying it doesn’t matter and it’s my responsibility to just “make it happen”. But if they don’t want to cooperate, what am I supposed to do? Send the boys around? I am being blamed for the (in) action of others. Which seems procedurally unfair.

  8. Directions were made to prepare the application for hearing.
  9. In addition, on 27 November 2025, by consent, an order was made staying the Internal Review Decision (Stay Order) on the following conditions:

Issues

    9. Relief Sought

    The Applicant respectfully seeks the following orders:

    The Applicant seeks that the Tribunal exercise its powers under s 43 of the AAT Act to:

  • substitute a decision that the Applicant not be disqualified, or
  • in the alternative, remit the matter to the Commissioner for reconsideration with directions that the Commissioner properly consider the full evidence, including the Applicant’s disclosures, the investment strategy, the contemporaneous documents, and the procedural fairness issues identified in this SFIC.
    9.3 Ancillary Orders

    The Applicant also seeks:

  • any consequential orders the Tribunal considers appropriate to give effect to the substituted decision
  • a direction that the Commissioner correct the ATO’s internal records to reflect that the Applicant was not disqualified
  • a direction that any references to alleged “penalties”, “contraventions”, or “personal benefits” be removed from the ATO’s internal notes and compliance history, as these findings are not supported by the evidence.
    9.4 Overarching Submission

    The Applicant submits that the Tribunal should intervene because:

  • the ATO’s decision was not based on a fair, balanced, or accurate assessment of the facts
  • the ATO’s internal file demonstrates significant contradictions, memory gaps, and post‑hoc rationalisations by ATO officers
  • the Applicant acted honestly, transparently, and in accordance with the fund’s investment strategy, and
  • the ATO’s use of the disqualification power in this case was excessive, punitive, and inconsistent with the principles of good administration.
  1. In a separate SFICS entitled Statement of Facts, Issues and Contentions (Forensic Version), contained in Exhibit A, the Applicant said:[3]
    SECTION 10 — ORDERS SOUGHT AND FINAL SUBMISSIONS (FORENSIC REWRITE)

    147. The Applicant submits that the Commissioner’s decision to disqualify him as a trustee of the Fund was affected by material factual errors, procedural unfairness, failure to consider relevant evidence, reliance on unreliable recollections, and legal unreasonableness. The cumulative effect of these defects is that the decision cannot stand (RSFIC, pp. 323–336; A5–A6, pp. 216–237).

    148. The evidence demonstrates that the investment was disclosed in the ROC prior to the transaction (T3, p. 41), was expressly permitted under the Fund’s investment strategy (T2, pp. 25–27), and was commercial in nature, supported by the investment agreement (T13, pp. 182–184), municipal tax documents (A3, p. 214), and the €25,000 reservation deposit from a third-party purchaser (A4, p. 215). The ATO’s assertions of concealment, impermissibility, and personal use are contradicted by the objective documentary record.

    149. The ATO failed to request key documents—including the investment agreement, bank statements, and evidence of arm’s-length dealings—until after the disqualification decision had already been made (A5–A6, pp. 216–237). A decision-maker cannot rely on a lack of evidence when they failed to ask for it. This failure deprived the Applicant of a meaningful opportunity to respond and constitutes a breach of natural justice.

    150. The ATO relied heavily on the retrospective and uncertain recollections of Officer Sultana Huq, who repeatedly stated she “does not recall” key matters (A5–A6, pp. 216–237). Her notes were not contemporaneous and were created long after the events they purport to describe. The ATO’s preference for these unreliable recollections over contemporaneous documents is procedurally unfair and inconsistent with administrative law principles.

    151. The ATO’s SFIC contains multiple contradictions, including assertions that the investment was hidden despite the ROC disclosure (T3, p. 41), that the investment was not permitted despite the investment strategy (T2, pp. 25–27), and that personal use occurred despite bank statements showing none (T11, pp. 167–175). These contradictions undermine the reliability of the ATO’s findings.

    152. The ATO failed to consider less severe regulatory options, such as education directions, rectification directions, enforceable undertakings, or administrative penalties (RSFIC, pp. 323–336). Instead, it proceeded directly to disqualification—the most extreme regulatory response available. This failure to consider proportionality is itself a reviewable error.

    153. The Applicant acted in good faith, relied on professional advice, attempted to comply with all obligations, and responded promptly to ATO communications (T8; RSFIC, pp. 323–336). There is no evidence of dishonesty, concealment, or personal benefit. The evidence instead demonstrates transparency, cooperation, and a genuine attempt to comply.

    154. The Tribunal is empowered under the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 to set aside the decision and substitute the correct and preferable decision. Given the factual errors, procedural unfairness, and legal unreasonableness identified, the correct and preferable decision is that the Applicant not be disqualified as a trustee of the Fund.

    155. For these reasons, the Applicant respectfully submits that the Tribunal should:

    • set aside the Commissioner’s decision to disqualify the Applicant as a trustee of the Fund; and

    • substitute a decision that the Applicant not be disqualified.

    156. In the alternative, if the Tribunal considers that further information is required, the Applicant submits that the matter should be remitted to the Commissioner for reconsideration according to law, with directions to consider the evidence identified in the Applicant’s bundle (T2; T3; T10; T11; T13; A3; A4) and the procedural fairness issues identified in the summons returns (A5–A6, pp. 216–237).

    157. However, given the completeness of the evidence before the Tribunal and the seriousness of the procedural defects, the Applicant submits that substitution is the appropriate outcome.

    Issue 3: Whether the Trustee breached section 65 of the SISA. Whether in addition, the Trustee breached section 34 by virtue of regulation 6.17 of the Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Regulations 1994 (Cth) (“SISR”).

The Tribunal explained to the Applicant that while the applicant raised various concerns about the manner in which the respondent had undertaken its review and collected evidence, the role of the Tribunal was to determine afresh whether there were circumstances enlivening the power under section 126A(2) of the Act to disqualify the applicant under that section and, if so, whether such a decision should be made.
  4. During those discussions, the Respondent indicated that the Commissioner relied on contraventions of the SISA identified in the issues which I have set out above. In doing so, it was made clear by the respondent during the course of the hearing that no assertion was made concerning a breach of section 109 (which relates to arm’s-length transactions) or Part 8 of the SISA (which relates to the “In-house asset rules” and limits thereon).[5]
  5. As a result of those discussions, and the submissions received, the issues for resolution by the Tribunal are those identified by the Commissioner set out above. In short that involves determining if there have been contraventions of the SISA and, if so, whether the applicant should be disqualified.

  2. It is convenient to deal with this matter by reference to the four issues identified by the Respondent which set out above.

Issue 1: Whether the Trustee contravened the sole purpose test as expressed in section 62 of SISA?

  1. Section 62 of the SISA provides:
    62 Sole purpose test

    (1) Each trustee of a regulated superannuation fund must ensure that the fund is maintained solely:

    (a) for one or more of the following purposes (the core purposes):

    (i) the provision of benefits for each member of the fund on or after the member’s retirement from any business, trade, profession, vocation, calling, occupation or employment in which the member was engaged (whether the member’s retirement occurred before, or occurred after, the member joined the fund);

    (ii) the provision of benefits for each member of the fund on or after the member’s attainment of an age not less than the age specified in the regulations;

    (iii) the provision of benefits for each member of the fund on or after whichever is the earlier of:

(A) the member’s retirement from any business, trade, profession, vocation, calling, occupation or employment in which the member was engaged; or

(B) the member’s attainment of an age not less than the age prescribed for the purposes of subparagraph (ii);

(iv) the provision of benefits in respect of each member of the fund on or after the member’s death, if

(A) the death occurred before the member’s retirement from any business, trade, profession, vocation, calling, occupation or employment in which the member was engaged; and

(B) the benefits are provided to the member’s legal personal representative, to any or all of the member’s dependants, or to both;

(v) the provision of benefits in respect of each member of the fund on or after the member’s death, if:

as, to any or all of the member’s dependants, or to both; or

(b) for one or more of the core purposes and for one or more of the following purposes (the ancillary purposes):
(i) the provision of benefits for each member of the fund on or after the termination of the member’s employment with an employer who had, or any of whose associates had, at any time, contributed to the fund in relation to the member;

(ii) the provision of benefits for each member of the fund on or after the member’s cessation of work, if the work was for gain or reward in any business, trade, profession, vocation, calling, occupation or employment in which the member was engaged and the cessation is on account of ill-health (whether physical or mental);

(iii) the provision of benefits in respect of each member of the fund on or after the member’s death, if:

(A) the death occurred after the member’s retirement from any business, trade, profession, vocation, calling, occupation or employment in which the member was engaged (whether the member’s retirement occurred before, or occurred after, the member joined the fund); and

(B) the benefits are provided to the member’s legal personal representative, to any or all of the member’s dependants, or to both;

(iv) the provision of benefits in respect of each member of the fund on or after the member’s death, if:

(A) the death occurred after the member attained the age prescribed for the purposes of subparagraph (a)(ii); and

(B) the benefits are provided to the member’s legal personal representative, to any or all of the member’s dependants, or to both;

(v) the provision of such other benefits as the Regulator approves in writing.

  1. The Applicant submits that the sole purpose test was not breached. In closing submissions, the Applicant said the transfer of funds should be properly characterised as a commercial investment of the Fund. [36] In his SFICS, the applicant says the investment in the project was not personal in nature or used for personal benefit. Rather:[37]
    8A.1.2

    ... The investment was:

    • disclosed in the ROC prior to the transaction,

    • expressly permitted under the Fund’s investment strategy,

    • supported by a commercial investment agreement,

    • funded entirely from the Fund’s bank account, and

    • never occupied, used, or accessed by the Applicant or any related party.

    ...

    8A.1.5

    On the evidence, the investment was a legitimate, arm’s-length, commercial investment consistent with the Fund’s strategy. No breach of s 62 occurred.

  2. The Respondent submitted that the circumstances surrounding the transfer of the monies out of the Fund in April 2022 and following support a conclusion the Fund was being maintained to benefit MPG. Consequently, a breach of section 62 of the SISA is established.
  3. In making this submission, the Respondent relied on the decision of Aussiegolfa Pty Ltd v Commissioner of Taxation[38] (Aussiegolfa). That case included consideration of section 62 of the SISA and the meaning of “purpose” used in that section.
  4. In Aussiegolfa, Moshinsky J (with whom Besanko J agreed) referred to various decisions of the Federal Court and the High Court concerning purpose in the context of deductibility of employer superannuation contributions to funds for employees.
  5. Of the High Court, Moshinsky J referred to the decision of Taylor J, sitting at first instance in the High Court in Driclad Pty Limited v Commissioner of Taxation[39] (Driclad). Moshinsky J observed:[40]
    At first instance in the High Court, Taylor J held (at 61-62), in relation to the “A” section of the fund, that the evidence (which included loans by the trustees to the companies) did not indicate that the fund was being used for the benefit of the contributing companies or that it was not being applied for the purpose for which it had been established. Taylor J considered it to be “erroneous to say that the evidence reveals that the trustees were not applying the fund for the purpose for which it was established but, rather, for the benefit of the companies concerned”. Taylor J continued (at 62):

    I do not say that if it could be shown that the trustees, in exercising their powers of investment, were applying the fund in derogation of, or, so as to defeat, the rights of employees in the fund as constituted, the conclusion would not follow that the fund was not being applied for the purpose for which it was established. But there is nothing to suggest that this was so in this case.

  6. Moshinsky J then notes that in Driclad, on appeal the Full High Court, Barwick CJ and Kitto J (with whom McTiernan and Menzies JJ agreed) accepted that the conclusion on this aspect of the decision of Taylor J was correct.[41]
  7. In one of the decisions of the Federal Court referred to by Moshinsky J, Raymor Contractors Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation[42] (Raymor), Davies J said of “purpose”:[43]
    In s 82AAC(1), the word “purpose” required that the sum set apart or paid in the year of income effected a contribution towards superannuation benefits for or for a dependant of an eligible employee. The term did not look primarily to the subjective factors actuating the setting aside or payment of the sum claimed. Thus, in the ordinary case, it was sufficient to found a deduction that a superannuation fund had been established solely for the provision of superannuation benefits for employees and their dependants, that the fund had been maintained for that purpose, that a sum appropriate, having regard to the provisions of s 82AAE and 82AAM, had been set aside or paid into the fund for the fund’s purposes and that the rights of the employees and dependants to receive benefits from the fund were fully secured. If such were the case, it was not pertinent that the sum was set apart and paid into the fund not out of beneficence but out of a duty imposed by law or by an industrial award and not of consequence that the employer had taken into account in establishing and maintaining the fund that incidental benefits such as taxation benefits or the borrowing of sums from the fund at a low rate of interest could be obtained.
  8. Also in Raymor, at 1423, Hill J said:
    There is a fundamental distinction between purpose and motive just as there is ambiguity in the concept of purpose. However, as Lord Wright observed in Crofter Hand Woven Harris Tweed Co v Veitch [1941] UKHL 2; [1942] AC 435 at 469, the words:

    “motive”, “object”, “purpose” are in application to practical matters difficult strictly to define or distinguish

    This difficulty arises in part because “motive” is often used colloquially as meaning “purpose” and because in some cases at least a finding of motivation may lead inexorably to a finding of purpose.

  9. Moshinsky J then continued:[44]
    176. Although the cases discussed above concerned other provisions, they usefully discuss the concept of “purpose” in a similar statutory context. (As noted by Gleeson CJ in News Limited v South Sydney District Rugby League Football Club Limited [2003] HCA 45; (2003) 215 CLR 563 at [18], the appropriate description or characterisation of the end sought to be accomplished (purpose), as distinct from the reason for seeking that end (motive), may depend upon the legislative or other context in which the task is undertaken.) Consistently with the approach taken in the above cases, the word “purpose” in s 62 of the SIS Act is concerned with the way in which the fund is being maintained; the term does not look primarily to the subjective factors actuating the trustee. Further, the statutory context in which s 62 appears does not suggest that a fund will not be treated as being maintained solely for the core purposes, or the core purposes and the ancillary purposes, simply because the trustee enters into a transaction with a related party. The statutory context includes s 66(2) of the SIS Act, which, as noted above, creates an exception to s 66(1) for the acquisition of a listed security acquired at market value and the acquisition of an asset that is “business real property” of the related party acquired at market value. The statutory context also includes s 71(1)(g), which, as noted above, exempts from the in-house asset test real property subject to a lease between the trustee of a regulated superannuation fund and a related party of the fund if the property is business real property of the fund throughout the term of the lease. Another provision forming part of the context is s 109, which relates to investments of a superannuation entity. Section 109(1) provides:

    A trustee or investment manager of a superannuation entity must not invest in that capacity unless:

    (a) the trustee or investment manager, as the case may be, and the other party to the relevant transaction are dealing with each other at arm’s length in respect of the transaction; or

    (b) both:

    (i) the trustee or investment manager, as the case may be, and the other party to the relevant transaction are not dealing with each other at arm’s length in respect of the transaction; and

    (ii) the terms and conditions of the transaction are no more favourable to the other party than those which it is reasonable to expect would apply if the trustee or investment manager, as the case may be, were dealing with the other party at arm’s length in the same circumstances.

    It may be observed that this provision does not preclude non-arm’s length transactions in the context of the investments of a superannuation entity, but rather requires the terms and conditions of the transaction to be no more favourable to the other party than those which it is reasonable to expect would apply in an arm’s length transaction.

  10. In determining in that case that a lease to a relative of a beneficiary of the superannuation fund did not contravene section 62, Moshinsky J continued:[45]
    Applying the principles discussed above to the present case, in my view the Benson Fund would not be maintained for a purpose other than the core purposes and the ancillary purposes set out in s 62 upon the leasing of the Burwood Property to Ms Benson. Importantly, in the present case, although the Burwood Property, being the underlying property associated with the Burwood Sub-Fund units held by Aussiegolfa, would be leased to the daughter of the sole member of the Benson Fund, the lease would be at market rent. In these circumstances, there does not appear to be any financial or other non-incidental benefit to be obtained by Ms Benson by leasing this property rather than another; nor does there appear to be any financial or other non-incidental benefit to be obtained by Mr Benson by the property being leased to his daughter rather than another tenant. It is true that Ms Benson would obtain a benefit in the sense that she obtains accommodation. But in circumstances where this is obtained at market rent, it does not appear to be a relevant benefit for present purposes. If and to the extent that there may be some comfort or convenience – from the perspective of Ms Benson or Mr Benson or both – from the arrangement, I would regard these matters as merely incidental benefits in the circumstances of the present case (including, in particular, the fact that the lease was at market rent). I do not consider the fact that Mr Benson submitted the application for lease on Ms Benson’s behalf, or agreed to act as guarantor of Ms Benson’s obligations under the lease, to suggest otherwise. These matters do not provide a basis to infer that either Ms Benson or Mr Benson would obtain a benefit from the arrangement other than, perhaps, some comfort or convenience. Further, I do not consider the fact that (as the primary judge found) the selection of Ms Benson as the tenant was explained, in part, by the desire of Mr Benson and DomaCom to test the ability for residential properties held by self-managed superannuation funds to be used by related parties, to be a relevant factor. This may be described as a matter going to motive rather than purpose. In any event, it does not affect the analysis of the way in which the fund would be maintained upon the leasing of the property to Ms Benson. In the absence of a financial or non-incidental benefit being obtained by Ms Benson or Mr Benson, and in the absence of any other facts or matters pointing to a collateral purpose, I conclude that the fund would be maintained solely for the core purposes, or the core purposes and the ancillary purposes, set out in s 62 upon the leasing of the Burwood Property to Ms Benson.
  11. Similarly, in Aussiegolfa, in concluding section 62 had not been contravene, Steward J set out the principles as follows:[46]
    231. I commence with the following legal propositions concerning s 62:

    (a) First, s 62 requires a fund to be maintained solely for a series of core and ancillary purposes. It is a question of fact for consideration in each year of income whether a fund is or is not so maintained. In this proceeding, the finding made below was an inference made by the judge from objective material. This is not a case where the primary judge’s finding depended upon an evaluation of the credit of any witness.

    (b) Secondly, the purpose here is that of Aussiegolfa and not that of Mr Benson, save in his capacity as a director and controller of that entity. In Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Whitfords Beach Pty Ltd [1982] HCA 8; (1982) 150 CLR 355, Gibbs CJ (as his Honour then was) said at 370:

    ...in deciding whether what was done was an operation of business, it is relevant to consider the purpose with which the taxpayer acted, and, since the taxpayer is a company, the purposes of those who control it are its purposes. In Ruhamah Property Co. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [(1928) [1928] HCA 22; 41 CLR 148] the majority of the Court regarded as important, if not decisive, the purposes with which the shareholders and directors of the company acted, although Isaacs J., who dissented, thought it erroneous to consider a company merely as machinery for carrying out individual purposes [(1928) 41 CLR, at pp 160, 162, 166]. However, in my opinion Isaacs J. took too rigid a view of the effect of Salomon v. Salomon & Co. [[1897] AC 22] if he thought that in determining the purpose with which a company acted it was not permissible to have regard to the intentions of the directors who controlled it.

    (c) Thirdly, the subjective motivation of a controlling director is not to be confused with the purpose of the corporation he or she may control.

    (d) Fourthly, purpose in the context of s 62 looks to the object of acts of maintenance of a fund on a yearly basis. If those acts have the sole object of achieving the core purposes and/or ancillary purposes, the provision is satisfied: see Raymor Contractors Pty Ltd v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1991] FCA 109; (1991) 21 ATR 1410 per Davies J at 1412 and Hill J at 1423.

    (e) Fifthly, the word “solely” may not add much to the statutory scheme. As Gibbs ACJ (as his Honour then was) observed in Ryde Municipal Council v Macquarie University [1978] HCA 58; (1978) 139 CLR 633 at 644: “[t]he word ‘solely’ may do no more than add emphasis, or perhaps precision”.

    (f) Sixthly, there is no necessary dichotomy between the maintenance of a fund for core and/or ancillary purposes and the receipt by a related person or entity of a benefit. In some cases, the conferral of a benefit may reveal the presence of a purpose which is collateral to the core and ancillary purposes defined by s 62. Investing directly in rental property which is leased to a relative for a peppercorn rent would justify an inference that there existed a collateral purpose. But if the rent paid is market value, and if the property otherwise constitutes a prudent investment, the personality of the tenant may not justify a similar inference. In such a case, the income and the assets of the fund are enhanced, or at least preserved, and the capacity of the fund to provide benefits to members in the future is not affected. Whether such an investment might otherwise be an in-house asset would require separate consideration.

    (g) The decision of the High Court in Driclad Pty Limited v Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) [1968] HCA 91; (1968) 121 CLR 45, supports the foregoing conclusion. As Moshinsky J has explained, that case relevantly concerned an application of the former s 23(j) of the Income Tax and Social Services Contribution Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) which exempted from tax the income of a fund established exclusively for the benefit of employees: see Compton v Commissioner of Taxation (Cth) [1966] HCA 1; (1966) 116 CLR 233. The fund in question lent money to, and invested in shares in, employer companies which had contributed to that fund for the benefit of their employees. The existence of these investments did not preclude satisfaction of s 23(j).

    (h) Seventhly, I do not think that it is necessarily of assistance to describe the criteria in s 62 as imposing a “strict standard” or a “high standard”. The provision does not adopt such language. There is a danger that such descriptions can unduly influence the construction of a provision or its application to the facts.

  12. Consequently, the Court in Aussiegolfa found that section 62 had not been breached simply by reason of a lease being entered into with the daughter of Mr Benson. In part, that included for the reasons that a transaction with a related party was not, of itself, a contravention of section 62 of the SISA.
  13. An application of the relevant principles is the decision of Merchant v the Commissioner of Taxation[47] (Merchant). Having referred to the reasons in Aussiegolfa, Thawley J, as Deputy President of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal (AAT), concluded:
    150. The predominant reason Mr Merchant, as director of GSMS, agreed to entering into the BBG Share Sale transaction was to crystallise a capital loss in the MFT which was expected to make significant capital gains from selling the Plantic shares. I accept that a substantial purpose (but not the predominant purpose) of GSMS was to keep ultimate beneficial or economic ownership of the BBG Shares within the Merchant Group. Neither of these purposes was a core purpose within the meaning of s 62(1).

    151. It follows that GSMS did not “ensure that the fund [was] maintained solely” for one of the purposes in s 62(1).

  14. Applying these principles to the present case, it is necessary to consider the manner in which the fund was maintained, including the nature of the transaction and the circumstances in which it was made, in order to ascertain whether the sole purpose test was met.
  15. The Agreement was, in effect, a loan by the Trustee to MPG to fund the project. The features of the Agreement were as follows:
    (a) the funds advanced were to be used “solely for the purpose of covering ongoing expenses relate to the completion of the Property and any associated costs necessary to prepare the Property for sale.”

    (b) The funds advanced were to be repaid upon the sale of the villa.

    (c) There is no evidence to suggest that the rate of 10% was not a commercial rate of interest.

    (d) The loan was unsecured.

    (e) The loan was subject to renegotiation if the villa was not sold in 3 years from the date of the agreement.

  16. The transaction was an interest-bearing loan, not a direct property investment. There was no security, although a substantial sum, in excess of $100,000, was advanced. There was no fixed date for repayment of the principal or interest. There was no payment due, of either principal or interest, until the property was sold. A failure to repay the funds if the property was not sold required the parties to renegotiate terms.
  17. In short, there were risks arising from the fact the advances were not secured, the project was overseas and, in the absence of accounts for the Fund, the investment resulted in a lack of diversification in the assets of the Fund. There was uncertainty as to when the Fund might be repaid any monies advanced and/or interest under the Agreement.
  18. Further, as the bank statements demonstrate, nearly all cash paid into the Fund upon its establishment, or earned as bank interest on deposit, was paid out to MPG pursuant to the Agreement. In the absence of other evidence, it would appear that the percentage of cash invested exceeded that specified in the November Investment Strategy as being an appropriate mix of assets to invest in.
  19. As set out above, the evidence of the Applicant indicated that one of the purposes was to allow MPG to complete the project and that advancing money from the Fund would “accelerate” completion and “tap in to [his] super”. In this regard, the Applicant said it was “a strategic decision to go forward” because he had had enough of what he described as “rinse and repeat”, which was a reference to him working overseas to generate funds to put into the project to carry out necessary work. Further, it was “one of about 20 ideas” which the Applicant says he had at the time he spoke to Ms Huq and the Fund was being registered with the ATO. However, there is no evidence that the other ideas or investment strategies were implemented.
  20. Taken as a whole, the evidence leads me to conclude the Fund was not being maintained solely for the core or ancillary purposes specified in section 62. There was another purpose, and that was funding MPG and thereby enabling MPG to accelerate completion the project. It was a purpose that the Applicant had from the time the Fund was formed and being registered. Being the guiding mind of the Trustee, it is a purpose that should be attributed to the Trustee.
  21. While I accept that a purpose was to derive a commercial return for the Fund which would meet a core purpose as set out in section 62, that was not the sole purpose. Consequently, I am satisfied there has been a contravention of this obligation by the Trustee.

Issue 3: Whether the Trustee breached section 65 of the SISA. Whether in addition, the Trustee breached section 34 by virtue of regulation 6.17 of the Superannuation Industry (Supervision) Regulations 1994 (Cth) (“SISR”).

  1. Section 65 of the SISA provides:
    65 Lending to members of regulated superannuation fund prohibited

    Prohibition

    (1) A trustee or an investment manager of a regulated superannuation fund must not:

    (a) lend money of the fund to:

    (i) a member of the fund; or

    (ii) a relative of a member of the fund; or

    (b) give any other financial assistance using the resources of the fund to:

    (i) a member of the fund; or

    (ii) a relative of a member of the fund

  2. Section 34 of the SISA provides:
    34 Prescribed operating standards must be complied with Standards must be complied with

    (1) Each trustee of a superannuation entity must ensure that the prescribed standards applicable to the operation of the entity are complied with at all times.

  3. Regulation 6.17 of the SISR relevantly provides:
    Restriction on payment

    (1) For the purposes of subsections 31(1) and 32(1) of the Act, the standards set out in subregulations (2), (2A) and (2B) are applicable to the operation of regulated superannuation funds and approved deposit funds.

    (2) A member's benefits in a fund:

    (a) may be paid:

    (i) by being cashed in accordance with Division 6.3; or

    (ii) by being rolled over or transferred in accordance with Division 6.4, 6.5 or 6.7; or

    (iii) by being allotted under Division 6.7; and

    (b) must not be paid in that way except when, and to the extent that, the fund is required or permitted under this Part to pay them; and

    (c) must be paid in that way when, and to the extent that, the fund is required under this Part to pay them.

  4. In relation to the alleged contravention of section 65 of the SISA, there is no suggestion that a loan was made directly to a member of the Fund or a relative of a member of the Fund. Rather, the Respondent contends section 65(1)(b) was contravened because financial assistance was provided to the Applicant. In the Commissioner’s SFICS, the Respondent says:[49]
    The Commissioner contends that while the Applicant did not directly receive the amounts transferred out of the Fund at paragraph 8, they were dealt with on his behalf.
  5. In support of this proposition, the Respondent relies on ss 307-5 and 307-15 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth). Of these sections the Respondent says:[50]
    46. Section 307-5 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (‘ITAA 1997’) sets out amounts which are superannuation benefits. Generally, an amount which is paid to a person from a superannuation fund because they are a fund member is a superannuation benefit by virtue of item 1 in the table in subsection 307-5(1) of the ITAA 1997.

    47. Section 307-15 of the ITAA 1997 has the effect that a payment is treated as being made to a person, or received by a person, if it is made for their benefit or to another person at their direction or request. The application of this rule can make taxpayers assessable on amounts that they do not actually receive but which are dealt with on their behalf.

  6. In response, the Applicant says:[51]
    8A.3.1

    Section 65 prohibits a trustee from providing financial assistance to a member or relative. The ATO asserts that the investment “assisted” the Applicant personally.

    8A.3.2

    The ATO has not identified:

    • any payment to the Applicant,

    • any loan to the Applicant,

    • any transfer of value to the Applicant,

    • any use of the property by the Applicant, or

    • any financial benefit received by the Applicant.

    8A.3.3

    The bank statements show no personal withdrawals or payments. The investment agreement shows a commercial structure. The property was never occupied or used by the Applicant.

    8A.3.4

    The ATO’s allegation is based on assumption, not evidence. The ATO did not request the relevant documents until after the disqualification decision.

    8A.3.5

    On the evidence, no financial assistance was provided to any member. No breach of s 65 occurred.

  7. The position of the Commissioner is set out in the reply submissions dated 10 March 2023. It is sufficient to set out part of these submissions to understand the contention made by the Respondent (citations omitted):[52]
    Financial Assistance to a Member– Section 65(1)(b)

    19. In never completing the now four outstanding SMSF annual returns for the Fund, the Commissioner is in the dark as to its financial position, what its assets are and how they are being accounted for. Pursuant to section 35C of the SISA, the Applicant needed to ensure that an approved SMSF auditor was appointed each year to provide a report on its operations and identify any breaches. Information in this audit report would have been used to prepare Fund’s Annual Returns required under section 35D.

    20. The Applicant refuses to do this, and instead asks the Tribunal to accept that the following is sufficient to account for an investment of his superannuation:

    a. evidence of transferring all monies out of the Fund’s bank account

    and;

    b. executing an agreement between the corporate trustee and his Spanish construction company.

    21. Without more, the Commissioner submits these transfers are properly characterised as the Applicant accessing his superannuation early in order to support a flailing construction venture. In doing so, the Applicant has provided financial assistance to a member of the Fund (specifically himself) in breach of s 65(1)(b)(i) of the SISA.

    22. In Merchant and Commissioner of Taxation, Thawley J (sitting as Deputy President) considered financial assistance through an intermediary was captured by section 65(1)(b) given the object of the SISA:

    “Having regard to the evident object of the provision, s 65(1)(b) is not limited to financial assistance of a direct nature. It prohibits financial assistance via an intermediary, including via a discretionary trust.”

    23. Mr Maietta has indirectly benefitted himself via an intermediary company, the Maietta Property Group SL, which he clearly controls and in light of the below circumstances, the Commissioner invites the Tribunal to conclude he benefitted from personally.

    24. The property, located at Jorge Luis Borges 1J, Montemayor Alto, Montemayor Urbanisation, Benahavis, 29679 (the Spanish Property), was already under construction when the agreement was entered into. The agreement does not seek to hide this fact, reciting that the agreement is made in circumstances where it “requires additional funding to complete.”

    25. The agreement defines the corporate trustee responsible for the Fund, RMaietta and DIglesias Pty Ltd, as an Investor. There is no mention of superannuation or the Fund that the corporate trustee is responsible for.

    26. At the direction of Mr Maietta, the corporate trustee agreed to “provide funds to the Recipient in multiple tranches, as necessary to complete the property construction process.”21 No specific amount is provided, instead it appears the corporate trustee can take money out of the Fund and provide it to the project as needed.

    27. 6 days after rolling over his superannuation into the SMSF, between 11 April 2022 and 6 May 2024, Mr Maietta in his capacity as corporate trustee made transfers of the Fund’s bank account in tranches. These tranches totalled the entirety of his superannuation rolled over into the Fund, plus interest ($121,271).

    28. The Maietta Property Group SL’s bank statements at T9 pages 88 – 99 demonstrate that at the same time as the company was receiving amounts from the Fund, the Applicant was personally receiving payments out of the company account (for example, in the statement debit amounts regular appear such as “-18.00” to “Mr Roberto John”).

    29. The $121,271 taken out of the Funds bank account and funnelled into the Maietta Property Group SL were superannuation benefits. Section 307-5 of the ITAA 1997 considers that payments to you from a superannuation fund because you are a fund member are superannuation benefits.

  8. I note the reference to “18.00” in the bank statements identified by the Respondent as evidence of a payment to the Applicant, has beside those entries “Comisione”, which the Applicant explained was bank commission on transactions. Otherwise, there is no evidence to suggest that the applicant was receiving payments from MPG during the period under consideration. To the contrary, the evidence shows that payments were made to MPG by the Fund, as provided in the Agreement, and that funds were disbursed by MPG to pay suppliers and other third parties who had dealings with MPG.
  9. It is against this background, and the other evidence that I have set out above, that contravention of section 65(1)(b) of the SISA needs to be considered.
  10. Section 65(1)(b) is concerned with the giving of financial assistance using resources of the fund to a member or relative of a member (emphasis added) of the fund.
  11. In Merchant, Thawley J said:[53]
    153. Section 65 is a civil penalty provision: s 166 of the SISA

    154. “Financial assistance” is not defined in the SISA. The phrase takes its ordinary meaning having regard to the particular statutory context in which the phrase is used. The reference to “any other financial assistance” in s 65(1)(b) refers to financial assistance other than lending money of the fund as covered by s 65(1)(a).

    155. Section 65 is intended to prevent the use of the financial resources of a fund as a means of providing current day or future financial support to members and their relatives by means other than the provision of retirement benefits in accordance with the governing rules of the fund.

  12. In finding financial assistance was given to Mr Merchant, His Honour said:[54]
    I am satisfied that financial assistance was given to Mr Merchant who was a discretionary object of the MFT. The BBG Share Sale was entered into for the predominant and immediate purpose of crystallising a capital loss in the MFT. GSMS purchased the shares with cash in the GMSF for the purpose of increasing the financial resources of the MFT for the benefit of the discretionary objects of the MFT, specifically Mr Merchant.
  13. His Honour then continued:[55]
    160. Having regard to the evident object of the provision, s 65(1)(b) is not limited to financial assistance of a direct nature. It prohibits financial assistance via an intermediary, including via a discretionary trust.

    ...

    162. To the extent that the provision of “financial assistance” is informed by whether the giver of the assistance has a financial or other detriment, the BBG Share Sale was to the detriment of the GMSF in that, objectively, the BBG Shares were unlikely to increase substantially in value and the purchase led to a risky and undiversified fund.

  14. However, the mere fact a transaction entered into by a trustee of a superannuation fund with a related party is not, of itself, sufficient to demonstrate contravention of section 65(1)(b). In this regard, the legislation and the authorities to which I have referred recognise that such transactions are, in some circumstances, permissible.
  15. In Merchant, Thawley J found that the predominant and immediate purpose of the transaction was to generate a benefit for a discretionary trust (not the superannuation fund) which, in turn, would benefit Mr Merchant, who was an object of that discretionary trust.
  16. In the present case, I am not satisfied the evidence establishes “current day or future financial support to [the applicant or his relatives] by means other than the provision of retirement benefits in accordance with the governing rules of the fund “.
  17. While there was a benefit to MPG in obtaining funding in order to complete the project, there was a corresponding obligation to pay interest, which, in the absence of evidence to suggest otherwise, I would consider to be at a commercial rate.
  18. Unlike the position in Merchant, I am not satisfied there was any direct or indirect benefit or assistance given to the applicant or his relatives. This view is reinforced by the terms of the Agreement which stated that any funds advanced "shall be used solely for the purpose of covering ongoing expenses relating to completion of the Property and any associated costs necessary to prepare the Property for sale”
  19. As the sole shareholder of the company, the Applicant was entitled to any dividends the company might declare by reason of the project being completed and the villa sold at a profit and/or to a distribution of the capital of the company if it was wound up. These entitlements existed whether or not the Agreement was entered into. As such, his position as a shareholder of the company did not, without more, give rise to any financial assistance.
  20. Otherwise, the evidence does not establish the transaction would give rise to some other assistance or financial support that the Applicant, as a member of the Fund, or that a relative of the Applicant would receive financial assistance directly or indirectly by reason of the entry into the Agreement.
  21. Accordingly, no contravention is established in respect of section 65 of the SISA.
  22. Similarly, the claim in respect of section 34 fails.

Issue 4: If yes to any of the above issues-

Whether the Applicant should be disqualified from being a trustee of the Fund pursuant to subsection 126A (2) of the SISA?

  1. Section 126A (2) provides:
    126A The Regulator may disqualify individuals

    ...

    (2) The Regulator may disqualify an individual who is, or was, a responsible officer of a trustee, investment manager or custodian (the body corporate) if satisfied that:

    (a) the body corporate has contravened this Act or the Financial Sector (Collection of Data) Act 2001 on one or more occasions; and

    (b) at the time of one or more of the contraventions, the individual was a responsible officer of the body corporate; and

    (c) in respect of the contravention or contraventions that occurred while the individual was a responsible officer of the body corporate—the nature or seriousness of it or them, or the number of them, provides grounds for the disqualification of the individual.

  2. Each of the mattes in (a)-(c) are jurisdictional facts of which the Tribunal must be satisfied in order to enliven the jurisdiction to disqualify a responsible officer.[56]
  3. There is no dispute that the Applicant was a responsible officer of the Trustee at the time of the contraventions. As noted above, I have found two contraventions by the Trustee. First is a failure to lodge any returns as required by section 34D of the SISA. Second is a contravention of section 62. Subsection 126A(2)(a) and (b) are therefore satisfied.
  4. Section 126A(2)(c) requires me to be satisfied that the nature and seriousness of the contraventions and/or the number of them provides grounds for disqualification of the Applicant. In addition, in exercising any discretion, I must otherwise determine that it is appropriate to do so in the circumstances of this case.
  5. In considering these matters, it is necessary to return to the evidence of the Applicant.
  6. First, there is no suggestion that the Applicant acted dishonestly or that he intentionally sought to contravene the legislation. The Respondent did not seek to rely on section 126A(3), which relates to an individual not otherwise being a fit and proper person to be a responsible officer.
  7. As a general observation, I note that on several occasions throughout his oral evidence and during questioning from the Tribunal the Applicant said he was not a lawyer or accountant. Some of his answers exhibited a lack of any detailed understanding of the legislative requirements applying it to him and the Fund.
  8. On the other hand, his answers concerning lodgement of returns are a different matter. He was cross-examined about a declaration he signed where he acknowledged the obligation to lodge annual returns for the Fund. The declaration is found at HB 29, his signature and the relevant acknowledgement at HB 31.
  9. The Applicant was reminded of the need to prepare audit accounts and lodge returns in the iCare email dated 27 July 2023.[57] In doing so, iCare identified the documents it required in order to complete this process. It would appear some documents were provided by the Applicant to iCare by email dated 19 January 2024.[58] However, following iCare sending information concerning its fees by email dated 22 January 2024[59] , the services of iCare were not engaged as revealed by the email exchange set out above.
  10. Before the Tribunal, the Applicant sought to divert responsibility by referring to those who had advised him or who he had approached for advice, those who had declined to act and those who had offered to provide services for which he said the fees sought were “extortionate”. However, these matters do not satisfactorily explain why, when this matter was heard on 19 and 20 March 2026, no returns had been lodged for the Fund at all and no one had been engaged to prepare and/audit accounts for this purpose.
  11. The Applicant had a proposal from iCare contained in the email from Mr Ma dated 29 January 2024. iCare offered to prepare returns for the previous 2 years for an amount of “$1672 ($616 + $1056), not $2000+”. This followed iCare’s earlier email in July 2023 reminding the Applicant of the need to lodge returns. The January email indicated the cost was for “2 years return and audit”. As the email correspondence discloses, the Applicant determined not to accept this offer, despite being told the returns were outstanding, instead informing iCare that he had spoken to a provider “closer to home”.
  12. Next, having been informed that iCare did not have any further documents, the Applicant took no steps to engage Mr Kauffmann to undertake the necessary work. Ultimately, Mr Kauffman indicated he was not prepared to act.
  13. While there are other, unidentified, professionals said to have been approached, they were not engaged to undertake the work. As to these others, there is no evidence about what their proposed fees were. In any event, insofar as fees needed to be paid to comply with the legislation, that was a consequence of establishing and operating the Fund.
  14. There was reference to attempts to discuss the matter with the ATO and suggestions that the Applicant had been given advice that any differences of opinion between the applicant and the ATO could be resolved by Tribunal proceedings. However, the ATO had no responsibility to prepare relevant accounting information. Further, any proceedings which might be taken in the Tribunal were not an alternative to the applicant ensuring that the Trustee complied with its reporting obligations, that accounts were prepared, audits conducted and required returns lodged.
  15. In Omibiyi and Commissioner of Taxation[60], Deputy President Britten-Jones said:
    There were contraventions of s 35D(1) because annual returns were lodged late for the 2015, 2018, 2019 and 2020 financial years. Non-compliance of this nature in a self-assessment system is very serious. The timely lodgement of annual returns is a fundamental requirement of the regulatory regime and is particularly important when there are actual contraventions which may go undetected if annual returns are not lodged.
  16. I agree with this statement. The legislation contemplates preparation of accounts, the auditing of accounts and the submission of that information to the Commissioner by way of annual returns. This requirement ensures the Fund complies with all statutory requirements and is operated in a manner which facilitates the provision of benefits to members.
  17. As a responsible officer of the Trustee, it was incumbent upon the Applicant to ensure these obligations were met.
  18. The Applicant failed to do so and the Trustee continues to breach the obligation under section 34D of the SISA.
  19. The explanations offered do not excuse the failures which occurred.
  20. At least in the case of iCare, the fees quoted to prepare returns and audit accounts appear modest. However, as evidenced by his email dated 29 January 2024 sent at 2:39 PM, the response from the Applicant was to seek to minimise the involvement of iCare and avoid paying fees which he thought were either unnecessary or unwarranted.
  21. The Applicant’s statement to iCare in that email that:
    “... no services are required going forward, and I’m not looking for any help. I just want someone to do the review ONLY to get the ATO off my back. Please let me know what the half hour of work will cost me”.

indicates, at least, that he misunderstood his obligations. Yet despite the emails from Mr Ma clearly indicating what was required, no steps were taken or have yet been taken by the Applicant to cause the Trustee to comply with the identified obligations.

  1. On any view, the Applicant’s contraventions of section 34D are serious and occurred in circumstances where the Applicant was clearly informed by a number of advisers about what was required to be done but failed to act.
  2. In relation to the contravention of section 62 if the SISA, I also regard this as a serious contravention.
  3. The contravention involved one transaction, namely the entry into the Agreement. However, the transaction involved the advancement of funds to MPG over a long period of time.
  4. There is no evidence of other assets of the Fund. As such, as stated above, the investment appears contrary to the November Investment Strategy which contemplated diversification of investments. This increased the risk to the members of the Fund due to the terms of the Agreement and the location of the project overseas. It also meant that, aside from interest on the bank deposit account, the Fund has not yet received any income from the transaction.
  5. It would be expected that if the requirement to lodge returns have been met and audits conducted, this contravention would have been detected at a much earlier stage.
  6. Consequently,
    (a) the repeated nature of the contraventions of the lodgement obligations under section 34D of the SISA, the time returns have been outstanding and the fact there is no evidence that steps satisfy me that they provide grounds for disqualification of the applicant; and

    (b) the contravention of section 62 of the SISA and the fact the funds advanced have not yet been repaid nor has any interest,

    satisfy me that there are grounds for disqualification of the Applicant.

  7. As to whether disqualification is appropriate and how the discretion under section 126A (2) should be exercise the observations of Deputy President Britten in Omibiyi are relevant. The Deputy President said:[61]
    34. Disqualification under section 126A is primarily aimed at protecting the integrity of the superannuation system. A key factor in making the decision to disqualify is whether the Applicant presents as a future compliance risk.

    35. The question of determining whether the applicant presents as a future compliance risk involves a forward-looking process to evaluate the risk of the applicant engaging in contraventions of the Superannuation Act in the future. In Minister for Immigration & Ethnic Affairs v Guo, Brennan CJ, Dawson, Toohey, Gaudron, McHugh and Gummow JJ observed as follows:

    The course of the future is not predictable, but the degree of probability that an event will occur is often, perhaps usually, assessable. Past events are not a certain guide to the future, but in many areas of life proof that events have occurred often provides a reliable basis for determining the probability — high or low — of their recurrence. The extent to which past events are a guide to the future depends on the degree of probability that they have occurred, the regularity with which and the conditions under which they have or probably have occurred and the likelihood that the introduction of new or other events may distort the cycle of regularity. In many cases, when the past has been evaluated, the probability that an event will occur may border on certainty. In other cases, the probability that an event will occur may be so low that, for practical purposes, it can be safely disregarded. In between these extremes, there are varying degrees of probability as to whether an event will or will not occur. But unless a person or tribunal attempts to determine what is likely to occur in the future in relation to a relevant field of inquiry, that person or tribunal has no rational basis for determining the chance of an event in that field occurring in the future.

    36. In Assistant Minister for Immigration and Border Protection v Splendido,24 Mortimer J said:

    [77] The bare recitation of what a person has done in the past, if used as a basis for a positive finding about what she or he may do in the future, is a reasoning process which is rejected by the judicial process in relation to fact-finding about legal responsibility for past events. More than the bare recitation of the past offending is required, and what is required for such evidence to be considered is strictly controlled by reference to the nature and circumstances of the offending, ...

    [78] The nature and circumstances of past offending are integral to any assessment of the risk, or likelihood, of future offending. Also of relevance are a range of other factors about the present circumstances of an individual which may bear on a risk of whether past offending conduct might or might not be repeated. It is these matters, and not the mere specification of a criminal record, which provide the probative basis for an assessment about the nature and extent of any risk of further offending.

  8. Also relevant are the observations of Thawley J in Merchant, where His Honour observed:[62]
    I do not place significant weight on protecting the investing public against the risk of re-offending. Having regard to his personal circumstances, Mr Merchant is only ever likely to be a director of the trustee of his own superannuation fund. To the extent that it is relevant, I do not believe Mr Merchant needs protecting from himself. To the extent he does, compliance with his undertakings offers sufficient protection.
  9. Notwithstanding Thawley J’s observations in the case of Mr Merchant, I would add that the protective nature of the disqualification power can provide general deterrence to those operating self-managed superannuation funds of which they are the sole member, or one of few related members, from failing to meet their obligations under the relevant legislation.
  10. In the present case, there is continuing non-compliance in respect of lodgement of returns and no evidence from the applicant to suggest steps have been taken to engage auditors and rectify these defaults. In my view, these facts, on their own, make disqualification appropriate.
  11. There is evidence that the funds provided under the Agreement are likely to be repaid in May of this year. There is no reason to suspect interest will not be paid to the Fund as required by the Agreement. However, this has not yet occurred.
  12. Following the disqualification of his wife, there is no evidence the Trustee has appointed another responsible officer other than the applicant. There is no evidence that the applicant, on behalf of the Trustee, has arranged to engage suitably qualified people to advise the Trustee and the Applicant about the requirements for properly administering the Fund. There is no evidence that the Applicant has because the Trustee to take steps to lodge outstanding returns as permitted by the Stay Order.
  13. Unlike Merchant, this is not a case where undertakings have been offered. It is not a case where the evidence establishes the Applicant has the skills and understanding of the legislative provisions to ensure compliance in the future. If he had had this understanding, he would have taken steps to rectify the identified contraventions.
  14. Finally, the circumstances of his wife, who is also a member, remain unclear. In the absence of evidence, there is a future risk to her interest in the Fund if the applicant were to remain a responsible officer.
  15. Having regard to the above, I am satisfied that the Applicant should be disqualified under section 126A(2) of the SISA and, in light of that conclusion, the decision of the Respondent should be affirmed.

DECISION

  1. The decision of the respondent to disqualify the applicant is affirmed.
