NT92/166 and Deputy Commissioner of Taxation [1993] AATA 500; (1993) 27 ATR 1102; 93 ATC 542 (18 May 1993)

Last Updated: 19 May 2009



INCOME TAX - Allowable deductions - Whether cost of medical insurance premiums incurred by professional footballer deductible - Whether difference between benefits received and treatment costs deductible - sec 51(1) Income Tax Assessment Act 1936.



Lunney v FC of T (1958-59) 100 CLR 478

FC of T v Cooper 91 ATC 4396



AAT Decision No 8721



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) NO. NT92/166

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re: APPLICANT



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION RESPONDENT



Before: Dr P Gerber, Deputy President

Date: 18 May 1993

Place: Sydney



DECISION



The Tribunal affirms the objection decision under review.



(Sgd P Gerber)

DEPUTY PRESIDENT



ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

) NO. NT92/166

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re: APPLICANT



And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION RESPONDENT



Before: Dr P Gerber, Deputy President

Date: 18 May 1993

Place: Sydney



REASONS FOR DECISION



1. Tom is a well known rugby league football player and a member of a New South Wales Rugby League Club ("the Club"). In the 1991 tax year he claimed as a deduction the amount he paid to a health fund ("the Fund") for the provision of comprehensive cover for both medical and hospital expenses, a cover which included, inter alia, the cost of a labour ward and up to $450 towards the provision of a hearing aid. In addition Tom claimed as a deduction some $950.40 incurred by him for medical expenses incurred in the treatment of injuries received on the field of battle (predominantly made up of physiotherapy), the amount being the "gap" between the actual cost of the treatment and the reimbursement received by him from his medical insurer. These claims were made in reliance on sec 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (as amended) ("the Act").



2. Exhibit "D" is the Playing Contract entered into by Tom with the Club. I was told that it was a standard form playing contract approved by the New South Wales Rugby League Ltd and The Association of Rugby League Professionals. After setting out the conditions of service and payment, the agreement provides as follows:



6. INJURY INSURANCE AND HEALTH FUND MEMBERSHIP



(1) ...



(2) The Player warrants that he is a financial member of, and contributor to, a recognised health fund approved by the Club and shall at all times during the continuance hereof ensure by prompt payment of contributions, as and when they fall due, and by compliance with all other rules and regulations of such fund, that he is and will remain at all times eligible to receive in case of sickness injury or other contingencies covered by such fund the maximum benefits offered by such fund and available on payment of the highest contribution. The Club shall at no time be liable for such contribution nor shall it be liable for any payments on the failure of the Player to comply with this condition.



7. PHYSICAL CONDITION

If prior to the commencement of the Sydney Premiership Competition in any year during the term of this agreement, the Club shall form the opinion that -



(a) The Player has failed to recover from an injury sustained during the preceding playing season or subsequently caused other than by playing the game for the Club or in the course of a training session conducted by the Club; and

(b) The Player has become unfit to play the game for the Club in consequence of such injury.



The Club may by notice in writing given to the Player within a period of seven (7) days prior to the first Premiership Competition match for the then current year require the Player to submit to a physical examination by a medical practitioner nominated by the Club. If the Player so requests the examination shall take place in consultation with a medical practitioner nominated by the Player and in that event the Club may request the President of the NSW Rugby League Limited or in his absence a person nominated by him to nominate a third medical practitioner to participate in such examination. The Player shall submit to such examination and shall truly answer questions asked of him in the course of the examination. Each of the medical practitioners so appointed are hereby authorised to furnish a report of such examination to the Club and the Player and to include therein his or their opinions as to whether or not the Player is unfit to play the game and, if unfit, as to whether or not he would be likely to be fit to play for the Club for a period of not less than six (6) weeks after the date of the examination. The said practitioners in furnishing such opinions shall act as experts and not arbitrators. If the opinion of any two of them is that the Player is unfit to play, and is likely to remain as such for the said period, the Club may by notice in writing to the Player terminate this Agreement, whereupon the Club shall be discharged from any liability to pay any instalments of playing fees or match fees which would otherwise have thereafter become due and payable to the Player.



3. Under "Special Conditions" appears the following:



G) The Player herein agrees that he will undertake his own personal Health Insurance to the minimum requirements of HCF Multi Cover.



4. Tom was called to give evidence and confirmed that it was a condition of his employment with the Club that he provide, at his own expense, the kind of personal health insurance cover here in dispute.



5. Not surprisingly, Mr Slater QC, who appeared for the respondent placed considerable reliance on the majority judgements in FC of T v Cooper 91 ATC 4396, just as Mr Healey of Counsel, who appeared for the applicant, sought to distinguish the case. At the time of lodging this reference, Cooper was the subject of a special leave application to the High Court of Australia, an application which, I understand, was subsequently withdrawn.



6. The facts in Cooper were as follows: Cooper, the taxpayer was a professional footballer who played with the Western Suburbs District Rugby League Football Club. He claimed as deductions the expenditure incurred by him for the purchase of additional food and drink which he consumed on the instructions of his coach in order to maintain an optimum playing weight of about 16½ stone and thus retain his strength to break the opponents' defensive line in First Grade Rugby League Football. The claim was made in reliance on sec 51(1) of the Act. The case had a chequered history. It first surfaced before a Board of Review (86 ATC 290). I gave an oral decision instanter in favour of the taxpayer; my two colleagues concurred in the decision. The Commissioner lodged an appeal to the Supreme Court of New South Wales which was dismissed (90 ATC 4580), a further appeal to the Full Federal Court was upheld by majority (Lockhart and Hill JJ; Wilcox J dissenting). At this stage the score stood 5:2 in favour of the taxpayer. As noted before, the applicant then sought special leave to appeal to the High Court which was subsequently abandoned. The majority decision of Lockhart and Hill JJ in Cooper thus stands as the final authority on the issue it decided, and is binding on this Tribunal. What, then, did the case decide? Looking at the majority decisions, one can safely predicate that the case is authority for the following propositions:



(i) outgoings on food and drink are generally of a private nature, and hence excluded from deduction under sec 51(1);



(ii) expenditure on food - even additional food - by a taxpayer who derives his assessable income from playing professional football, cannot be said to form part of the expenditure related to his income earning activity;



(iii) it was not a condition of the taxpayer's employment to consume the additional food. However, (obiter) even if it were, it did not follow that the expenditure was deductible, as the expenditure lacked the necessary connection with his income producing activity of playing football.



7. Cooper is clearly an important landmark, if only because three judges of the Federal Court took the opportunity to re-examine the leading cases which provide the litmus test of deductibility. Thus Lockhart J commenced his enquiry by restating some basic axioms of tax law:



(i) The phrase "incurred in gaining or producing assessable income" has been construed to mean incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable income: Amalgamated Zinc (De Bavay's) Ltd v FC of T [1935] HCA 81; (1935) 54 CLR 295;



(ii) "For expenditure to be an allowable deduction as an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, it must be incidental and relevant to that end; Ronpibon Tin" [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47. (at p 4400);



(iii) Outgoings incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and outgoings of a capital or domestic nature are not mutually exclusive; Ronpibon Tin at CLR 56; FC of T v Forsyth (1980-1981) 148 CLR 210; at 214; John v FC of T [1989] HCA 5; (1988-1989) 166 CLR 417 at 427. It was decided in John that there is no "necessary antipathy between a loss or outgoing incurred in gaining or producing assessable income and a loss or outgoing of a private nature.": John at CLR 431.



(iv) The question whether the additional expenditure of the taxpayer is deductible under s.51(1) cannot be answered simply by a process of reasoning that, because expenditure of this kind is a prerequisite to the earning of the taxpayer's assessable income (in the sense that it is necessary if assessable income is to be derived), it must be incidental and relevant to the derivation of income. It does not follow that such expenditure is incurred in or in the course of gaining or producing the income. The deductibility of the expenditure depends upon determining the essential character of the expenditure itself and not upon the fact that, unless it is incurred, the taxpayer will not be able to engage in the activity from which his income is derived.



8. Applied to the facts in Cooper as assumed by the majority of the Full Federal Court, the fact that the taxpayer believed he was required to follow his coach's "instructions" was a relevant consideration but not determinative of the question of the deductibility of the expenditure. Thus, his Honour noted:



For myself I would not interpret the [coach's] letter as an `instruction' to consume additional food and drink. ...



However, I am content for the purposes of this appeal to take the letter at its face value. Even if the letter is treated as an `instruction' to the taxpayer to eat the additional food and drink prescribed in it there is no necessary nexus between the instruction and the playing of football by the taxpayer. His activity as a professional footballer did not include the consumption of additional food and drink. (at p 4402).



9. Hill J, going over much the same ground, came to much the same conclusion. Thus, his Honour noted:



The income-producing activities to be considered in the present case are training for and playing football. It is for these activities that a professional footballer is paid. The income-producing activities do not include the taking of food, albeit that unless food is eaten, the play would be unable to play. Expenditure on food, even as here `additional food', does not form part of expenditure related to the income-producing activities of playing football or training. ...



In my view, the expenditure in question was not incurred by Mr Cooper in gaining or producing his assessable income; it lacked the necessary connection with his income-producing activities. (at p 4414).



10. In the result, the majority concluded that the expenditure on food and drink (additional or otherwise) was of a private nature. Hill J put it succinctly:



Food and drink are ordinarily private matters, and the essential character of expenditure on food and drink will ordinarily be private rather than having the character of a working or business expense. However, the occasion of the outgoing may operate to give to expenditure on food and drink the essential character of a working expense in cases such as those illustrated of work-related entertainment or expenditure incurred while away from home. No such circumstance, however, intervenes here. In particular, the mere fact that [the coach] suggested or even directed Mr Cooper to eat particular food, does not convert the essential character of the food as private into a working expense. (at p 4415).



11. Wilcox J (dissenting) noted:



Not many taxpayers would be able to satisfy a Board of Review or a Court that the consumption of a particular amount of food and beer was an incident of their employment. But the [taxpayer] did so. It seems to me to follow that he showed that the occasion of his expenditure on this additional food and beer was his employment as a footballer (Ronpibon Tin) and that the expenditure was connected with his operations as a professional footballer (Charles Moore [1977] HCA 38; (1977) 139 CLR 449). The [taxpayer] brought his expenditure on this food and beer into the same category as expenditure incurred by professional sportspeople on more usual ways of maintaining fitness for their sport - for example, physiotherapy or gymnasium fees - the deductibility of which would scarcely be doubted. (my emphasis).



His Honour continued:



Counsel for the Commissioner placed considerable weight on cases in which the High Court has adopted the `essential character' test to determine deductibility. They referred in particular to Lunney v FC of T (1958) 11 ATD 404; (1957-1958) 100 CLR 478, a case involving the deductibility of the cost of travelling between a taxpayer's home and his place of work, and Handley v FC of T [1981] HCA 16; (1980-1981) 148 CLR 182 and FC of T v Forsyth [1981] HCA 15; (1980-1981) 148 CLR 203, both cases involving the deductibility of expenses related to a barrister's home study. They submitted that, in the present case, the essential character of the expenditure was the nourishment of the taxpayer. Given that the respondent already consumed three meals a day and that the relevant expenditure was for a fourth meal directed to the maintenance of his optimum playing weight rather than his mere sustenance, I would not myself have chosen this description. But even if their description is correct, or if one substitutes some other description such as food and drink, does this mean that the claim must be rejected?



The problem about the `essential character' test, of course, is that it tends to beg the critical question. Everything depends upon the ambit of the facts selected for inclusion in the description of essential character; so that the making of that selection predetermines the outcome of the case. Take the instance of a taxpayer visiting another city for business purposes. The taxpayer incurs expenditure for meals at his or her hotel. On one view, the essential character of the expenditure is the sustenance of the taxpayer. Such a purpose has no connection with the derivation of assessable income other than in the broad sense - irrelevant because it is applicable to everyone - that one must eat to live and, therefore, to work and to earn assessable income. However, the expenditure may also be characterised as being the cost of sustenance incurred by the taxpayer because of his or her absence from home on business. The difference between the two characterisations is that the latter takes account of the occasion of the expenditure. When this characterisation is adopted, a work-connection immediately appears and a deduction is granted. So in the present case. If one characterises Mr Cooper's expenditure merely as food and drink, there is no nexus with the earning of assessable income; the claim must fail. If, on the other hand, the occasion of the expenditure is considered, it is seen as being for the additional food and drink necessary to maintain an optimum playing weight, and so safeguard the respondent's First Grade status and earnings. The nexus with assessable income becomes clear.



I think that it is misleading simply to characterise the [taxpayer's] expenditure as being for food and drink and to say, therefore, that the claim must fail. The whole of the relevant circumstances must be looked at in order to determine whether the expenditure was incurred in gaining assessable income. (pp 4404-5).



12. Applying what I perceive to be the decision of the majority in Cooper with respect to the "essential character" test, it must be said in this case that notwithstanding that it was a condition of this taxpayer's employment that he join a medical benefits fund, that expenditure lacks the necessary nexus with his income earning activities. So much had already emerged from Lunney v FC of T (1958-1959) 100 CLR 478:



.... It is, of course, beyond question that unless an employee attends at his place of employment he will not derive assessable income and, in one sense, he makes the journey to his place of employment in order that he may earn his income. But to say that expenditure on fares is a prerequisite to the earning of a taxpayer's income is not to say that such expenditure is incurred in or in the course of gaining or producing his income. Whether or not it should be so characterised depends upon considerations which are concerned more with the essential character of the expenditure itself than with the fact that unless it is incurred an employee or a person pursuing a professional practice will not even begin to engage in those activities from which their respective incomes are derived. (at pp 498-499)



13. Applied to this case, I consider that the expenditure on health insurance was incurred in consequence of the income earning activities rather than in gaining assessable income from those activities, and is thus not deductible under sec. 51(1) of the Act.



14. Much was made by Mr Slater of the fact that the health insurance cover included provision for reimbursement for services far wider than those required of professional footballers (pregnant footballers wearing hearing aids are unlikely to be selected). In the result, having concluded that the decision in Cooper is fatal to these claims, it is unnecessary to deal with that aspect of Counsel's submission.



15. Dealing with the medical expenses, I was briefly attracted to the proposition that they may have been part and parcel of the process by which the taxpayer retained that degree of fitness which is necessary for the income-earning activity of this taxpayer, i.e. an "expenditure incurred by professional sportspeople on more usual ways of maintaining fitness for their sport - for example, physiotherapy or gymnasium fees - the deductibility of which could scarcely be doubted"; (per Wilcox J in Cooper at p 4404). However, on reflection, bearing in mind the strong views expressed by the majority in that case, I have concluded that the expenditure on medical fees incurred in this case by this professional sportsman lacks the necessary connection with his income producing activities. Further, I am satisfied that, in the circumstances of this case, the essential character of the expenditure is nevertheless of a private nature, and thus excluded from deductibility under sec 51(1) of the Act.



16. Mr Healey, who "inherited" this case on short notice, said all that could be said in favour of his client, made a valiant attempt to salvage something from the wreckage. Thus, he submitted that if I were against him on the basis that the expenditure on health insurance was of a private nature, there was at least some aspect of the medical insurance cover which "has a character that attaches itself to a business expense" and hence would justify some kind of apportionment. It is an interesting submission and one with which I have some sympathy. However, I am satisfied that this argument is foreclosed by the majority judgments in Cooper. In any event, I have no basis on which I could arrive at any meaningful apportionment, even if such a procedure were open to me on the law.



17. For the above reasons the objection decision under review is affirmed.



18. This case was listed with two other cases involving two taxpayers whom I shall refer to as Dick and Harry (NT92/165 and NT92/270). Both are professional footballers and members of the Club. Each incurred medical insurance premiums in the amounts of $663 and $835 respectively and Dick incurred medical expenses (again representing the difference between treatment costs and medical insurance benefits received) in the amount of $337 (as to which I have no evidence as to the reasons for which he sought treatment). I was advised that Dick was unavailable to give evidence. The parties agreed that the result in Tom's case would govern the outcome in the other two cases subject to satisfactory evidence being provided to the respondent that Dick's medical expenses were related to injuries he received while performing his contract with the Club. In these circumstances, and for similar reasons to those given in respect of Tom's claims, I affirm the objection decisions under review in Cases NT92/165 and NT92/270.
