ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL



TAXATION - self-education expenses - taxpayer employed as an economic analyst in communications field - employer subscribes to relevant Scandinavian language magazines - limited interpreter services given by employer - taxpayer without support of employer undertakes university course to learn Swedish and undertakes summer school at Swedish university - whether knowledge of Swedish language will assist taxpayer to better carry out his function as an employee - employee’s motive in undertaking course at the Swedish university - remoteness of expenses claimed for deduction



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s.51(1)

REASONS FOR DECISION



14 July 1995 Deputy President G.L. McDonald



THE APPLICATION



1. This is an appeal against a decision of the respondent not to allow the applicant’s self-education expenses claimed under the provisions of s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”) for the 1990 and 1991 tax years. If the expenses are an allowable deduction, then there is no dispute as to quantum.



2. At the hearing Mr P. Roach, of counsel, represented the applicant. Oral evidence was given by the applicant and (via video) by Mr A, Mr L and Mr M, all of whom are employees of the same employer as the applicant and, each of whom was, at various times, the manager to whom the applicant reported. Mr Foley, a departmental advocate, represented the respondent. The respondent called no evidence. The Tribunal has before it the documents filed for the purposes of s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (“the T documents”) and other extensive documentation filed by the applicant, some of which duplicated material already contained in the T documents.



THE BACKGROUND FACTS



3. There is agreement in a large part between the parties as to the facts. Taking account of the points agreed in their statements of facts, issues and contentions and having regard to the other evidence, the Tribunal is satisfied as to the following. The applicant initially qualified as a pattern maker and subsequently as a mechanical design draftsman. He worked in those capacities with a firm of marine engineers until 1975 when, following an internal reorganisation of the firm, he was retrenched. At the time he was retrenched, he was enrolled, on a part-time basis, to complete a Bachelor of Science degree at the local university. Following retrenchment, he converted his part-time enrolment into full-time enrolment, and completed his degree specialising in mathematical modelling and statistics. After graduation, he commenced permanent employment with his current employer as an economist. In 1992 he completed a Bachelor of Arts degree, majoring in economics, which he had been studying on a part-time basis as an external student through an interstate university.



4. In 1990, he was employed in the international area of the employer at its corporate centre in Melbourne. The applicant told the Tribunal that he was interested in and responsible for the technology marketing area of the international service division of the employer. Exhibit F contains a copy of his duty statement where his responsibilities are described as:



“ Under the limited guidance, undertake activities required for the successful marketing of the [international] service, in particular market and economic analysis, to assist with the development of service and tariff package, financial analysis and transfer pricing. The successful applicant will be required to assist staff in other [international] marketing areas when necessary.”



5. In carrying out those responsibilities, it was the evidence of his supervisor, Mr E, that some initiative was useful, but in the major part of his work the applicant was directed in his activities.



6. The applicant’s employer operates in the field of communications, both in Australia and overseas. It is agreed that the area of communications in which the employer is engaged is a field in which there is rapid technological change occurring. The applicant told the Tribunal that knowledge of technological change and overseas market developments were fundamental to fulfilling his position. If the applicant had a knowledge of a language other than English, while not essential, it would be beneficial and could be useful to gain additional technological background and market knowledge, containing information not all of which was available in English and which would enable him to better fulfil his job specifications.



7. The applicant told the Tribunal that approximately 30 publications per month in his area of expertise were received in his employer’s library in Melbourne. He said it was his habit to visit the library and skim the publications, to take photocopies and file those articles which were of particular interest in his job. Of the 30 publications received, 5 or 6 were in foreign languages - all or nearly all being in Scandinavian languages. The applicant told the Tribunal that the publications provided no summaries in English (with a minor exception in the case of one Danish publication where a brief overview paragraph in English was provided). The applicant felt a knowledge of a Scandinavian language would help his understanding of those publications. He said that he had no other interest in learning Swedish. Initially, he enquired at The University of Melbourne to learn Norwegian but was steered towards Swedish after he was assured a knowledge of Swedish would also allow some facility in other Scandinavian languages and also in German. He enrolled to complete a unit in Swedish at The University of Melbourne in 1990. At that time, he was in the course of completing the Bachelor of Arts degree, mentioned in paragraph 3 herein. He told the Tribunal that, at about the same time, he was considering converting his enrolment from the Bachelor of Arts degree to a Bachelor of Economics degree and, if he had converted his enrolment, then the unit completed at The University of Melbourne in the Swedish language would have been credited to the converted degree. However, for a variety of reasons, which need not be explored here, he did not convert to a Bachelor of Economics degree. Consequently, the unit he undertook in Swedish at The University of Melbourne was not as part of a degree course.



8. The applicant told the Tribunal that The University of Melbourne course approached the study of the language through its literature, some of which used archaic wording. Obviously, this was not so relevant for his purposes. In order to get a better understanding of current language usage, he determined to enrol in a summer course at Uppsala University in Sweden, in the northern summer of 1990. The course was for four weeks, then originally the minimum time allowed for such courses. The applicant told the Tribunal that 300 to 400 non-Swedish students, many of whom were English speaking, and some American, were enrolled in the course. He said that he lived on site in a university college. The formal lecture time involved was four days per week from 8:30 a.m. to 3:00 p.m. Some after hours grammar and vocabulary exercises in preparation for the next day’s lectures were involved. The use of English in both classes and after hours was discouraged. A course outline for both 1990 and 1991 years is set out in exhibits C1 and C2, respectively. The Tribunal is satisfied that the courses offered at Uppsala University were aimed at providing students with a working knowledge of current usage of the Swedish language. After completing his course in 1990, he apparently spent two days on holiday in the United Kingdom. In 1991, he flew straight to Uppsala and returned directly to Australia. He apparently attended the third course in 1992, but no claim in relation to expenses incurred in attending that course is before the Tribunal.



THE CONTENTIONS



9. The applicant claims that the outgoings were incurred in gaining or producing assessable income, and he maintains that they were not losses, or outgoings of capital or of a private or domestic nature and, therefore, he is entitled to the deduction under s.51(1) of the Act. On the other hand, the respondent claims that the expenditure was not incurred in gaining assessable income and was private in nature. Alternatively, the respondent contends that there is an insufficient nexus between the skills acquired as a result of the expenditure incurred and the skills required for the applicant’s job as business analyst/planner. The respondent further claims that if there is some connection between the language skills and the applicant’s work duties, the expenditure in acquiring language skills was incurred at a point too soon to be considered to have been incurred in gaining or producing assessable income.



CONSIDERATION



10. The question for the Tribunal to determine is whether the taxpayer can establish a connection between the expenditure incurred and his assessable income. The fact that a claimant is an employee, rather than self-employed, does not affect the characterisation of the expenditure, providing it is incurred in the course of gaining or producing an assessable income (Commissioner of Taxation v Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 CLR 60 at page 64 per Dixon CJ; and also John Fairfax and Sons Pty Ltd v FC of T [1959] HCA 4; (1959) 101 CLR 30 at page 40 per Fullager J). There must, however, be a sufficient, as distinct from a general, connection between the outgoings and the assessable income, e.g. in FC of T v Hatchett 71 ATC 4184, Menzies J said in relation to that part of a claim made by a qualified primary school teacher for deduction of university fees incurred in the gaining of a university degree (at p.4187):



“ . . . I am not able to find any connection between the payments of fees and the assessable income of the taxpayer beyond the circumstance, which I take to be self-evident, that a teacher who has pursued university studies is likely to be a better teacher than if he had not done so and is therefore more likely to obtain promotion within the department. In my opinion this general consideration is not enough to make the fees deductible; there must be a perceived connection between the outgoing and assessable income.”



It should be recognised that:



“ The fact that the relationship between the expenditure and the gaining of income can vary in degree and extent from case to case has given rise to a multitude of decisions upon the section . . . .“



(FC of T v Highfield 82 ATC 4463 per Lee J 4470.) The decision of each case will depend on the facts and, with that in mind, there will be finely balanced decisions, e.g. FC of T v Wilkinson 83 ATC 4295 where an air-traffic controller successfully claimed a deduction for costs incurred in taking flying lessons (see also the case of a flight engineer who successfully claimed a tax deduction for expenses incurred in undertaking flying lessons - FC of T v Studdert 91 ATC 5006) whereas in Case Q88 (83 ATC 437) a claim by an air-traffic controller, who also held a pilot’s licence, for a deduction for expenses incurred in overseas travel during the course of which he visited control towers, attended “a flying convention” and additionally incurred expenses keeping up his flying activities in order both to retain his licence and to acquire a commercial pilot’s licence was disallowed - despite the taxpayer obtaining promotion following his return.



11. In determining whether such a sufficient connection exists, Dixon CJ in Finn’s case (supra) set out a number of factors which, in the circumstances of that particular case, lent support to the conclusion that the expenditure incurred by a senior design architect in the Western Australian Public Works Department, in travelling overseas to bring himself up to date with current architectural trends and to better his prospects for promotion, as follows (at p.67):



“ . . . three or four conclusions may be drawn which perhaps may be considered to govern the question whether the expenditure was incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income. In the first place it seems indisputable that the increased knowledge the taxpayer sought and obtained of his subject and the closer and more realistic acquaintance he secured of modern developments in design and construction made his advancement in the service more certain, and that in respect of promotion to a higher grade these things might prove decisive . . . In the second place, so far as motive or purpose is material, advancement in grade and salary formed a real and substantial element in the combination of motives which led to his going abroad. In the third place it is apparent that the heads of his Department, and indeed the Government itself, treated the use which he made of his long service and other leave to study architecture, increase his professional knowledge and study modern trends, as a matter not only of distinct advantage to his work for the State but of real importance in at least one project in hand. In the fourth place it was all done while he was in the employment of the Government, earning his salary and acting in accordance with the conditions of his service.”



Not any one of these factors will necessarily by itself, or in combination with others, lead to a finding that self-education expenses incurred by an employee will be allowable. The factors outlined are indicative only but do provide some guidance to fact finding bodies of the type of circumstance which may be looked at in reaching a determination.



12. In the instant case, the employer did not require, endorse, or, in any way, encourage the applicant to undertake the learning of Swedish, or to undertake the course at Uppsala University. It was not a necessary prerequisite of his employment to have either Swedish or any other second language. He undertook the study at his own initiative, independent of support from his employer. In Griffin v FC of T 86 ATC 4838, Yeldham J, in the Supreme of New South Wales, held that the taxpayer, a professional electrical engineer, employed by the New South Wales State Rail Authority, was entitled to a deduction when, during a period of leave, he travelled overseas to familiarise himself with developments of overseas railway electrical systems, and the absence of any express request by the employer to undertake the familiarisation did not stand in the taxpayer’s way of claiming a deduction (see particularly at pp.4845-4846). A request or some encouragement from an employer is an enabling factor (as in Finn’s case), but the absence of such a request is not a disentitling factor.



13. The applicant claimed his purpose in undertaking the study was so that he could keep up to date by reading material relating to his area of expertise which was in Swedish (or other Scandinavian languages which would be able to be understood by him once he acquired a working knowledge of Swedish). The Tribunal accepts the applicant’s evidence that he had no interest in learning the Swedish language other than arising in relation to the carrying out of his work functions. Motive was identified by Dixon CJ in Finn’s case as a relevant factor in deciding deductibility. The High Court in Fletcher v FC of T 91 ATC 4950 has recently affirmed this position when it stated (at p.4957):



“ At lease in a case where the outgoing has been voluntarily incurred, the end which the taxpayer subjectively had in view in incurring it may, depending upon the circumstances of the particular case, constitute an element, and possibly the decisive element, in characterisation of either the whole or part of the outgoing for the purposes of . . . [s 51(1)].”



Motive played a key role in the decision of the Federal Court in allowing a deduction in Studdert’s case (supra). In the instant case, the Tribunal is satisfied, having regard to the facts set out in paragraph 8 hereof, that the applicant’s only purpose in travelling to Uppsala was to improve his familiarity with modern usage of the Swedish language so that he could use it in the course of his employment. The fact that on the first visit he combined it with a two-day holiday in the United Kingdom is incidental and does not detract from what the Tribunal has found as being his purpose.



14. Another of the factors mentioned by Dixon CJ in Finn’s case was whether, as the result of incurring the expenditure, the taxpayer is likely to be promoted. In Finn’s case, the prospects of a promotion for the taxpayer, who was a senior architect employed by the Public Works Department of Western Australia, were found to be enhanced as the result of his being up to date with overseas trends in his profession. In the instant case, Mr L and Mr M both told the Tribunal that knowledge of the Swedish language would not be a decisive factor in any job promotion prospects available to the applicant. The applicant maintained that obtaining promotion in part involved a comparison of skills of the various candidates, and an additional language skill may, all other things being equal, be decisive. The circumstances in the applicant’s case are clearly different from those outlined in Finn’s case. Here, with respect to his future, what the applicant is saying reveals a general advantage, rather than specifies a sufficient connection of the sort contemplated as being necessary by Menzies J in Hatchett’s case (supra). Accordingly, the Tribunal is not satisfied that the applicant is likely to be promoted as a result of his learning Swedish, nor that it is likely to be a decisive factor in his acquiring a promotion. Again, a prospect of promotion if it is found to exist may enhance the taxpayer’s claim for a deduction. However, the absence of the prospect of promotion is not a disentitling factor.



15. The next issue to determine is whether the expenses incurred will enable the applicant to discharge his duties more effectively. Mr A stated (exh G) that a number of Swedish and Norwegian language articles would be relevant to the performance of his past, present and, given his experience, likely future functions with the employer. Later in his statement (exh G), Mr A said of the Swedish language articles:



“ . . . they provide a source of specific and unique techniques and information that can be utilised by him in undertaking his duties as outlined above. Some clearly provide enhanced or unique methodologies that are able to be utilised by [the taxpayer] to produce better forecasts or enable analyses or evaluations to be undertaken more effectively or efficiently. Other articles provide information that would result in a more reliable analyses or appraisals being provided with respect to his responsibilities in relation to Tariff Submissions, Business Planning and Product Planning.”



Mr L described the ability to access material in Swedish as not necessarily being a prerequisite but as adding value and enhancing the robustness and quality of output in what is “a difficult and somewhat unscientific field of business” (exh H). Mr M said in his statement (exh J):



“ Proficiency in written and spoken Swedish enlarges his capacity to perform his duties by enabling him to directly access information in the Swedish language.”



The Tribunal is satisfied that the evidence of each of the applicant’s supervisors supports the applicant’s contention that a knowledge of the Swedish language would enhance his ability to carry out his work functions.



16. The applicant told the Tribunal that the employer provided a translation service. It was said that to access the translation service, the employee wanting the translation, in the absence of an ability to translate himself or herself, was required to identify in some way the need for the translation before the translation could be authorised. That, on the face of it, seems to contain an inherent contradiction. According to the evidence of the applicant, the employer has had such a service available since the end of the Second World War. To access the service, the employee would need to travel to a university where the document could be translated for him/her. It was evident from what the applicant claimed that accessing the service was, at best, cumbersome. Despite its shortcomings, the Tribunal considers it relevant that the employer did provide access to an interpreter service and has taken this aspect into account in reaching its decision. However, it is apparent that the interpreter service was not designed to be utilised on a regular basis, whereas the material the applicant wanted to access was being provided on a regular monthly basis.



17. Taking into account all the above factors, the Tribunal is satisfied that the applicant would, having regard to the duties assigned to him and the position he occupied in the employer’s organisation, be enabled to carry out his duties more effectively if he could, as the result of having the language facility, access Swedish/Scandinavian language material provided by the employer.



18. The respondent sought to draw a distinction between the applicant incurring expenses to update or enhance his professional skills (e.g. in statistical analysis) which would, or may, provide a sufficiently direct connection between expenditure and deductibility - as distinct from the learning of a language which, of itself, does not provide any enhancement to his professional qualifications and could be categorised as providing him with, at best, an indirect connection. This argument raises the issue that the applicant has incurred expenditure at a point too soon, i.e. having the language skills does not of itself provide a connection to the assessable income: it is too remote. In support of this proposition, the respondent cited Lunney v Commissioner of Taxation (1958) 100 CLR 478, particularly at page 499. That case determined that expenses incurred travelling to work were in the nature of personal expenditure, rather than expenditure incurred necessarily in the gaining of income. In the instant case, the applicant’s skill allows him to read magazines considered relevant by the employer, purchased by the employer and placed in the employer’s library and which dealt with topics relevant to the taxpayer’s employment. In those circumstances, in the Tribunal’s view, a sufficiently close connection to allow for the conclusion that the expenditure has been incurred in order for the applicant to perform his functions as an employee more proficiently has been demonstrated. Accordingly, the amounts claimed for deductions are properly allowable under the provisions of s.51(1) of the Act.



19. The respondent also submits that the expenditure is private in nature and drew the Tribunal’s attention to Case U189, 87 ATC 1082 where the expenses of a medical practitioner undertaking a drama course were disallowed, and in Case V13, 88 ATC 163 where expenses claimed by a life insurance saleswoman who claimed to have undertaken courses in communications offered by the Church of Scientology in order to enhance her personal development and business skills, which she felt were inadequate, were discussed. The expenditure in the instant case is of a different nature from that outlined in the latter two cases. A doctor, with an interest in drama, and a life insurance saleswoman, in need of personal development, are very different situations from that of the applicant. One needs only ask the question, what other motivation other than that set out earlier in this decision could there be for the applicant to undertake the course of study? On the evidence presented, there is none.



20. For the above reasons, the decision under review is set aside, and the matter is remitted to the Commissioner with a direction that the applicant’s claim for self-education expenses for the taxation years ending 30 June 1990 and 1991 be allowed as deductions pursuant to the provisions of s.51(1) of the Act.
