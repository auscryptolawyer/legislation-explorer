ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Allowable Deductions - Repairs to property used by company to derive rent - whether expenditure on revenue account

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 : s.53

Lindsay v. FCT [1961] HCA 93; (1961) 106 CLR 377

Thomas & Go Pty Ltd [1965] HCA 54; (1965) 115 CLR 58

REASONS FOR DECISION

Before: Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Date: 31 July 1989

Place: Brisbane

The question to be decided is whether certain of the expenses incurred by the applicant company during the year ended 30 June 1984 in renovating a home unit are allowable as deductions. Essentially the claim is for allowable deductions on those aspects of the renovation which are claimed to be repairs not of a capital nature,

2. The relevant statutory provision is section 53 of the Income Tax Assessment Act ("the Act") which reads, so far as is relevant:

"53(1) Expenditure incurred by the taxpayer in the year of income for repairs, not being expenditure of a capital nature, to any premises, or part of premises, ... held, occupied or used for the purpose of producing assessable income ... shall be an allowable deduction."

3. In its return of income for the year ended 30 June 1983 the company is described as a property developer and made the following claim for repairs:

"During the year the company undertook a major repair programme of its rental property. These repairs consisted of the remodelling of bathrooms, powder rooms and kitchens due to age deterioration and general wear and tear.

$

Plumbing 60

Kitchen Repairs & Replacements 10,852*

Replacement of Stoves & Appliances 2,457*

Replacement of Carpets 3,140*

Replacement of Curtains & Blinds 6,017*

Structural Repairs 5,677*

Painters 3,955*

Replacement of Glass 326

Replacement of Antenna 290

Repairs to Rocks 105

Pest Control 140

Electrical Repairs 37

Sundries 20

38,076

The claims disallowed, in effect, by the respondent Commissioner when making the assessment for the year of income ended 30 June 1984 have been indicated with an *. The issue arises in the 1984 year because of unrecouped losses carried forward resulting in no taxable income for the year ended 30 June 1983.

4. In its objection the company conceded the claims in respect of carpets - $3,140, curtains and blinds - $6,017 and the structural alterations - $5,677. In making the assessment the respondent had allowed depreciation in respect of the carpets, curtains and blinds.

5. Upon determination of the objection the respondent accepted that the claim for painting - $8,955 should be allowed and amended the assessment to allow a deduction for that amount.

6. When the matter came on for hearing the applicant was represented by its accountant and the respondent by an officer of the Australian Taxation Office.

  1. 7. At the commencement of the hearing the applicant's representative sought to amend the notice of objection by deleting the concession in relation to the structural repairs ($5,677) and to increase the amount in dispute from $22,264 to $27,941.

8. The respondent's representative opposed the application. However, the Tribunal ruled that the justice of the matter required that the grounds of the objection be so amended, the respondent conceding that he did not suffer any prejudice.

9. At a later stage in the hearing the applicant's representative made a further application to amend the objection by increasing the claim for structural repairs from $5,667 to $19,000. That was the first occasion that the applicant had asserted that the amount was an allowable deduction. No such claim was made in the return or the notice of objection, nor had it otherwise been asserted to the respondent Commissioner. The increased claim was clearly not even contemplated by the applicant until part way through the hearing. Nothing was said about such a claim at the start of the hearing. The Tribunal decided that the applicant could not raise new grounds of objection at this late stage and the application was therefore rejected.

  1. Evidence was given before the Tribunal by an architect who had responsibility for the work carried out and also the Public Officer of the company who was called by the respondent.
  2. The Public Officer's evidence established that the home unit in question is owned by himself but used by the company for the purpose of deriving rental income. The evidence to establish that the company in fact derived the rental income was less than logically probative. The only relevant item appearing in the applicant's balance sheet as an asset is the cost of the renovations not charged to the Profit and Loss Account. (Exhibits B and 5). For present purposes I am prepared to infer that the rental income was derived by the applicant company although it is by no means clear that is the case. However, as the respondent has allowed some expenses as deductions and also allowed depreciation in respect of carpets, curtains and blinds it is apparent that the Commissioner is content to accept that the applicant derived the rental income.

12. What happened in the year ended 30 June 1983 is that the company incurred expenditure of $76,017 in renovating the home unit of which it treated $38,076 as repairs chargeable to the profit and loss account and $37,941 as an investment shown in the balance sheet. The latter amount, it must be inferred, was thought to be of a capital nature not chargeable to revenue account for accounting purposes. That position has caused me considerable concern given that the Company does not own the property and appears to have no rights in respect of the property other than the alleged right to receive the rents therefrom. In this situation it is difficult to understand why an apportionment between capital and revenue was thought to be necessary for accounting purposes. The renovation might be expected to increase the rental value of the property but it is not clear that any other lasting benefit accrued to the applicant because of the renovations. Any such benefit accrued to the owner of the home unit who was the occupant.

  1. It will be apparent that there are a number of unanswered questions in this application. However in view of my ultimate conclusion it is not necessary to pursue them.
  2. The rental income derived by the Company may be set out as follows:

Year Ended 30 June Rental Income

$

1980 NIL

1981 14,587

  1. 2,816
  2. 5,006
  3. 3,200

The evidence is less than clear as to why the amount of rent derived fluctuated by such large amounts. The only plausible explanation on the evidence is that the company ceased to derive rent on an arms length commercial basis when the renovations were commenced in 1982 and in the year ended 30 June 1983, after the renovations had been completed it derived rent from a person associated with the company. The Public Officer stated in evidence that he resides in the home unit. Given the location of the property in one of the harbourside eastern suburbs of Sydney the Tribunal would be entitled to infer that a home unit recently renovated would command an economic rental considerably in excess of the amounts derived in each of the years ended 30 June 1983 and 30 June 1984. It is not necessary to pursue this point.

15. Exhibit C is a copy of the plan detail and specification of the renovations drawn by the architect who gave evidence before the Tribunal. The plan is dated 28 July 1982.

16. The position is that the company has derived assessable income from the property and the respondent Commissioner made no issue of the quantum of that income before the Tribunal. It is also clear that the Company undertook a major refurbishment of the home unit within approximately two years of it becoming entitled to derive the rental income.

17. Was the expenditure incurred in making repairs to the premises? The answer must be yes in the sense that the home unit is the entirety to be considered.

18. In Lindsay v. F.C. of T. [1961] HCA 93; (1961) 106 CLR 377 Kitto J posed the question to be answered as follows at pages 383-4:

"The problem is to characterise the expenditure according to the familiar distinction between repair, in the sense of restoration by renewal or replacement of subsidiary parts of a whole, and renewal in the sense of reconstruction of the entirety, meaning by the entirety not necessarily the whole but substantially the whole of the subject matter under discussion."

19. Further clarification was provided by Windeyer J in W Thomas & Co v. F.C. of T. [1965] HCA 54; (1965) 115 CLR 58 where his Honour considered the general principles to be applied in deciding whether expenditure had been incurred on repairs may be summarised as follows:

(a) repair" means works done to make good a deterioration that had occurred by ordinary wear and tear or by the operation of natural causes during the passage of time (p.72);

(b) "repair" involves a restoration of a thing to a condition it formerly had without changing its character (p.72);

(c) in the case of a thing considered from the point of view of its use as distinct from its appearance, it is restoration of efficiency in function rather than exact repetition of form or material that is significant (p.72);

(d) expenditure is on revenue account when the repairs are to defects resulting from wear and tear arising from the operation of the person who incurs the expenditure (p.72); and

(e) the cost of putting into good order an asset acquired for use in the buyers business is part of the cost of acquiring the asset (p.72).

(cf. Case V2 88 ATC 107)

  1. This application must fail because it has not been established before the Tribunal that the expenditure claimed was to repair defects resulting from wear and tear arising from the applicant's operations. The works carried out were extensive and expensive. In summary, they can be described as a complete renovation of the unit designed to radically improve the unit rather than simply restore it. That conclusion is illustrated and supported by the fact that the contract to "repair" the kitchen was really a contract to build a new kitchen at a cost of $10,285. That a new kitchen was built is established by Exhibit C and the evidence of the architect. Such expenditure is on capital account and therefore of a capital nature. It has not been established that the repairs were on revenue account and therefore that the expenditure claimed represents allowable deductions within the terms of section 52.
  2. Given that the applicant has failed to establish that the assessment is excessive the objection decision under review will be affirmed.
