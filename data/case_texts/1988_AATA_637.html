ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - whether an administration fee was an allowable deduction under s 51 - purpose of the expenditure: Ure followed

REASONS FOR DECISION

30 August 1988 Mr B J McMahon (Senior Member)

  1. The applicant is a property owning company. On land that it owned in suburban Newcastle, it built a showroom in 1960 and in 1965 a 2-storey office and spare parts store. From these premises a motor trading business was carried on by another company which had the same ownership and directors as the applicant. The trading company had the franchise in the Newcastle area for a well known brand of motor vehicle.
  2. The building of the improvements on the land was financed by loans to the applicant from the trading company and from its directors. In evidence, the managing director of both companies was not sure of the terms of the loans. It seems likely that they would have been repayable at call. He was unsure of what interest had been arranged. No minutes were available to record the terms of any such advances. In the profit and loss statement of the trading company for the year ended 30 June 1980, with comparable figures for the year ended 30 June 1979, there does not appear to be any figure representing receipt of interest. In the corresponding profit and loss account of the applicant company, an amount of $2,545 is shown for interest paid in 1979. The corresponding amount in 1980 was $4,895. However, the managing director gave evidence that the property owning company had also borrowed from a public finance company. This could account for the interest payments. It seems to me more probable than not that the loans to the property owning company by the trading company and its directors were repayable at call free of interest. Because of the rental policy designed to give a minimum rental to the property owning company, it would have been impossible, in any event, for the property company to pay interest from its cash flow.

3. The rent paid by the trading company to the property company was an amount designed merely to cover expenses and outgoings of the applicant company. This arrangement continued until 1979. By then, the proprietors of the trading company decided that they wish to retire from the business and to assign the franchise to another company which would occupy the premises as tenant of the applicant. In order to do this, it was necessary to obtain the consent of the national franchise holder. The managing director put up the name to that holder of a company that he considered suitable as an assignee but it was rejected. Ultimately, through contacts between the proprietor of the national franchise holder and a business acquaintance in Hong Kong, a Hong Kong trading company was selected. The proprietor of the Hong Kong company was a German national who had not previously dealt with the trade or with local officials in Australia. The managing director was obliged to accept the nominee as the new holder of the franchise despite his reservations as to its suitability. As he put it in evidence before me "I had no say in it".

  1. In January 1979, a formal agreement was concluded between the applicant and the new franchisee to lease the premises for a gross annual rent of $54,000. The lease was to commence on 1 July 1979. For 6 months after the documentation was signed the managing director spent, as he said, considerable time with the new franchisee, introducing him to clients and sorting out his problems with the local council as to signs and other matters connected with the new business. As the new franchisee had no experience of operating in Australia he felt in need of this assistance. In cross-examination, the managing director agreed that it was a term of the assignment arrangement that he should offer this assistance to help the transition by which he extricated himself from the dealership. The trading company did not sell any goodwill to the new franchisee, but spare parts and other current assets changed hands.
  2. Two days prior to the commencement of the lease, namely on 29 June 1979, there was said to have been a meeting of directors of the applicant company held at its accountant's office. The following is the relevant text of a minute tendered in evidence -

"The board was advised that [trading company] had agreed to sell its business to [new franchisee] and as the current lessees of the premises at [address] they supported the granting of a lease or sub-lease to [new franchisee].

It was further agreed that during the next 12 months considerable demands would be made upon the company to provide additional services and these would develop on a day to day basis. The offer of [trading company] to provide all services required for a fee of $42,250 was accepted."

6. There was apparently no corresponding minute at any meeting of directors of the trading company. The minute tendered in evidence as Exhibit A is the only written record of the arrangement between the 2 parties. A sum of $42,250 for an administration fee was debited to the property company and credited to the trading company for the financial year ended 30 June 1980 by way of journal entry. A claim for a deduction under s 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act for this administration fee was then made by the property company and was disallowed. In its objection, the applicant set out the services which it alleged the trading company supplied as consideration for the administration fee of $42,250. Those services were said to be as follows -

"(1) Negotiations and arrangements with tenants in connection with arranging suitable tenants and tending to their needs as and when required. These needs are accepting or rejecting changes to the premises eg display, advertising, etc and also arranging any necessary maintenance with approved contractors.

(2) The company's $54,000 rental income is collected on its behalf by members of the group.

(3) The company shares the use of office space with other members of the group. The company has the use of 240 sq ft.

(4) During the year the company has been provided with loan funds from shareholders and members of the group totalling $114,725-92 and the fee is also paid to cover the cost of the use by that company of these funds."

  1. The objection was disallowed and this application is brought to review that objection decision. In statutory reasons provided by the respondent for rejection of the claim, it was alleged that firstly the amount claimed was not allowable as a deduction under s 51 of the Act. Secondly, it was said that the arrangements giving effect of the claim did not have, and were not intended to have, the legal consequences apparent on the face of the transaction and documents, with the result that those arrangements amounted to a sham. Thirdly, it was said that one or more of the transactions giving rise to the claim for a deduction was void as against the Commissioner, having regard to the operation of s 260 of the Act. On the hearing of these proceedings, the sham and the s 260 arguments were abandoned. The issue therefore is whether or not the amount claimed is deductible under s 51 of the Act.
  2. Evidence was given that between 1960 and 1979, any rent payable to the property owning company had been designed so that it simply covered the property owning company's expenses and did not expose it to any tax liability. When asked by me why the administration fee had been thought of in the first instance, the managing director was frank enough to admit that its purpose was to reduce the company's tax and to maintain the fiscal status quo. He later resiled from that position during the course of his evidence. However, it is a fact that it did result in a favourable tax situation for both companies. After debiting the administration fee of $42,250, the applicant returned in its profit and loss statement for the year ended 30 June 1980 an operating profit of $633. However, as it had deductible losses of previous years of $628 available to it, its taxable income was reduced to $5. In its corresponding profit and loss account, the trading company brought in the administration fee as a receipt, but because of overhead and other expenses it returned a net operating loss of $8,542 for the year. Had the administration fee remained in the property owning company, tax would have been payable on $42,245. By moving the fee to the trading company which was then operating at a loss, that tax was avoided. The full amount of $54,000 rent was paid by the new franchisee to the applicant during the year in question and thus was received by the group virtually free of tax.

9. The managing director was asked how the original sum of $42,250 was arrived at during the meeting of 29 June 1979. He remembered some conversation about the company's taxation but could not remember how the sum was calculated. He said to me : "Possibly it was an accountant's figure ... I did not understand it myself at the time." The way in which the amount was arrived at also puzzled officers of the respondent Commissioner. In response to their questions, a document was prepared by the applicant's accountants and was tendered in evidence as Exhibit B. It was quite clear that the document was not in existence at the time that the administration fee had been agreed upon. It was also quite clear that it was prepared some time subsequently in order to show, not so much the way in which the amount had been arrived at, but the way in which it could be justified from a commercial point of view. The managing director was cross-examined on this document and its various component parts.

  1. The first item of justification was that the administration fee was partly to service shareholders' loans. Amounts were shown as owing to the managing director, to his wife and to the trading company. Interest was calculated at 20% per annum on those balances and the results were shown as a way in which part of the administration fee could have been accounted for. In my view, this is a theoretical exercise and does not, in any way, assist in identifying the purpose of the payments. Firstly, the payment was made only to the trading company. No payments were made to the 2 individuals referred to. Secondly, the evidence indicates that the loans in question were more probably than not repayable at call free of interest. The suggestion of interest at the rate of 20% per annum is an afterthought to account for $18,128-34. A further sum of $8,450 was added on to this to represent interest on the administration fee itself of $42,250. Clearly, there is no basis in fact for accepting this as a factor in the thinking of the directors when the amount of the administration fee was arrived at.
  2. There were 3 other items included in the document. The first was a charge for management of properties and a sum of $1,000 was suggested. There was no evidence that the trading company continued to manage the property. There was evidence that the managing director of the trading company assisted the new franchisee in settling in, and did what he could to make the new franchisee's business profitable, so that at the very least it could pay the rent. This is far from activities that could properly be described as management of properties.
  3. The next item of $3,169 is for collection of income. It is calculated as 7 1/2 per cent of $42,250. I will leave aside whether such a calculation should have been made on the basis of a percentage the gross rent of $54,000 rather than on the basis of a percentage of the administration fee. Neither calculation seems to me to be based on anything but fancy. The managing director's evidence was that the rent was paid directly by the new franchisee to the applicant company. His point was that if he went away an estate agent would have had to be authorised to collect it and that this was the collection fee that he would have charged. In fact this did not happen. Such a fanciful possibility cannot be relied upon as evidence of any basis of the directors' thinking when the amount of the administration fee was set.
  4. The "share use of office facilities" amount of $1,040 was left unexplained as to the amount or as to the basis of any agreement between the parties. To this total sum was added what was described as a profit margin of 50%, $9,238-50. This odd figure does not seem to correspond with anything in Exhibit B. Certainly there is no figure of $18,477 of which this could be half. The managing director could not explain that item. He said somewhat wistfully that it was a good profit margin and one that they would like to earn if they could. Even with all these imaginative items, the total could be brought up only to $41,025-84. The administration fee charged was $42,250.
  5. In the profit and loss account of the trading company, there are no items of expense that could be attributed to the gaining of this assessable income of $42,250. The only items to which the managing director could point were travelling and telephone expenses incurred in assisting the new franchisee. However, it appears from the corresponding figures for the previous year for both of those items, that they were less in 1980 than they had been in 1979. The objection also justified part of the administration fee on the basis of expenses incurred for arranging suitable tenants. The evidence was that there was only one tenant and that the trading company did not arrange for that tenant. As the managing director said, he had no say in the matter.
  6. In cases where a deduction is claimed under s 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, one must ask oneself what in fact was the expenditure for. The generally accepted starting test is the classic and much quoted passage in Ronpibon Tin NL v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1949] HCA 15; 78 CLR 47 where a Full Bench said at page 60 -

"It is important not to confuse the question how much of the actual expenditure of the taxpayer is attributable to the gaining of assessable income with a question how much would a prudent investor have expended in gaining the assessable income. The actual expenditure in gaining the assessable income, if and when ascertained, must be accepted. The problem is to ascertain it by an apportionment. It is not for the Court or the Commissioner to say how much a taxpayer ought to spend in obtaining his income but only how much he has spent."

  1. Nevertheless, there is ample authority for the proposition that if the expenditure is not commercially justifiable, this may well be an indicator that the expenditure was not for an appropriate purpose. This view is referred to by Fisher J in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v Phillips 78 ATC 4361 at 4371. It was adopted as the basis of the decision in Ure v Federal Commissioner of Taxation 81 ATC 4100. In that case a Full Bench of the Federal Court applied a "purpose" test in determining whether outgoings were wholly or partially deductible. Deane and Sheppard JJ took the view that where there is no obvious commercial explanation for an outgoing, the Court may look into the indirect objects and advantages which the taxpayer sought in making the outgoing. As such, the Court was justified in looking beyond the immediate object or purpose to the private or domestic uses ultimately made of the principal sums in that case. Brennan J took a similar view. His Honour said that the disparity in interest rates between the borrowing and lending described in that case suggested the existence of purposes ulterior to the earning of interest at 1%.
  2. In the present case the only identifiable purpose or object for the payment of the administration fee is, in my view, to move the rental income received from the new franchisee, which would have been taxable in the hands of the applicant, to a vehicle where it would not have attracted such tax. Expenditure of the administration fee was therefore not for the purpose of gaining assessable income, it was not incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, nor was it necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income. It is therefore not deductible under the terms of s 51 of the Income Tax Assessment Act nor is it deductible under the terms of any other section of the statute.

18. The objection decision under review is therefore affirmed.
