Re David Alan Knight Ex Parte: Official Trustee In Bankruptcy; Beverley Carver; Philip Kingston Carver and the Registrar General of New South Wales [1992] FCA 286 (19 June 1992)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

Re: DAVID ALAN KNIGHT

Ex parte: OFFICIAL TRUSTEE IN BANKRUPTCY; BEVERLEY CARVER; PHILIP KINGSTON

CARVER and THE REGISTRAR GENERAL OF NEW SOUTH WALES

No. N B445 of 1992

FED No. 431

Bankruptcy

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

BANKRUPTCY DISTRICT OF THE STATE OF NEW SOUTH WALES

GENERAL DIVISION

Wilcox J.(1)

CATCHWORDS

Bankruptcy - Application by trustee to declare transaction void - Disposition of house in return for an interest in right to publish material - Whether transaction intended to disguise an arrangement to defeat the claim of a creditor - Whether applicant had established that transferee did not act in good faith.

Bankruptcy Act 1966, s.121

HEARING

SYDNEY

19:6:1992

Counsel for the Applicant: B. Skinner

Solicitors for the Applicant: Australian Government Solicitor

Counsel/Solicitors for First Respondent: Appeared for Self

Counsel for the Second and

Third Respondents: W. Terracini

Solicitors for the Second and

Third Respondents: Barnetts

ORDER

THE COURT DECLARES THAT:

1. The transfer by the first respondent, David Alan Knight, to

the second respondent, Beverley Carver, on or about 3

January 1992 of the land comprised in Folio Identifier

17/2/1951 is void as against the applicant, the Official

Trustee in Bankruptcy.

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

2. Within 28 days of this day or such further time as the Court

or a Judge may allow, the said second respondent execute a

memorandum of transfer in registrable form of the said land

in favour of the said applicant.

3. The second respondent produce all such documents; and do all

such things as are necessary to procure the registration of

the said applicant as registered proprietor of the said

land.

4. Pending compliance with the said orders the second

respondent, her servants and agents, be restrained from

disposing of or transferring the said land to any other

person or mortgaging or otherwise encumbering the same; and

5. The second and third respondents pay the costs of the

applicant and the fourth respondent.

Note: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

The transaction which gives rise to this case was negotiated between two inmates of the Metropolitan Remand Centre at Malabar. That much is common ground; but there is a major disagreement between the parties as to the substance of the negotiations.

2. The applicant, the Official Trustee in Bankruptcy, is the trustee of the bankrupt estate of David Alan Knight, the first respondent. Mr Knight was made bankrupt on his own petition on 18 February 1992. On the same day he completed a Statement of Affairs wherein he revealed that, on 3 January 1992, he had disposed of to Beverley Carver, the second respondent, a semi-detached house situate at 3B Bellevue Gardens, Bellevue Hill. The Statement of Affairs stated that the unit was worth $250,000 but that nothing had been received for it. Mr Knight claimed that he had been defrauded of this property. The Statement of Affairs showed a debt of "approx $18,000" as being due to the Australian Taxation Office and that "J Poulton", the bankrupt's former de facto wife Janette Poulton, was a secured creditor in the sum of $250,000 - her security being the Bellevue Hill property.

3. Also on 18 February, Mr Knight made a statutory declaration in which he said that he had been "tricked, manipulated and threatened" by Philip Kingston Carver, the husband of Beverley Carver and third respondent to this proceeding, into signing over the property to Mrs Carver "for worthless or near worthless consideration". Particulars were set out.

4. The Official Trustee became aware of these allegations. Mrs Carver had put the house on the market. It was to be offered for sale at auction at 6.30pm on 20 February 1992. The Official Trustee sought an interlocutory injunction restraining the sale. Notice of the application was given to Mrs Carver. Her solicitor appeared to contest the granting of interlocutory relief. However, for reasons which I then gave, on the afternoon of 20 February I acceded to the application and made an order restraining Mrs Carver, her servants and agents, from selling, mortgaging, encumbering or disposing of the property. I also ordered that the Registrar General of New South Wales be joined as a respondent to the proceeding and restrained from registering any dealings in respect of the property until further order. At a subsequent directions hearing, the Registrar-General appeared, by a solicitor, to submit to the order of the Court, except as to costs, and was excused from further attendance.

5. On 28 February the Official Trustee filed a Further Amended Application and an Amended Statement of Claim. The Further Amended Application sets out the relief now claimed:

"1. A declaration that the bankruptcy of David Alan Knight be deemed

to have relation back to, and have commenced on 3 January 1992.

2. A declaration that the transfer by the First Respondent to the

Second Respondent of the land comprised in Folio Identifier

17/2/1951 is void as against the applicant.

3. A declaration that the land comprised in Folio Identifier

17/2/1951 is property divisible amongst the creditors of the First

Respondent.

4. An order that the Second Respondent do all thing(s) necessary

including the execution of a memorandum of transfer is registrable

form to transfer the land comprised in Folio Identifier 17/2/1951

to the applicant."

6. Folio Identifier 17/2/1951 is the certificate of title relating to the Bellevue Hill house. Mr Knight does not dispute the Official Trustee's claim; indeed he provides the evidence critical to its success. And, as mentioned, the Registrar-General takes no active role. But Mr and Mrs Carver, whom I will for convenience refer to as "the respondents", strenuously resist the claim. The claim is based on s.121 of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 which provides:

"121.(1) Subject to this section, a disposition of property, whether

made before or after the commencement of this Act, with intent to

defraud creditors, not being a disposition for valuable consideration in

favour of a person who acted in good faith, is, if the person making the

disposition subsequently becomes a bankrupt, void as against the trustee

in the bankruptcy.

(2) Nothing in this section shall be taken to affect or prejudice the

title or interest of a person who has, in good faith and for valuable

consideration, purchased or acquired the property the subject of the

disposition or any interest in that property.

(3) In this section, 'disposition of property' includes a mortgage of

property or a charge on or in respect of property."

7. It is common ground that Mr Knight made a disposition of property on 3 January 1992 - namely the transfer of the house - and that he subsequently became bankrupt. Mr Knight says that the disposition was made with intent to defraud a creditor; namely Ms Poulton. The Official Trustee relies on this evidence. Although Mr and Mrs Carver make no concession, their major line of defence does not depend on traversing that evidence. Their primary case is that, whatever Mr Knight's intent, the disposition was made for valuable consideration in favour of a person who acted in good faith. That answer depends upon the Court accepting Mr Carver's version of the discussions between himself and Mr Knight. The primary issue between the parties is whose version of those discussions is to be preferred. Counsel for the respondents strongly attacks Mr Knight's credit. Counsel for the Official Trustee makes a similar attack upon Mr Carver. In order to understand the cases made by the parties, and to place in context the attacks upon credit, it is necessary to refer to the personal histories of Mr Knight and Mr Carver, insofar as they are revealed by the evidence, and the critical events of December 1991 and January 1992.

8. According to evidence elucidated from Mr Knight during the course of cross-examination, he grew up under the name David Alan Knipe. Apparently his parents, or the people he believed to be his parents, separated at some stage. When Mr Knight was asked about his date of birth, he responded that there was doubt about that "because I was given one date by my mother and another by my father when I moved in with him when I was 18 or so". Mr Knight also said: "when I was 21 or 22 my father, my so-called father, told me that in fact he wasn't my father". As a consequence, Mr Knight said, he began to assume the name "Knight", instead of the original "Knipe". When he needed a passport, he applied for one under the name "Knight". For this purpose he used a fabricated birth certificate which stated that he was born in Melbourne in 1953, whereas Mr Knight's belief is that he was born in Sydney in 1952. One of the charges upon which Mr Knight is presently being held in custody concerns this false passport application. Mr Knight has already indicated an intention to plead guilty to that charge. By way of explanation, Mr Knight said before me:

"... the passport was in the name I was assuming. I did not get the

passport in an attempt to defraud anyone. I simply wished to assume an

identity and forget my past with my father and its associated trauma".

9. According to a curriculum vitae of Mr Knight which was annexed to an affidavit of Mr Carver, Mr Knight attended the University of Melbourne between 1974 and 1977. He graduated with the degrees of Bachelor of Science and Bachelor of Arts. Mr Knight conceded in cross-examination that some of the material contained in the curriculum vitae was wrong, but he said that the information about his degrees was accurate. He was not challenged on further claims that, at about this time, he became involved with a student publication, "Farrago", and that he participated in some on-campus theatre. Certain it is that, in the late 1970's or early 1980's, he began to write plays. It appears that several of his plays have been publicly read or workshopped. At least two have been performed, one by a major theatre company. It seems that Mr Knight is a gifted playwright.

10. In about 1976 Mr Knight commenced to live, in Melbourne, in a de facto relationship with Ms Poulton. They had two sons, born in 1982 and 1984. Possibly because of the financial pressures placed upon him by these family responsibilities, possibly for other reasons, in about 1986 Mr Knight embarked upon a scheme to defraud the Commonwealth by making a series of applications for the Austudy allowance, using false names. The scheme involved his opening a separate bank account under each false name. The scheme was successful, in the sense that it yielded Mr Knight some $15,500. But it was soon discovered. Mr Knight was prosecuted and served a term of imprisonment. Some moneys were repaid to the Commonwealth but Mr Knight concedes that about $4,000 is still owing. This sum is apparently additional to his tax debt.

11. At about this same time, Mr Knight and Ms Poulton separated. The children remained with their mother. In February 1987 Mr Knight purchased the Bellevue Hill house. He apparently came to Sydney at that time and commenced to reside in the house. Notwithstanding this move, Mr Knight maintained contact with Ms Poulton and the children. But he was not a good provider, with the result that Ms Poulton made an application to the Family Court of Australia for a maintenance order. On 12 April 1991 that Court made a consent order whereby Mr Knight was obliged to pay Ms Poulton the sum of $7.100 within 30 days. At that time it was apparently envisaged that Mr Knight would rent out his house. The order provided that, until he received rent, he bear certain recurrent outgoings incurred by Ms Poulton in respect of the children. Thereafter he was to pay $30 per week for each child, in lieu of the specified outgoings. According to Mr Knight, neither party was happy with those orders. He was concerned that the orders did not guarantee him access to the children. Ms Poulton was dissatisfied with the figure of $7,100. As a consequence, he said, he had trouble seeing his children, "there was a lot of bitterness which we decided to resolve at a later date". I suppose that Ms Poulton's attitude was not improved by the fact that even the $7,100 was left unpaid. The order provided for interest on that sum at 14% per annum. As a result, by the end of 1991 the outstanding debt was approximately $8,000.

12. At some stage, the evidence does not reveal the date, there were further negotiations between Mr Knight and Ms Poulton or her solicitor. Ms Poulton was pressing for a larger settlement. A draft order was prepared. Mr Knight applied for legal aid in connection with these negotiations. But, before there was any response to his application, on 29 August 1991 he was arrested on ten charges. The major charges referred to Mr Knight's alleged involvement in a scheme to counterfeit banknotes. He told me that he intended to plead guilty to the charges alleging his assistance in the project but he denies that he was a principal. After Mr Knight's arrest a letter arrived at his home from the Legal Aid Commission offering the services of a named solicitor in connection with the family law problems. But Mr Knight did not become aware of this offer until some months later.

13. Although bail was fixed for Mr Knight, in the sum of $10,000, he has been unable to raise that money or to arrange for anyone else to go surety for him. Consequently, he remained at the Remand Centre throughout the whole of the period with which this case is concerned. Between that day and the day he gave evidence before me he attended committal proceedings at which he indicated an intention to plead guilty to nine of the ten charges. When he gave evidence he was being held at Parramatta Gaol awaiting sentence.

14. Shortly after his arrest, in September 1991, Mr Knight wrote a letter to Ms Poulton from the Malabar Remand Centre. He proposed that she come to Sydney as soon as possible and arrange to have the Bellevue Hill property transferred to her. He said that, once it was sold - he thought it would realise at least $200,000 - he would like $100,000 to be held for him in an interest bearing deposit; inferentially, Ms Poulton was to have the remainder. The letter asked whether Ms Poulton would consider living in Sydney. Mr Knight expressed the wish to have the boys near him. There was much emphasis upon ensuring that the "windfall", as he put it, of the property settlement should be so arranged as to benefit the boys; not other people, in particular not the family of Ms Poulton's now de facto husband. The letter was amicable in tone.

15. The evidence does not reveal any response to this letter. At some stage Mr Knight (and Ms Poulton) signed the draft Family Court order. The circumstances of execution are not revealed in the evidence but there is in evidence a copy of a letter dated 29 October in which Mr Knight wrote: "I enclose the relevant pages of the new consent orders". So it seems likely that the document was signed by Mr Knight at the Remand Centre and returned to Ms Poulton by post. This letter, also, was amicable in tone; even affectionate. Mr Knight thanked Ms Poulton "for giving me those things, those unique things no one else could give - your ?, your love, your buoyant spirit, those good times we had, your piano playing, your motherliness".

16. The draft order bears the date 10 October 1991 but the document is incomplete. It envisaged the sale, not the leasing, of the house. The recurrent expenses were to continue to be borne by Mr Knight until the sale of the house. Provision was made for payment of capitalised child maintenance, apparently out of the proceeds of sale, but the amount attributable to each child was left blank. The draft provided that, within 30 days of the order, Mr Knight transfer his interest in the house to Ms Poulton and, that pending the transfer, Ms Poulton have the right to occupy the property. The purpose of those provisions is obscure. It does not appear that Ms Poulton had accepted Mr Knight's suggestion that she move from Melbourne to Sydney; and, as mentioned, the draft envisaged a sale. Possibly, the transfer was intended to give Ms Poulton added capacity to ensure that a sale would actually be effected and capitalised maintenance paid to her. Mr Knight says that the draft order was never filed in the Family Court. There is no evidence to the contrary. If only because of the blanks in the document, the draft is not in a form appropriate for the making of orders. Accordingly, I approach the case on the basis that the only Family Court orders are those of 12 April 1991. This has the consequence that Ms Poulton is a creditor of Mr Knight in respect of the arrears of maintenance and interest, but she is not entitled to any interest in the house.

17. Notwithstanding the fact that no orders were made in accordance with the October draft, it seems that the Bellevue Hill certificate of title came into the possession of Ms Poulton's solicitor. There is no direct evidence of this fact. The only relevant evidence is that Mr Knight deposes to a conversation with Mr Carver in December 1991, whilst he was at the Remand Centre, in which he said that Ms Poulton had told him that her solicitor had the title deed. The certificate of title was certainly not in Mr Knight's possession at that time; as will appear, a substitute certificate of title was prepared to enable registration of the transfer to Mrs Carver.

18. Whilst at Malabar Remand Centre Mr Knight met Mr Carver. Mr Carver was convicted in 1981 of a number of charges of causing money to be deposited with intent to defraud. He was placed on a three-year good behaviour bond. In the period 1990-1991 Mr Carver was, what he called, "the team leader" in respect of takeovers of Occidental Life Insurance Company Limited and Regal Life Insurance Limited by Heath Holdings Pty Limited. Those takeovers resulted in the collapse of the two companies, causing substantial losses. The takeovers and the resultant collapses attracted considerable publicity. On 21 December 1990 Gray J, of the Supreme Court of Victoria, made orders that Mr Carver surrender his passport and not leave Australia. Mr Carver appealed against those orders, but unsuccessfully. So he obtained a passport in the false name of Robert Jones. He used this passport to make a trip to the United States in September 1991. On 9 October 1991, when about to leave Australia for another trip to the United States, he was arrested at Sydney airport. Mr Carver was again travelling under the name Robert Jones. He was accompanied by his wife.

19. Mr Carver was charged with making a false statement to obtain a passport. He pleaded guilty and was sentenced to four months imprisonment. He lodged an appeal against that sentence. When he gave evidence before me on 27 April 1992 the appeal had not been heard.

20. Mr Carver was also charged with other offences. Their nature was not specified in evidence. They apparently were unrelated to the Occidental/Regal affair but they must be of a serious nature. Mr Carver applied for bail. His application came before Badgery-Parker J of the New South Wales Supreme Court on 27 November. Mrs Carver gave evidence in support of the application, during which she said that she had no property other than a deposit paid on the purchase of some real estate at Palm Beach. On 11 December 1991 Badgery-Parker J set bail for Mr Carver: a cash deposit of $30,000 and security over property worth $150,000. Mr Carver's passport was to be returned to him but any overseas travel was to be the subject of a previously - lodged itinerary. Mr Carver was to report daily at the Pymble police station.

21. Mr Carver was made bankrupt on 10 December 1991.

22. The evidence does not reveal the precise date upon which Mr Carver and Mr Knight met. However, according to Mr Carver, between 27 November and 6 December he had his first discussion with Mr Knight about the possibility of Mr Knight's purchasing information about the Occidental/Regal takeover. Mr Carver says that Mr Knight became acquainted with him as a result of an article about him (Mr Carver) which had appeared in a business magazine. According to Mr Carver, in December 1990 he had been approached by persons interested in publishing "a story on what happened with Occidental". Apparently, their interest was conditional on the outcome of certain then - pending civil litigation and the absence of any criminal prosecution of Mr Carver in respect of Occidental/Regal. No firm agreement was reached. However, in January 1991 Mr Carver assigned to his wife "my interest in the story in the files and the story itself". No payment was made for the assignment. The consideration for the assignment was "forgiveness of a debt which was incurred in 1986". Notwithstanding the assignment, Mr Carver said, "I was still corresponding with solicitors and other interested parties during the time when she had the rights to the files and I was acting as her employee". He said that all his conversations with Mr Knight in connection with the Occidental/Regal information were undertaken as agent for his wife. Although no documents regarding an assignment were produced in evidence, Mrs Carver agreed with the substance of her husband's account of this subject. She agreed that all the discussions undertaken by her husband with Mr Knight, in connection with the Occidental/Regal story, were undertaken on her behalf.

23. According to Mr Carver, the first discussion between himself and Mr Knight was precipitated by Mr Knight. He claims that Mr Knight told him that he was a successful playwright, that he had contacts in the United States who would be interested in the Occidental/Regal story and that he had recently been living a de facto relationship with Robyn Grundy, the daughter of Reg Grundy "the TV and film producer and owner of the Grundy Organisation". (Mr Knight confirmed this relationship during the course of his oral evidence though he said that he "lost contact" with Ms Grundy while he was in the Remand Centre "for approximately three months".) Mr Carver says that Mr Knight inquired whether he would be interested in either selling an interest in the story or having an agent act for him; but that he responded: "At this stage I am talking to a number of people and agents and I really want to wait until the outcome of the court action eventuates". Later, on about 15 December, according to Mr Carver, Mr Knight again approached him, saying that he had spoken to Robyn Grundy, who had in turn "spoken to some of the producers in the Grundy organisation and they feel it could be a big seller". Mr Knight is also said to have spoken further about United States contacts and expressed interest in buying equity in the rights. Mr Carver says that Mr Knight then handed him a copy of his curriculum vitae; which, according to Mr Carver, he had with him in prison.

24. Somewhere between 15 December and 20 December, again according to Mr Carver, he had a conversation with Mr Knight during which Mr Knight mentioned his house at Bellevue Hill. Mr Knight's first proposition, according to Mr Carver, was to pay $250,000 for a 50% interest in the rights to the story providing that he could act as the agent in selling the rights. He offered $70,000 cash and a mortgage for $180,000 over the Bellevue Hill property. Mr Carver says that he rejected this proposal, saying that, if he sold an interest, he "would insist on cash up front and not security. Also I am not interested in selling 50% as I want control over the rights".

25. On about 21 December, according to Mr Carver, Mr Knight returned to the subject. This time he offered to give Mr Carver his house, which he said was worth about $200,000, "for a 38% interest in all rights to the Occidental story as well as your life story, providing you agree to sell for at least $100,000 a further 12% equity to the Grundy organisation. If I sell the 12% for $100,000 or more then I want 25% of whatever you get. If I can't sell to Grundy for at least that then you keep the 12%". Mr Carver said he would discuss the matter with his wife. He telephoned his wife, he says, and subsequently told Mr Knight that he would accept his offer.

26. On about 22 December, according to Mr Carver, Mr Knight told him that he had just telephoned Janette Poulton "and she told me she knew nothing about the title deed or its whereabouts. That was my last idea to (sic) where the title deed could possibly be. I had better apply for a replacement". He says that Mr Knight asked for his assistance in arranging a replacement title quickly and he promised to have his wife find out from the Land Titles Office the appropriate procedure. There is no evidence of any inquiry by Mrs Carver of the Land Titles Office; or even of any request to her to make an inquiry.

27. Mr Carver said that "later in December" - that is, after 21 December - it was agreed that Mr Knight would transfer the property to his wife. Asked to say when the negotiations for transfer to Mrs Carver were finally concluded, he replied: "I would say somewhere between 21 and 24 December".

28. In his affidavit of 11 March Mr Carver denied that the sale of the property to Mrs Carver "was to put the property out of the reach of Janette Poulton". He said:

"It is my belief that KNIGHT was genuinely excited by the prospect of

being active in the publication of a book about the takeover of the

Occidental and Regal Life Insurance companies that I was involved in

September 1990. To the best of my knowledge and belief KNIGHT'S only

purpose in entering into the transaction effected by the Deed dated the

3rd January 1992 between himself and my wife was to acquire an interest

in the story which fascinated him."

29. Mr Knight says he showed Mr Carver a copy of the draft Family Court orders dated 10 October 1991. At that time, Mr Knight said in evidence, his relationship with Ms Poulton "was getting testy and had broken down to a certain extent". He explained:

"A: ... We had had telephone conversations and she had written me what

the capitalised maintenance was to be which was double the figures

that I'd worked out but that was ongoing. It had broken down

because she had no sympathy for my situation and she's making

demands and the access thing hadn't been thrashed out fully and I

still hadn't had bail.

Q: But, Mr Knight, how on earth were you going to have access whilst

you were in custody?

A: Well, I presumed that I would be released at sometime. Q: Well,
did you mention to your wife that she might be prepared to

go surety for you?

A: Yes, but as I said they were reluctant to do it. Q: She had
refused as opposed to being reluctant, or hesitant; she

refused?

A: She wanted things ratified first. Q: Yes; she was in effect for
whatever reason holding you to ransom,

so to speak. She was saying, I will go surety for you if you

assign over to me your interest in the home at Bellevue Hill, and

in addition to that I'll sweeten it by allowing you to have

appropriate access to the children; isn't that right?

A: Well, it's more complex than that. And my initial suggestion was

that, to disperse my property so she could then be custodian of my

affairs and I gave her power of attorney to do so. But subsequent

to that, and the expressions of her coldness towards me, I came to

distrust her a bit more and I preferred, I decided it would be

better if I could handle the property myself.

Q: You would have no intention of assigning that property over to her

whether she was going to go surety to get you bail or not; that

was the stumbling block. You weren't going to hand it over to

her; correct?

A: I was if she was prepared to at least give me some sort of comfort

as regard the children and providing for me while I was on bail

and paying lawyers and so forth.

Q: All right; well why didn't you file the consent orders that

weren't filed that were signed on 10 October 1991 then?

A: Because we had not finalised fully the provisions regarding access

and capitalised maintenance. There was still a lot to work out

and I couldn't fully gauge her attitude. Also there was her

husband, de facto husband, who was interfering and introducing

another element.

Q: You had no real intention of assigning the property over to her

then or at any time; isn't that the situation?

A: Well, I did if things were going to be, you know, things were

going to work out for both of us, if I was going to be able to see

my kids when I got out on the basis that I wanted to, that is

semi-unconditional, and that I had a say in their education and so

forth. All I am saying is that a lot had to be thrashed out and

it was hard to because of the limited communication available from

MRC which was five minute phone calls three times a week."

30. Mr Knight says that, in "late December 1991", Mr Carver had a conversation with him as follows:

"Carver said to me:-

'My bail is being finalised and now all we have to work out is yours. I

want you to get out of here, set you up with a means to earn money and

get rid of the problems with your wife. I know your wife has been given

your house by the Family Court and I have read the consent order you

showed me. But to get this house back, we need to get hold of the title

deeds to the house.

I said:-

'Well, Janette has told me that her solicitor, Andrew Ryan, has the

title deeds to the house.'

Carver said:-

'Well don't worry, Bev will help us get a copy of them from the Land

Titles Office. I'll also get her to find some local council rates

notice or other council bills showing that you are the current owner of

the house. Then we will change you back to the current owner.'"

31. Mr Knight was not asked to explain what he meant by "late December". He may not necessarily have been referring to the last few days of December; although it was only in that period that action was taken to implement whatever arrangement was made. But Mr Knight clearly disputes the claim that there were serious negotiations between him and Mr Carver before 6 December, or even between 15 and 20 December. He says that it was "a day or so later" when the statutory declaration was prepared. That document is dated 27 December.

32. On 27 December Mr Knight signed an authority, addressed "To Whom it May Concern", authorising Mrs Carver "to access my property at 3B Bellevue Gardens, Bellevue Hill to collect documents and other material on my behalf". On the foot of the authority Mr Knight wrote the words: "Box on top of wardrobe in rear bedroom". On the same day, he typed out a statutory declaration. Mr Knight says that the declaration was prepared at the suggestion of Mr Carver and that Mr Carver dictated it to him. Mr Carver says that the compilation was a joint effort. In the declaration Mr Knight stated that he had mislaid the certificate of title to the Bellevue Hill property. The declaration detailed various searches which Mr Knight claimed to have made. It also stated that the certificate had not been lodged with any person for any purpose. The declaration ended:

"13. The replacement of the certificate of title is urgently required

to assist in a personal matter which has recently arisen".

Mr Knight says that, when he made the statutory declaration, he knew the whereabouts of the certificate of title and that signing the document was wrong.

33. The declaration contained no indication that Mr Knight was in prison. The address stated in the declaration was 3B Bellevue Gardens, Bellevue Hill. The declaration was witnessed by Mr Knight's cell mate, who claimed to be a Justice of the Peace. It was given to Mrs Carver along with an authority signed by Mr Knight and addressed to "The Registrar, Land Titles Office". This document appointed Mrs Carver Mr Knight's agent to lodge an application for a new certificate of title and to uplift the new certificate when available.

34. Mrs Carver acted with dispatch. Twenty-seventh December was a Friday. Upon the following Monday, 30 December, a new certificate of title was issued by the Land Titles Office in respect of the property. It showed Mr Knight as registered proprietor. A copy of this document is in evidence as exhibit F. When it was produced, the document was said to be a search of the title made on 30 December 1991. But this is not so: note the reference in the Second Schedule to s.111 of the Real Property Act 1900 (NSW), the provision authorising the issue of substitutes for lost certificates of title. The number of the new certificate differs from that of the old.

35. According to her account of the matter, Mrs Carver went to Mr Knight's home on Friday, 27 December. She uplifted some documents, including a rate notice. Mrs Carver says that she lodged the rate notice with the Land Titles Office in support of the application for a new certificate of title.

36. At about this same time, in the last days of December, Mrs Carver made inquiries about obtaining a valuation for the property from a valuer in private practice. But she could not locate an immediately available private valuer, due to the holiday period. Mrs Carver did, however, learn that someone from the Valuer General's Department would be available from 2 January. On that day Mrs Carver contacted the Department. She was able to arrange an immediate inspection. Upon the following day, 3 January, Mrs Carver uplifted from the Department a certificate valuing the property at $200,000.

38. In his affidavit of 27 February 1992, Mr Knight gave the following explanation for signing the Deed of Sale:

"11. My purpose in signing the Deed of Sale was to disguise an

arrangement whereby Beverley Carver would become the registered

39. Mrs Carver immediately lodged the Memorandum of Transfer for registration. It was apparently registered that same day, 3 January 1992; so she then became the registered proprietor of the property. There were no encumbrances.

40. Also on 3 January Mrs Carver had contact with a number of estate agents. On 4 January 1992 a local agent inspected the property and told her that it would realise about $250,000. Apparently he put this opinion in writing, because Mrs Carver passed on this information to the Prothonotary of the Supreme Court, as well as lodging with him a copy of the Valuer General's certificate, in an endeavour to have the Bellevue Hill property accepted as adequate security for her husband's release on bail. The Prothonotary accepted the deposit of the certificate of title as an appropriate security. On 16 January 1992 Mr Carver was released on bail. Mrs Carver then put the property up for sale, leading to the auction aborted by my order of 20 February.

41. No action was taken by Mrs Carver to procure Mr Knight's release on bail. Mrs Carver says that this was never discussed between her husband and herself; neither was she made aware of any claim to the property by Ms Poulton until 8 January. She says that a friend who was interested in purchasing the house inspected it that day. The adjoining semi-detached house had been renovated and the friend spoke to the owners of that house about the renovations they had made. During the course of this conversation, according to Mrs Carver, the adjoining owners referred to a visit to the house by Ms Poulton during December. Mrs Carver says that the neighbours told her friend that Ms Poulton had said that she planned to sell the property. She says that, when she learned this, she telephoned Ms Poulton and told her that she (Mrs Carver) had purchased the property and that she (Ms Poulton) had no claim.

42. According to Mr Carver's affidavit of 11 March 1992, he had no knowledge or information "pertaining to the relationship between Knight and Janette Poulton and in particular any Family Law proceedings between those parties" until 8 January. On that day he told Mr Knight that Ms Poulton had a conversation with his wife on the previous night "regarding an amount of $7,100 allegedly owed by you to Poulton". He says that Mr Knight responded:

"The only thing I owe her is $7,100

and I don't have to pay that now. Here is the original of the Family

Court Order. That is the only thing I owe the greedy bitch. Please

give this to Bev and tell her not to be concerned. It has nothing to do

with Bev. The worse thing that can happen is that I will have to pay

her the $7,100.00 and I am not going to do that right now although I

have got the money. The reason I have not paid her is that she has

refused to honour the access orders for my children."

43. According to Mr Carver, he told Mr Knight that Ms Poulton had said that she would place a caveat on the title as he owed her moneys under the Family Court order; also that she had the certificate of title. He says that Mr Knight responded abusively towards Ms Poulton, during the course of which he said that he had telephoned her on 22 December asking whether she had the title and she said she did not. Mr Carver also alleges a conversation on 9 January, during which Mr Knight again abused Ms Poulton and assured him (Mr Carver) that she had no claim to the property.

44. Once again, Mr Knight's version is different. He agrees that he heard, through Mr Carver, of Ms Poulton's claim to the property. But he says that Mr Carver said to him:

"Express your outrage in a letter. The best way to handle people who

say things and demand things is to immediately write a letter of

outrage. You have to do things in writing. Once something is in

writing you can produce it as evidence".

45. Whatever passed in conversation between the two men at this time, it is clear that, on 9 January, Mr Knight wrote a letter to Ms Poulton. A copy is in evidence. Its tone is vitriolic. It is unnecessary to set out the whole of the letter. In the letter Mr Knight expressed his "utter outrage" that Ms Poulton should claim that he knew she had the certificate of title prior to 8 January. He claimed that, when he rang her on 22 December, she said that she did not have the title. References were made to theft, greed, opportunism etc. Asked about this letter in cross-examination, Mr Knight said:

"... this is after Carver had given me this tale about the treacherous

Janette and what she was doing behind my back and I'm ashamed to say I

believed him".

46. At another point in his evidence, he said that Mr Carver:

" .... was telling me my wife was plotting against me, she was out to

take everything, that she was diverting mail from my house and many

other things. He told me to express a letter of outrage".

47. According to Mr Carver, on 10 January Mr Knight said to him: "Now that you are getting out on bail could you please assist in trying to get someone go surety for me"; and gave him a list of potential sureties. A list of names was attached to his affidavit. Mr Carver did not succeed in obtaining a surety for Mr Knight; how much effort he made is impossible to say.

48. Mr Knight's version of the breakdown of his friendship with Mr Carver is that on about 10 January Mr Carver said to him: "Don't worry mate, I'll look after you. As soon as Bev gets the title deed, you're out. Bev will go surety for you. I'll set you up with money as you need it from Bev's trust fund account, a car and lawyers". Then Mr Carver was released from prison. Mr Knight was not bailed. Mrs Carver "would not honour the agreement to secure my release on bail". Then he learned that the property was to be auctioned. He said:

"I realised at that time that Janette Poulton and my children would

receive no benefit from the property. In those circumstances and in an

attempt to correct my earlier attempt to defeat the claims of Janette

Poulton I made application to the Federal Court of Australia to become

bankrupt."

49. During February and March 1992 there were exchanges of acrimonious correspondence between Mr Knight and Mr Carver. It is not necessary to refer to them. Neither counsel suggests that they cast any light upon the critical question: whose version of the Remand Centre discussions is to be believed.

50. In approaching this question I bear in mind the comments made by counsel for the respondents regarding Mr Knight's credit. Mr Knight has a significant criminal record, involving offences of dishonesty. I do not put much weight upon his use of false information to obtain a passport; that offence was apparently committed a long time ago, when he was quite young and possibly under some emotional strain as a result of the information given to him by his supposed father. But the 1986 convictions are more recent and more serious. They arise out of a calculated scheme to defraud the Commonwealth, involving the use of a number of false names. When he devised and implemented the scheme, Mr Knight was aged about 34 years; these are not impetuous acts of dishonesty by a very young man. More recently, Mr Knight has been involved in another fraudulent scheme; this time in connection with counterfeit bank notes. True, he says that he was not the principal malefactor, and there is no evidence before me to the contrary. But the scheme was on a substantial scale. Mr Knight admits that, if the scheme had come to fruition, the total nominal value of the counterfeit money would have been about $1.25 million. Whatever Mr Knight's precise role in the scheme, his involvement reflects seriously upon his integrity and credit-worthiness.

51. Moreover, passing from general character to the facts of this case, Mr Knight's own version of the relevant events reflects adversely upon him. On his account of the matter, the facts set out in his statutory declaration were a tissue of lies, the telling of which was made more serious by the solemnity of the document and the purposes for which it was executed. The immediate purpose was to procure the issue of an important official document, a document which ought not be procured by false information. The longer-term purpose, on Mr Knight's own evidence, was to defeat a claim made by the woman with whom he had lived for 10 or 11 years for a contribution towards the maintenance of their two children, a claim by then enshrined in a court order to which he had consented. As Mr Knight now concedes, the conduct which he claims was extremely reprehensible.

52. Notwithstanding all of the above, I have no hesitation in preferring the substance of Mr Knight's account of his discussions with Mr Carver to that of Mr Carver. There are several reasons for this. In the first place, there was an element of pot and kettle in counsel's comments on Mr Knight's credit. Mr Carver also has a history of dishonesty. His 1981 convictions were for fraud. He also obtained a false passport, but much more recently and in defiance of a court order. He also is currently facing other criminal charges. In the civil litigation arising out of the Occidental/Regal takeover Mr Carver refused to give evidence on the ground that this might incriminate him. In evidence before me Mr Carver adopted the word "sting" to describe the Occidental/Regal transaction. He described himself as "a principal player in the transaction". He explained "we took advantage of an outdated Life Insurance Act by selling the assets of the company to an off-shore entity in a tax-free haven". He said that the Bank of Melbourne put up money for the transaction and "did lose out on it".

53. Turning to demeanour, I was not impressed with Mr Carver. Indeed, I cannot recall a witness in whose evidence I have had less confidence. Whenever confronted with a problem about his evidence, he offered material not previously mentioned. Two examples will suffice. Early in his evidence, Mr Carver was asked: "What guarantee did you and your wife have on or about 21 December 1991 that the property was worth $200,000". He replied: "Mr Knight's value". When later asked about the agreement, he agreed that "on 21 December or within at the most a couple of days thereafter" he had reached a "firm agreement" with Mr Knight "that he would transfer to you his house, which he described as being worth about $200,000 in return for 38% interest in the rights". This, Mr Carver said, was "a binding agreement". It was then pointed out to him that, at that stage, there had been no inspection of the house; so he was committing himself to letting go 38% without knowing the condition of the house he was getting in return. In response, Mr Carver said - for the first time - that there was an understanding for an adjustment of the percentage if the value of the house was lower than $200,000; in other words there was not a firm agreement for 38%. Pressed with this inconsistency, Mr Carver responded: "Oh, I knew the street. I knew land value was at least $200,000, or thereabouts".

54. The second example arises out of a comment made by Mr Carver early in his evidence that the valuer had originally been minded to value the property at $180,000, but that his wife had said that the price was $200,000 so he wrote that figure. On his story, the sole purpose of the valuation was the assessment of stamp duty. It was pointed out to Mr Carver that the effect of lifting the figure of $180,000 to $200,000 would be to increase stamp duty. He then thought that the variation "was the other way around", a reduction from $220,000 to $200,000.

55. For Mr Knight's version of his discussions with Mr Carver to be true Mr Carver would have to be a person with a dominant and manipulative personality; and he himself would need to be weak and gullible. Having observed both these men in the witness box over periods of some hours, my assessment is that both have these characteristics. It may seem curious to say so, given his record, but my belief is that Mr Knight is a fundamentally decent man; but emotionally immature, weak and easily overborne by others. He does, I believe, have genuine affection for his two sons and respect for Ms Poulton. It is true that he has not shown this affection and respect in a very useful way, by providing adequate financial support. But the letters which he wrote from the Remand Centre to Ms Poulton before he met Mr Carver suggest that he then wished to ensure that Ms Poulton and the boys would benefit from the disposal of the house. For him to barter away the house in exchange for an interest in the Occidental/Regal story was inconsistent with that attitude. The tone of the September and October letters is consistent with Mr Knight's past history, so far as it is revealed by the evidence. The contrast between these letters and that of 9 January, when on any view of the evidence he was in close daily contact with Mr Carver, is striking.

56. Mr Carver has a forceful personality. This was well illustrated by his exchanges with counsel during cross-examination. His willingness to manipulate others to his own advantage is shown by two documents in his handwriting which are in evidence. Both these documents were written shortly after Mr Carver and Mr Knight learned of Ms Poulton's caveat; the first (exhibit H) on about 12 January, the second (exhibit J) a couple of days later. Each document consisted of a list of points, devised by Mr Carver, for Mr Knight to make in his contacts with Ms Poulton. The points set out in exhibit H were:

"1. Buyer is about to commence proceedings against me.

2. To placate buyer could he pls. fax to pay the total claim under

the caveat is no more than $10,000 02 988 4299 - Mrs Carver.

3. You are proceeding to offer better settlement terms. 4. Buyer will
not object to caveat remaining providing the caveat

indicates $10,000 on title.

5. The caveat is causing major problems with a commercial transaction

the buyer is committed to and has caused great difficulties for

you.

6. You can settle all matters including outstanding maintenance -

within 30 days.

7. If I can't satisfy the buyer by 5.00pm to-morrow I will lose

everything - including the opportunity to pay and improve

agreement with Janette."

Asked about this document,Mr Carver said in evidence:

"They were points which I thought that he should make in a rather strong

way to the solicitor acting for Mrs Poulton, that unless the caveat was

removed, then certain things would flow from it. It was totally illegal,

the transaction, and it was my view that he should put those to him,

which he did."

57. Mr Carver went on to say that he wished to establish that Ms Poulton's claim was limited to $7,100, as Mr Knight had informed him when told of the caveat. This is understandable, though it should be noted that the caveat itself referred to that amount. Mr Carver said that he told Mr Knight to ring Ms Poulton's solicitor "and get answers to these questions to satisfy myself and my wife, that is the only claim; determine the amount of the caveat for me and I'll be happy". But the points listed on exhibit H went well beyond an inquiry as to Ms Poulton's total claim. The clear intention was to have Mr Knight make statements which would intimidate Ms Poulton from pursuing her claim, or at least any claim above $10,000. For this purpose Mr Carver urged Mr Knight to make statements which he knew to be false. For example, there was no basis for saying that the "buyer", Mrs Carver, was about to commence proceedings against Mr Knight. She had become the registered proprietor of the property before the caveat was lodged. There could be no possible claim against Mr Knight, nor was one threatened. Similarly, there was no basis for the statement that Mr Knight was proceeding to offer better settlement terms. What could they be? Mr Knight had divested himself of his only significant asset. He did not even have the $10,000 deposit needed for his release on bail. There is no suggestion in the evidence that Mr Knight had the means to pay the $7,100; he could not "settle all matters" within 30 days. Still less could he improve on that figure. There was no basis for a claim that Mr Knight would offer better terms. There is no evidence of any discussion between Mr Knight and Mr Carver to that effect.

58. In evidence Mr Carver asserted that it was true to say that the caveat was "causing major problems with a commercial transaction the buyer is committed to". Asked about this, Mr Carver said:

"...at that stage it was my intention to re-instigate discussions with

Macmillan (publishers, who Mr Carver claimed to be interested in

publishing a book about Occidental/Regal). I required funds to get to

the point of negotiations with - finalise the negotiations with

Macmillans, and I was saying that it was my intention to be out on bail

at that point in time, that my first priority was to start

re-negotiating the agency for the story, particularly in the United
States.

Q: So the relevant transaction was a transaction between you and

MacMillan?

A: Or - on behalf of my wife, correct, yes.

Q: All right, between your wife and MacMillan, and the caveat was

causing major problems with your wife's negotiations with

MacMillans?

A: No, I was saying that unless it was clarified fairly quickly then

Mr Knight was not going to partake in any negotiations or to do

any of the works which he said he wanted to do as regards to the

story.

Q: So, from Knight's point of view, it was causing a problem because

he'd be left out of the deal with MacMillan?

A: Correct, and also that if it was going to be embroiled in a legal

action, and that was the threat at that stage, that Mrs Poulton

was threatening all sorts of legal action against Mr Knight, he

was going to be painted with a - because she says that he swore a

false statutory declaration at that point in time, I wasn't

prepared to have the transaction tainted any further by having

legal action on the side occurring at the time when we were

proposing to negotiate with various agents and various

representations were being made to me here in Sydney."

59. That explanation is very difficult from the burden of point 5.

60. Similar comments apply to exhibit J. It set out eight points:

"1. No cash offer to remove caveat.

2. Limit caveat to maximum of $30,000.

3. Caveat must be limited by Wednesday.

4. You cannot provide new suggested consent orders until tax and

legal advice is given. Further access consideration etc. have to

be negotiated/determined.

5. Unless caveat limit has been registered with Lands Title by

Wednesday, you will tell buyer to proceed with litigation.

6. You need 30 days to submit new proposed consent orders.

7. The caveat action has caused a number of breaches in the contract.

I have to immediately remedy the breaches.

8. I am swearing statutory declarations to pay for both Land Titles

and legal reasons stating

(1) Janette advised on 22/12 she did not have title deed

(2) I am not in receipt of your letter re title deed (NB

send/fax copy)

(3) Mail??

(4) Other"

61. Mr Carver's explanation of this document was as follows:

"Q: When do you say that note was brought into existence?

A: Probably a couple of days after that document, because Mr Knight

was saying that - and I proceeded to negotiate on this basis that

he was going to pay $30,000 to Mrs Poulton, and I just said that I

would accept that position because he, in turn, was paying $30,000

cash to her, providing you limit the caveat or get Mrs Poulton to

limit the caveat to $30,000. All I'm doing here that to

demonstrate that we're willing, or Mr Knight was willing, to

accept a payment of $30,000. I thought that was the amount. It's

coming back to me, now, right. Mr Knight accepted that he wanted

to pay Mrs Poulton $30,000 cash in lieu of a settlement and he

said he had the cash available to pay her and that providing she

limited the caveat on the property to $30,000, he could pay her

out. So, what I'm suggesting here is that he get some undertaking

that the caveat is for no morethan $30,000. He is asking 30 days

to submit new proposed consent orders, which was the case, he

submitted consent orders and I negotiated on his behalf. The

caveat's action has caused a number of breaches in the contract,

which it did. I had to immediately remedy the breaches which he

had to. He is swearing statutory declarations today for both land

titles and legal reasons stating Janette advised him on 22

December she did not have the title deed: 'I'm not in receipt of

your letter re the deed and the mail and the other issues'. ...

This was what he was - the note said he was going to discuss with

her solicitor."

62. There is no evidence that Mr Knight had $30,000. I do not accept that Mr Knight told Mr Carver he would pay this sum. There is no evidence to substantiate the statement that, unless the caveat was limited by Wednesday, "you will tell buyer to proceed with litigation". Nor could it be truthfully said that the "caveat action has caused a number of breaches in the contract".

63. There are several improbabilities and inconsistencies in Mr Carver's version of the Remand Centre discussions. First, Mr Carver concedes that, before the discussions commenced, Mr Knight's bail had been fixed at $10,000 and that throughout December and January Mr Knight was anxious to be released on bail. He had not yet attended a committal hearing. The fact that, despite his anxiety for release, Mr Knight remained in custody is powerful evidence that he did not have access to any significant amount of cash. It seems to me highly unlikely that, in such a situation, Mr Knight's preference would be to expend his sole significant asset in obtaining an interest in the Occidental/Regal story; an interest which would give him only a minority holding without any creative or commercial control; and a story about which he knew very little. Mr Carver showed Mr Knight some documents concerning Occidental/Regal but he conceded in cross-examination that they were mainly magazine articles and that he did not show Mr Knight anything not already in the public domain.

64. Secondly, Mr Knight's lack of cash resources would have been painfully obvious to him and apparent to Mr Carver. In such circumstances it is not credible that Mr Knight's opening offer was to pay $70,000 in cash and give a mortgage for $180,000, as Mr Carver claims. Asked about this, Mr Carver said "the $70,000 cash was coming from a false bank account at the Sun Bank-Sunshine - a bank in Queensland and I didn't want to get involved with that exercise". Mr Carver does not seem to have asked himself why, if there was such a bank account, Mr Knight was still in prison. I regard Mr Carver's reference to the Queensland bank account as another example of his propensity for manufacturing a new assertion to meet a difficult question.

65. Thirdly, in explanation of the fact that he had shown Mr Knight so little Occidental/Regal documentation, Mr Carver referred to the difficulty of providing a prisoner with access to documents. He referred to the gaol procedures regarding documents and the discretion of the prison authorities to deny access. Yet, according to Mr Carver, the idea was that Macmillans would publish a book, apparently written by Mr Knight. Mrs Carver said in evidence that her understanding was that Mr Knight would write something whilst in gaol, that the confidential information would go to him there. How this would be accomplished was not explained. When asked, Mr Carver spoke only of Mr Knight's hopes of being released on bail.

66. The fourth improbability is one to which I have already made a reference: the making of a firm agreement before Mr Carver had any information about the condition or value of Mr Knight's house.

67. Fifthly, a relatively small point: it was put to Mr Carver in cross-examination that he was aware before 27 December that Mr Knight had been in contact with Ms Poulton regarding the raising of bail. Mr Carver responded:

"I never heard of Mrs Poulton's name until 8 January in 1992. I

remember quite clearly how that arose. I knew he was having

difficulties with a wife. I knew that he wasn't married to the person

he was having difficulties with and I was unsure - you know, I had no

need to know her name."

68. Later he repeated that, prior to 8 January: "I did not know Ms Poulton ... I had no need to have known of Ms Poulton or her name". Yet, in para.7 of his affidavit of 11 March 1992 Mr Carver deposed to a conversation with Mr Knight of 22 December in which Mr Knight said to him:

"I have just telephoned Janette (Poulton) and she told me she knew

nothing about the title deed or its whereabouts. That was my last idea

to where the title deed could possibly be. I had better apply for a

replacement. Can you assist or have someone arrange a replacement title

quickly?"

69. It is not difficult to see why Mr Carver should assert that there was such a telephone call. The making of a telephone call would tend to negative any suspicion that Mr Knight's purpose, or one of his purposes, in entering into the transaction was to defeat Ms Poulton's claim to the property; the telephone call would give her notice that something was afoot. Moreover, if a telephone call was made and Ms Poulton denied possession of the certificate of title, Mr Carver's role in obtaining the issue of a new certificate loses its sinister aspect. But it is difficult to accept that there was a telephone call on 22 December. The first contact between Mrs Carver and Ms Poulton was on 8 January when Mrs Carver telephoned Ms Poulton. The caveat was lodged on 10 January, two days later. If Mr Knight had telephoned Ms Poulton on 22 December enquiring about the certificate of title, it seems unlikely that she would have waited until 10 January. Of course, it is theoretically possible that Mr Knight did not telephone Ms Poulton but falsely told Mr Carver that he did. This hypothesis is consistent with Mr Carver's innocence over the application for a substitute certificate of title, but it would powerfully support a conclusion that Mr Knight was actuated by a desire to defeat Ms Poulton.

70. There are three additional considerations which cause me to accept Mr Knight's version of the discussions. The first consideration is the timing of the events. Mr Carver's application for bail had been heard on 27 November. On 11 December bail terms had been set. They included security over property worth at least $150,000. Mr Carver had just been made bankrupt; in any event, he had no assets. Mrs Carver's only assets were her interest in the Occidental/Regal story and a deposit on a purchase of a property at Palm Beach. Neither of these assets was likely to be accepted by the Prothonotary as security. So, notwithstanding that bail had been set, Mr Carver continued to languish in gaol. Yet, according to Mr Carver, fate smiled. There happened to be in the Remand Centre a person so interested in the Occidental/Regal story as to approach Mr Carver offering a deal. That person was prepared to transfer his house to Mrs Carver in return for a minority interest in the story. Moreover, his house was said to be worth more than $150,000 and be unencumbered. Counsel put to Mr Carver that, from the moment he heard that Mr Knight's property was unencumbered, "it was like an opportunity too good to be true". Mr Carver did not assent to this, but he did agree that it provided "an opportunity for you to use Mr Knight and his property to get out on bail".

71. In a recent s.121 case, Grellman v P T Garuda Indonesia Ltd (1991) 101 ALR 135, Hill J referred at 145 to the unusual nature of the subject transaction, and the fact that it was completed in haste and without the usual searches and rate adjustments, as factors allowing him readily to infer that the transferee was a party to the fraud. That approach was endorsed on appeal: see P T Garuda Indonesia Limited v Grellman (Wilcox, Gummow and von Doussa JJ., 4 May 1992, not yet reported). The same comments may be made about the present case. The transaction evidenced by the Deed of Sale signed at Malabar was highly unusual. If the parties seriously intended to create a relationship whereby they would share the rights to the Occidental/Regal story, there were a host of matters which needed to be addressed, in their mutual interests. Yet neither sought any legal advice; and this despite the fact that the Carvers had solicitors normally acting for them. According to her evidence, Mrs Carver had not previously been personally involved in any real estate transaction; yet she was content to attend to the legal work attaching to the application for a new certificate of title and the registration of the transfer. Mr Carver said that he knew that there were expenses owing on the property, including rates. He thought they added up to about $7,000. Yet he was prepared to allow his wife to take a transfer without any allowance for these expenses. Moreover, the transaction was effected with astonishing speed. The earliest documents were executed on Friday, 27 December. One week later, on Friday, 3 January, Mrs Carver was the registered proprietor of the land - a new certificate of title and a certificate of valuation having issued in the meantime.

72. Having regard to the abovementioned matters, I accept the case advanced by the Official Trustee in relation to the discussions between Mr Knight and Mr Carver. I think that Mr Carver befriended Mr Knight in the Remand Centre and that, encouraged by his friendly approach, Mr Knight told Mr Carver about his anxiety for bail, his problems with Ms Poulton and his financial affairs. I think that Mr Carver recognised an opportunity to obtain Mr Knight's house as his own bail-security and that he induced Mr Knight to enter into the Deed of Sale by fostering a resentment towards Ms Poulton and by promising that Mrs Carver would use the property to procure his (Mr Knight's) release on bail. In my opinion Mr Knight had two reasons for the transaction: to procure his release on bail and to defeat the claim of Ms Poulton. It is enough for s.121 purposes that the defeat of creditors, or even a single creditor, is one of the purposes of an impugned transaction: see Garuda v Grellman in the Full Court. I do not think that Mr Knight was seriously interested in the Occidental/Regal rights. I think that he naively believed that, in due course, the property would be retransferred to him or sold for his benefit.

73. Counsel for the respondents submits that proof of Mr Knight's intent is not enough; the applicant must show that the transaction was not one for valuable consideration undertaken in good faith by Mrs Carver. Here there was valuable consideration, counsel says; and it is not shown that Mrs Carver lacked good faith.

74. I agree with counsel's submissions as to what must be established. And the Full Court decision in Garuda v Grellman establishes that, under s.121, the applicant bears the onus of negativing good faith. There is an issue in the present case whether what was assigned to Mr Knight constituted valuable consideration. Counsel for the applicant points out that there is no evidence of value of this unusual property. Counsel for the respondents says that, if this is the only difficulty in his way, the Court ought to adjourn the matter to give him a further opportunity to put on evidence of value. I need not determine the issue of valuable consideration or the application for an adjournment. I am of the opinion that the applicant has negatived good faith.

75. In Garuda v Grellman the Full Court referred to several authorities explaining the nature of "good faith" in this context. In Re Hyams; Official Receiver v Hyams (1970) 19 FLR 232 at 256 Gibbs J treated the words "good faith" as meaning "without notice that any fraud or preference contrary to the statute is intended". In Official Trustee v Marchiori (1983) 69 FCR 290 at 298 Fisher J applied to ss.120 and 121 of the Bankruptcy Act a statement of Kay L.J. in Mogridge v Clapp (1892) 3 Ch. 382 at 401 that "good faith" "must mean or involve a belief that all is being regularly and properly done".

76. If my findings regarding the discussions between Mr Knight and Mr Carver are correct, there is no doubt that Mr Carver knew the reasons inducing Mr Knight to transfer the house to his wife. Mr Carver himself had implanted those reasons into Mr Knight's head. To use another phrase of Gibbs J, in Re Barnes; ex parte Stapleton (1962) Qd R 231 at 240, he was "privy to the fraud".

77. The evidence establishes a close relationship between Mr and Mrs Carver. Although he was in custody, Mr and Mrs Carver had frequent telephone conversations. Mrs Carver visited the Remand Centre from time to time. She was loyal to her husband. She said in evidence: "I still stick by him". Having regard to this relationship, it is almost inconceivable that Mrs Carver did not ask her husband how this fortuitous transaction came about. She certainly knew the nature of the transaction and the need to consummate it promptly. Most of the factors applied by Hill J in Grellman v Garuda to infer a lack of good faith by Garuda are to be found in this case, as against Mrs Carver. Mrs Carver may not have known all the details of Mr Knight's affairs. She may not have known Ms Poulton's name, before 8 January, or the exact nature of her claim to the property. Until that day she was probably unaware of Ms Poulton's visit to the house before Christmas - if in fact it occurred, there is no evidence about the matter. But she knew that the transaction was a most unusual one, explicable only upon the basis that it was designed to put the property out of somebody's reach. If it is necessary for the applicant to establish that Mrs Carver herself had sufficient knowledge to negative good faith, this finding should be made.

78. Counsel for the applicant disputes that it is necessary for him to show that Mrs Carver had sufficient personal knowledge of the transaction as to negative good faith. He argues that, where an impugned transaction is negotiated on behalf of a principal by an agent, it is enough to show that the agent had the requisite knowledge. I agree. Where a principal acts through an agent, the knowledge of the agent concerning a material matter which it is the agent's duty to communicate to the principal is imputed to the principal: see Halsbury "Laws of England" 4th edition vol 1(2) paras. 149, 150. The circumstances surrounding the present transaction were material matters which Mr Carver was bound to communicate to his wife. There is nothing to exclude the operation of the general principle referred to in Halsbury to cases arising under s.121 of the Bankruptcy Act. Grellman v Garuda provides a recent example where the knowledge of the agent (Woerjanto) was held to negative the good faith of the principal (Garuda).

79. In the present case the applicant is entitled to succeed. I do not think that declarations 1 or 3 are necessary. The order for transfer needs to be amended so as to include a time limit. I propose to declare that the transfer by the first respondent, David Alan Knight, to the second respondent, Beverley Carver, on or about 3 January 1992 of the land comprised in Folio Identifier 17/2/1951 is void as against the applicant, the Official Trustee in Bankruptcy.
