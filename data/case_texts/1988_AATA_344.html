Re Elizabeth Ann Cox and Commissioner of Superannuation [1988] AATA 344 (29 November 1988)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: ELIZABETH ANN COX

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. S87/262

AAT No. 4783

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.A.Layton(1) (Deputy President), B.C. Lock(1) (Member) and Dr D.B. Williams(1) (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - invalidity retirement benefits - whether applicant previously suffered from "migraine" - whether applicant failed to furnish or furnished false information - whether respondent estopped by conduct from relying on discrepancy - whether this condition, or a condition connected therewith, caused or substantially contributed to, her retirement - whether BCC specifying condition "migrainous headaches" to be deemed to have been issued for the purposes of para.66(2)(b) - whether incapacity caused or substantially contributed to, a condition or conditions connected with the applicant's retirement.

Words & Phrases: "false"; "incapacity";

"substantially"

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.66(2), 154(4), 184(5)

Superannuation Act 1922, s.5(1)

Re Ghose and Commissioner for Superannuation V.86/449, delivered 15 March

1988 (Decision 4213)

Re Whiteford and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 6 AAR 70

Re Joyce and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority S.87/141, delivered 28 April 1988 (Decision 4301)

Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 3 ALN N1

Commissioner for Superannuation v Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 8 FCR 153

HEARING

ADELAIDE

29:11:1988

ORDER

The decision under review is affirmed.

DECISION

This is an application for review of a decision made by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation on 11 June 1986, to issue a Benefit Classification Certificate ("BCC") in respect of the applicant pursuant to sub-s.184(5) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") specifying the conditions "Migraine" and "History of Anxiety/Depression". The delegate further decided pursuant to para.66(2)(c) of the Act that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by a condition or conditions specified in the BCC or by a condition or conditions connected with such condition or conditions.

2. Subsequently, on 22 October 1987, another delegate of the Commissioner on reconsideration of this decision pursuant to sub-s.154(4) of the Act varied the delegate's decision of 11 June 1986 by amending the specified conditions in the BCC to the one condition of "Migraine", but confirmed that aspect of the original decision that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement was caused or substantially con tributed to by migraine or by a condition or conditions connected with migraine.

3. This review involves the determination of a number of questions of fact, namely whether the applicant failed to furnish information or furnished false information at or in connection with a medical examination; whether if the applicant had furnished the correct information, she would have been accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund; and whether the incapacity which was the ground of the applicant's retirement was substantially caused or contributed to by a condition of "migraine" or by a condition or conditions connected with "migraine".

4. At the hearing, the applicant was represented by counsel, Mr R. Bonig and the respondent was represented by Mr R. Eadie. At the hearing, the documents lodged pursuant to s.37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the T documents") were accepted as part of the information before the Tribunal, and further documentation was tendered during the course of the hearing. In addition, the applicant gave oral evidence and called a psychiatrist Dr Raymond Taylor. The respondent called a psychiatrist Dr Carl Radeski, a former departmental medical officer Dr John Carter, a current senior medical officer Dr Allen Gormly, and the applicant's general medical practitioner Dr Wolodymyr Skybyneckyj.

5. The relevant background facts are that the applicant was born in England on 12 October 1938 and is now 50 years of age. At various times, she has given a history of having suffered from headaches which were apparently diagnosed as "migraine" headaches since she was aged 11 or 12 (although at the hearing, she was only able to say that these headaches commenced whilst she was still a schoolgirl). After finishing her schooling, she worked in a woollen mill in England and then emigrated to Australia in 1956. She obtained employment initially as a telephonist in the Postmaster-General's Department for a period of 12 months, and thereafter worked in a factory for approximately 4 years. In 1961, she commenced employment at the Weapons Research Establishment as a process-worker in the radio workshop and she continued in this employment for 11-1/2 years (the last 3 of which she worked as a telephonist).

6. The applicant gave evidence that certainly from the time she came to Australia in 1956 until the time she ceased employment with the Weapons Research Establishment, she continued to have episodes of what she regarded as "migraine" headaches.

7. We use the word "migraine" in quotation marks to mean the condition as describe by the applicant, commencing with symptoms of tightness, an ache across the back progressing up to her neck and culminating in a throbbing head, nausea and on occasions disturbed vision (see pp 20, 54 and 58 of transcript). The medical evidence suggests, however, that the applicant's "migraines" do not fit the classical pattern of migraine headaches, but may be more related to tension, and are headaches of a migrainous type. In fact the applicant herself associated the symptoms as described as occurring at or following times of stress or tension (see pp 25, 60 of transcript). The nature of the applicant's headaches will be discussed in greater detail later in these Reasons for Decision.

8. The applicant married in 1969 and after this time, commenced seeing her husband's local medical practitioner, Dr Skybyneckyj, who then became her own local doctor. Her initial complaints in 1969 and 1970 seemed to relate to hypertension and problems associated with her contraceptive pill. This information and that pertaining to other consultations referred to specifically hereafter is gleaned from Dr Skybyneckyj's notes which were made available to the Tribunal, but which have since been released as they are medical records needed for the continued treatment of the applicant. The numbers of consultations given are not exact but approximations, as the dates of consultations were at times confused.

9. The applicant first complained to Dr Skybyneckyj of "migraine" headaches in December 1971 and was prescribed Diazepam for relief. She also gave a history at that time of the "migraines" occurring either before or just after her menstruation.

10. In 1972, the applicant attended Dr Skybyneckyj's rooms on 5 occasions, most of which appear to relate to complaints of headaches which she said were occurring monthly. On some of those occasions, injections of Pethidine and Stematil were given to alleviate the symptoms of her "migraines". Other symptoms complained of appear to relate to an anxiety state.

11. In 1973, there were 6 consultations with Dr Skybyneckyj again all relating to "migraine" or symptoms of being worried and anxious and the applicant was prescribed various medications including Diazepam.

12. During 1973 and 1974 the applicant performed relief telephonist work through two employment agencies, however, she was looking for a permanent job.

13. In 1974, the applicant attended Dr Skybyneckyj's rooms on 7 occasions and again the complaints were of "migraine" headaches and the prescriptions mainly seemed to consist of tranquillisers such as Valium or Diazepam.

14. In January 1975, the applicant attended Dr Skybyneckyj's rooms being anxious and also presented with an elevated blood pressure. She was given a prescription for her hypertension and prescriptions for tranquillisers, Ergodryl and Valium, which were usually prescribed for her "migraine" condition. On 9 June 1975, the applicant again attended Dr Skybyneckyj's rooms being anxious, for which she was given a prescription for Valium.

15. On 11 August 1975, the applicant obtained full-time employment with the Health Insurance Commission as a telephonist. On 15 August 1975, the applicant underwent a medical examination for the purposes of the Act. Prior to the examination, the applicant was required to complete the Personal Details section of the Medical Examination Form issued pursuant to the Act. In Part "B" of that form, in answer to the question "Are you now suffering or have you ever suffered from any of the following disabilities - 1. Mental or Nervous Disorder including neurosis, or anxiety state", the applicant answered "No". As to question 2. "Heart disease, coronary artery disease, recurrent pain in chest, high blood pressure, frequent palpitations", the applicant answered "No" and to question 11, "Fits, epilepsy, migraine, persistent headaches", the applicant answered, "No". On presentation of this form to the Examining Medical Officer, the applicant signed the declaration in his presence, declaring that all the information supplied was true and correct to the best of her knowledge.

16. The applicant, in examination-in-chief, stated that she could not remember whether she had completed any forms in relation to her medical examination (p 23 of transcript). However, after lengthy cross-examination, the following information was adduced. The handwriting on the form set out under the heading of "Personal details" on T6/38, Part "A" "Family Medical History" and Part "B" "Personal Medical History" on T6/39, and the continuation on T6/40 as well as Part "C" on the left-hand side, being T6/40 was identified by the applicant as the handwriting of her husband. She also gave evidence that her husband usually filled in forms for her and that this form would have been completed by her husband after she had provided him with the appropriate answers (p 173 of the transcript). The applicant, however, could not provide any explanation as to why she provided the answer "No" to the question in relation to migraine and persistent headaches at the time her husband filled in the forms. In response to counsel for the respondent's question, "... why is the answer to that question "No", she stated:

"Because when I went for my medical examination with

Dr Carter he examined me, asked me all these

questions, and he said to me do I have migraines, and

I said, "I do". He said, "Are they under control?"

and I said, "Yes, they are because I am on tablets".

(transcript, pp 175-176)

17. As to why she answered "No" to the question about anxiety state, the applicant said:

"... if I had time off from work with a headache I

would be given tablets or an injection, tablets at

that time, and my doctor just said it was just a bit

of anxiety state that you are under, a bit of stress.

.....

... he is only saying anxiety state just for the day

that I go to see him."

(transcript, p 175-176)

and as to why she answered "No" opposite the question about high blood pressure, she stated it was because her blood pressure at that time was not being treated and had not been treated before August 1975 when she started work for the Health Insurance Commission (p 175-176 of transcript). This, however, does not accord with the medical notes which indicate that the applicant first received a prescription for Lyndiol to treat her hypertension on 4 November 1974 with a repeat prescription on 17 January 1975.

18. The applicant gave evidence that at the time of her medical examination, she recollects the following interview with the attending Medical Officer:

"Q: Did he (the Medical Officer) ask you questions

then?

A: Yes, he did ask me questions.

Q: Did he record his answers in any way?

A: He was writing or ticking; he was doing

something, going down a column and doing

something but, I mean, I cannot read upside-down.

I know he was doing something, but what I do not

know.

Q: Do you remember what the questions he asked you

were?

A: Yes and no. He was asking me like health

problems: had I had this, had I had that? It

was no all the way through until he came to

"migraine". Well, I said yes because I have

"migraines", but I said no to all the others

because I did not have them and it did not mean

anything to me, the others, not having them; but

"migraine", I remember that.

Q: After you said yes, did he say anything, the

doctor, say anything more to you about your

"migraines"?

A: He asked me was I taking anything for them and I

said, "Yes", and he said, "Are they under

control?" I said "Yes" which they were."

(transcript, p 23)

19. On cross-examination, the applicant gave evidence that in addition to the above conversation, the Medical Officer had said, "We will forget about that ... what is said between these walls will stay here. Outside I will deny it" (p 179 of transcript). That was the last matter which the applicant could remember the doctor discussing with her. She could not recall whether he asked her about any condition other than migraines and in particular, could not recall being asked about high blood pressure or anxiety state.

20. Dr Carter, who was the attending Medical Officer, gave evidence which contradicted that of the applicant. Essentially, his evidence was that he would not have asked the applicant about any of the conditions mentioned in Part "B" of the form in which a "No" was recorded, and that he would only have questioned her in relation to Part "C". In any event, the doctor said that if it had come to his attention that the applicant had suffered from "migraine" and if the conversation as alluded to by the applicant had occurred, he would have asked her to change her answer to "Yes" and would certainly have asked her questions which would have been recorded under Part "C".

21. The Tribunal is of the view that the applicant, in providing her answers for her husband to record, particularly in answering "No" to the questions with respect to high blood pressure and migraines or persistent headaches, and in signing the declaration (stating the information was true and correct), gave false information. The Tribunal does not accept the events which the applicant describes as having occurred but instead prefers the evidence of Dr Carter who gave a more likely explanation of what occurred during the examination.

22. To continue chronologically, on 24 October 1975, the applicant was accepted into the Superannuation Fund under the Act as it then stood. For the remainder of 1975, the applicant saw Dr Skybyneckyj on two occasions in relation to "migraine" for which she was given a prescription of either Pethidine and Valium or, alternatively, Valium and Ergodryl. The applicant was absent from work one day during 1975, namely on 12 November 1975, for a bilious attack.

23. In 1976, the number of consultations which the applicant had with Dr Skybyneckyj rose to 15. Most of these consultations related to "migraine" and in September of that year, the applicant associated these "migraines" with stress at work. At most of the consultations, the applicant appears to have received an injection of Pethidine and Stematil for her symptoms. During 1976, the applicant took approximately 10 days away from work, 6 of which were related to pharyngitis.

24. During 1977, the applicant's consultations with Dr Skybyneckyj increased to 20 with 17 days absent from work. Most of the time away from work and the consultations were related to "migraine" and again, she was mostly treated with an injection of Pethidine and Stematil. The doctor's notes also indicate that at some consultations, the applicant complained of being worried and tense.

25. In 1978, the number of consultations with Dr Skybyneckyj further increased to 24 with 43 days absent from work, of which 22 related to hypertension, the remainder being mainly related to "migraine" as well as "nausea" which, on looking at the doctor's notes, appears to be connected with "migraine" as the prescription includes injections of Pethidine and Stematil.

26. In 1979, the applicant attended Dr Skybyneckyj's rooms on 9 occasions for a combination of her hypertension and her "migraines". The applicant was still being treated for her "migraine" with injections of Pethidine and Stematil. On 27 February 1979 the applicant collapsed at work and seemed to lapse into unconsciousness. She was taken to hospital and discharged on 1 March 1979. The opinion of Professor Shearman was that the attack suffered by the applicant was a "migrainous attack" and a notation was made that it was an "atypical attack of migraine" (T28/94).

27. During 1980, the applicant continued to see Dr Skybyneckyj on 13 occasions, mostly for "migraine" and sometimes hypertension. Again, the treatment for "migraine" was injections of Pethidine and Stematil. During 1980, the applicant was absent approximately 16 days from work, mostly for "migraines".

28. In 1981, the number of consultations which the applicant had with Dr Skybyneckyj rose significantly to 27 and the number of days absent from work was 53, of which 19 days related to hypertension and the balance mostly for "migraine". At the consultations relating to "migraine" headaches, the applicant was treated with injections, which she was receiving almost fortnightly. The applicant also presented with hypertension and complaints of difficulty in sleeping and dizziness.

29. In 1982, the applicant consulted Dr Skybyneckyj on 18 occasions, usually in relation to "migraine" and hypertension. Of particular note, on 29 June 1982, the applicant indicated she "wants to give up work" and the possibility of retirement on sickness grounds was discussed with her doctor. Her doctor expressed the view as recorded in his notes "I do not feel that she should entertain these thoughts at this stage". A further note was made on 8 July 1982 by her doctor that "At this stage I feel that she should be back at work. I do not consider there are justifiable grounds for retirement". On the other hand, at the hearing, the applicant gave evidence that she had never considered resigning at any time before she was retired by her employer (see transcript, p 190). During 1982, the applicant had a total of 72 days away from work of which 56 were related to hypertension and the balance due to mainly "migraine".

30. In 1983, the number of consultations which the applicant had with Dr Skybyneckyj decreased to 11. These consultations were for hypertension and "migraine" for which the applicant was still being treated. The number of days absent from work was 17, most of which were related to "migraines".

31. In 1984, the applicant consulted Dr Skybyneckyj on 9 occasions, the majority of consultations being for "migraine" and the number of days absent from work was 27, of which 7 related to a "spinning syndrome" which Dr Skybyneckyj diagnosed as "vertigo", and the balance for "migraine".

32. In the first half of 1985, the applicant had only a few consultations with Dr Skybyneckyj, again mainly related to "migraine" as well as an upper respiratory tract infection ("URTI") which seemed to continue to affect the applicant throughout May and June 1985.

33. At a consultation on 25 June 1985, Dr Skybyneckyj noted that the applicant "has the jitters all the time, her knees are jumpy. She feels all geared up, nervy all the time, not sleeping" and following this, the applicant was referred by Dr Skybyneckyj to the psychiatrist, Dr Taylor.

34. The applicant was off work continuously as from 30 May 1985. Initially, her absence was stated as "URTI" (upper respiratory tract infection) but as from 26 June 1985, her absence was said to relate to "anxiety state".

35. On 1 July 1985, the applicant was seen by Dr Taylor who formed the view that the applicant was suffering from a combination of the following:

(1) A personality structure prone to anxiety

(2) A depressive illness essentially of the endogenous

type but with some component being reactive

depression

(3) Headaches of a migrainous type which were in part

tension related and for which she was routinely

treated with frequent doses of Pethidine and

Stematil. The Pethidine may have in itself induced

headaches and the Stematil may have produced the

side effects of the churning stomach, restlessness

and increased muscle tone and that "these factors

may have contributed to her cycle of anxiety,

depression and headache"

(4) That the history suggested her depression

progressed from 1983 increasing more rapidly in

recent times but may have gone back to as early as

1979 following her attack.

36. In addition to the above, the applicant gave evidence that ever since 1973, she has been taking Valium and Diazepam on a regular basis (four half tablets a day) and that this continued up until the time when she saw Dr Taylor. This medication was in addition to the injections she had been receiving for her "migraine" headaches.

37. The applicant's condition dramatically deteriorated in September 1985 when the number of medical consultations over one month from 5 September 1985 until the beginning of October 1985, increased to some 34 consultations with locum practitioners and 6 consultations with Dr Skybyneckyj for "migraines". On each of these occasions, the applicant appears to have been given injections. In addition, there are references to her having attended at a local hospital in order to obtain injections. Therefore, throughout almost the whole of September 1985, the applicant was receiving injections for Pethidine and Stematil approximately every two days, and during October 1985, she was receiving these injections almost daily from locum practitioners. Some records indicate that there were days when she had more than one injection of Pethidine and Stematil.

38. On 25 September 1985, the applicant was examined to ascertain whether she was fit for continued employment or whether she should be retired on the ground of invalidity. Dr A. Dean, the examining Commonwealth Medical Officer, diagnosed the applicant as suffering from the condition of "endogenous agitated depression" and "chronic migraine" with these two conditions contributing 80% and 20% respectively to the applicant's incapacity. Dr Dean considered that the applicant was unfit for continued employment and should be retired on the grounds of invalidity but may, in due course, partially or completely recover and again become fit for Commonwealth employment.

39. On 1 October 1985, Dr Pamnanay, a Senior Medical Officer, in oversighting Dr Dean's report, concurred with that opinion and recommended that the applicant be retired on the ground of invalidity.

40. The applicant was subsequently retired with effect as from 25 September 1985 and the notification was expressed in the following terms:

"I hereby retire Mrs. Elizabeth Ann Cox on the grounds

of invalidity due to Endogenous Agitated Depression

and Chronic Migraine, under Condition 102 of the

Health Insurance Commission's Terms and Conditions of

Employment, with effect from 4.51 p.m. on 25th

September, 1985."

(T15/59)

41. On 23 October 1985, Dr Taylor wrote to Dr Skybyneckyj expressing his concern about the possible addictive nature of the injections that the applicant was receiving for her "migraine" headaches and suggested an alternative treatment of a combination of anti-depressants and minor tranquillisers. The applicant's treatment was altered and she was also sent to a clinic for the purposes of detoxification and her "migraine" headaches gradually decreased in frequency.

42. At the present time, the applicant still suffers from "migraines" but to a much lesser extent than in 1985, and she still suffers from depression.

43. Turning specifically to address the medical evidence. Dr Taylor's views are summarised at para. 35 above. Concerning the relationship between the applicant's depression and head aches, Dr Taylor made the following additional observations. Firstly, that the Pethidine treatment the applicant was given for her "migraine" headaches (which headaches, in his view, were related to stress), would have increased the frequency of those headaches, as well as contributing to her depression; and the Stematil treatment would produce symptoms of tremor and shaking. Secondly, that the depression itself would have possibly increased the frequency of her headaches as well as her appreciation of those headaches. Thirdly, that the symptoms of depression included being tearful, sleeping difficulties, mood disturbance, appetite disturbance, loss of libido and energy.

44. Dr Radeski, a psychiatrist and former Commonwealth Medical Officer, was asked to assess the applicant on 15 September 1986 and after obtaining a history which is set out in a report dated 21 September 1986 (T26/89), he reached the conclusion that at the time of retirement, the applicant was suffering from two conditions, namely "migraine" and "depression", but that in his view, at the time of his examination, the applicant's main complaint was "migraine" and she did not present with a significant depression. Dr Radeski concluded that the major reason for the applicant's retirement would have been the condition of "migraine". In the course of giving evidence, Dr Radeski expressed the view that there was no causal relationship between "migraine" and the depression from which the applicant suffered, although he agreed with the view expressed by Dr Taylor in his report dated 16 December 1987 (T33) that the Pethidine and Stematil injections (received by the applicant to treat her "migraines") were possibly addictive and that those injections could have aggravated feelings of restlessness which may in turn have aggravated the applicant's anxiety/depression as well as her headaches. Dr Radeski differed from Dr Taylor in that he considered the depression would not increase the frequency of the headaches, but merely increase the applicant's appreciation of her headaches.

45. The two psychiatrists also held varying opinions as to what was the predominant incapacitating condition suffered by the applicant at the time of her retirement. Dr Taylor considered the major condition was depression, her headaches merely being secondary and not a reason for retirement. Dr Radeski, however, formed the view, in retrospect, that "migraine" was the major reason for the applicant's retirement, although he did concede that a person may place more emphasis on the pervading symptomatology than on the disabilities from the depression.

46. Both psychiatrists agreed that the applicant's headaches did not fit the classical migraine pattern and that her headaches were related to tension, stress and anxiety and may have been headaches of a migrainous type. Dr Taylor and Dr Radeski also agreed that it was difficult to distinguish tension headaches from those which the applicant regarded as migraine headaches and that the applicant may not be able to appreciate the difference.

47. Taking into account all of the medical evidence and the evidence given by the applicant, we have reached the following conclusions:

(1) That the applicant since the age of 11 years to the date

hereof, has suffered from headaches which, although

described as "migraines", have not been classical

migraine headaches, but headaches of a migrainous type

closely connected with tension, stress and anxiety.

These headaches we will hereinafter refer to as

"migrainous headaches";

(2) That the applicant was treated for her migrainous

headaches by injections of Stematil and Pethidine from

1976 until that treatment ceased in late October 1985;

(3) That the applicant as at June 1985 was suffering from a

depressive illness essentially being endogenous agitated

depression with a component of reactive depression,

hereinafter referred to as "depression". It is

uncertain as to when this depression commenced; it may

have been 1983 or as early as 1979;

(4) That there was no causal relationship between the

migrainous headaches and the onset of the depression;

(5) That the applicant became addicted to the injections of

Stematil and Pethidine such that by early September

1985, the Pethidine injections for treatment of her

migrainous headaches significantly increased the

frequency of those headaches as well as to a minor

extent aggravating her depression. In addition, the

Stematil injections caused symptoms of tremor, shaking

and her complaint of jumpy knees;

(6) That the applicant's depression to some minor extent

aggravated her migrainous headaches by increasing their

frequency and her perception of those headaches;

(7) That as at the date of retirement on 25 September 1985,

the applicant was suffering from migrainous headaches

and depression. The symptoms of the migrainous

headaches were as described in para. 6 hereof, and for

which she was being treated daily. The symptoms of her

depression which was predominantly endogenous agitated

depression, included feeling nervy, jittery, tearful and

sleeping difficulties, appetite disturbance, loss of

libido and energy;

(8) That as at the date of retirement, the applicant had not

yet been withdrawn from injections of Pethidine and

Stematil (this did not occur until after 23 October

1985). Therefore, any withdrawal symptoms suffered by

her such as headaches, diarrhoea, muscle cramps and

nausea, post-date her retirement.

(9) That as at the date of retirement, the major and

predominant incapacitating symptoms of the applicant

were those related to depression and to a lesser extent

the symptoms related to migrainous headaches. In making

this finding, the Tribunal is accepting that a small

proportion of the applicant's migrainous headaches and

their symptoms were contributed to by the depression as

set out in (6) above but that is offset by the

migrainous headaches in turn aggravating the applicant's

depression to a minor extent as set out in (5) above.

48. We will now consider the relevant legislation in the light of the aforementioned facts.

49. The Tribunal in Re Ghose and Commissioner for Superannuation V.86/449, delivered on 15 March 1988 (Decision 4213) discussed the meaning of "false" in para.9:

"The Shorter Oxford English Dictionary provides two

principal relevant meanings of "false", namely

"erroneous" and "purposely untrue". It is clear from

the decision in Fry v Commissioner for Superannuation

[1984] FCA 211; (1984) 6 ALD 229 (as to which see paragraph 29 infra)

that the meaning to be given to "false" in sub-section

16(11) is "purposely (or knowingly) untrue".

50. In this review, we are satisfied that the applicant at her medical examination by Dr Carter, in terms of sub-para. 184(5)(d)(i) of the 1976 Act, failed to furnish information or furnished false information in that she did not disclose that she had in the past suffered from "migraines" and the word "No" was inserted alongside the relevant question.

51. We are also satisfied and accept the evidence of Dr Carter that had the applicant supplied information to Dr Carter concerning her "migraines", Dr Carter and in turn the Superannuation Board would not have been satisfied under sub- s.5(1) of the Superannuation Act 1922, that the applicant's health and physical fitness were such as to justify her being accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation Fund. Instead, the applicant would have been accepted as a contributor to the Provident Fund.

52. We are satisfied that the condition of "migraines" was therefore a condition of the applicant which was "relevant" for the purposes of the superseded Act.

53. The next issue to be decided is whether there would by virtue of sub-s.184(2) of the Act have deemed to be in force a B.C.C. specifying the condition causing the applicant's retirement. This in turn requires the Commissioner to consider whether the incapacity which was the ground of the applicant's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by "migraines" or alternatively by a condition connected with "migraines", pursuant to sub-para.184(5)(d)(ii).

54. The requirements of sub-para 184(5)(d)(ii) have been carefully analysed by the Full Federal Court in McMullen v Commissioner for Superannuation 61 ALR 189 at 198, wherein their Justices stated that sub-para 184(5)(d)(ii) required the Commissioner to address two questions:

"... The first was whether, if the person had not

failed to furnish the false information, he would,

immediately before the commencing day, have been a

contributor to the Provident Account. The second was

whether, if he had then been a contributor to the

Provident Account, there would, by virtue of s 184(2)

be deemed to have been in force in respect of him at

the time he ceased to be an eligible employee a

benefit classification certificate specifying the

condition causing death or retirement."

This interpretation was also adopted by the Tribunal in Re Ghose and Commissioner for Superannuation (supra) at para.13.

55. The meaning of the word "substantially" in this context has been considered by the Tribunal in Re Whiteford and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 6 AAR 70. At pp 78-79, the Tribunal concluded that the word "substantially" meant more than "not trivial, minimal or nominal", but not as far up the scale as "in the main" or "in the greater part".

56. We also endorse the view expressed by the Tribunal in Re Joyce and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority, S.87/141 delivered on 28 April 1988 (Decision 4301), where the Tribunal in a not dissimilar context, noted that the word "substantially" was to be treated in a "qualitative not quantitative way" (para. 53 thereof).

57. It has also been noted in the decision of Re Whiteford (supra) that the Commissioner and this Tribunal are not bound by the original description and diagnosis of the condition for retirement.

58. Applying these principles to the findings of fact. The applicant's retirement on 25 September 1985 was in part caused or contributed to by "migrainous headaches". The condition of "migrainous headaches" consisted of what had previously been described as "migraines". The migrainous headaches were increased and aggravated by their prescribed treatment, and by the time of the applicant's retirement, these migrainous headaches were being treated daily. Leaving aside any aggravation which the condition of depression may have had on the condition of migrainous headaches, we are of the opinion that although the major cause of the applicant's retirement was her depression, the condition of migrainous headaches was a significant contributing factor to her retirement. The contribution was greater than trivial, minimal or nominal although it was lesser than in the main or the greater part.

59. Therefore, had the applicant been accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account, a B.C.C. specifying the condition of "migraines" or more correctly with retrospective knowledge, "migrainous headaches" would, on her retirement, have been issued in respect of her pursuant to sub.s.184(2) of the Act and would be virtue of that sub-section have been deemed to be in force immediately before her retirement.

60. Consequently, the Commissioner is required to issue, under sub-s.184(5) of the Act, a B.C.C. specifying the condition "migrainous headaches", which certificate is, by virtue of that sub-section, to be deemed to be in force immediately before the applicant's retirement for the purposes of the Act.

61. Paragraph 66(2)(c) requires the decision-maker to be satisfied that the incapacity which was the ground for the applicant's retirement was caused or contributed to by the condition of migrainous headaches or, alternatively, was connected with migrainous headaches.

62. The word "incapacity" has been interpreted as the outward result of the physical or mental condition which leads to the person being unable to perform work (Re Burr and Commissioner for Superannuation (1980) 3 ALN N1 at pt.(ii)).

63. In the present review, the symptoms of migrainous headaches were those described in para. 6 hereof and when suffered by the applicant, led to her inability to work. The frequency with which the applicant suffered from those symptoms and the frequency of her treatment (which in turn aggravated the migrainous headache condition) was such that the incapacity which was a ground for the applicant's retirement, was caused or substantially contributed to, by the condition of migrainous headaches.

64. In the alternative, on the facts, the incapacity which was the ground of the applicant's retirement was connected with the condition of migraines or migrainous headaches, the words "connected with" being interpreted in the manner set out in the decision in Commissioner for Superannuation v Miller [1985] FCA 445; (1985) 8 FCR 153.

65. Further, as the Tribunal in Re Ghose (supra) observed, the answer to para.66(2)(c) would also in effect answer the requirements in sub-para.184(5)(d)(ii), although the former refers to incapacity which was the grounds for retirement, whereas the latter refers to the condition causing retirement. Those observations by the Tribunal are true of the facts in this review for the reasons expressed above.

66. In conclusion, the Tribunal finds that the delegate's decision should be upheld and that the BCC deemed to be in force in respect of the applicant immediately before her retirement should specify the condition "migrainous headaches". Further, the Tribunal considers that the condition of "migrainous headaches" was the condition which caused or substantially contributed to the applicant's retirement and that the incapacity being the ground for the applicant's retirement was caused or substantially contributed to by the condition of "migrainous headaches".

67. A final matter which we address is an additional submission made by the applicant, namely that as the applicant's employer brought to the Commissioner's attention in August 1982 the discrepancy between the applicant's personnel file (which contained a report prepared by a Commonwealth Medical Officer in 1981, stating that the applicant had suffered migraines for a period of 30 years) and the employment medical record (which recorded that the applicant had never suffered from migraines), the respondent was estopped from relying on the discrepancy, or had waived its right to rely on the false information contained in the medical report.

68. The fact that the Commissioner was aware of the discrepancy in 1982 but failed to act on the information as provided until the applicant retired in 1985, is not sufficient grounds, in our view, to raise estoppel. The applicant must be able to show that the Commissioner, by his conduct, caused her to act to her detriment. The applicant submitted that in continuing to pay contributions to the Superannuation Fund in the intervening three years, in the mistaken belief that she would be eligible for a superannuation payout, was detrimental to her. But even if the Commissioner had informed the applicant that she would not be eligible under the Fund in 1982, she would have had no alternative but to continue contributing to the Fund because after 1976, the Provident Account was discontinued. Further, after 1976 contributions to the Superannuation Fund were equivalent to that previously paid to the Provident Account. Therefore, the applicant suffered no detriment due to any failure by the Commissioner to inform her that she would not meet the eligibility criteria.

69. Further, estoppel is an equitable remedy and a principle of equity is that a person should seek equitable relief with "clean hands". It was the applicant's own conduct in failing to furnish accurate information concerning her past medical history, which precipitated the action taken by the Commissioner in issuing the BCC upon her retirement.

70. Nor can it be said that the respondent has waived its right to act on the medical evidence and issue a BCC in respect of the applicant. In 1982 when the Commissioner first became aware of the discrepancy, there was no administrative action which he could have taken. At that time, it was only upon the applicant's retirement that the Commissioner was empowered to issue a BCC under sub-s.184(5) of the Act. Therefore, even if the Commissioner had chosen to carry out inquiries and such inquiries confirmed the discrepancy, he could not have taken any action at that time. It was only upon retirement that the condition "migraines" became relevant, and then only if it were established that that condition or a condition connected therewith caused or substantially contributed to the applicant's retirement.

71. The decision of the delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation is therefore upheld.
