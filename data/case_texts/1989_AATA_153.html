ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - Moneys received by company for pest treatment to premises - Company to conduct inspection of premises after 10 year - Whether moneys received are income derived at time of receipt - Penalty - Moneys described as unearned income - Whether omission of assessable income.

Income Tax Assessment Act - ss.17, 226. Law of Property Act 1936 (SA) - s15.

Arthur Murray (NSW) Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 58; (1965) 114 CLR 314.

REASONS FOR DECISION

The applicant company in these proceedings at all relevant times carried on a business at Adelaide. I shall call it the taxpayer company. The taxpayer company objected to its assessments of income tax for the income years 1982 and 1983.

In both these income years the respondent commissioner has included amounts of assessable income which are disputed by the taxpayer company. The amount so included in 1982 was $100,700 and in 1983 the amount was $111,584.

There were other adjustments which have been described by counsel as incidental and not material to this application therefore they are not before me as part of this review. As the applications for review in respect of both 1982 and 1983 tax years raise identical issues, the relevant applications were heard together.

The evidence in this case largely is not in dispute. What is in dispute depends largely on the inferences that should be drawn and the legal consequences that flow from the basic facts.

The taxpayer company claims that part of its income for the income years 1982 and 1983 was not derived at the point of receipt of the income in question but was to be derived in futuro and on the event of the provision of its services at future times.

The objections in respect of those tax years are concerned with a particular operation of the business. That operation involved what the taxpayer's counsel described as a "once and for all fee" for work known as "pre-treatment". This involved the application of a chemical process in order to prevent infestation of premises by certain pests. This was to be followed some 10 years later by certain other services, basically inspection and remedial work if needed, provided by the taxpayer company at the site of the provision of the original services. The combined operation was referred to as a "pre-treatment" to distinguish it from other work operations carried on by the taxpayer company.

At the time of the first provision of the relevant service to a customer the taxpayer company provided a document which in some cases referred to a warranty or guarantee. The services to be provided after 10 years from the date of the pre-treatment service included an inspection and a report. If infestation by pests had come about the taxpayer company saw that it had an obligation to re-apply the chemical process and to carry out remedial work if certain damage had been caused by the infestation. The taxpayer company kept records of the transactions in question which shall be described below. I shall also discuss below the effect of some agreements that the taxpayer company entered into in respect of pre-treatment work.

The actual work in issue before me, what the taxpayer company called pre-treatments, was carried out by the taxpayer company for various building contractors who were building houses for sale or for clients. There was no distinction so far as the taxpayer company was concerned. The taxpayer company entered into a contract with the builder. (Exhibits R2 and R3 are examples). It is the case that in some instances the house and land upon which the treatment work was carried out by the taxpayer company was sold by the builder. In other cases the builder was building for an owner. Ten years on the land may or may not be owned by the person, who purchased from the builder or who had the builder build a house the subject of a pre-treatment service.

There was also evidence that the taxpayer company carried out other work referred to as domestic maintenance. This work involved a payment to the taxpayer company for a service rendered to a householder and as part of that agreement there are follow-up quarterly visits. Work of that sort is not under consideration in this review. Yet another arrangement is evidenced by exhibit R1. In that situation a commercial undertaking agreed to pay for treatment and for 9 years maintenance paid in advance. Again arrangements of that sort are not in issue in this case.

The evidence of the taxpayer company was that infestation of pests after pre-treatment work was very low - possibly less than 1 per cent of the pre-treatment jobs which the taxpayer company carried out.

The evidence of the taxpayer company's managing director was that shortly before the expiration of a 10 year period after carrying out pre-treatment work, the company would write to a householder advising the householder of the approaching expiry of the 10 year "warranty period". The company kept records of the addresses of premises that had had the pre-treatment service and went to some trouble at the ten year mark to identify the householder. Sometimes various letters were sent if no response was received.

Examples of the taxpayer company's letters form exhibit A4. The letters are addressed to "The Property Owner". The letters are couched in terms which, at the least, expressed a sense of obligation on the taxpayer company's part under a warranty given by the taxpayer at the time of the original agreement (again see R2 and R3). I shall discuss this question below.

There was also tendered a document which was admitted as exhibit A2 described as a "preventative treatment guarantee." That document sets out certain benefits. These include:

"1. The Company guarantees to service and treat at no extra cost any occurrence of "[pests]" during the term of the this guarantee which is Ten Years from the Date Treatment completed.

  1. any ....damage as a result of ... during the ten years of this guarantee will be replaced by the Company and treated at no extra cost to the client.
  2. FULL benefits under this guarantee are transferable upon written application within 90 days of change of ownership.

7. An inspection of premises treated will be made at end of 10 year warranty and report submitted.

CONDITIONS

To ensure full benefits under this warranty complete the attached details and return to "[taxpayer company]" within 90 days of taking up residence. Payment must be made by due date to qualify for warranty."

Attached to the document was a cut off or tear off "certificate". This document had spaces to include the owner's name, street, suburb, telephone number, name of builder, address, date treatment completed and space for comments.

The evidence which I accept from the taxpayer company's managing director is that during the early part of the 1980s the level of notification was about 50 percent of all the pre-treatments undertaken. I accept the evidence that the taxpayer kept the returned notification slips on file. I also accept that hat the taxpayer company kept on file records referred to as builders' cards. Exhibit A3 is an example.

The evidence also reveals and I find that the taxpayer in 1974 had acquired the business of the Z Co. That company had, prior to its acquisition by the taxpayer company, conducted its business arrangements in a way similar to that described above. There was a family connection between the Z Co. and the taxpayer company.

After 1974 the taxpayer had carried out "ten year" work in relation to pre-treatments that had been carried out by Z Co. This it had contracted to do after the Z Co was acquired. The evidence also was, and I accept it, that from 1984 on, the taxpayer company commenced 10 year inspections of pre-treatment work which it had carried out, in its own right, from and after 1974. The taxpayer relied on this evidence as an expression of the reality of its obligations under pre-treatment contacts.

The uncontradicted evidence was that in October 1985 settlement occurred of a sale by the taxpayer company of its business to the Y Co. The Y Co. is connected with persons who were former employees of the taxpayer company. Exhibit A5 is the relevant memorandum of agreement. The taxpayer company's managing director held the view that Y Co. "assumed the liability of carrying out future inspections and providing reports" in respect of pre-treatments carried out by the taxpayer. The case was argued before me on the basis that the taking over by the Y Co. of the taxpayer Company's obligations effected in any relevant way the manner in which money invoiced and receivable in the income year should be treated as being totally earnt when the invoice was delivered or whether future services remained to be done outside the income year in question.

The taxpayer company's case was that the facts fell squarely within the principle of Arthur Murray (NSW) Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 58; (1965) 114 CLR 314. Mr. Evans for the taxpayer submitted that the pre-treatment contracts involved two separate distinct considerations for payment. First there is payment for an initial application or treatment. That was a fairly short operation often no more than 15 minutes on site. The chemical costs as opposed to the labour component is remarkably small. Mr. Evans submitted that the second consideration involved the obligation to carry out the ten year inspection and provide any "warranty" service.

The taxpayer company's case was that until the obligation at the ten year mark has been totally discharged the taxpayer company is not entitled to regard that part of its fee which relates to the ten year aspects as income earnt. That part of the fee which amounts to a pre-payment for the future services does not form part of taxable income. It was submitted that the quid pro quo for that payment has not been received. The taxpayer took some comfort from the Commissioner's statement set out at 1 Aust FTR 6854 and 6857.

Mr. Evans submitted that what he described as the two obligations of the applicant were, of their very nature, different. They were designed to achieve different results. The initial treatment was to set up a barrier against the particular pests. The purpose behind the inspection and report was to ascertain at the end of the ten year period whether the initial treatment had been successful or not with the result that the householder was thus reassured or given the opportunity to have remedial work carried out.

The evidence also suggests to me that the contact with the householder would be of commercial advantage to the taxpayer company. This advantage is the opportunity provided to the taxpayer company to offer other services to the householder at that point. The applicant's counsel submitted that if that effect occurred (which he did not concede) then it was merely an incidental benefit that flowed to the taxpayer company. He submitted that this did not affect the contractual right which he submitted existed between the taxpayer and its customer or the assignee of the customer. I shall return to the question of assignment later.

On the application of the Arthur Murray case (supra) Mr. Slater, for the respondent, submitted that the income with which the Tribunal is concerned is income which arose from contracts with builders, the benefit was not assigned in any way and that the income was fully earnt in the income years in question.

The taxpayer company's managing director gave evidence that the company always contracted a builder in respect of pre-treatment work. I accept that evidence. It was submitted that in these cases the taxpayer company approached builders for work and sought all of the builders' work. It mattered not to the taxpayer company whether the builder on the one hand was building a project house or a house built for sale as a 'spec house' or, on the other hand, was building for a particular landowner. I accept that as the case.

Mr. Slater submitted that there was no assignment of any of the contracts in question. This relates to the warranty benefit forming part of the agreement with the builders who, in all cases, had sold the house and land in question during the income year or at any rate had no interest under the contract with the taxpayer company which could be enforced after the income year in question. It was argued for the applicant that assignments of the benefit of the warranty did take place and this was shown to have occurred when the tear-off certificate (part of exhibit A2) was returned to the taxpayer company.

As to the existence of a lawful assignment, it was submitted on behalf of the respondent that there was nothing in the evidence which allowed s.15 of the Law and Property Act 1936 (SA) to be satisfied in respect of the alleged assignments. That section provides:

"Any absolute assignment by writing under the hand of the assignor (not purporting to be by way of charge only) of any debt or other legal chose in action, of which express notice in writing has been given to the debtor, trustee, or other person from whom the assignor would have been entitled to receive or claim such a debt or chose in action, shall be effectual in law (subject to equities having priority over the right of the assignee), to pass and transfer from the date of such notice -

(a) the legal right to such debt or chose in action;

(b) all legal and other remedies for the same; and

(c) the power to give a good discharge for the same, without the concurrence of the assignor:

provided that, if the debtor, trustees or other person

liable in respect of such debt or chose in action has notice -

(a) that such assignment is disputed by the assignor, or any person claiming under him; or

(b) of any other opposing or conflicting claims, to such debt or chose in action,

he may, if he thinks fit, either call upon the persons making claim thereto to concerning the same, or pay the debt or other chose in action into court under the provisions of the Trustee Act."

It was submitted that the evidence in this case does not reveal that there is any assignment in the manner provided by s.15. There was no "writing under the hand of the assignor". I accept this submission (see Olssen v. Dyson (1970) 120 CLR 365 at 374, per Kitto J.).

It was also submitted on behalf of the respondent that there was no assignment in equity. In that regard Mr. Slater relied upon what was said by Windeyer J. in Norman v. Commissioner of Taxation [1963] HCA 21; (1963) 109 CLR 9 at 26-34. I note in particular the passage at 28 and 29:

"If an attempt is made to assign, by way of gift, a chose in action assignable under the statute, then, as I see the matter, the requirements of the statute cannot be ignored; for the general rule of equity is that an effective assignment occurs only if the donor does all that, according to the nature of the property, he must do to transfer the property to the donee. But the weight of authority is, I think, in favour of the view that in equity there is a valid gift of property transferable at law if the donor, intending to make, then and there, a complete disposition and transfer to the donee, does all that on his part is necessary to give effect to his intention and arms the donee with the means of completing the gift according to the requirements of law: see Brunker v. Perpetual Trustee Co. (Ltd. ) (1) per Dixon J. (2); Re Smith (3) ; In re Rose (4); In re Rose (5) . I think therefore that, if a man, meaning to make an immediate gift of a chose in action that is his, executes an instrument that meets the requirements of the statute and delivers it to the donee, actually or constructively, he has put it out of his power to recall his gift. It is true that until notice is given to the debtor or person against whom the chose is enforceable at law, all the requirements of the statute have not been complied with. But the notice can be given by the donee; and, if the donee has express or implied authority to give it, I think that equity would not allow the donor to deny the right of the donee to do so and so intercept his gift."

I also refer to what Kitto J. has said in Olssen v. Dyson (supra). That case specifically considered the effect of s.15 of the Law of Property Act 1936 (SA). His Honour said (at p.375 and 376):

"In particular, the general provision in s.15 of the Law of Property Act, 1936 (S.A.) enabling debts to be assigned In the manner there provided is inapplicable here, for there was no 'writing under the hand of the assignor'. There was no valid assignment in equity either, for property which is assignable at law but is not assigned in the manner which the law requires for a legal assignment of it cannot be held in equity to be assigned unless by reason of some fact or circumstance which a court of equity regards as binding the legal owner in conscience to hold the property upon trust for the assignee. A promise for valuable consideration to assign the property is enough for this purpose, for equity, regarding that as done which ought to be done in return for the consideration given, holds the assignee to have an equitable interest commensurate with the legal interest which specific performance of the promise would give him. But there is no equity to perfect an imperfect gift: ...."

Menzies J. (at p.380) agreed with Kitto J.

Windeyer J. adhered to what he had said in Norman v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (supra) and went on to say (at p.386):

"There is no equity to perfect an imperfect gift. This sentence, extracted from the well-known judgment of Turner L.J. in Milroy v. Lord (2) has become an aphorism But, because equity does not assist a volunteer, the rule stands firm."

So far as the evidence in this case goes there is no evidence of any consideration moving from any of the relevant housholders to the builder of the house in question for an assignment of the benefit of the contracted service to be rendered by the taxpayer company at the 10 year mark. There is no evidence of any assignment in writing under the hand of the donor as required by s.15 of the Law of Property Act. The return of the tear-off certificate by house owners could not amount, in my view, to anything more than a notice that an assignment had taken place if I suppose for the moment that the builder had in some way assigned the benefit of his agreement with the taxpayer company to some other person.

There may be any number of different ways in which property owners at some point after a house was built came into possession of the tear-off certificate before returning it to the taxpayer company. I am not satisfied that any were returned by the owners who had taken an assignment of the benefit of the builder's contract with the taxpayer company either in law or in equity. It will be remembered that the 10 years in respect of the tax years 1982 and 1983 had not yet expired. Two things therefore must be said. Counsel for the applicant pointed to the way the taxpayer company had conducted itself generally in regard to the execution of 10 year work in respect of other years.

I am not satisfied on the balance of probabilities that any assignments occurred which preserve benefits for owners beyond the income years in question arising out of the pre-treatment contracts entered into between the taxpayer company and builders in the 1982 and 1983 tax years.

The state of the evidence is such that I am not able to be satisfied directly or by inference from the facts that there was any assignment either at law or in equity of the benefit of the contractual arrangements, including the services to be rendered at the 10 year mark by the taxpayer company, between the taxpayer company and the builders. This is in respect of the pre-treatment agreements which are in issue in this case.

I find that the taxpayer company had and has no legal obligation to any relevant owner to carry out inspections and be obliged to rectify damage of the sort contemplated in the agreements with the builders. I find that 10 year inspections already carried out in respect of other years' contracts were done voluntarily and with the incidental benefit that the taxpayer company made business contact with the property owner at the ten year mark. This finding is supported by the evidence that in very few cases was the pre-treatment unsuccessful - no more than 1 percent of all pre-treatments. The taxpayer company was not running any great commercial risk by carrying out voluntary inspections.

In the light of that finding, I must consider if any obligations to the builders themselves to inspect and or to give a warranty or guarantee under contracts with the various builders existed outside the income years in question. Is the taxpayer company under some continuing obligation to the builders?

Counsel for the respondent relied upon Ziel Nominees Pty. Ltd. and Anor. v.VACC Insurance Co and Anor. (1975) 50 ALJR 106; 7 ALR 667. That case concerned an appeal from a judgment in favour of an insurance company. A vendor of land and premises entered into an enforceable contract for the sale of the land and premises. Thereafter the premises were destroyed by fire. The premises were insured against that event. The insurer stood to be liable to the extent of $60,000. The vendor made a claim on the policy. Subsequent to the fire settlement of the sale " occurred. On the day before settlement the vendor signed an authority addressed to the insurers to pay the proceeds of the claim to the purchaser together with an appropriate release. On settlement day the vendor assigned, by an endorsement on the policy, all his interest in the policy to the purchaser. Notice was given to the insurer. Harris J. in the Supreme Court of Victoria held that as the contract of insurance was for an indemnity the insurer had a complete defence. Barwick C.J. in the course of his judgment said (50 ALJR at 107; 7ALR at 669).

"It follows, in my opinion, that at the time the document of 20 February became effective, ie on settlement of the contract of sale, the vendor was not and could not at that time have become entitled to any moneys under the policy: and this for the simple and direct reason that he had not and could not suffer any loss by reason of the destruction or damage of the improvements on the land which he had sold. In other words he had nothing to assign."

Kern Corporation Ltd. v. Walter Reid Trading Pty. Ltd. And Ors. [1987] HCA 20; (1987) 61 ALJR 319; 71 ALR 417 is another example which dealt with the loss of insured premises between contract and completion. Ziel Nominees (supra) was cited with approval by Mason J. (61 ALJR at 321; 71 ALR at 423) and by Wilson and Dawson JJ. (61 ALJR AT 327; 71 ALR at 426-427). Wilson and Dawson JJ said (61 ALJR AT 327; 71 ALR at 427):

"Where, as in this case, a vendor has insured himself against loss or destruction of a building by fire the purchaser does not acquire any right to the benefit of that insurance. A policy of that kind is a contract of indemnity which entitles the insured to recover his actual loss up to the amount insured or, to use the words of s.58, 'the money for which the building is insured'. Ultimately settlement by the purchaser, who is bound by contract to complete, means that the vendor suffers no loss."

Mr. Slater submitted that after settlement of the sale by the builder of a house which was built for sale there was then no possibility of the builder suffering a loss or damage in respect of which the guarantee or warranty of the taxpayer could operate. There was no loss or damage which could arise in respect of which a builder could seek indemnity under the terms of his agreement with the taxpayer company. There is no evidence before the Tribunal which even suggests that any builder had any continuing interest in the premises he had built or maintained any obligation in regard to information of or damage caused to the premises in question by the pests the subject of the pre-treatment work.

On the evidence before me I cannot find on the balance of probabilities that the obligation of the taxpayer company to the particular builders with whom it had entered into pre-treatment contract extended beyond the date of settlement of the sale of the land and premises subject to any particular pre-treatment work. On the evidence before me and in respect of houses that were built by builders for existing landowners, I am not satisfied that any builder was in any way obligated as builder to the landowner which would allow the builder to enforce any obligation which might be said to arise under the pre-treatment contracts against the taxpayer company.

The sample of the agreements between the taxpayer company and one of the builders raises a further question. This relates to the use of the word "client" in the sample. Does this give any rights to the "client" as a third party under the contract? Exhibit R3 the document, which is in the form of a quotation which apparently was accepted so that the terms of the quotation became terms of the agreement, provides in part that:

"A 10 year guarantee certificate covering full timber replacement and any retreatment necessary at no cost "[builder]" or client."

The High Court of Australia has recently considered the case where a public liability insurance policy indemnified a company against liability in respect of personal injuries suffered at specified building sites, and where the definition of "insured" in the policy included the company's contractors. The question was whether a contractor who was not a contractor at the time the policy issued was covered by the terms of the policy. When damages were recovered against the contractor, the contractor sought the benefit of the indemnity. The High Court had to consider whether "the old rules apply to a policy of insurance". This refers to the rules relating to privity of contract and consideration. (See per Mason CJ. and Wilson J 165 CLR at 123). Their Honours went on to say:

"In the ultimate analysis the limited question we have to decide is whether the old rules apply to a policy of insurance. The injustice which would flow from such a result arises not only from its failure to give effect to the express intention of the person who takes out the insurance but also from the common intention of the parties and the circumstance that others, aware of the existence of the policy, will order their affairs accordingly. We doubt that the doctrine of estoppel provides an adequate protection of the legitimate expectations of such persons and, even if it does, the rights of persons under a policy of insurance should not be made to depend on the vagaries of such an intricate doctrine. In the nature of things the likelihood of some degree of reliance on the part of the third party in the case of a benefit to be provided for him under an insurance policy is so tangible that the common law rule should be shared with that liklihood in mind."

This was not the view of Brennan Deane Dawson and Gaudron JJ. They held in separate judgments that the contractor was not a party to the contract of insurance and could not enforce it as a party. Deane J. preferred the view that it was open to the contractor to argue that it had the benefit of a trust. (See 165 CLR at 151 and 152). Toohey J. proceeded on the basis that it was reasonable to expect that the contractor may order its affairs on the basis of the existence of the indemnity and it may sue on the policy despite the absence of the usual requirement for consideration moving from the contractor to the insurer. (See 165 CLR at 172).

Whether any views expressed in relation to a policy of insurance ought to be applied in the case of the guarantee referred to in exhibit R3 is not a matter which I have to pursue here. This is because there is no satisfactory evidence before me which establishes on the balance of probabilities either directly or by inference that the reference to "or client" which appears in R3 refers to any relevant owner of any particular property subject to pre-treatment work. The applicant has not adduced evidence of this sort at all. The applicant has not adduced evidence even as to how many contracts of this nature were relevant in 1982 and 1983 tax years.

In exhibit R2 a simpler form applies. There the relevant words are: "A certificate is issued on completion, certifying that treatment has been carried out to code .... including a 10 year timber replacement warranty ... An inspection of premises treated will be made at end of 10 year warranty and report submitted." The question referred to above in relation to exhibit R2 does not arise in relation to R3.

On the evidence before me I am satisfied on the balance of probabilities that the taxpayer company has and had no obligation to the builders or to owners or subsequent owners of the relevant premises which survives the disposal of the land and premises in question by the particular builder contracting with the taxpayer company for pre-treatment work. I am also satisfied on the balance of probabilities that where the builder built on an owner's land no unearnt income existed after the builder had paid the contractor pre-treatment fee. For completeness I mention that I am satisfied that the facts do not reveal that a novation occurred in any of the contracts which are relevant in this review whereby any subsequent owners became entitled under the terms of a contract in terms similar to that originally entered into between the taxpayer company and a particular builder.

On the view I take of the facts, I find that there was and is no real or practical possibility of the taxpayer company being called upon to make a refund of the amounts paid to it in relation to the 10 year mark services.

The case has been argued on the basis that the relevant fees were not earned in the income years in question because services remained to be performed at the expiration of 10 years. The applicant's case was presented on the basis that if I found in its favour on that question the appeal should be allowed .Conversely if I was against the applicant on that issue the amount of fees in contention for 1982 and 1983 income years should form part of the assessable means for those years. I therefore do not have to go on and consider whether or not land and premises were disposed of by builders during the income years in question in relation to the continuation of any liability to the builder under the pre-treatment contracts beyond the end of the financial year in question.

On the evidence before me I am satisfied on the balance of probabilities that the fees received in the income years 1982 and 1983 do not contain any prepayment for services obliged to be performed in the future and not wholly earnt in the income year 1982 and 1983 as the case may be.

In arriving at the above findings I have applied s.90(b) of the ITAA.

As to how these relevant fees should be treated for the assessment of income tax in the light of the findings I have made I turn to what was raised in Arthur Murray (NSW) Pty. Ltd. v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1965] HCA 58; (1965) 114 CLR 314 at 319 (per Barwick CJ., Kitto and Taylor JJ):

"Likewise, as it seems to us, in determining whether actual earning has to be added to receipt in order to find income, the answer must be given in the light of the necessity for earning which is inherent in the circumstances of the receipt do not prevent the amount received from becoming immediately the beneficial property of the company; for the fact that it has been paid in advance is not enough to affect it with any trust or charge, or to place any legal impediment in the way of the recipient's dealing with it as he will. But those circumstances nevertheless make it surely necessary, as a matter of business good sense, that the recipient should treat each amount of fees received but not yet earned as subject to the contingency that the whole or some part of it may have in effect be paid back, even if only as damages, should the agreed quid pro quo not be rendered in due course. The possibility of having to make such a payment back (we speak, of course, in practical terms) is an inherent characteristic of the receipt itself. In our opinion it would be out of accord with the realities of the situation to hold, whilst the possibility remains, that the amount received has the quality of income derived by the company."

The Court in the Arthur Murray case (supra) observed further (at 320) :

"In so far as the Act lays down a test for the inclusion of particular kinds of receipts in assessable income it is likewise true that commercial and accountancy practice cannot be substituted for the test. But the Act lays down no test for such a case as the present. The word "income", being used without relevant definition, is left to be understood in the sense which it has in the vocabulary of business affairs. To apply the concept which the work in that sense expresses is not to substitute some other test for the one prescribed in the Act; it is to give effect to the Act as it stands. Nothing in the act is contradicted or ignored when a receipt of money as a prepayment under a contract for future services is said not to constitute by itself a derivation of assessable income. On the contrary, if the statement accords with ordinary business concepts in the community - and we are bound by the case stated to accept that it does - it applies the provisions of the Act according to their true meaning."

In this case the approach of the taxpayer company's accountants to the manner in which they dealt with fees in relation to the 10 year services reflected the view that their client considered that the money should be regarded properly as prepayments under contracts for future services. If, on the other hand, the situation is not one involving prepayments for future services under contracts then the accounting approach adopted does not reflect the state of affairs as they have now been found to be by the Tribunal on the evidence before it.

Accordingly, I am of the view that the assessable income of the applicants ought to reflect the amounts of money which have been treated as unearnt income in the relevant years. The taxable income for the income year 1982 ought to include the sum of $100,700 and the taxable income for the income year 1983 ought to include the sum of $111,584.

A further matter argued before the Tribunal is concerned with the position that may arise if I do not find that the fees paid to the taxpayer company in respect of 1982 and 1983 pre-treatment contracts involves a component for unearned future services. Mr. Slater has argued that the estimates of the amount of such fees as the applicant claims is unearnt income for those years are inaccurate. In effect he submits that the applicant has overestimated that component of the relevant fees. In the view I have taken that there was no unearnt income for the relevant tax years it is not strictly necessary to consider this further question. But in the event I shall go on and make findings on this question.

Mr.Slater has submitted that the calculations contained in exhibits A8, All and A12 are "both arbitrary and unrealistic."

In support of this submission counsel pointed to a number of matters relating to the taxpayers method of calculation of the costs of the future services.

Unearnt income did not appear in the taxpayer company's books until income year 1982. This seems to have come about because the taxpayers accountant formed a view about this matter during the course of a field audit by officers of the Commissioner. The taxpayer company had been carrying out pre-treatment work prior to that income year.

It appears from a note to the accounts in the 1982 tax return (Exhibit A9 at p.17) that the taxpayer company calculated the cost of performing future services as $25 per contract totalling $100,700. This is the amount that was excluded from income in arriving at the results of the company for the tax year 1982. In regard to income year 1983 the tax return for that year (exhibit A10) contains a note that unearned income for the year was calculated as 5,072 contracts at a cost of $22 per contract totalling $111,584 for that income year.

Document exhibit 8 refers to calculations which were made to arrive at the figure of $25 per 1982 tax year contract. The calculations were criticized in the submissions for the Commissioner.

The taxpayer company's managing director stated that he had provided details for the calculations to the company's accountant. The managing director agreed that the calculations were arbitrary but that they understated the actual inspection cost. He said that it was a fair calculation and in "fairly close proximity to what it cost to do the inspection." In relation to 1983 he said that the operation had become a little more efficient. This I understand to be his reason for the reduction of the service cost estimate to $23 per inspection. The total individual unit cost for pre-treatment work in 1982 and 1983 tax years was $50-$55 over the relevant period.

The specific matters to which Mr. Slater referred in his submissions included:

Motor vehicle costs: Three bases were referred. The calculation refers to a commercial traveller's rate, a petrol rate and a rate per kilometre. The rates may overlap. Mr. Slater submitted that the taxpayer company's managing director did not know or did not care whether these related to the same outlay. Mr. Slater described this as a casual and arbitrary approach.

Telephone costs: The taxpayer paid the whole of the telephone calls of certain employees. This allocation of costs to the service work was in his submission not shown to be on any particular sensible basis.

Weekly inspections: 21 weekly visits are allowed as a basis for calculation in exhibit A8. Mr. Slater submits that this does not accord with the taxpayer company's actual experience. The taxpayer's employees were in actual fact able to make up to 40 inspections a week. Mr. Slater submitted that it was not a reasonable or a realistic estimate to divide the weekly cost by 21 visits rather than by 40 or some figure much closer to 40 than 21. In the result he submits that the method of estimation does not allow the income to be estimated properly so far as the requirements of the ITAA are concerned.

Mr. Slater also submitted that a far more accurate guide to the costs to the taxpayer company of future services is to be discovered in the terms of the sale of the taxpayer company's business to the Y Co. in October 1985. The persons associated with that company were former employees of the taxpayer company. The contract exhibit A5 makes provision for all future inspections in the 'sum of $200,000. This was in relation to some 49,000 contracts to carry out service. The individual rate is $4.08 per inspection. I do not consider that I can accept that figure as an accurate measure of the costs of future inspection. It does show that was the cost for the purposes of the sale then underway that the taxpayer company was prepared to put on the inspections. Mr.Slater contrasts the amount of $200,000 with the amount of unearned income over 4-1/2 years claimed by the taxpayer company. The sum of $200,000 is considerably less.

Mr. Slater goes on to make further submissions relating to the way in which costs were arrived at in regard to future services. He refers to the process adopted in income year 1983 to arrive at the cost. As he describes it an amount of $25 was a starting figure for future inspection costs relating to income year 1983. Thereafter calculations were used to arrive at a figure of $33 which was then adjusted down to $22. The taxpayer company's accountant denied that this was an arbitrary exercise. Mr. Slater made further criticisms of the budget which was done to arrive at the cost of future inspections.

The conclusion which the Tribunal is asked to draw is that the estimated figures for the cost of future inspections in relation to fees received in income years 1982 and 1983 is unrealistic - the estimates made by the taxpayer company are simply to provide an adjustment to taxable income which would ensure that the taxpayer company did not have a liability to pay income tax in the two income years relevant to this review. Mr. Slater submits that the inference to be drawn is that the calculations for raising provision for unearned income was affected not by any real appraisal of what the costs involved were but by the prospect of having -or perhaps not having - to pay tax.

I accept the submissions of Mr. Slater on this matter. I accept that the relevant facts are as Mr. Slater submits them to be. I accept his arguments critical of the estimating process that was used by the taxpayer company.

I am not able to find on the balance of probabilities what amount should be set aside out of the fees received for pre-treatment work in income years 1982 and 1983 in regard to any warranty or guarantee given or inspection service promised to be performed 10 years after the carrying out of the pre-treatment work. The applicant taxpayer has not satisfied the onus placed upon it by s190(b) of the ITAA. For this reason I would also dismiss the applications before me.

PENALTY:

In 1982 tax year there is an amount of $8,216 additional tax for an incorrect return and in 1983 amounts of $3,152 additional tax for a late return and an amount of $5,754 additional tax for an incorrect return, assessed against the taxpayer company.

The Commissioner's argument before me was that in the returns for 1982 and 1983 tax years the taxpayer company had omitted assessable income. The omission in both years related to the income that was said by the taxpayer company to be unearned income received. Section 226 of the ITAA as it stood in 1982 and in 1983 made provision for the assessment of additional tax where the taxpayer omits from his return any assessable income.

Mr. Evans for the taxpayer company relied upon "note 11 -unearned income" which appears at p. 17 of exhibit A9 of the tax return for 1982 and "note 11 - unearned income" which appears in exhibit A10 the tax return for 1983.

Save for individual amounts and details the notes are in similar terms. I set out as a sample that which appears in the return for 1982:

"Note 11 - Unearned Income.

During the year the Company entered into 4,028 contracts which provided for services to be rendered in the financial year ended 30 June, 1992.

These future services have been invoiced during the current year. It is expected that the cost of performing the future services is $25 per contract totalling $100,700 which amount has been excluded from income in arriving at the results of the company for the year ended 30 June 1982."

Mr. Evans in his submissions described this as an unequivocal factual disclosure that brought relevant matters clearly to the Commissioner's attention.

Mr. Slater submitted that this was not an example of an error of classification as occurred in North Coast Grazing Pty. Ltd. v. Commissioner of Taxation (1987) 15 FCR 104; 87ATC at 4553. In a passage which is reported in 15 FCR at 109 and 87ATC at 4557 the Full Federal Court (Bowen CJ, Neaves and Burchett JJ) said:

"It must be stated that in view of the findings of the learned trial judge this is not a case in which one is able to view with any degree of enthusiasm the disclosures made by the taxpayer in its returns. However, it appears to our minds that a taxpayer, who actually includes the precise item in his return which turns out to be assessable income, and indeed draws attention to it, notwithstanding he claims in one form or another that it is not assessable income as, for example, by classifying it as or asserting it to be a capital receipt, has not omitted it from his return notwithstanding it turns out to be assessable income. In our view, s226(2) is dealing with an objective position, either omission of items which are assessable income from the return, that is leaving them out, or stating expenditure to be an amount in excess of that actually incurred, whether in respect of a claimed deduction or a claimed rebate. It is not, in our opinion, directed at the degree of disclosure which the taxpayer makes in his return where he includes the item."

There is a further passage in the court's judgment (in 15FCR at 110; 87ATR at 4558) to which I refer:

"It appears to us that to construe s.226(2) as requiring the word 'omit' to be read as applying where an item has in fact not been left out but indeed has been included, or to treat assessable income as having been omitted where additional information constituting sufficient information to enable the Commissioner to arrive at a conclusion about its accessibility has not also been included, it to place too much weight altogether on a fairly simply word."

Has there been an omission in this case? In my view, the taxpayer company by note 11 to 1982 and 1983 returns did not provide "additional information constituting sufficient information to enable the Commissioner to arrive at a conclusion about its accessibility." The taxpayer company declared the income as unearned. The taxpayer company did not disclose that its contract in each case was with a builder who in each case was building premises on another's land or on land to be disposed of by the builder.

The taxpayer company did not disclose facts relating to any assertion that it had a continuing obligation in respect of the fee which had been invoiced. Disclosure of facts of this sort would have assisted the Commissioner to arrive at a conclusion about the "unearned" income's accessibility.

In applying the last mentioned passage from the judgment in North Coast Grazing Pty. Ltd. v. The Commissioner of Taxation (supra) I am satisfied that an omission of assessable income has occurred in both 1982 and 1983 returns and I reject the appeals against additional tax for omission of assessable income.

In respect of both tax years I affirm the decisions under review.
