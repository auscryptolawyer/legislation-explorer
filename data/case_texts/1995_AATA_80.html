QT94/179 and Commissioner of Taxation [1995] AATA 80; 95 ATC 208; (1995) 30 ATR 1224 (27 March 1995)

Last Updated: 11 February 2009

TAXATION : Income tax - Assessable Income - connection to employment or services rendered - salvage reward - payable only on successful salvage - taxpayer employee of salvage sub-contractor - classic "windfall gain" - not assessable



Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 : Ss.6, 25. 26

Taxation Administration Act 1953 : S. 14ZZJ

REASONS FOR DECISION



Before: Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Date: 27 March 1995

Place: Brisbane



By letter dated 25 May 1994 the respondent notified an objection decision in which the applicant's objection against an amended assessment for the year of income ended 30 June 1993 was disallowed. On 1 July 1994 the applicant filed an application for review of that decision in this Tribunal.



2. The primary relevant statutory provision is subsection 25(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). So far as is relevant subsection 25(1) provides that the assessable income of a taxpayer shall include, in the case of a resident, the gross income derived directly or indirectly from all sources whether in or out of Australia, which is not exempt income and certain other exclusions which are not relevant in the facts of the present case. The respondent also relies on paragraph 26(e) of the Act which reads as follows:-



"26 The assessable income of a taxpayer shall include -

(e) the value to the taxpayer of all allowances, gratuities, compensations, benefits, bonuses and premiums allowed, given or granted to him in respect of, or for or in relation directly or indirectly to, any employment of or services rendered by him, whether so allowed, given or granted in money, goods, land, meals, sustenance, the use of premises or quarters or otherwise, not being -

. . . "



3. At the hearing before the Tribunal the applicant conducted his own case and Mr Moynihan of counsel appeared for the respondent Commissioner. The documents prepared pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 were put into evidence as the T-documents. Certain other documents were marked as exhibits and Exhibit C is a video recording which gives a graphic representation of the events which give rise to the present dispute.



4. Exhibit 1 is a Statement of Agreed Facts as agreed between the parties, which I will set out in full subject to editing to comply with section 14ZZJ of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 so as not to enable the identification of the applicant in these proceedings. The Statement of Agreed Facts, as edited by the Tribunal, reads as follows:



"The applicant . . . was a member of the crew of the oil rig tender Lady Kathleen on 21 July 1991.



2. In July 1991 the oil tanker, Kirki, was bound for Kwinana from the Arabian Gulf. It was laden with a cargo of Murban light crude oil. The tanker was an elderly ship and it encountered severe weather conditions as it neared Western Australia. On 21 July 1991, after several hours of gale force winds, the vessel's forepeak was found to be leaking and fires broke out. The main engine was stopped and a MAYDAY message was broadcast at about 0300 hours. A request was made for assistance from any ship within 5 hours steaming time of the Kirki. An airliner passing overhead saw the fire and relayed the position to the ground authorities.



3. The Lady Kathleen, upon which the taxpayer was employed, is an anchor handling tug and supply vessel. At the time in question, the Lady Kathleen was on charter to an oil company. She was working as an anti-pollution vessel close to the semi-submersible rigs Southern Cross, about 135 kilometres from the Kirki. The Mayday message was relayed to the Lady Kathleen by Perth Radio. Captain Farstad, the Master of the Lady Kathleen obtained permission from the Southern Cross to leave the rig and sailed for the casualty's position at 0440 hours, 21 July 1991. He decided that lives were at risk and it was his duty to go.



4. At about the time the Lady Kathleen sailed, the Australian Maritime Safety Authority informed Captain Ross, the managing director of United Salvage Ltd (the Head Contractors of the salvage operation), of the circumstances of the Kirki. Captain Ross immediately made arrangements to mobilise salvage personnel and equipment. He contacted the Kirki's managers through his London agents to offer Lloyd's Form. This was agreed to by the Kirki's managers. A Lloyd's Form is effectively a salvage agreement based on the principle of "no cure no gain". Under a Lloyd's Form, if a vessel is salvaged the salvors are entitled to a payment (a salvage reward). If the vessel is lost, no entitlement arises.



5. By 0800 hours, P & O Maritime Services Pty Ltd, the owners of the Lady Kathleen, entered into the salvage agreement as a subcontractor (in the Lloyd's Form) to United Salvage Ltd, the Head Contractors (Document T2). Helicopters arrived at the Kirki and started to take men off. The Kirki was drifting towards the shore. By 1000 hours, the Lady Kathleen was less than ten miles from the Kirki which was within sight of land. Captain Farstad was concerned that the casualty would strand in Jurien Bay within a matter of hours. This would cause considerable damage to the environment. The vessel, too, would become a total loss and so would her cargo and bunkers.



6. By noon all the crew were lifted off the Kirki and taken ashore. The Lady Kathleen started to prepare to make a towage connection. Three seamen (not the applicant) volunteered to jump across from the Lady Kathleen to the Kirki to enable the ship to be taken in tow. They successfully judged the relative movements of the two vessels and jumped across the chasm. The towing connection was made and, at 1500 hours, the towage began.



7. The Lady Kathleen turned on to a westerly heading and towed the Kirki slowly so as to ensure that the towage connection remained intact. In case it did not, two of the three seamen who boarded earlier remained on board and kept watch. The State authorities required the Kirki to be taken more than 100 miles off the coast.



8. Other vessels organised by the salvors gathered near the casualty and the salvage personnel boarded the Kirki. For the next two days the Kirki was towed in large circles off Jurien Bay so that men and material could be brought on board her. The salvage operation was managed by Captain Hancox, a very experienced salvage master who was despatched from the east cost of Australia with a team of divers and mechanics.



9. On 24 July 1991, the Lady Kathleen started to work her way 1000 miles to the north, to Glomar Shoal, at the insistence of the WA Government. The Lady Kathleen was replaced at about 0800 hours on 25 July 1991 by the Lady Elizabeth, another tug owned by P & O. The tow wire was disconnected from the Lady Kathleen and reconnected to the Lady Elizabeth. The Lady Kathleen proceeded to Fremantle before returning to her station beside the Southern Cross.



10. The towage to the north continued. The salvage crew improved the condition of the ship and got her equipment to function. They transferred the cargo from the Kirki to another tanker despite poor weather conditions. By 19 August 1991, the transhipment was completed. The salvors began a program of demobilisation. Helicopters arrived to remove personnel. The Lady Elizabeth towed the Kirki to a position off Bear Rock. All the salvage personnel went ashore by 21 August 1991.



11. A detailed factual account of the circumstances covered by paragraphs 2-10 above is contained in paragraphs 1 - 54 of the reasons of the Lloyd's arbitrator who was required to determine the amount of the salvage award to be awarded to the salvors. The case is simply referred to as the Kirki decision. [The arbitrator's] reasons are attached as Attachment 1 of the Agreed Documents and for the purposes of this matter paragraphs 1 - 54 of the reasons are agreed by both parties as being an accurate account of the material facts involving the salvage of the oil tanker Kirki in July and August 1991.



12. (Deleted pursuant to an order of the Tribunal made under section 35 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975).



13. The Lady Kathleen was engaged for 6 days, the Sonya Viking for 3 days and the Lady Elizabeth for nearly a month. The Lady Kathleen was the vessel which responded promptly and her crew were in real danger when coming alongside the Kirki at the time she had been abandoned. The Lady Kathleen provided assistance at a critical time. Four individuals (not the applicant) provided exceptionally meritorious services. In recognition of their services in helping to salvage the Kirki, the crew of the Lady Kathleen, including the applicant, was awarded a Group Citation for Bravery (see Document T5 of the section 37 statement. Also attached as part of Document T5 are various letters congratulating the applicant for his part in the salvage operation).



14. The Lady Elizabeth provided her services in arduous conditions and over a long period, but at the time of her contribution, the Head Contractors were fully mobilised, and the salvors and ships brought in by them played a substantial part in the operation. The Sonya Viking made only a small substantive contribution to the ultimate success and her Captain's responsibilities were not exacting.



15. [The arbitrator's] opinion contained the distribution of the award between P & O and each crew member. P & O honoured the opinion and each crew member involved received part of the salvage award as determined by the arbitrator. The applicant's share of the award was US$16,482 which converted to A$23,381 (Documents T6 -T9 of the section 37 statement).



16. The applicant was at the time of the salvage (and still is) an employee of Australian Offshore Services, a division of P & O Maritime Services Pty Ltd. At the time of the salvage the applicant's work conditions were covered by the Maritime Industry Offshore Oil and Gas Operations Award 1991, a copy of which is attached as Attachment 2 of the Agreed Documents.



5. The factual situation of what happened on 21 July 1991 is well summarised in the Governor-General's citation for bravery which is part of document T5 in the documents before the Tribunal. That citation for bravery was awarded to the members of the crew of the Lady Kathleen, who were on board on 21 July 1991, including the applicant in these proceedings. The citation reads as follows:



"In July 1991 the ship's company of the oil rig tender 'Lady Kathleen' displayed high courage which, aided by initiative and exemplary seamanship, enabled them to avert a major pollution disaster by removing a stricken oil tanker to safety. On the morning of 21 July 1991 'Lady Kathleen' responded to an emergency call from a burning oil tanker which had lost its bow in heavy seas off the reefs north west of Wedge Island. When 'Lady Kathleen' arrived the tanker was spilling oil and being driven shorewards. Operating in conditions of extreme hazard and difficulty, with strong winds and heavy seas and in imminent danger of conflagration, 'Lady Kathleen' closed on the tanker to pick up a useable line streaming from its stern. A towing attempt was frustrated when the line parted. Returning to the danger zone, 'Lady Kathleen' lay alongside the tanker and succeeded in putting a line party aboard, enabling the connection of a towing rig. Despite the difficult conditions 'Lady Kathleen' was able to hold the tanker until wind and sea abated sufficiently to enable her to tow the vessel further off shore. Throughout this demanding action, the ship's company of 'Lady Kathleen' maintained a calm and purposeful dedication to the operation and performed their dangerous tasks with courage and enterprise."



6. As already noted, a detailed explanation of the facts surrounding the incident, is set out in paragraphs 1 to 54 of the opinion of the Lloyd's arbitrator. It is not necessary for the purposes of deciding this matter that I set out herein paragraphs 1 to 54 but there is no reason to doubt that all the facts as set out in the arbitrator's reasons are anything other than a true statement of the facts surrounding the payment of the award to the crew of the Lady Kathleen and I so find.



7. Document T/2 is a copy of an agreement between United Salvage Limited as the contractor and P & O Maritime Services Pty Ltd trading as Australian Offshore Services as the subcontractor. The agreement was made on 23 July 1991 and relates to the salvage of the Kirki including her freight, bunkers and stores under a Lloyds' standard form of salvage agreement "no cure - no pay" of the same date. Under the subcontract P & O Maritime Services Pty Ltd provided the personnel, equipment and services which were required by United Salvage Limited during the salvage of the Kirki and for the first six days of the period of the salvage those services were in the main provided by the crew of the Lady Kathleen and the Lady Kathleen herself. As the arbitrator makes clear in his opinion the salvage of the Kirki took considerably longer than the six days that the Lady Kathleen and her crew were involved and it is the fact that the crew of the Lady Kathleen participated in what was clearly the most dangerous part of the salvage operations but did not participate in what might be thought to be the more arduous part of those operations in getting the disabled Kirki into port at Singapore. That was the responsibility, in the main, of the sister ship, the Lady Elizabeth.



8. It is clear from the terms of the contract that P & O Maritime Services Pty Ltd was only entitled to receive remuneration for the provision of their personnel, equipment and services if the salvage was successful. If the salvage had not succeeded then nothing would have been payable to P & O Maritime Services Pty Ltd and it follows that nothing would have been payable to the crew of the Lady Kathleen. Throughout the period that the crew of the Lady Kathleen was engaged in the salvage operations, the crew was paid its normal remuneration under the terms of employment with P & O Maritime Services Pty Ltd.



9. The crew performed its services in relation to the salvage of the Kirki in July 1991 but did not receive its share of the reward from the salvage until the end of March 1993. On 31 March 1993 the applicant received an amount of $23,381 from P & O Maritime Services Pty Ltd and received a further amount of $474 on 22 April 1993. Yet a further amount in relation to interest was received by the applicant on or about 3 September 1993 in an amount of $2,057. Although this last amount was included in the applicant's assessment for the year of income ended 30 June 1993, it has since been excised from that assessment by an amended assessment. The amount of $474 for interest is not in dispute before the Tribunal.



10. It might be surprising to realise, as counsel for the respondent told the Tribunal, that there is no precedent decision in relation to amounts received by crews as a reward for participation in the successful salvage operations under Australian law. As a consequence counsel relied on the considerable number of authorities dealing with the question of income for the purposes of the Act and also to a lesser extent dealing with the operation of paragraph 26(e) of the Act.



11. I mean no disrespect to the applicant when I say that the applicant's argument before this Tribunal had a somewhat simplistic basis. In essence his case was that the payment made to the crew was a reward in relation to an extraordinary event that had not occurred previously during the applicant's 20 years at sea and was unlikely to occur again during his lifetime. He, in effect, attributed the payment with the character of a windfall which had come to the crew, including himself, unexpectedly and not because they performed their duties as seamen on the days in July 1991 when they effected the tow of the Kirki. The applicant pointed out that he had been paid his normal remuneration by his employer and that that was all he was entitled to claim against the employer in relation to the services rendered at that time. Although he did not say so in so many words it follows, in my view, that the applicant's case must adopt a proposition that the payment was made under the terms of the salvage agreement and depended entirely on the successful completion of the salvage operations; it being clear from the contract that if the salvage had not been successful, nothing would have been paid to the crew of the Lady Kathleen and others who participated in the salvage operation.



12. Counsel for the respondent made detailed submissions summarised as follows. The payment received by the applicant was paid by P & O Maritime Services Pty Ltd during the course of the applicant's employment which was the essential feature in the applicant being entitled to participate in a proportion of the amount paid to the salvors. The crew used the employer's boat to effect the tow of the Kirki and were acting within the course of their contract of service with the employer when they did so. The payment made to the applicant by his employer, P & O Maritime Services Pty Ltd, was due under the contract of salvage for services rendered by the applicant. That payment was intended by the applicant's employer to be remuneration for services rendered by him during the salvage of the Kirki and the amount of payment was directly related to, and calculated by reference to the service or function performed by the applicant as a member of the crew of the Lady Kathleen. Counsel asserted that there was a legal liability upon the employer to pay the employees who formed the crew of the Lady Kathleen and who rendered the services and that they had a legal right for receiving remuneration for providing services in the successful salvage of the Kirki. To perform such a salvage operation was not an unusual or isolated act and it was not outside the scope of the applicant's employment with P & O Maritime Services Pty Ltd.



13. I turn now to what will be a somewhat lengthy consideration of the authorities in relation to assessable income within the terms of the Act.



14. It is clear enough that section 25 provides for the inclusion in the assessable income of the taxpayer the gross income derived from all sources, in the case of a resident, but the Act does not provide a definition of the meaning of the word "income". "Assessable income" is defined in subsection 6(1) of the Act so as to mean the amounts which under the provisions of the Act are included in the assessable income and the definitions of income from personal exertion and income from property also contained in subsection 6(1) merely provide a basis for the classification of income into two classes but do not really assist in the context of this case in defining the meaning of the word "income" which appears in each of those definitions. By contrast section 26 does set out a number of matters which are to be included as assessable income by providing for specific items which, as has been held on many occasions, may or may not fall within the general meaning of the word "income".



15. The general proposition in relation to income having its ordinary meaning is explained by the dicta of Dixon CJ and Williams J in their joint judgment in FC of T v Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 CLR 540 at 555 where their Honours said:



"Section 25 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936-1943 provides that the assessable income of the taxpayer shall include the gross income derived directly or indirectly, according to his residence from all sources or from all sources in Australia. Section 6 defines 'assessable income' to mean all the amounts which, under the provisions of the Act, are included in the assessable income. As a result of s.25 what is gross income derived directly or indirectly from all sources or all sources in Australia, as the case may be, depends upon what is income. The Commonwealth Act, unlike the Income Tax Act 1952 (15 & 16 Geo. VI. & 1 Eliz. II. c. 10) (Imp.), does not make the question of what is assessable or taxable income depend upon a series of express provisions dealing with the various kinds of income, such as those in schedules A, B, C, D and E of the British Act. It begins with the general conception of gross income and specifies in s.23 what is exempt and in s.26 and other sections particular classes of income that are to be included. Sometimes these classes of income appear to be specified simply for greater certainty, sometimes because they do not fall within the natural understanding of gross income, as, for example, in the cases mentioned in s.36. The definition in s.6 of 'income from personal exertion' or 'income derived from personal exertion' has always been used as a possible guide or test in cases where the question is whether a particular receipt is income or not. It is true that the definition is concerned only or chiefly with the difference, for the purposes of the rates of tax, between income from property and income from personal exertion, but, where any of the expressions contained in the definition are relevant, it is logical enough to use them as an indication that a given receipt is income."



16. Dixon's case involved the makeup of pay to a former employee while that former employee was enlisted for service with the Australian Imperial Forces during World War II. Mr Dixon's former employer had, by a voluntary act, decided to make up his pay to an amount which was equivalent to his remuneration as an employee prior to his enlistment in the Army. The issue before the High Court was whether that voluntary payment, which was paid by the former employer, was income to be assessed within the terms of section 25. The Court decided, by majority, (Dixon CJ, Williams and Fullagar JJ, McTiernan and Webb JJ dissenting) that the amount paid by the former employer was assessable income within the terms of section 25. It is clear from the report of this case that the matter was very fully argued before the High Court. In the course of their judgment Dixon CJ and Williams J stated that if payments are really incidental to an employment it is unimportant whether they come from the employer or from somebody else and are obtained as of right or merely as a recognised incident of the employment or work (at p.556). Their Honours cite authority for this proposition and go on to say that the employment of Mr Dixon was as a soldier but there was some expectation that the former employer would provide an addition to the income derived as a soldier and it seems their Honours were of the view that there was a fairly general understanding that that was the case; and once again their Honours cite authority to rely upon that proposition albeit a proposition as to fact. Their Honours gave weight to the fact that the character of the amounts received was to be determined in part by the fact that there were regular periodical payments made to Mr Dixon while he was a soldier. Their Honours concluded that because the £104 was an expected periodical payment arising out of circumstances which attended the war service undertaken by Mr Dixon and, because it formed part of the receipts upon which he depended for the regular expenditure upon himself and his dependents and was paid to him for that purpose, it had the character of income and formed part of his gross income within the meaning of section 25 of the Act (at p.557).



17. Fullagar J arrived at a decision that the amount in question was assessable income by a different route to Dixon CJ and Williams J. The essence of the reasons of Fullagar J are set out at pages 567-68 of the report as follows:



"It seems to me that the appellant's receipts from Macdonald, Hamilton & Co. must be regarded as having the character of income. They were regular periodical payments - a matter which has been regarded in the cases as having some importance in determining whether particular receipts possess the character of income or capital in the hands of the recipient, see eg. Seymour v. Reed (1927) A.C. 554 at p.570 and Atkinson v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1951] HCA 64; (1951) 84 C.L.R. 298. This consideration, while not unimportant, is not decisive. What is, to my mind, decisive is that the expressed object and the actual effect of the payments made was to make an addition to the earnings, the undoubted income, of the respondent. What the employing firm decided to do, and what it really did, in relation to the respondent and others in the same position, was 'to make up the difference between their present rate of wages and the amount they will receive'. What is paid is not salary or remuneration, and it is not paid in respect of or in relation to any employment of the recipient. But it is intended to be, and is in fact, a substitute for - the equivalent pro tanto of - the salary or wages which would have been earned and paid if the enlistment had not taken place. As such, it must be income, even though it is paid voluntarily and there is not even a moral obligation to continue making the payments. It acquires the character of that for which it is substituted and that to which it is added."



It will be apparent that their Honours, the Chief Justice and Williams J decided in favour of the revenue because of payments to Mr Dixon being of a regular periodical payment as part of his income as a soldier and payments upon which he had come to depend. That, in their Honours' view characterised the amounts received as income in the hands of the taxpayer. Fullagar J also placed weight upon the fact of regular periodical payments but also looked to the expressed object and effect which was to make an addition to the taxpayer's earnings as a substitute for his salary that he would have otherwise earned and his Honour took the view that because it was a substitute for salary, the payments received by the taxpayer acquired that character.



18. In dissent McTiernan J found that the payments had been made under a scheme to give financial relief to those who suffered financially because they enlisted. His Honour found that the payments were made voluntarily and without obligation. Following a line of English decisions, his Honour decided that the payments did not have the character of income. Also in dissent Webb J characterised the payments as a reward given for enlistment and once again found that they did not have the character of income.



19. It will be apparent from this analysis of the decision in Dixon that the decision of the High Court is of no assistance in deciding the question which is before the Tribunal.



20. The respondent also relies upon the decision of the Privy Council in F C of T v Squatting Investment Co Limited [1954] UKPCHCA 2; (1954) 88 CLR 413, a decision of the Privy Council on appeal from a decision of the High Court (reported [1953] HCA 13; (1953) 86 CLR 570) which had found that an amount received by the company as its share of a distribution by the Wool Realization Commission was not assessable income in the hands of the company which carried on business as a wool grower. The High Court decision was, in effect, reversed in the Privy Council. It is of interest to note that the hearing of the Squatting Investment case took place in the High Court after the hearing in Dixon but before the decision in Dixon was handed down. Four of the Judges who participated in Dixon also participated in the Squatting Investment case.



21. The headnote at 88 CLR 413-4 sets out the basic facts as follows:



"In 1939 a Wool Purchase Arrangement was made between the Governments of Great Britain and the Commonwealth of Australia, whereby the former Government agreed to purchase all wool produced in Australia for the period of the war and one wool year thereafter, except wool required for the purpose of woollen manufacture in Australia. It was a term of the arrangement that the two Governments would divide equally any profit arising from the resale by the Government of Great Britain outside the United Kingdom of wool bought pursuant to the arrangement. To give effect to this arrangement the National Security (Wool) Regulations were notified which set up a Central Wool Committee charged with the administration of the regulations and empowered under reg. 30(2) to deal in its absolute discretion with any moneys received by it under or in consequence of the arrangement. Under the regulations the sale of wool was by appraisement, upon the completion of which, the property in every parcel of wool passed to the Commonwealth and the suppliers were paid the whole of the compensation money to which they were legally entitled. From the inception of the arrangement the Central Wool Committee contemplated that any profit which the Government of the Commonwealth received from the Government of Great Britain, in respect of wool sold outside the United Kingdom would be divided between persons who supplied wool shorn from the living sheep and that suppliers of skin wool would not participate. For this purpose wool was classified as 'participating' and 'non-participating'. In 1945, in order to dispose of large stocks of carryover wool held by the United Kingdom under the arrangement, the Governments of Great Britain and the Commonwealth agreed upon a Disposals Plan under which these stocks of wool were to be transferred into a joint ownership of the two Governments, and all wool subsequently acquired pursuant to the scheme was to be held in joint ownership. The ultimate balance of profit or loss arising from the venture was to be shared equally between the Governments of Great Britain and the Commonwealth. This wool was to be held and disposed of by a joint organization to be incorporated as a private company in England and to have an Australian subsidiary. The Australian subsidiary of this company was the Australian Wool Realization Commission, set up by the Wool Realization Act 1945. Section 9 of that Act provided that the Wool Realization Commission should be substituted for the Central Wool Committee and should have, and perform, all the duties, and should have, and might exercise, all the powers, authorities and functions of the Central Wool Committee under, inter alia, the National Security (Wool) Regulations. The Wool Realization (Distribution of Profits) Act 1948 made provision for the distribution, both interim and final, of the profit accruing to the Commonwealth as a result of the activities of the Australian Wool Realization Committee."



In 1949, the taxpayer company which continued to carry on business as a woolgrower, received by way of distribution a part of the profit accruing to the Commonwealth. This was not withstanding that the company had been paid the whole of the compensation to which it was entitled for wool supplied during 1939 to 1946.



22. The advice of the Privy Council was to reverse the decision of the High Court. It held that the distribution received by the company was for wool supplied and the amount of the distribution was assessable income in the terms of section 25 of the Act.



23. The reasons for decision appear at page 432 as follows:



"The respondents were in business as wool suppliers at all material times, and the payment was made to them, not because of any personal qualities, but because they, among others, supplied participating wool. They supplied the wool in the course of their trade and this further payment was made to them because they supplied it. In the present case the respondents were still trading when the payment was made. It was in their hands a trade receipt of an income nature. Their Lordships express no opinion as to the proper description of the payment in the hands of a payee who was not trading at the date of the payment, as this case is not before the Board."



The qualification in the last sentence becomes very relevant in this case.



24. In coming to its decision the Privy Council essentially upheld the dissenting judgments of Fullagar and Kitto JJ in the High Court. Fullagar J concluded at page 623 that "the moneys were paid in respect of wool supplied in the course of a business carried on by the taxpayer and were therefore proceeds of the taxpayer's business.



25. In coming to his decision that the receipt in question was a receipt on income account Kitto J discussed the cases at pages 633-4. After referring to the requirement of the scheme that a person's entitlement was referable to the person having supplied particular wool for appraisement his Honour went on to say:



"This being so, it may seem somewhat odd that support for the contention that the amount received is not income is claimed from the well-known line of decisions upon the question whether gratuitous payments are assessable as profits arising out of the recipient's employment or by reason of his office, within the meaning of English taxing statutes. The distinction those decisions have drawn between taxable and non-taxable gifts is the distinction between, on the one hand, gifts made in relation to some activity or occupation of the donee of an income-producing character, such gifts being variously described as accruing to the donee in virtue of his office (Herbert v McQuade (1902) 2 K.B. 631, at p.649), or as remuneration (Beynon v Thorpe (1928) 14 Tax Cas. I, at p.11; Seymour v Reed (1927) A.C. 554, at p.559), or in respect of his past services (Beynon v Thorpe (1928) 14 Tax Cas., at p.14), or substantially in respect of his services (Blakiston v Cooper [1908] UKHL 1; (1909) A.C. 104, at p.107); and, on the other hand, gifts referable to the attitude of the donor personally to the donee personally, such as those which have been called mere gifts or presents made to the donee on personal grounds (Seymour v Reed (1927) A.C. at p.559), mere donations (Stedeford v Beloe (1932) A.C. 388, at p.391), gifts moved by the remembrance of past services already sufficiently remunerated as services in themselves (Beynon v Thorpe (1928) 14 Tax Cas. at p.14), payments peculiarly due to the personal qualities of the particular recipient or personal gifts as marks of esteem and respect (Blakiston v Cooper (1909) A.C. at pp.107,108). The application of the distinction thus drawn ought surely to be that amounts such as that now in question are to be regarded as income if they were received in relation to wool supplied for appraisement in the course of a business carried on for profit. The Act makes it plain that these amounts are made payable in respect of the wool which was supplied and because it was supplied; not because of any admiration for the personal qualities of the suppliers or because of gratitude for their having supplied wool for which adequate payment was considered to have been made already."



26. It will be clear on the above analysis that the ultimate decision in Squatting Investment depended upon wool being supplied in the course of business so that the distribution received was also an amount received in the course of business. This is consistent with subsequent decisions of the High Court, see AL Hamblin Equipment Pty Ltd v FCT [1974] HCA 1; (1974) 2 ALR 347 at 358 and FCT v Myer Emporium Ltd [1987] HCA 18; (1987) 71 ALR 28 at 32.



27. However, in the High Court, the majority had held that the distribution did not form part of the taxpayer's assessable income because the amount of the distribution was in the nature of a gift. McTiernan and Williams JJ decided that the payments were a voluntary gift and were of that character notwithstanding that the recipients were those who had supplied wool within the terms of the legislation. It followed that such amounts could not be characterised as income according to ordinary usages and concepts.



28. On the other hand Webb J gave more weight to the fact that the Court was not dealing with recurrent payments at pages 612-3 as follows:



"But to the Commissioner's claim that it is assessable income the answer is, I think, that the term 'personal gift' was used to denote that precise quality; that its meaning is certain and not indefinite, is constant and not variable; and that it excludes income in the ordinary acceptation of the term i.e. as the term is used in s.25 of the Income Tax Assessment Act. The quality of personal gift was not attributed to the Easter offerings to the clergy in Blakeston v. Cooper [1908] UKHL 1; (1909) A.C. 104 and those offerings were held to be income; it was attributed to the gift to the cricketer in Seymour v. Reed (1927) A.C. 554 and it was held that the gift was not income. In this regard I can see no difference between income from employment or from an office and income from a business. I realize that income may be assessable under s.25 although it is not from any of those sources. In Commissioner of Taxation v. Dixon [1952] HCA 65; (1952) 86 C.L.R. 540 this Court held that gifts that were not derived from such sources were nevertheless income under s.25. That was because they were periodical and were for the maintenance of the donee and his dependants. That case indicates that even such undoubted personal gifts as charitable payments made e.g. to a pauper in a hospital or other institution for his maintenance therein are income within s.25. They are not income from personal exertion or from property, apart from the statutory definitions, but they are still to be regarded as income within the ordinary meaning of the term. However, that is because the payments are recurrent, a consideration which had weight with Lord Phillimore in Seymour v. Reed (supra). Here, however, we are dealing not with recurrent payments but with a single payment which moreover was not made for the maintenance of a donee and his dependants, as the payments in Dixon's Case (supra) were assumed to be."



29. In FCT v Harris 80 ATC 4238 the Federal Court (Deane J dissenting) decided that an ex gratia payment made to a retired bank officer by his former employer was not assessable income. Bowen CJ made a careful examination of the authorities and continued at pages 4242-3 as follows:



"Turning to the $450.00 received by Mr Harris from the Bank, the first question which arises (applying the test generally applied to determine whether a gift is income) is whether that receipt was the product of his employment with the Bank. It is clear that in one sense his former employment was a cause of the payment being made, that is, in the sense that if he had not been an employee he would not have been a pensioner and would not have received the payment. However, on the evidence, the receipt clearly was not the product of that employment in the sense of being produced by his service in that employment; the amount was in no way related to the length or quality of his service with the Bank. It depended upon other considerations concerning his pension.



If one seeks to apply the reasons of Dixon CJ and Williams J in Dixon's case, it is clear the payment received by Mr Harris was not periodical within the income year and was not incidental to any current employment. Nor did it form part of the receipts upon which he depended for regular expenditure upon himself and his dependants. If one seeks to apply the reasoning of Fullagar J in Dixon's case, the question is whether the payment should be regarded as being in substitution for or an addition to earnings of a revenue character. In this case, in contrast with the facts of Dixon's case, the payments were not in any sense in substitution for salary or wages forgone or lost. On the other hand, they were intended by the Bank to be an addition to a pension which, although it does not constitute earnings, is a class of receipt which is of an income character. This raises one of the critical questions in the case: is the fact that the payment was intended to be supplementary to the pension which was regarded as depreciated by the forces of inflation a situation sufficient to require it to be regarded as having the same character as the pension and to be treated as an income receipt? Certainly, as I have pointed out, it was not received or relied upon by Mr Harris for regular expenditure upon himself and his dependants, nor was there any assurance as there was in a qualified form in Dixon's case, that the payment would be regularly forthcoming as a periodic addition to his income. In the present case, the pension was under r.17 of the pensions scheme payable fortnightly or monthly. But the ex gratia payment by the Bank was made once a year. It was not periodic within any single income tax year.



Furthermore, in the circumstances of Dixon's case, the payment was made by the taxpayer's former employers because of his enlistment in the military forces as an addition to his military pay in order to bring that pay up to the level which he has enjoyed in his civilian employment. In this case, the payment made to Mr Harris by the Bank was not designed to bring the pension up to any particular figure; it was simply an ex gratia payment made in accordance with the bounty of the Bank as one lump sum during the income year to assist him because of the effects of inflation.



It appears to me that the circumstances of this case are insufficient to lead to the conclusion that a gift, not normally to be regarded as being of an income nature, should be so regarded because the Bank as payer intended it to be, and it in fact was, a supplement to the taxpayer's pension."



30. Fisher J found that in the hands of Mr Harris the payment in question did not have the character of income (p.4247) and concluded at page 4248 the payment could properly be regarded as a single lump sum gift and the other relevant circumstances did not justify characterising it as a payment of income.



31. Deane J dissented. His Honour found that there was a relevant connection with the former employment and that the payment was one of a series of annual payments so as to give the payment the character of a periodical payment (p.4245). Further, his Honour found that the payment had been made to supplement the pension income as a result of inflation by the former employer. His Honour therefore followed the decision of the High Court in Dixon.



32. A case not cited to me but which must be considered is the decision of the Federal Court in Reuter v FCT 93 ATC 5030. This was an appeal against a decision at first instance (Hill J) that a "success fee" received in connection with the successful takeover of John Fairfax Ltd was assessable income. Hill J had concluded that the relationship between the provision of services by Reuter and the receipt of the payment by Reuter was more than temporal and that relationship characterised the payment as income in the hands of Reuter. Referring to the decision of the High Court in Hayes v FCT [1956] HCA 21; (1956) 96 CLR 47, the Full Court defined the issue to be decided as whether the payment was a "product" of the taxpayer's services, having regard to all the material (p.5036). The Full Court in dismissing the appeal concluded at page 5037:



"Part of the relevant evidence was within the Deed but significant evidence outside the Deed made it obvious that the nexus between the sum of $8m received by Reuter from BML, and the services provided by Reuter to

Rothwells was such that it could be said that the sum received, however described, was a product of those services and properly characterized as income according to ordinary concepts."



33. In Kelly v FCT 85 ATC 4283 the question before the Supreme Court of Western Australia was whether a prize of $20,000 received by a footballer because he had won a fairest and best player award in his code was assessable income in the taxpayer's hands. The Court upheld the Commissioner's assessment. In doing so Franklyn J referred to a number of the leading cases. In particular his Honour referred to the issue of the motive of the donor as follows at page 4288:



"The appellant urged that substantial weight be given to the motive of the donor, Channel 7 in making the award - that is, to secure benefits to itself by aligning itself with the award of the Sandover Medal thereby increasing its viewer coverage. The donor's motive may in some cases be found to be relevant to the determination but it cannot be the determining factor. As was said by Windeyer J in Scott v F.C. of T. [1966] HCA 48; (1966) 117 C.L.R. 514 at p.526 in a helpful passage:



'Whether or not a particular receipt is income depends upon its quality in the hands of the recipient. It does not depend upon whether it was a payment or provision that the payer or provider was lawfully obliged to make. The ordinary illustrations of this are gratuities regularly received as an incident of a particular employment. On the other hand, gifts of an exceptional kind, not such as are a common incident of a man's calling or occupation, do not ordinarily form part of his income. Whether or not a gratuitous payment is income in the hands of the recipient is thus a question of mixed law and fact. The motives of the donor do not determine the answer. They are, however, a relevant circumstance.'



His Honour then quoted from the judgment of Kitto J in The Squatting Investment Co Ltd v F.C. of T. [1953] HCA 13; (1953) 86 C.L.R. 570 at pp.627-628:



'The question whether a receipt comes in as income must always depend for its answer upon a consideration of the whole of the circumstances; and even in respect of a true gift it is necessary to inquire how and why it came about that the gift was made.'



In my view the overall circumstances show in this case that Channel 7 was providing for a period of years a sum of money which in the end result would in each year be paid to a footballer playing in the league competition as a consequence of him playing in that year to such a standard that he would receive sufficient votes to win the Sandover Medal. From the point of view of the recipient it was a sum which he was eligible to receive (unless disqualified as previously mentioned) by virtue of his employment if he could play well enough to secure the most votes from umpires as fairest and best player. In the appellant's case at least this followed from the pursuit of his employment. If he secured those votes he would receive not only the Sandover Medal but the money."



34. Counsel relied upon a decision of this Tribunal (constituted by Senior Member McMahon as he then was) reported as Case W26 89 ATC 273. In that case the taxpayer was carrying on business as a licensed bookmaker and also had extensive real estate interests held through a company structure. I need not discuss the facts in detail because it is apparent that there is no similarity between that case and the present. Counsel did not suggest otherwise. However, he sought to rely on the following proposition of law set out at page 279:



"So long as the service is rendered to any party and moneys are paid in relation to the rendering of that service by any party, then the amount so paid is taxable (sic) in accordance with the terms of the subsection."



It is clear from a reading of the reasons that the reference to the subsection was intended to be a reference to paragraph 26(e). With respect that proposition as stated is too wide and fails to take into account the requirement in the authorities that the character of the payment must be construed objectively from all the circumstances and depends upon its quality in the hands of the recipient.



35. The leading authority on paragraph 26(e) is the High Court decision in Smith v FCT [1987] HCA 48; (1987) 164 CLR 513. The decision of the Court (Deane and Gaudron JJ dissenting) was that a study allowance paid by a bank to one of its officers was assessable income by virtue of paragraph 26(e). The reasons for decision of the majority are encapsulated in the judgment of Toohey J at pages 533-4 as follows:



"The amount received by the present appellant was an amount allowed, given or granted to him in respect of his employment. It was paid by the bank in accordance with a policy designed to encourage its employees to increase their knowledge in subjects relevant to the banking industry and therefore to increase their proficiency as employees. It is not to the point that the courses might stand employees in good stead if they later changed their employment. The sum paid to the appellant was one of many such sums paid by the bank to its employees under its 'Encouragement to Study' scheme. There was an evident connexion between the appellant's employment and the sum he received. And in a very real sense the payment was a consequence of the existing relation of employer and employee. It was only as an employee that the appellant qualified for the benefits payable under the scheme. Borrowing the language of the majority in Savage (1983) 83 D.T.C. 5409, at p.5414, there was no element of gift or personal bounty or of considerations extraneous to the appellant's employment."



36. Brennan J would have gone further. His Honour stated the question at page 524 as follows:



"That is not a problem in this case, for the character of the allowance paid by the bank to the appellant is not in doubt. Its character is established by the scheme promulgated by the bank and acted on by the appellant. The allowance was paid because the bank had a scheme for paying allowances to its employees and the appellant fulfilled the two requirements on which the payment of allowances under the scheme depended: the appellant was employed by the bank and he completed a prescribed course. The question of law that arises on those facts is whether such a payment was made in consequence of the appellant's employment."



His Honour then discussed certain authorities including Dixon where he drew attention to the judgment of McTiernan J and in particular that the scheme in that case was ultra the employment relationship between Dixon and his previous employer. His Honour then went on to say at page 526:



"But if the employment (or some aspect of the employment) is the reason or one of the reasons why the allowance is paid, the allowance falls within s.26(e). A reason which is an insubstantial cause of the payment is immaterial, as the judgments in Dixon illustrate. But if an employee's employment or some aspect of that employment is a substantial reason why the allowance is paid, it cannot be said that the allowance is merely personal or that the payment is made for reasons extraneous to - or ultra - the employment. As the requisite relationship may be indirect as well as direct, it is immaterial that there is another reason why the allowance is paid or even that the other reason is the dominant one."



37. The contrary view is found in the judgment of Gaudron J (with whom Deane J agreed) at page 537 as follows:



"The consequential relationship between payment of the benefit and the relation of employer and employee is not constituted simply by a definitional requirement of a continuing employer and employee relation as a condition of eligibility to receive payment, for the paragraph is concerned with the allowing, giving or granting of the relevant benefit, and not with eligibility to receive the benefit."



In stating that proposition her Honour relied on a passage in the joint judgment of Dixon CJ and Williams J in Dixon at 86 CLR 554 as follows:



"We are not prepared to give s.26(e) a construction which makes it unnecessary that the allowance, gratuity, compensation, benefit, bonus or premium shall in any sense be a recompense or consequence of the continued or contemporaneous existence of the relation of employer and employee or a reward for services rendered given either during the employment or at or in consequence of its termination."



38. Little was said about the character of salvage rewards. The essential elements of a salvage claim are summarised at paragraph 270 - 1685 of Halsbury's Laws of Australia. A claim will not lie unless when the service was rendered life or property was in danger. It is beyond doubt in the present case that when the Lady Kathleen came alongside the Kirki both life and property were in danger.



39. A claim for salvage will not usually succeed unless the service was rendered voluntarily or the service is rendered under a salvage agreement. In the present case the initial action of the Lady Kathleen in sailing to the aid of the Kirki was voluntary albeit in compliance with the Master's statutory duty to render assistance to persons in danger at sea. At 8 am on the same day the Lady Kathleen's owners entered into a salvage contract as a sub-contractor. From that point the Lady Kathleen was under a contractual obligation to carry out the salvage.



40. As a general rule only those rendering actual personal service are entitled to claim salvage reward. There is no dispute that in the present case the applicant participated in the salvage operation.



41. It is clear from the terms of the salvage agreement that the prize was only payable on successful completion of the salvage.



42. I turn now to apply the law to the facts of this case. The payment received by the applicant arose out of the salvage of the Kirki and was paid because of the success of the salvage. It was not a payment to the applicant for services rendered. His employer had paid him his normal wages in accordance with the relevant award for the time that the Lady Kathleen was engaged on the salvage. There is no question that the applicant's payment of his share of the salvage was paid in substitution of his normal income nor as an increment to his normal income.



43. The obligation to pay the money to the applicant arose out of the success of the whole salvage operation. The applicant had no right nor any expectation that he would receive anything over and above his normal wages except in the circumstance that the salvage was successful and that success was a condition precedent to the applicant's entitlement (and expectation) to a share in the salvage award, ultimately fixed by the arbitrator.



44. It is the condition precedent which severs the link between the applicant's services rendered on board the Lady Kathleen with the ultimate payment to the applicant of his share of the salvage award. That payment was made to the applicant because he was a person eligible to participate in the apportionment of the salvage award and because the salvage was successful. The payment was not made to the applicant for services rendered. His employer had already paid him in full for those services and there is no question of substituted or top up payment for the services rendered.



45. The payment received by the applicant was a classic "windfall gain", which does not have the attributes of income according to ordinary concepts and usages.



46. I am also satisfied that the payment was not made to the applicant in circumstances in any way connected to his status as an employee. He was eligible to participate in the salvage award because he was a member of the crew of the Lady Kathleen at the time of the salvage operation but that eligibility does not depend on his employment or services rendered by him. Although the payment was effected through his employer his entitlement to payment is not founded in the relationship of employer/employee but arises because of the law relating to salvage awards given effect by the salvage contract and the fact of the success of the salvage operation. In no sense can the "prize" be said to have any relationship with the applicant's employment by P & O Maritime Services Pty Ltd albeit that if he was not such an employee at the relevant time he would not have been entitled to a share of the salvage award.



47. Therefore, the payment received is not an allowance, gratuity, etc allowed, given or granted to the applicant in respect of, or for, or in relation directly or indirectly to the applicant's employment or services rendered by him.

48. For these reasons I am satisfied that the objection decision under review should be set aside and the objection allowed by reducing the assessable income by $23,381. There will be a certificate that these proceedings have terminated in a manner favourable to the applicant.
