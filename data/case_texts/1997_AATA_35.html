TAXATION - LEGAL EXPENSES - DEDUCTION - CRIMINAL CHARGE - PRIVATE EXPENSE - WHETHER SECURITY AT A NIGHTCLUB A NECESSARY EXPENSE - NEXUS BETWEEN CRIMINAL CHARGES AND BUSINESS OPERATIONS -

Income Assessment Act 1922 s. 23(1)(a)

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s. 51(1), 108

Liquor Act (NSW) 1982 s. 125(1),(4)



AGC (Advances) Limited v FCT [1975] HCA 7; (1975) 132 CLR 175

FCT v Snowden & Willson [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431

Herald and Weekly Times Limited v FCT [1932] HCA 56; (1932) 48 CLR 113

Magna Alloys and Research Pty Ltd v FCT 80 ATC 4542

Putnin v FCT 91 ATC 4097

Ronpibon Tin NL and Tongkah Compound NL v FCT [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47

REASONS FOR DECISION

  1. This is an application for review of a decision made by the Appeals and Review Branch of the Australian Tax Office (ATO) on 4 March 1996 which disallowed a deduction claimed by the Applicant (a company) under s 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (“the Act”). The claim related to legal expenses paid by the Applicant on behalf of its manager during the years of income 1991 and 1992 in the following circumstances.
  2. The Applicant operates a nightclub in a country town in New South Wales. In the early hours of Sunday 11 November 1990 the manager of the nightclub, T, who is also the son of the managing director of the business, became involved in an altercation with an intoxicated patron who had earlier been asked to leave the premises following complaints about his behaviour. A physical confrontation ensued when the patron tried to regain entry to the nightclub. T punched him on the cheek causing him to fall backwards and strike his head on the pavement rendering him unconscious. He was still unconscious a few minutes later when T moved him a short distance away from the nightclub’s steps. However about half an hour later he was no longer in the position to which he had been moved. That afternoon he was found unconscious in a school yard about two hundred metres from the nightclub. He was taken to hospital where he underwent emergency surgery for brain injuries but he died eight days later.
  3. In December 1990 T was charged with manslaughter. He defended the charge but was nevertheless committed for trial on that charge. However by the time the trial was due to proceed in the District Court the Crown conceded that, in the light of the extensive head injuries sustained by the deceased, and the time which had elapsed between his being struck by T and being found some distance away, there was inadequate material to link T’s actions with the injuries which led to his death. Accordingly, the charge of manslaughter was withdrawn and T was charged with assault occasioning actual bodily harm. In August 1992 he pleaded guilty to that charge before Judge McGuire of the District Court who placed him on a good behaviour bond for a period of three years. The whole of his legal expenses were paid by his employer, the Applicant. It is the deductibility or otherwise of these expenses which is the central issue in these proceedings. The expenses amounted to $28,159.00 of which $6,130.00 was paid in the 1991 year of income and $22,029.00 was paid in 1992.
  4. Not only did the ATO deny the deductibility of these costs to the Applicant company, but it also sought to treat them as a distribution of profits under s 108 of the Act, and thus included them in the assessable income of T for the two relevant years. This was possible because T, being the son of Mr M, the principal shareholder of the company, is a “related person” under s 108. T unsuccessfully objected to these assessments and we were told at the hearing that he has also applied to this Tribunal for review of the decisions disallowing his objection.

THE HEARING

  1. At the hearing of the matter, the Applicant was represented by Mr M, the managing director and principal shareholder of the company. Mr M said that the company was unable to afford any external representation. Mr M came to the hearing well prepared to argue the s 51(1) point on behalf of the Applicant, but with no instructions from T to argue the s 108 point. It was thus agreed that we would proceed only to deal with the s 51(1) issue and would defer consideration of the s 108 point. This, in any event, had significant practical advantages. For if, as is now the case, the Applicant was to be successful in its applications, then it would render the s 108 issue nugatory. This is because the inclusion of the relevant amounts as part of T’s assessable income under s 108 was entirely dependent on the proposition that the expenses were not deductible to the company. Accordingly, our finding in favour of the Applicant will, we assume, automatically lead to a reversal of the Respondent’s previous decisions relating to T.
  2. The Respondent was represented at the hearing by one of its very experienced officers. As the Applicant was, in effect, unrepresented, the Respondent’s officer very responsibly approached the hearing in a non-adversarial manner. He discussed the issues in an objective and extremely fair manner, pointing out not only those aspects which would favour the Respondent but also those which would favour the Applicant. He is to be commended for the thoroughness and fairness of his approach.
  3. Mr M had also done a great deal of preparation for the hearing. And although he did not purport to be familiar with the fine nuances of the case law in this complex field, he nevertheless had an excellent grasp of the issues involved and was able to put all relevant material before us, both factually and conceptually, on behalf of the Applicant.

THE LEGISLATION

  1. Section 51(1) provides as follows:
“51. (1) All losses and outgoings to the extent to which they are incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income, or are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income, shall be allowable deductions except to the extent to which they are losses or outgoings of capital, or of a capital, private or domestic nature, or are incurred in relation to the gaining or production of exempt income.”

The Tax Commissioner’s Public Rulings

  1. The Tax Commissioner’s Public Rulings determined since June 1992 have the force of law. Public Ruling IT 149, which is the relevant Ruling on legal expenses, was determined in 1983. Therefore it can only be used as a guide but the decision-maker must provide reasons if it is not followed.
“IT 149 LEGAL EXPENSES INCURRED FOR FINES AND/OR BREACHES OF THE LAW IN THE COURSE OF CARRYING ON A BUSINESS

Preamble

These guidelines have issued as a result of several decisions concerning the deductibility of legal expenses, to assist in the treatment of such expenses.

2. As a result of a number of decisions which are conveniently set out in para. 512 and 513 of Challoner & Greenwood’s Income Tax Law and Practice, it has become a fairly well established principle that deductions are not allowed for fines incurred for breaches of the law committed in the course of carrying on a business. In normal circumstances, the legal expenses incurred by a taxpayer in defending himself or his employees from prosecutions of this nature would also fail to qualify for deduction. The principle no doubt has its justification in an assumption that if a taxpayer chooses to operate in breach of the law, the expenditure is not incurred in the furtherance of his business but rather in connection with the imposition of penalties for unlawful acts.

3. Notwithstanding these principles there is a marginal type of case in which a taxpayer who is carrying on a business in good faith and in a reputable manner necessarily exposes himself to some risk of occasional prosecution because, in the day to day conduct of his business, he must operate through employees and there is always a risk that more or less inadvertently he may fall into a breach of the law.

4. This situation arose in a case where a dairy company of good repute was prosecuted for a minor breach of cleanliness involving the work of one employee. There was no indication that the company had adopted a practice of ignoring its responsibilities under the law or taking a calculated risk with prosecutions. It rather appeared that the company, quite by accident, had been held responsible for the casual negligence of one person. This was recognised by the Court in not imposing a pecuniary penalty for the offence.

Ruling

5. In these circumstances the decision was taken that it would be consistent with the decision of the High Court in Herald & Weekly Times Ltd v FC of T [1932] HCA 56; (1932) 48 CLR 113 to regard the expenditure as a normal incident to which the taxpayer had been exposed in the day to day conduct of its business and which satisfied the tests for deductibility under sec. 51.

6. The decision may be followed in other cases of a similar nature as, for example, where a reputable restaurant proprietor finds it necessary to incur legal expenses in connection with action taken against him for an accidental breach of the Government regulations relating to hygiene and cleanliness. It will not, however, extent to expenditure incurred in meeting fines imposed on the taxpayer or his employees or to legal expenses incurred by taxpayers such as transport operators who, as a calculated risk, persistently take liberties with the law and incur fines regularly in the interests of the efficient operation of their business. There are a number of such cases which are waiting decision by the Board of Review and further rulings will be given in the light of the outcome in these cases.”

Discussion of Issues

  1. The sole issue in the case is the deductibility to the Applicant of the legal expenses paid on behalf of T. As the Respondent said at the hearing, this involves an assessment of the degree of connection between the expenses and the income earning activities of the Applicant. The Respondent conceded at the hearing, very fairly, that this case was “very close to the borderline”, but nevertheless submitted that it fell upon the side of it which would lead to the non-deductibility of the expenses.
  2. We should say by way of additional background that the nightclub in question has been under Mr M’s control for about 18 years. It was in 1989 that the Applicant company was interposed as owner. At that time, Mr M owned 80% of the shares and a non-family member, Mr B, owned 20%. Mr B left the company in February 1992. Since then Mr M has owned 60% of the issued shares and his wife 40%. T is not a shareholder of the company. Nor was there a written contract between him and the company when he assumed duties as manager earlier in 1990.
  3. Also relevant are the provisions of the Liquor Act (NSW) 1982 which create an offence for a licensee to permit intoxication on licensed premises. At the relevant time section 125(1) and (4) provided as follows:
    • (a) (1) A licensee shall not:
    • (b) permit his licensed premises to be used for the purposes of prostitution; or
    • (c) permit intoxication, or any indecent, violent or quarrelsome conduct, on his licensed premises.
    • (d) ...

Where a person is intoxicated on licensed premises, the licensee shall be deemed to have permitted intoxication of the licensed premises unless he proves that he and his employees took all reasonable steps to prevent intoxication on the licensed premises.”

  1. Returning now to the terms of s 51(1). There are two limbs to this provision, either of which can be availed of by a taxpayer. They are:

1. That the outgoings are incurred in gaining or producing assessable income; and

2. That the outgoings are necessarily incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of gaining or producing such income.

  1. The first limb of s 51(1) has a longer statutory history than the second, having had its predecessor in s 23(1)(a) of the Income Assessment Act 1922. The second limb was added in 1936.
  2. The leading exposition of the first limb remains that of the High Court in Ronpibon Tin NL and Tongkah Compound NL v FCT [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47 at 566 - 567:
“For expenditure to form an allowable deduction as an outgoing incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income it must be incidental and relevant to that end. The words ‘incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income’ mean in the course of gaining or producing such income ....

In brief substance, to come within the initial part of the subsection it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income or, if none be produced, would be expected to produce assessable income.”
  1. As Dixon CJ pointed out in FCT v Snowden and Willson [1958] HCA 23; (1958) 99 CLR 431 at 436, the words in the first limb “gaining or producing income” have a very wide operation and will cover almost all the ground occupied by the second limb. Similarly, Mason J (as he then was) made the following comment in AGC (Advances) Limited v FC of T [1975] HCA 7; (1975) 132 CLR 175 at 198:
“.... in the Ronpibon Case (38) the Court stated that ‘to come within the initial part of the subsection it is both sufficient and necessary that the occasion of the loss or outgoing should be found in whatever is productive of the assessable income’. So also it may be said that it is enough to satisfy the second part of the subsection that the occasion of the loss or outgoing is to be found in the carrying on of a business for the production of assessable income.”
  1. This prompted Brennan J (as he then was) to comment in Magna Alloys and Research Pty Limited v FCT 80 ATC 4542 at 4545:
“The requirement in the second limb that expenditure be incurred in carrying on a business parallels the requirement in the first limb that the expenditure be incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income.”
  1. It is clear that either of the two limbs of s 51(1) could be sought to be availed of by the Applicant in this case. However as the Respondent rightly commented at the hearing, the second limb is probably more appropriate to the Applicant’s factual circumstances. Accordingly we shall, from now, focus our discussion upon the requirements of this limb.
  2. Nevertheless, any discussion of the case law relating to the deductibility of legal expenses must include the judgment of the High Court in The Herald and Weekly Times Limited v FCT [1932] HCA 56; (1932) 48 CLR 113, which related to s 23(1) of the then Income Tax Assessment Act 1922. That case related to the deductibility to a newspaper proprietor of monies paid by way of compensation for defamatory material published in the newspaper and by way of legal costs for defending defamation proceedings. The Supreme Court of Victoria had declined deductibility, but this decision was reversed by a majority of the High Court. As Gavan Duffy CJ and Dixon J said at page 118:
“None of the libels or supposed libels was published with any other object in view than the sale of the newspaper. The liability to damages was incurred, or the claim was encountered, because of the very act of publishing the newspaper. The thing which produced the assessable income was the thing which exposed the taxpayer to the liability or claim discharged by the expenditure. .... Expenditure in which the taxpayer is repeatedly or recurrently involved in an enterprise or exertion undertaken in order to gain assessable income cannot be excluded by sec. 25(e) simply because the obligation to make it is an unintended consequence which the taxpayer desired to avoid.”
  1. A little later their Honours said:
“When it appears that the inclusion in the newspaper of matter alleged by claimants to be defamatory is a regular and almost unavoidable incident of publishing it, so that the claims directly flow from acts done for no other purpose than earning revenue, acts forming the essence of the business, no valid reason remains for denying that the money was wholly and exclusively expended for the production of assessable income.” (p 119)
  1. Rich J made the following comment upon the closeness of the connection between the payments claimed as deductions and the income earning activities of the appellant:
“Matter set up in a newspaper is published for the purpose of increasing its circulation and attracting advertisements. Income is gained or produced and liability is sometimes incurred. Publication is at once the source of income and the cause of liability. Payments subsequently made by way of compensation in respect of this liability or for costs to escape such liability relate back to publication. As publication is the common source of income and liability, the necessary connection between the carrying on of the business of the newspaper and the liability which causes the expenditure is complete.” (p. 121)
  1. In Snowden & Willson the High Court upheld the deductibility under s 51(1) of payments made by a company engaged in speculative building, which had been the subject of numerous complaints from its customers. Considerable publicity had surrounded these complaints, and a Royal Commission was established to inquire into them. The company expended money in advertising in an endeavour to counter the adverse publicity, and also incurred legal costs in defending itself and its officers before the Royal Commission.
  2. Dixon CJ was concerned with the requirement, in the second limb of s 51(1), that the outgoings be “necessarily” incurred in carrying on a business for the purpose of producing income. He said:
“If it were not for the word ‘necessarily’ there would be no difficulty, in my opinion, in treating the expenditure in the present case as coming within the conception expressed by this part of s 51(1). In saying this I am pronouncing upon a question of fact rather than of law. But, as it appears to me, the carrying on of the business of the nature described brought with it the attacks against which the taxpayer company sought to defend itself. The attacks touched its business nearly; they disparaged the methods by which it was conducted; they were calculated to deter intending or likely customers from dealing with it and to destroy the faith of existing customers in their current relations with the company. ....The word ‘necessarily’ does, however, seem to me to require consideration. Clearly its operation is to place a qualification upon the degree of connexion between the expenditure and the carrying on of the business which might suffice in the absence of such a qualification. ....

Logical necessity is not a thing to be predicated of business expenditure. What is meant by the qualification is that the expenditure must be dictated by the business ends to which it is directed, those ends forming part of or being truly incidental to the business.

In the present case it appears to me that the taxpayer company could do nothing else but defend itself, if it was to sustain its business and continue carrying it on in anything like the same volume or according to the same plan. That seem to me to be enough. (p 436 - 437)
  1. Fullagar J, in his judgment, expressed concern about the meaning of the word “necessarily” in s 51(1). He commented upon a text upon the subject, and concluded the word “necessarily” did not denote legal compulsion but could extend to “business expenditure arising out of exigencies created by unusual or difficult circumstances”. (p 443) Later he went on to say: “It means for practical purposes that, within the limits of reasonable human conduct, the man who is carrying on the business must be the judge of what is ‘necessary’”. (p 444)
  2. As to the ultimate merits of the matter, Fullagar J said:
“It would naturally seem essential to the company’s directors that a vigorous effort should be made to repel those attacks, and no defence could have any prospect of being effective which did not involve the expenditure of substantial sums of money. The relation between the expenditure and the carrying on of the business is clear. The expenditure was incidental to the carrying on of the business. It was incurred in carrying on the business, and it was necessarily incurred because the exigencies of the business imperatively demanded that it should be incurred.” (Ibid)
  1. The case which is probably most favourable to the Applicant in these proceedings is Magna Alloys, decided by the Federal Court in 1980. The taxpayer company in that case had claimed a deduction for legal expenses incurred in defending conspiracy charges brought against certain of its directors and agents. The charges related to the sales techniques of the company which, it was claimed, involved offering secret commissions to employees of existing and potential customers. The Supreme Court of New South Wales had found that the expenses were not deductible because the dominant reason for incurring them was to satisfy the personal needs of the directors and agents. The full Federal Court allowed the appeal, thus upholding the deductibility of the expenses. Because of the nature of the findings in the Supreme Court, much of the discussion in the Federal Court involved consideration as to the role of motivation and purpose when applying the second limb of s 51(1). Brennan J emphasised the objectivity of the test when ascertaining whether an expenditure is incurred for the purpose of carrying on a business. The taxpayer’s state of mind will always be relevant, he said, in determining the nature and scope of the business, for that is essentially a matter within the taxpayer’s control. It may also be relevant in ascertaining the relationship between the expenditure and the business, but only in circumstances where the objective facts do not provide adequate identification of the object of the expenditure. As his Honour said at page 4551:
“Given a sufficient identification of what the expenditure is for and the character and scope of the taxpayer’s income-earning undertaking or business, the question whether expenditure is incurred for the purpose of carrying on a business or for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income does not depend upon the taxpayer’s state of mind.”
  1. In any event, Brennan J said, the motive which the directors had in agreeing to the payment cannot affect the objective purpose or tendency of the payment. His Honour said at page 4552:
“In the present case, the character and scope of the taxpayer’s business is known without reference to its state of mind. Equally, it is objectively certain that the relevant expenditure was incurred to defray the legal costs of the directors and agents in the criminal proceedings brought against them. The connection between the legal services thus acquired and the taxpayer’s business neither requires nor permits reference to the taxpayer’s state of mind. The nature of that connection is to be found in the objective facts found by his Honour or not in dispute between the parties.

The conduct of the directors and employees which was impugned by the laying and prosecuting of the charges was conduct by which a part of the appellant’s business was carried on. Allegations reflecting on the integrity of those conducting the appellant’s business and their conduct in carrying on that business reflected on the appellant and its reputation. True enough, the allegations carried the risk of conviction and punishment only for those accused of offences. Apart from a time during the trial when the taxpayer was joined as a defendant on the indictment, the allegations did not carry the same risk to it, but the allegations nevertheless attacked its business methods. The interests of the taxpayer and of the accused were inseparable.”
  1. Deane and Fisher JJ agreed with this assessment. At page 4559 they said:
“Where an outgoing which was not involuntary has actually achieved the purpose for which it was incurred or where the connection between an outgoing and the relevant business is direct and obvious, there will ordinarily be little practical point in distinguishing between characterization of the outgoing by reference to what it achieved and characterization in the light of the purposes and objects of those responsible for incurring it. .... Cases where the outgoing does not achieve its intended purpose or where the connection with the business is indirect and remote demonstrate, however, the need to distinguish between the character of an outgoing determined merely by reference to objective factors and its character determined in the light of subjective purpose in any precise formulation of the ingredients of the second limb of sec. 51(1). The key to the role of the objective and subjective in such a formulation is, in the case of a voluntary outgoing, to be found in the statement of Fullagar J in FC of T v Snowden & Willson Pty Limited (supra at p 444) to which reference has already been made, namely, that ‘within the limits of reasonable human conduct the man who is carrying on the business must be the judge of what is ‘necessary’. The controlling factor is that, viewed objectively, the outgoing must, in the circumstances, be reasonably capable of being seen as desirable or appropriate from the point of view of the pursuit of the business ends of the business being carried on for the purpose of earning assessable income. Provided it comes within that wide ambit, it will, for the purposes of sec. 51(1), be necessarily incurred in carrying on that business if those responsible for carrying on the business so saw it.”
  1. As to the motivation of a company or its directors in approving such expenditure, Deane and Fisher JJ said at pp 4560 - 4561:
“Where a business is carried on by a company, many outgoings may advance the personal interests of individual directors, employees or agents, or indeed, of their relatives or friends. It is not essential, for the purposes of sec. 51(1) of the Act, that such outgoings be shown to have been incurred with a dominant motive characterized by lack of self-interest or generosity before they can be said to have been necessarily incurred in carrying on the particular business (see Cecil Bros. Pty Ltd v FC of T (Supra); FC of T v Phillips 78 ATC 4361). An outgoing can, in the relevant sense, be necessarily incurred in carrying on a business notwithstanding that it flows from a sense of moral obligation to those involved in the business. In particular, the fact that the needs of some directors and agents provided the occasion of an outgoing and that the resulting benefit to directors or agents constituted the dominant motive of a taxpayer for incurring it does not, of itself, preclude the outgoing from being necessarily incurred in carrying on the taxpayer’s business for the purposes of sec. 51(1). Whether a voluntary outgoing was so incurred depends upon the answer to the composite question which we have indicated, namely, whether the outgoing was reasonably capable of being seen as desirable or appropriate from the point of view of the pursuit of the business ends of that business and, if so, whether those responsible for carrying on the business so saw it.”
  1. In that case, their Honours found that the interests of the appellant company were inextricably bound up with those of its directors and agents and it was plainly in the company’s interests that the directors and agents be properly represented. In those circumstances the payments were deductible under s 51(1).
  2. Numerous other cases were cited during the course of the hearing in the present case. However those we have mentioned are directly relevant to the issues in the case and there is little point in going beyond them.
  3. We return to the circumstances of this case, and there are two matters which need to be mentioned at the outset, if only to be set aside as irrelevant for the purposes of the present discussion.
  4. The first is the relationship between T and Mr M. The Respondent conceded at the hearing that the fact that T is the son of the managing director and principal shareholder of the Applicant company is irrelevant for the purposes of s 51. This was an appropriate concession. It is clear from Magna Alloys that the existence of a personal gain on the part of the taxpayer or its associates is irrelevant to the issues under s 51(1). Moreover, from a factual point of view, there is nothing to indicate that the relationship between T and Mr M was a determining or even a relevant consideration in the company’s decision to pay his legal expenses. Indeed we accept the opposite to be the case. Mr M said in evidence that the fact that T was his son had nothing to do with the matter. This is supported by the fact that an employee’s legal expenses were paid by the employer in 1981 in very similar circumstances. On that occasion the doorman, who was no relation of Mr M’s, was charged with assault arising out of an altercation outside the nightclub. Furthermore, as Mr M pointed out, the initial decision to pay T’s costs was made at a time when Mr B, who was no relation, was a director and a 20% shareholder in the company.
  5. The second matter which must be set aside as irrelevant is the fact that the expenses in this case related to the defending of a criminal charge. It was argued in Magna Alloys that this was a limiting factor under s 51(1), but this submission was unanimously and emphatically rejected. Similarly, in Putnin v FCT 91 ATC 4097 an accountant’s legal costs in defending a charge of criminal conspiracy were found to be deductible. The Federal Court in that case (Burchett, French and Lee JJ) made the following observation:
“It may be a natural incident of the conduct of the operations of a particular kind of business that claims of the commission of torts, or even crimes, may arise, although it is to be hoped not often, and have to be repelled.” (p 4100)
  1. The Respondent in this matter, whilst conceding that this case is close to the border line, nevertheless submits that there is insufficient connection between the payment of T’s legal expenses and the income earning activities of the Applicant to enable the expenses to be deducted under s 51(1). It points out that all expenses which have been found to be deductible in the cases thus far have been outlaid in defending activities which have been integrally connected with the income producing activities of the taxpayer. By way of illustration the Respondent said that had the Applicant’s employee been charged with selling liquor after hours, then his expenses would have clearly been deductible to the company, as the sale of liquor is its primary income earning activity. However, it was submitted that manhandling intoxicated patrons is too peripheral to the essential activities of the business for it to be said that expenses incurred in defending a charge which arises from this activity should be deductible for the Applicant.
  2. The Respondent also pointed out that all cases thus far have involved “white collar” offences committed by employees. None of them have, as here, involved offences of violence. To allow deductibility in the present circumstances would, it said, involve an unwarranted extension of the existing principles.
  3. It is beyond dispute that the present case involves factual issues which have not been directly raised in any of the decided cases thus far. But this can hardly be a reason for finding against the taxpayer. The real question is whether the present circumstances fall within the principles which have been enunciated in those cases. In our opinion they clearly do.
  4. As the law stands at present, there is a real possibility that s 51(1) will extend to the payment of all legal expenses incurred by employees who have been charged with criminal offences as a result of activities directly relating to their employment. As Deane and Fisher JJ said in Magna Alloys:
“It is not necessary for the purposes of the present appeal to determine whether outgoings incurred in providing appropriate legal representation for an employee on criminal charges directly arising from his activities in the course of a business are, without more, reasonably capable of being seen as desirable or appropriate in the sense we have indicated. The circumstances in the present case plainly involved elements transcending the mere provision of legal representation for those whose activities, on behalf of the taxpayer, had subjected them to criminal prosecution.” (p 4561)
  1. Nor do we find it necessary in this case to attempt to rely on such a general proposition as the above. For we are firmly of the view that the circumstances of this case bring it within the specific principles of deductibility which were enunciated by Deane and Fisher JJ in Magna Alloys.
  2. Mr M gave unchallenged evidence that he regarded it as essential to preserve the nightclub’s reputation to do everything possible to assist T in order to counter the potentially adverse public reaction arising from the fact that he had been charged with manslaughter. Running a nightclub, he said, can be a very risky business. The success of a nightclub depends on its image and prestige being constantly maintained at a high level. Once numbers start to dwindle, the attractiveness of the venue decreases and the patrons cease to come altogether. In fact, the nightclub’s business did suffer after this incident, which Mr M attributed to the media publicity surrounding the death of the patron and the charging of T. He countered this, he said, by using the company’s staff to promote the goodwill of the nightclub by “word of mouth type advertising, as we could not place an ad in the paper to counter all the hype that was created around T’s arrest”. This required the co-operation and enthusiasm of the staff - something which would have been impossible to achieve had the company failed to stand behind T. As Mr M put it, “I’m sure that if we’d just thrown him out the staff morale would have suffered and the business would have also”.
  3. In addition, Mr M said, he was concerned to protect the company’s licence, as continual breaches would lead to its withdrawal. The fact that staff are required under the terms of the licence to physically remove intoxicated patrons means that there is always the risk that they might inadvertently use more force than necessary, thus leaving themselves open to charges of assault. There is little or no control which the company can exert in these situations, he said. In fact there had only been one previous charge of assault against a company employee, back in 1981. On that occasion the company paid the employee’s legal fees and he was acquitted.
  4. In Snowden and Willson Dixon CJ said that it was irrelevant to the issues in that case whether the allegations against the company and its officers were well founded or whether they could be successfully defended. That comment was no doubt appropriate to the circumstances of that case, but this case is entirely different. In this case, the fact that T was initially charged with manslaughter - a charge which could not, in the end, be sustained - is by no means irrelevant to the issues under s 51(1). For it is immeasurably more serious, and requiring of a much more concerted response, to be charged - or to have an employee charged - with one of the most potentially serious offences in the criminal calendar involving the death of another human being, than to be charged with a lesser offence such as assault. Faced with this situation, the company’s officers had a choice. On the one hand they could have stood by T, and assisted him in defending this most serious charge which they and he considered inappropriate, but which clearly arose out of the performance of his duties for the company. Alternatively, they could have left him to fend for himself. As Mr M said, this would have been disastrous to staff morale, particularly in the light of the seriousness of the charge and the fact that it was rightly perceived to be unwarranted. Similarly, it would have done nothing to assist the public image of the nightclub had it become known as a place where a doorman was so rough with a patron as to inflict potentially fatal injuries upon him.
  5. Even if T had been charged with assault, we consider that the costs of his defence would have been deductible to the company. The physical handling of members of the public, particularly when they are intoxicated, always carries a risk that the situation might suddenly and unexpectedly escalate. It is a fine judgment as to how much force is reasonable in the circumstances. There is always a danger that an employee might misjudge the situation and exert excessive force, thus laying himself open to a charge of assault. This risk is incidental to the fact that a nightclub licensee is required to keep intoxicated people off its premises and, at the relevant time, the Liquor Act provided that “all reasonable steps” should be taken to ensure this. Certainly the police can be called in to assist, but as Mr M observed, it is detrimental to the reputation of a nightclub for it to be constantly calling in the police. Calling for police assistance is generally left as a last resort to be used in exceptional circumstances only.
  6. The Respondent submitted that none of the decided cases have extended to defending employees on charges of violence. But none of those cases have involved situations where it could be a part of an employee’s duties to use physical force against members of the public. The relevant issue in our view is not whether the offence involves personal violence, but whether it otherwise falls within the composite test posed by Deane and Fisher JJ in Magna Alloys.
  7. The Respondent also relied upon the lack of proximity between T’s assault of the patron and the income earning activities of the Applicant. In all cases thus far where employees’ legal expenses have been deductible for their employers, the employees’ activities giving rise to the allegations have been inextricably intertwined with the employers’ income earning activities. This is not the case here, the Respondent submitted. A nightclub gains its income from selling alcohol and food, not from ejecting its patrons.
  8. This argument ignores the fact that in order to maintain a pleasant environment within a nightclub, where people will be prepared to come and pay for food and alcohol, it is often necessary to remove intoxicated, violent and abusive patrons. Indeed the Liquor Act requires that intoxicated patrons be kept off licensed premises. In other words, the income earning activity is, to a large extent, dependent upon the effective physical control of the premises by the licensee’s staff.
  9. In any event, Deane and Fisher JJ in Magna Alloys stressed that in order for an outgoing to be deductible under s 51(1) it does not have to produce a direct advantage for the taxpayer:
“It is implicit in what has been said that the distinction between immediate direct purpose or object and remote indirect purpose or object does not play an essential part in determining whether an outgoing is properly deductible pursuant to the second limb of sec. 51(1) of the Act. To the extent that the subjective element is relevant, what is important in the case of a voluntary outgoing is the identification of the advantage or advantages which the outgoing was intended to achieve on behalf of the taxpayer regardless of whether that advantage or those advantages were seen as the direct result of the outgoing or as indirectly flowing therefrom or of whether the pursuit of them should be seen as ‘purpose’ or as ‘object’ or as ‘motive’. Business outgoings may be properly and necessarily incurred in pursuit of indirect and remote, as well as direct and immediate, advantages. The fact that the business advantage sought is indirect or remote will not of itself preclude the pursuit of that advantage from characterizing the outgoing as an outgoing necessarily incurred in carrying on the relevant business.” (p 4559)
  1. We return finally to the composite test posed by Deane and Fisher JJ in Magna Alloys. That test is whether the outgoing was reasonably capable of being seen as desirable or appropriate from the point of view of the pursuit of the business ends of that business and, if so, whether those responsible for carrying on the business so saw it. It is implicit from all we have said that an affirmative answer must, in our view, be given to both questions. This being the case, we would set aside the decision in each case and allow the legal expenses to be deductible to the Applicant.
  2. One final matter must be mentioned, if only again to set it aside. This arose from a number of observations made by Mr M during his evidence at the hearing, in which he repeatedly stressed the importance to the Applicant of maintaining its liquor licence and therefore its business. On one way of looking at it, this might create the impression that the expenses were incurred in order to protect a capital asset and were thus of a capital nature. It was never submitted by the Respondent during the hearing that the payment of T’s legal expenses might be categorized as a capital outgoing. Nevertheless, had they been paid for the purpose of protecting the Applicant’s licence, and thus of preserving a capital asset, they would appropriately have been regarded as capital outgoings, and it is thus a matter which bears consideration. However there was never any suggestion that T’s conviction of manslaughter could result in a withdrawal of the Applicant’s licence. Indeed the evidence was to the contrary. Mr M said that it was repeated transgressions which could potentially jeopardise the licence. Therefore, although the maintenance of the licence may well have been one of the factors motivating the company in paying T’s legal expenses, it could by no means be said that the outgoing was incurred for the purpose of protecting a capital asset.
