Eid and Commissioner of Taxation [1998] AATA 73; (1998) 38 ATR 1066; 98 ATC 2053 (11 February 1998)

Last Updated: 16 September 2009





Administrative

Appeals

Tribunal



DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No NT97/431

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re NASRAT EID

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



Decision No 12610



DECISION



Tribunal      Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)

Date      11 February 1998

Place      Sydney

Decision      The decision under review is affirmed.



(Sgd) BJ McMahon

..............................................

Deputy President     



CATCHWORDS



TAXATION - departure prohibition order - request for departure authorization certificate - no security offered by taxpayer - no ties to Australia - conviction for attempt to depart from Australia - unsatisfactory evidence of value of security volunteered - decision affirmed



Taxation Administration Act 1953 s 14U(1)

     

REASONS FOR DECISION

11 February 1998 Deputy President McMahon           

  1. This is an application brought under section 14Y of the Taxation Administration Act 1953, to review a decision to refuse a departure authorisation certificate under section 14U.

  2. The Commissioner alleges that the applicant was one of four proprietors of a joint venture trading in tobacco products, through the mechanism of a company called Walter Hammond and Associates Pty Ltd. The Commissioner believes that over a period, the joint venture purchased tobacco products in NSW, transported them out of, and later back in to the State, and arranged for their sale illegally, without paying sums said to be due under the Business Franchise Licences (Tobacco) Act 1987 (NSW). In the course of the joint venture’s activities, it is alleged that more than $40 million was sent out of Australia through a scheme involving the purchase of US dollar draft cheques from Thomas Cook and Son by a duty free store proprietor on their behalf for a commission. On the basis of these beliefs, and on the basis of other information available to the Commissioner, assessments were raised against the joint venturers under section 167 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936. On 28 July 1997, amended assessments were issued against the applicant in respect of the years ended 30 June 1995 and 1996, resulting in a taxation liability of $3,909,693. I was informed that no part of this assessment has been paid and additional tax, by way of late payment penalty, is accumulating at the rate of between $1,800 and $2,000 per day. The applicant denies the allegations upon which the assessments are based. Objections to the assessments were lodged on 22 August 1997. A decision has not yet been given in response to those objections.

  3. A departure prohibition order was made under section 14S on the basis set out in paragraph 14S(1)(a)(b). It was alleged that the Commissioner believed on reasonable grounds, that it was desirable to ensure that the applicant did not depart from Australia without wholly discharging the tax liability or making arrangements satisfactory to the Commissioner for the tax liability to be wholly discharged. No such arrangements were made. An application was made to the Federal Court under section 14V to set the order aside. This application was heard by Einfeld J on 29 August 1997, when an ex tempore judgement was delivered refusing to make the order sought. His Honour’s written reasons have not yet been published.

  4. The applicant was charged with criminal offences under the Business Franchise Licences (Tobacco) Act 1987. In Walter Hammond & Associates Pty Ltd v State of NSW and Ors [1997] HCA 34; (1997) 146 ALR 355, the crucial provisions of that Act were held to be invalid because they imposed a duty of excise contrary to the exclusive power of the Commonwealth Parliament, conferred by section 90 of the Commonwealth Constitution, to impose such duties. The applicant and one of his associates, Mr Mcheileh, appeared as interveners and were represented by counsel. Following the High Court’s decision, the criminal charges against the applicant were withdrawn. Whilst they were pending, the applicant was on bail. One of the conditions of the bail was that he was not to depart Australia.

  5. It was the Commissioner’s view that the withdrawal of the prosecution and the consequent bail condition, made it imperative that the departure prohibition order be made. It was issued on 8 August 1997, but was not served until 12 August 1997. In the mean time, the applicant had made arrangements to leave Australia. On 8 August 1997, he attempted to board an international flight from Kingsford Smith airport but was stopped because of the existence of the departure prohibition order which, at that time, had not then been served upon him.

  6. On 3 September 1997, his solicitors made an application on his behalf for a departure authorisation certificate under section 14U. The purpose of the application, it was said, was to enable the applicant to travel to Israel for the wedding of his brother. The application pointed out that the applicant had little by way of funds in Australia but that friends were able to lodge security over four houses to the value of $340,000, to ensure his return to Australia. It was said in response to an inquiry from the respondent to the applicant’s solicitors that:
“Our client is the eldest son of the Eid family and according to family tradition the younger brothers (our client’s brothers) will not marry until Mr Eid returns to Israel. There have previously been two arrangements made for the wedding so that our client could attend and as he was unsuccessful in having the bail conditions varied to enable him to go to Israel the arrangements were cancelled. If permission is not given the marriage will again have to be postponed until such time as our client is able to leave.”


  1. The application was refused on the grounds set out in section 14U(1). The wedding subsequently took place.

  2. A second application for a departure authorisation certificate was made on the applicant’s behalf on 20 November 1997. Again, security was offered over four houses then said to be worth approximately $690,000, and said to be unencumbered. The four properties were owned by four different individuals or families who were prepared to offer their houses as security. The reasons given for the application were to enable the applicant to visit his wife and four children who were in Israel, and to attend his 67 year old mother, who was ill. Again, the application was refused on the grounds set out in section 14U(1), on 25 November 1997. The humanitarian aspects of the application were specifically addressed in the reasons given by the Commissioner.

  3. It is from this decision that the present application is brought to the Tribunal.

  4. Section 14U is in the following terms:
“14U(1) Where, on application made by a person in respect of whom a departure prohibition order is in force -

(a) the Commissioner is satisfied -

(i) that, if a departure authorisation certificate is issued in respect of the person, it is likely that -
(A) the person will depart from Australia and will return to Australia within such period as the Commissioner considers to be appropriate in relation to the person; and

(B) circumstance of the kind referred to in paragraph 14T(1)(a) will come into existence within such period as the Commissioner considers to be appropriate in relation to the person; and

(ii) that it is not necessary or desirable for the person to give security under sub-section (2) for the person’s return to Australia; or
(b) in a case where the Commissioner is not satisfied with respect to the matters referred to in paragraph (a) -

(i) the person has given security under subsection (2) to the satisfaction of the Commissioner for the person’s return to Australia; or

(ii) if the person is unable to give such security, the Commissioner is satisfied that -

(A) a departure authorisation certificate should be issued in respect of the person on humanitarian grounds; or

(B) a refusal to issue a departure authorisation certificate in respect of the person would be detrimental to the interests of Australia,

the Commissioner shall issue a certificate authorising the person to depart from Australia for a foreign country on or before the seventh day after a day (being a day later than, but not more than 7 days later than, the day on which the certificate is issued) specified in the certificate.

14U(2) For the purposes of this section -

(a) a person may give security, by bond, deposit or any other means, for the person’s return to Australia by such day as is agreed between the person and the Commissioner;

(b) the Commissioner may, in the Commissioner’s discretion and on application by the person or on the Commissioner’s own motion, substitute a later day for the day so agreed (including a day substituted by virtue of a previous application of this paragraph); and

(c) the Commissioner may refuse to substitute such a later day unless the person -

(i) increases, to the satisfaction of the Commissioner, the value of the security given by the person under this sub-section; or

(ii) gives a further security, to the satisfaction of the Commissioner, by bond, deposit or any other means, for the person’s return to Australia by that later day.

14U(3) As soon as practicable after a departure authorisation certificate is issued in respect of a person, the Commissioner shall -

(a) cause a copy of the departure authorisation certificate to be served, as prescribed, on the person; and

(b) cause a copy of the departure authorisation certificate to be given to each person to whom a copy of the departure prohibition order made in respect of the person was given.

14U(4) As soon as practicable after a decision is made under subsection (1) refusing to issue a departure authorisation certificate in respect of a person or a decision is made under subsection (2) refusing to substitute a later day in relation to the return of a person to Australia, the Commissioner shall cause to be served, as prescribed, on the person notification of the decision.”


  1. It will be seen from the above, that the Tribunal is required to issue a departure authorisation certificate if it is satisfied that it is “likely” (equated with “probably” in Macquarie Dictionary) that the applicant will return within an acceptable period, and that section 14T(1)(a) circumstances will come into existence. This is a reference to payment or giving security for payment, of the whole of the taxation debt. The relevant subsection is in the following terms:
“14T(1) Where a departure prohibition order is in force in respect of a person and -

(a) the tax liabilities to which the person is subject have been wholly discharged and the Commissioner is satisfied that it is likely that the tax liabilities to which the person may become subject in respect of, or arising out of, matters that have occurred will be -

(i) wholly discharged; or

(ii) completely irrecoverable; . . .”


  1. Clearly, there are no available arrangements here for liquidation of the whole of the taxation debt. Accordingly, the applicant must rely upon paragraph 14U(1)(b) namely, that the applicant has given security to the satisfaction of the Commissioner for the applicant’s return to Australia. Although the second application for an authorisation certificate referred to the health of the applicant’s mother, I was informed at the hearing that reliance was no longer placed on humanitarian grounds. The question to be determined, therefore, is simply whether the applicant “has given security” to the satisfaction of the Commissioner and therefore of this Tribunal for his return to Australia.
  2. The security offered has not been “given”. It consists of offers by four persons, or groups of persons, to encumber houses owned by them in favour of the Commissioner, upon condition that each would forfeit the whole of their interest in their respective property, if the applicant did not comply with any conditions that might be imposed upon him requiring him to return to Australia.
  3. Marie Eid, who now lives in Queensland, offered her former home at 49 Jasper Road, Baulkham Hills, which is presently let to tenants. She is shown as the registered proprietor and no encumbrances are noted. A local estate agent has given a market opinion that the property could be sold for between $250,000 and $255,000. In each of four cases where property has been offered as security, evidence has been given not by way of a formal valuation but by way of an informal market opinion. It is not clear whether the person giving the opinion has formal valuation qualifications or is simply a local salesman. All of the opinions are hedged about with qualifications that they are not to be relied upon as information for any purpose other than a market opinion.
  4. Catrine and Issa Kalifih offered their home at 185 Woodstock Avenue, Dharruk (near Mount Druitt). Living with them in the property are their two children, aged 16 and 19 years. They too, said that they would agree to forfeit the whole of their interest in the property, should Mr Eid not comply with a condition to return to Australia. Market opinion was given for their house at between $110,000 and $115,000.
  5. Tawfik and Jackleen Awad offered their house at 21 Broadmeadow Street, St John’s Park. Although they said in their statement of evidence that their property was unencumbered, it is clear from the title search that there is a registered mortgage to Australia and New Zealand Banking Group Limited. There was no evidence before me as to the amount owing under this mortgage. In all four cases, the properties had been offered to support bail conditions imposed on the applicant while his criminal trial was pending. Tawfik Awad and Jackleen Awad said in their statement of evidence that after the charges against Mr Eid were dropped, the title deeds were returned to them. In view of the fact that there is a registered mortgage, it seems to me most unlikely that this event happened. None of the proprietors of any of the four properties was required for cross examination.
  6. The fourth property was owned by Issa Awad and Ghada Awad. Situated at 2 Bringelly Place, Bonnyrigg Heights, the house was given an estimated sale price of between $380,000 and $400,000. The title search attached to their statement, shows that a caveat by Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth II was lodged against the title, even though the registered proprietors have stated in their statement that the property was unencumbered. A further search of this caveat, tendered in evidence by the respondent, shows that it was lodged to support an unregistered mortgage given by Issa Awad on 26 April 1996. It was suggested from the bar table, that this may have referred to the giving of security over the property to support the applicant’s bail conditions. This seems unlikely for two reasons. Firstly, there is no similar caveat on the other three titles. Secondly, the caveat refers to an unregistered mortgage given only by Issa and not by Ghada Awad. There was no evidence before me of the amount secured by the unregistered mortgage as at the date of these proceedings.
  7. The applicant contended that these statements amounted to offers to encumber properties with market values of between $930,000 and $970,000, and that they should be accepted as security for the applicant’s return.
  8. For four reasons I am not satisfied that they can be reasonably accepted as such security.
  9. Firstly, the evidence as to value is unsatisfactory. Whilst market opinions might be general indicators to a vendor of a price likely to be realised in the market, they are not evidence of value for present purposes. The exculpatory terms of the opinions indicate that they are not to be relied upon by any person, including the registered proprietors. Furthermore, the values take no account of the cost of realisation, the cost of providing security, and the difficulties inherent in obtaining vacant possession from owner occupants. The fact that suggested values have increased from $340,000 to $690,000 to $970,000 over a period of four months shows how rubbery the figures are.
  10. The second defect is that no details have been given of the extent of the encumbrances on two of the properties.
  11. The third defect is that even if all of the anticipated sale prices were realised, they would still amount to less than one quarter of the taxation debt. That percentage is diminishing every day with the additional tax mounting up.
  12. The fourth and most important objection however, is that the applicant has not “given” security, nor is he to give that security in the future. Others are to give security voluntarily. There is no evidence of any financial impairment which the applicant is prepared to undergo in order to secure the Commissioner’s satisfaction that he will return. I see no reason in logic, nor has any reason been given in the evidence why the applicant should return to Australia simply because four other people, or groups of people, have given security over their assets. It was suggested that the applicant had funds overseas from which he could reimburse these people if their assets were lost. This was denied by Mr Eid. It is not necessary to enquire into the plausibility of such a suggestion. The fact is, that on the face of the proposals, there is nothing before me which would tend to secure the applicant’s return to Australia simply by virtue of the charges to be given over the four houses.
  13. On the other hand, there is a good deal of evidence which would indicate that it is most unlikely that the applicant would return to Australia if a certificate were issued. The fact that security must be given “to the satisfaction of the Commissioner” indicates to me that it is relevant to take account of surrounding circumstances in evaluating the security and in reaching a state of satisfaction.
  14. His wife, his four children, his mother, father, sisters and brothers, all live in a village in Israel. In his evidence, he said that he wished to visit them for a period of four weeks after which, he would return to Australia. He told me that as far as he knew, it was not the intention of his wife or children to return with him. His twelve year old son visited Australia last year, but returned to Israel after the visit was concluded. Clearly, all personal ties are in Israel. There is no evidence of any personal ties in Australia.
  15. There is also no evidence of any financial or other tie to Australia. The applicant’s house at Minchinbury was sold some time ago and the proceeds of sale were remitted to Israel. He now lives in a rented house. He says he has no assets to speak of in this country, although he agreed in evidence that he owned real estate in Israel to the value of some $80,000. Both the applicant and his wife were born in Israel, but they are now Australian citizens. The four children were all born in Australia. Their citizenship appears to be the only link with this country. In the present circumstances, it appears to be quite a weak link.
  16. I have no confidence in the truth of the applicant’s denials that he has considerable assets in Israel or other parts of the world. A statement of evidence by Mr Tran detailed the transactions which he had with a person he knew as Michael, and which involved sending large sums of money out of Australia. A further statement by Detective Sergeant Egan sets out the procedures adopted to establish that the applicant was the person known to Mr Tran as Michael. A judgement of Smart J in the Supreme Court certainly establishes that at one stage, the applicant was a trader in tobacco and had sought to recover a large quantity which had been seized by the Chief Commissioner for Business Franchise Licences. None of the persons who made these statements was cross examined. Their positive evidence must therefore be accepted with this qualification. Nonetheless, what has been said about the applicant is consistent with his having access to far more money than he would now admit.
  17. He has been involved in extensive litigation in the past few years. He insists that his considerable legal expenses were paid by his brother and his wife, although his wife is not in employment, and has no known assets. He was unable to say where his brother obtained the money. The fact that there may be substantial assets outside Australia was taken into account when the original departure prohibition order was made. There has been no evidence that the situation has changed to any material degree.
  18. One of the most telling pieces of evidence tending to support the unlikelihood of the applicant returning to Australia, concerns the incidents that occurred on 4 October 1997. On that day, the applicant travelled on a chartered aircraft with his associate Mr Mcheileh and three others, from Bankstown airport to Lord Howe Island. He then declared that he was leaving Australia and his passport was appropriately endorsed. The aircraft landed at Norfolk Island to refuel. Its flight plan indicated that it was then proceeding to Auckland. At the airport, the applicant was interviewed by an officer of the Australian Federal Police, Detective Colbran. He was accused of attempting to leave Australia, notwithstanding the existence of a departure prohibition order. By this time, there can be no doubt that he was well aware of the nature of this order. An account of the conversation that took place at the airport is confused, although it was all recorded. Evidence was later given at the Local Court in Sydney. On a charge of attempting to leave Australia contrary to the provisions of section 14R, the applicant was convicted, given a suspended sentence of six months, and was released on $500 surety and a three year good behaviour bond. The applicant did not give evidence at these proceedings. It was submitted on his behalf at the hearing that the evidence brought by the prosecution did not amount to a prima facie case. The magistrate did not uphold this submission and proceeded to convict the applicant. An appeal has now been lodged, not on the general basis and not seeking a re-hearing, but on the basis that the magistrate was in error in holding that the evidence adduced by the prosecution was sufficient to support a prima facie case.
  19. Whether or not the applicant attempted to leave Australia while an order was in effect, the fact is that he has been convicted of doing so and, for the time being, this conviction has remained undisturbed. In evidence before me, he again said that he had not intended to leave Australia. It is difficult to understand why he was on the aircraft if that were the case. A chartered aircraft might be thought to offer a better chance of a surreptitious departure than a commercial flight. After he left it at Norfolk Island, he told me that he went to a hotel. Although he said that he was visiting Norfolk Island for a holiday, he had not made any reservation at the hotel. Mr Mcheileh continued on the flight to Auckland.
  20. Having regard to all these facts, it is clear to me that it would be most unlikely the applicant would return to Australia within four weeks or at all, if an authorisation certificate were issued. Furthermore, the security offered is not in accordance with the Act and is, in any event, not sufficient to establish to my satisfaction that he would return to Australia, having regard to all the other relevant considerations.
  21. This being so, the decision under review is affirmed.
