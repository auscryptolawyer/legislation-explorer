REASONS FOR DECISION

25 November 1988 Peter M. Roach (Senior Member)

1. On 7 February 1983 officers of the Police Force of the Commonwealth apprehended two Australian-born residents in the act of attempting to depart the country while carrying with them $47,000 in Australian currency and bank cheques, contrary to the provisions of the Banking (Foreign Exchange) Regulations. The money was impounded and each man was charged on indictment with an offence. (The figure was sometimes referred to as $47,300. Hereafter, I shall refer to the lower figure). It is common ground that the entirety of the money impounded was the property of one man: the Applicant. At that point the Applicant abandoned his attempt to leave the country and remained to await his trial. In due course he was convicted in the District Court for the State of Victoria and fined $1,000. He was then free to leave the country and did so. Pursuant to the provisions of the Regulations the money impounded was liable to be forfeited to the Commonwealth (Reg 42(2)). As I was not advised that any Order in that regard was made, I presume that no Order was so made.

2. In due course the Commissioner, by one of his officers working in association with other officers, commenced an investigation into the affairs of the Applicant. He obtained information as to the taxable income assessed to the Applicant since 1968; as to the bank accounts of the Applicant; as to his dealings in motor vehicles; as to his acquisitions of travellers cheques; and as to his expenditure on international air fares. Armed with that and other information he prepared draft calculations as to the taxable income of the Applicant for the years of income ended 30 June 1978 to 1982 using the "asset betterment" method of calculation. At the time those investigations were carried out the investigating officer believed the Applicant to be overseas. He had little information as to the personal history of the Applicant although he acknowledged in the course of cross-examination that perusal of Police records indicated that the Applicant may have been a heavy drinker. The officer recommended that taxable income should be determined in accordance with his draft calculations; that additional tax be levied in accordance with the Commissioner's published rulings using a culpability factor of 50% of tax avoided; and that provisional tax be so assessed as to ensure that the Commissioner would be entitled to require payment of the entirety of the sum of $47,000.

3. On 8 July 1983 the Commissioner issued notices of amended assessment of taxable income against the Applicant in relation to the years of income ended 30 June 1978 to 1981 inclusive and an original assessment for the 1982 year. (I shall refer to them collectively as "the July 1983 assessments"). Additional tax was also levied, as was provisional tax. The result was that the Applicant was required to pay to the Commissioner the sum of $47,143.82. The assessments which issued were made up as follows:

7. It is also appropriate to observe that not too much significance ought be attributed to the circumstance that the "unexplained omissions" in two years (1980 and 1981) appear in the Table as an over-return of income. Such a circumstance is not necessarily an indication of overall inaccuracy for it may have been explained by an overstatement or understatement of opening or closing assets not capable of being precisely evidenced.

8. Put another way, the issues to be resolved are:-

(a) whether the amounts of cash on hand at 30 June 1977 and 1982 should be as alleged:

(d) whether provision should be made for an undischarged loan liability to the family company (cf. post) in the sum of $7,800 first existing on 30 June 1980 and subsisting thereafter through the betterment and, in consequence, have the effect of explaining apparent omissions in the year of income ended 30 June 1980 in the sum of $7,800 and having the same effect overall.

9. The investigations of the Commissioner's officer had provided, as it were, a skeleton of a particular taxpayer. It remained to flesh out the individual by considering all manner of information which came to be known in the course of the hearing, but which was previously unknown, or not so fully known, to the investigating officers.

10. At the time of hearing the Applicant was the owner and manager of three "bars" in a major city in South-East Asia: something which would appear to be highly improbable had the possibility been considered at earlier stages in his life. The Applicant was one of several children born to the family of his parents. He was born in 1952. From that date until 1983 he resided with his parents on their family property some 5 kilometres from the nearest hamlet and 12-13 kilometres from the nearest township. (In this context I identify a hamlet as a community big enough to support one hotel and a township as large enough to support more than one hotel). The principal field of activity of the family was forest work. Before he attained the age of 15 years, the Applicant left school to join his father in that activity. He worked in the bush for many years and in time came to work as a sub-contract faller. That occurred after the advent of the woodchip industry. In that capacity he was one of many contract fallers working in association with the family business alongside skidder operators, tractor drivers, truck drivers and others working in the harvesting and delivery of pulpwood and, where appropriate, sawlogs: the latter being delivered to sawmillers. At some stage which was not specified the family business came to be operated by a company. When that entity came into existence, the parents became Directors of the company. Although the Applicant was a shareholder in the company, I am satisfied that he played no part in the management of its operations. Although based throughout at the home of his parents, his work was carried out from a wide range of sites in the forests of his State.

11. Four things were significant about his work as a sub-contract faller. First, the nature of the work made it appropriate for him to camp in the bush, usually from a Sunday evening until the following Friday. He resided in a caravan. Secondly, his evidence (supported by that of his father) was that all the provisions necessary for his sustenance in the bush were provided for him by his parents, just as they were provided for him when he was residing in their home: without cost to himself. Thirdly, his social opportunities were restricted: a subject to which I shall return. Fourthly, his opportunities to earn moneys were very limited. Unlike his city counterpart, so long as he was in "the bush", opportunities to earn by "moonlighting", or by trading, or by dealing were for all practical purposes non-existent. If he earned significant sums in "the bush" the moneys had to come from contractors such as the family company. Such sources could be undisclosed. If identified, their records might not disclose all that was paid to the Applicant and such as was paid to him could easily have come to his hands as cash.

12. When he turned 17 years of age his father bought him his first car - a new sedan. The Applicant's evidence was that thereafter he was never without one. I accept that. Later he acquired his first four-wheel-drive vehicle for use in the bush and I also accept that thereafter he always had one such vehicle. In time he was to acquire such sporting equipment as a 17'6" boat powered by an outboard motor.

13. When in his late 20's he was "going steady", he contemplated the prospect of building a home. The matter was discussed with his parents who approved the idea. Although nothing in particular was planned, some thought at least seems to have been given to the prospect of him constructing the residence on the family farm. The parents undertook to render assistance by advancing moneys to the Applicant by instalments on the basis of a loan from the family company. The accountant to the family and to the company prepared a minute which was signed by both parents on 1 July 1979. It read:-

"Resolved that an amount of $10,000 or such further amounts as agreed be loaned to (the Applicant) by means of weekly advances of $150 to commence 1st July, 1979 for the purpose of meeting expenditure proposed to be incurred by him in purchasing or building a home, to be used as his principal place of residence."

14. I am satisfied that no other formalities were involved. It was said that advances were made; that amounts advanced were recorded by the Mother in a notebook which she maintained at the time but which has since been destroyed; that the information so supplied by the Mother was incorporated into company accounts prepared by the accountant; and that the amounts so advanced stood at $7,800 at 30 June 1980 and at each material date thereafter. The fact that any money which was advanced was not repaid within the period was not disputed. Neither the possible marriage nor the proposed residence eventuated.

15. Those are the general circumstances which caused the Applicant to contend that the Commissioner's calculations erred in failing to make provision in opening assets for the vehicle which he had on hand at 30 June 1977 and which was traded in on the acquisition of another sedan for $1,800 in the twelve months following; and in failing to bring to account the sum of $7,800 for which he was indebted to the company at 30 June 1980 and at all material times thereafter.

16. As to the matter of the Commissioner overstating the private living expenses of the Applicant, the Commissioner's calculations had brought to account $4,160 in each of the years in question: $80 a week. The Applicant sought to make out a case that his living expenses did not exceed $2,000 per annum in the same period. He claims that in that period the only expenses he had to meet were those of providing for his drinking money. Neither his work nor his other activities in the bush, nor his life on the farm or elsewhere called for an elaborate or expensive wardrobe. I accept that he had little need for clothes beyond those given him annually by his parents. I find that his provisions at home and in the bush were provided by his parents and that he was able to draw on the company's supplies for his fuel for his motor vehicles. His only frequent need for money, he said, was to provide for his nights out at one of the local hotels when he was home from the bush. He acknowledged in evidence that on such nights he would spend up to $50 on beer but I am not persuaded that even after a hard week in the bush that he expended so much on beer for himself only. He acknowledged occasional minor gambling and, as will appear later, contended for at least one substantial and successful bet. He made no acknowledgment of having incurred any of the expenses which commonly attend courtship. Nor did he acknowledge any expenses being incurred on the occasion of annual football excursions or on the occasion of other rare visits interstate. However, one possibly expensive interest was acknowledged. In association with others he had an interest in a thoroughbred racehorse. Little more is known that that it won a race in August 1982. The Applicant claimed at interview in 1983 that he had won $12,000 on that occasion. I am not persuaded he won that much but for the present it suffices to note that the Applicant did not acknowledge that the keeping of the horse put him to any expense. I am not persuaded that that would be so.

17. The Applicant thought that he had undertaken his first journey interstate in about 1971 - 1972. However, in early 1982 he became far more venturesome. Some of his local friends, including a butcher and a school teacher, had previously travelled to South-East Asia and they urged him to join them. As a result he travelled to South-East Asia and stayed there for some six weeks. He met a girl whom he favoured and was attracted to the prospect of setting up in business there. He returned to South-East Asia in the following December and at that time committed himself to the purchase of the bars which he now operates. Having paid a deposit of $1,000 he returned to Australia to gather up his financial resources in order to return and complete his purchase. Such was his hurry to get hack and complete the acquisition that he decided to take the required moneys in cash. So it was that on 7 February 1983 he and his companion were apprehended by the Police and deprived of possession of the $47,000.

18. Still being anxious to proceed with his purchase, he returned home and this time disposed of several assets in order to convert their value to money. Being unable to leave the country lawfully himself, he arranged for one of his companions of the earlier journey to travel on his behalf and arrange completion of the purchase. His friend carried out that mission at the expense of the Applicant and then returned to Australia. The Applicant remained here until sentence was passed in the District Court. Then, at the first opportunity, he proceeded to South-East Asia. There he proceeded to learn the art of conducting such bars under the direction of those more experienced and, in due course, he wed the lady mentioned earlier. Two children have since been born of the marriage.

19. The major factor in the dispute revolves around the competing contentions as to the quantum of cash funds at diverse dates. In the first place, the investigating officer worked to a premise that he would not bring to account any cash fund on hand unless there was some evidence to persuade him that such a fund existed and then only to the extent indicated by the available evidence. That, was not an unreasonable approach. In consequence his figures were dominated by two considerations. By a series of calculations he advanced the contention that the amount of cash on hand at 30 June 1982 - several months before the relevant attempt to leave Australia - the sum of cash on hand must have been $46,450. He determined on a figure of $46,450 as follows:

Known cash deposit to bank account (8.10.1982) 10,000

Cash expenditure post-30 June 1982:

Air fares (known) 5,515

Air fares (estimated) 1,000

Traveller's cheques 8,834.50 15,350

Cash proportion of the $47,000 later confiscated 21,100

$46,450

He acknowledged in evidence that he had made no provision for any savings by the Applicant from earnings during those eight months and no provision for any gambling winnings during the same period for the simple reason that no documentary evidence was placed before him to indicate that either factor could have contributed to any cash fund on hand in February 1983.

20. Secondly, he did not bring to account any figure for a cash fund on hand at 30 June 1977 because he saw no evidence indicating that such a fund had existed. However, he did bring to account the sum of $8,400 as being on hand at 30 June 1978. He reached that conclusion by acknowledging that in August 1978 the Applicant had been able to make a cash deposit to a bank account of $10,000. He assumed that that had been accumulated over a period commencing from 1 July 1977 and on that basis brought to account savings from that date at the rate of $700 per month to deduce a figure of $8,400 at 30 June 1978. Thereafter he allowed for annual increments of $3,175 for each of the four years following plus a further $25,350 in the year of income ended 30 June 1982. He reached that result by assuming that, of the $46,450, the element of $21,100 was accumulated up to 30 June 1982 and the other sums of $10,000 and $15,350 only in that year of income. In that way he made provision in the calculations

for a "cash fund" as follows:-

---- 8,400 11,575 14,750 17,925 46,450

21. The Applicant, on the other hand, contends that those figures were quite inaccurate. He contends that as at 30 June 1977 he had already accumulated as a cash fund held at the home of his parents of not less than $25,000 and that in the years following that sum had ebbed and flowed but so as to result in only $20,000 being on hand at 30 June 1982 rather than $46,450. The balance necessary to make up the February 1983 cash fund impounded was attributed to earnings and gambling winnings.

22. In consequence, one way of explaining the difference between the competing contentions is to say that, by not bringing any cash fund to account as being on hand at 30 June 1977, the Commissioner had understated opening assets in the betterment period by $25,000: and that in fixing the cash fund at close of the betterment period at $46,450 the Commissioner had overstated closing assets by $26,450. If those contentions should be established, the Applicant has explained effectively $51,450: all but $27,151 of the unexplained omissions attributed to him.

23. One feature of this case which has troubled me is whether the sworn testimony presented before me is consistent or not with the contemporary documentary evidence which has also been placed before me. I refer in particular to the evidence of the Mother of the Applicant. In the course of her evidence she stated that she had maintained Cash Books and (based on cheque records as to payments) thought that some of them were still at home. An adjournment was granted to provide her with an opportunity to produce them. Upon the resumption, she produced four handwritten Cash Books purporting to record details of all receipts and payments by the family company. They were not maintained with the order, precision and neatness which would be expected of an experienced clerk, but they show the hallmarks of endeavours to write up accurately the primary records on which they were based. I refer in particular to cheque butts. Her evidence was that she prepared all cheques and that, during most periods, she wrote up the Cash Books, although not always in the same sequence as that in which the cheque books had been used. If any impropriety was involved in the way of use of false names to the advantage of the company, or of members of the family (and in particular the Applicant), or of strangers, she is the person most likely to have known of it.

24. In the course of the investigation a number of circumstances had come under notice in relation to the family company which were reasonably treated with suspicion by the Commissioner's officers. Their suspicions obviously were:-

(a) that some "sub-contractors" did not exist - so that the moneys which left the bank account and which were attributed to them might have been retained by the company or persons associated with it - such as the Applicant; and

(b) that, although some "sub-contractors" to whom recorded payments were made certainly existed, the payments they received were recorded under false names, so that in that way, although the company's expenses were correctly quantified, the identity of the payees was concealed - the Applicant might have been such a person.

25. Such suspicions seemed to be all the more reasonable when the Cash Book recorded names which the Commissioner could not relate to taxpayers; some names were so common as to be doubted, despite the fact that many persons are possessed of surnames which are common; that several persons, including some with the same initial or initials, are recorded under the family surname of the Applicant; and that some cheques in favour of payees seemingly not closely associated with the family were cashed by the Mother. A further circumstance was that my examination of the Cash Book produced - only conducted after the hearing - indicated that, in the twelve months to 30 June 1980, $27,226 was paid by the company to the Applicant for his services as a "sub-contractor" when, in the same period, he only acknowledged receipts as a sub-contractor of $18,326. The difference is largely explained by the contention that $7,800 had been loaned to the Applicant during that period pursuant to the resolution recorded as of 1 July 1979. It was said that, although the moneys so advanced were not specially identified in the Cash Book, that there was a record maintained by the Mother until the accounts of the company were professionally prepared and that those records were then no longer preserved. It is also to be noted that, even so, a discrepancy of $1,100 in what should have been" a simple calculation remains unexplained. On the other hand, neither Mother nor son was called on to explain the difference.

26. In considering all of the foregoing it is important to bear in mind, if it be true that the Applicant was substantially confined to the bush, opportunities to earn money otherwise than through the family company were very limited indeed. Similarly, opportunities to spend would be restricted. Those comments do not take into account one possibility: that the Applicant was felling in the bush for another contractor and the income from that source was not disclosed. His evidence, by implication, denied that in as much as his evidence was to the effect that he was felling only for the family company. That assertion was not challenged. Nor was any evidence presented which suggested that at the relevant times a "faller" such as the Applicant who was working skillfully and diligently on a full-time basis should have earned more than the Applicant received from the family company. Although the Commissioner bears no obligation to make out a case establishing any particular source of income, I am persuaded on the balance of probabilities that in fact the Applicant had only one employer in the bush: the family company. On the other hand I recognize that, if his time commitment to the bush was much less than has been suggested, there would have been ample opportunities to pursue other income-earning activities such as have not been acknowledged at all. But there was no suggestion that that was so either. Then again, if there had been collusion, or something equivalent to it, between the Applicant and the family company to avoid tax, it might reasonably be expected that inconsistencies would appear between the returns of the Applicant and the records of the company. However, despite the production by the Applicant's Mother of the Cash Books and, through him, of some of the annual accounts of the company, those possibilities were not extensively pursued in cross-examination and no significant inconsistency was established.

27. In face of all that an assessment of the evidence of the Mother is of critical importance. She gave evidence; she produced the Cash Books after the adjournment allowed for the purpose; she was cross-examined as to some matters; and was not cross-examined as to other matters which it is not unreasonable to think might have been the subject of cross-examination. She denied being party to or aware of any falsification in those records in any respect. I accept that. The names of strangers as recorded may not have been accurate, but I accept that she recorded those names as she knew them. Further, I find nothing inherently unreasonable in the circumstance that, in a community and environment such as that in which the company operated, and particularly having regard to difficulties the workers would have had in endeavouring to cash large cheques outside banking hours, the Mother, having drawn cheques in favour of workers who were strangers, if requested to do so, would cash them for those workers. I find that on the balance of probabilities the assessable income of the Applicant was confined to earnings from the family company and interest.

THE CASH FUND

28. The issues which lie at the heart of this reference all relate to funds held in cash. Resolution of those issues calls for findings of fact and those findings have to be made on the balance of probabilities: recognizing all the while that, when one is persuaded by that standard, one accepts that it is quite possible that one is in error. The findings of fact which have to be made as to the amounts at which the fund stood have to be made in relation to the amount on hand at the commencement of the betterment period (30 June 1977); at the close of the betterment period (30 June 1982); and at each 30 June in between those dates. It is possible that findings can and should be made as to the amounts of cash on hand at other intermediate dates but it is the findings of fact which are made in relation to those dates which will have a direct bearing on the assessment of taxable income for each of the years under reference. Over and above the findings as to dates within the betterment period, a consideration of the availability and application of funds after 30 June 1982 will be relevant. Similarly although, because of the passage of time it is less easy to be precise about the matter, it is also appropriate to consider such evidence as there is as to activities undertaken prior to 30 June 1977 for it is those activities which will have determined the extent of any cash fund on hand at that date.

29. The evidence of the accumulation of the cash fund, as is to be expected, was imprecise. Both parents gave evidence that they knew he held large sums in cash in his room but neither claimed to know even approximately how much. The mother believed that it would have been sufficient to enable the Applicant to repay the $7,800 loan but I accept that she did not call for that to be done. The Applicant's contention was that from childhood he had accumulated cash, occasionally drawing on the fund to deposit large amounts (cf. Account 678) or to lend to friends. Having regard to his limited earnings in earlier years as proved by assessments of tax for those years, the amount claimed to have been on hand is remarkably high. But the personal history of the Applicant was also exceptional. At all times he resided with his parents; they (or the company) bore nearly all the costs of his accommodation, his clothing, his food, and his fuel. His spending opportunities were limited and his tastes neither extravagant nor very expensive. In those circumstances it is likely that his savings would have substantially exceeded those of an urban-dweller of the same age and earning capacity.

32. There is no difference between the parties as to the principles to be applied in reaching those determinations but the conclusions for which they respectively contend differ substantially, principally because each argues from a different premise. Understandably, from the Commissioner's point of view, the starting point was an endeavour to determine the amount of cash on hand at 30 June 1982. Having fixed upon an amount on hand for that date other judgments were made by working backwards to findings consistent with the finding adopted for 30 June 1982. Conversely, the case basically argued for the Applicant was that there was not at any time any avoidance of taxable income and, therefore, there must have been on hand at 30 June 1982 and at each earlier material date a fund which, when coupled with other receipts, was sufficient to account for the expenditure, identified by the Commissioner. (In so summarizing the argument for the Applicant, for the sake of simplicity of expression, I have assumed that there never was a dispute about the "undisputed omissions" amounting overall to $6,751. I have not overlooked its significance).

33. The figure fixed upon by the Commissioner's officers in their calculations as being the amount on hand at 30 June 1982 was $46,450. They determined upon that sum on the basis that that sum needed to be on hand in order to account for the known expenditures and applications of funds to 7 February 1983.

34. As to the commencement of the betterment period, as the Commissioner's officers found no documentary evidence which persuaded that any cash fund existed at 30 June 1977, they concluded that there was no such fund. The first consequence flowing from those two determinations on the part of the Commissioner is that the Applicant is confronted with the task of proving that, in this respect, the increased cost of his net assets at 30 June 1982 was in this respect something less that $46,450; and that, to the extent to which there was an increase during that period, it was not gained by way of the derivation of assessable income.

35. But, because taxable income has to be measured on an annual basis, it is not sufficient to attribute the increase in net assets of $46,450 to the period: the increase must be distributed over the years. That calls for findings as to the size of the cash fund on hand at 30 June in each year commencing with 30 June 1978. In addressing that task the Commissioner's officers were satisfied that a sum of $10,000 identified as deposited to a bank account on 25 August 1978 did not reflect any asset on hand but not brought to account at 30 June 1977. Accordingly, as part of the process of assessment, it was concluded that the $10,000 had come to hand since 30 June 1977; that it had come to hand at the rate of the order of $700 per month to 30 June 1978; and that it had all come to hand as assessable income. In consequence, it was concluded that $8,400 (12 x $700 per month) constituted cash on hand at 30 June 1978, whereas no cash was on hand at 30 June 1977. It then remained to distribute $38,050 over the remaining four, years of the betterment. Although, by reason of findings previously made, $1,600 was considered to have come to hand in the first two months of the year of income ended 30 June 1979, only a further $1,575 was considered as having come to hand in the remaining ten months of that year - a total for the year of $3,175. Similarly, amounts of $3,175 were considered as coming to hand during the years of income ended 30 June 1980 and 1981. It then followed that $28,525 must have come to hand during the year of income ended 30 June 1982. A number of comments may be made. If the $46,450 had been spread equally over the sixty months of the betterment, the rate of accretion would have been of the order of $775 per month. But, as the Commissioner's figures have been composed, the rate of accretion at $700 per month in the first fourteen months fell to well under $200 per month in the third and remaining months of the second year, rising to some $265 per month in each month of the next two years; and then rising to nearly $2,400 per month for the 1982 year. That the rate of evasion should have fallen after the first year and fallen substantially, as is alleged, is somewhat surprising. So too is the thought that the rate of evasion would increase some ninefold during the 1982 year. On the other hand, to have a substantial part of the omissions assessed in only the last year of the period would confer a substantial advantage on the Applicant in that the period during which he would be answerable for a compensatory rate of interest by way of additional tax would be much less. All in all I find nothing unreasonable about the approach of the Commissioner to the task which faced him. But that is far from saying that the results reflected by the assessments are correct or that the Applicant has failed to discharge the burden he bears of showing the assessments to be excessive.

36. Having weighed the considerations favouring the Commissioner (the burden of proof; the absence of documentary records; the logical possibility of undetected and, therefore, unknown sources of income; and the possibility of undetected income from known sources) and weighed them against the considerations favouring the Applicant (his evidence and that of his parents - the effect of which would be to deny him the opportunity to have had any other significant source of income or any additional income from or through the family company; the evidence that he had maintained substantial sums of cash; the evidence indicating that from time to time he did produce substantial sums of cash; and the evidence of receipts post 30 June 1982 - up to $18,380 from earnings; equipment sales $1,519; and possible racing winnings: to be measured against recorded expenses requiring a cash outlay of $3,615 and living expenses to February 1983) I am satisfied that, on balance, the calculations made on behalf of the Commissioner are excessive and substantially so.

37. In determining to what extent the assessments are in this respect excessive, I have had to bear in mind:-

(a) that the figures put forward on behalf of the Applicant as to cash on hand to 30 June 1982, even when coupled with the monetary resources apparently available between 30 June 1982 and February 1983, are not sufficient to wholly answer the contentions of the Commissioner;

(b) the Applicant has not advanced any evidence to challenge the assessments to the extent of $6,751; and

(c) although I am persuaded that claimed betting wins during the betterment period had been overstated, I am not persuaded that there were no such net betting gains even though any claim to answer the Commissioner's allegations by reference to such gains was abandoned.

38. I bear it in mind that what is essentially called for in this determination is a finding of *fact as to the quantum of taxable income and not a multiplicity of findings of fact as to the elements of the taxpayer's affairs which gave rise to that taxable income. That being so, I say no more about the matter of the cash fund than that, on the balance of probabilities, I am satisfied that the Applicant was at all material times seized of a substantial cash fund such that, had the opening fund been brought to account as an asset and provision been made for its subsequent reduction as an alternative to the claims of the Commissioner, the "betterment" would have been substantially explained in the sum of $51,450.

CAR ($4,800) AND LOAN ($7,800)

39. For the reasons already indicated, I accept the Applicant's explanation in relation to these matters and find that the sums to be brought to account are $1,800 and $7,800 respectively.

COST OF LIVING

40. The remaining matter to be addressed as to taxable income relates to "cost of living". Bearing in mind the findings I have made as to the lifestyle of the Applicant, particularly in the earlier years of the betterment period, I consider the item "other private expenditure" to be overstated in the earlier years but not for the year ended 30 June 1982. I think a more reasonable provision would be $40, $50, $60, $70, $80 per week in the years of income ended 30 June 1978 to 1982 inclusive. On that basis I would allow for the reduction in "other private expenditure" of $2,080, $1,560, $1,040 and $520 in the years of income ended 30 June 1978 to 1981 inclusive.

CONCLUSIONS

41. Having been persuaded on the balance of probabilities:-

(a) that the Applicant had no source of assessable income other than interest and his earnings from working in the bush;

(b) that in the bush he had worked only for the family company;

(c) that all income from the family company had been returned; and notwithstanding

(d) the imprecision of his evidence;

(e) suspicions as to the evidence of the cash fund and as to work for the family company being the only source of bush income; and

(f) concern that there was no attempt to establish an explanation for alleged omissions of $6,751;

nonetheless, on the balance of probabilities, I have concluded that the assessments are all excessive. However, the Applicant has failed to persuade me that the increased assessments are wholly excessive [cf s190(b)].

42. To give effect to the findings I have made reducing the overall understatement of taxable income simply by adjustments to the annual assessments of income tax which are in issue would produce results seemingly so odd as to make the adjustments themselves quite improbable. Therefore, it could be argued, no adjustments ought be made because to do so would produce results which render quite improbable the premise upon which they are based.

43. However, that arises because the assessments are necessarily expressed in precise figures which present an image of certainty and accuracy which, upon examination, can be quite illusory. That is so because of differences in the character of the figures brought to account in determining taxable income in particular sums assessed year by year. Some items are so precisely known and measured that there can be no basis for challenge to them - bank account balances provide an example. Other items are estimates which have no effect beyond the year to which they relate - personal living expenses are an example. Another category is far more complicated in its effect. The cash fund is such an example. The findings in relation to it affect the assessments in many ways. In relation to the entire betterment period the taxpayer is called upon to account for the difference between the opening cash fund and the closing cash fund. Either figure may be inaccurate. The inaccuracy may be to the advantage or the detriment of the taxpayer or of the community. But it would be a rare case in which the sum of the cash fund attributed to any intermediate year is as precise as, let alone more precise than, the figures adopted for opening and closing cash funds. That being so, it can very easily be that the figures adopted by the Commissioner in distributing the effect of the cash fund throughout the betterment may be quite inaccurate even though the total sum to be accounted for is not shown to be wrong: a fortiori if an Applicant establishes that the aggregate figure is incorrect. CI say that fully recognizing the soundness of the decision of the High Court of Australia in Trautwein v FCT [(1936) [1936] HCA 77; 56 CLR 63} to the effect that the improbability of an arbitrary distribution of an undisputed aggregate figure for omitted income being correct is not alone sufficient to establish any one, or all, of the assessments of the period to be excessive, let alone to establish the extent of the excess.] Recognizing those factors persuades me that I should distribute the understatements of income so as to produce what seems to me to be a reasonable result.

A - TAXABLE INCOME

44. I have not been persuaded as to $12,351 that the assessments of taxable income before me are excessive. That being so, I will direct that the taxable income of the Applicant for the years of income ended 30 June 1978 to 1982 inclusive should be reduced from $15,988 to $5,894; from $25,343 to .$10,186; from $16,086 to $12,184; from $27,769 to $11,262 and from $33,399 to $12,809 respectively.

B - ADDITIONAL TAX

45. There remains the question of penalties imposed by way of additional tax. I am satisfied that the question of remission of additional tax was approached by applying the principles expressed in the directions of the Commissioner as recorded in his Income Tax Rulings. On that basis, in addition to the compensatory rate of interest exacted, a culpability factor was fixed upon at 50% of tax avoided. The reasons for fixing upon the latter rate were said to be to the extent of the omissions (approximately 60% of income assessed); the attempted movement of moneys from Australia; and especially the large amount of cash involved. In view of the findings I have made, the omissions are not a substantial percentage of taxable income. Further, the circumstance that large amounts of cash were involved does not bear upon the gravity of the omissions. However, I accept that the attempted movement of cash from Australia is material. Had it been effected successfully, the prospect of exposing such evasion of income tax as there was would have been much reduced. However, even so I am not persuaded that those considerations warranted exacting a greater penalty than is commonplace and is frequently exacted of persons more deserving of punishment than the Applicant.

46. For those reasons, I will direct that additional tax be reduced by lowering the "culpability factor" as related to the reduced taxable incomes to 40% of tax avoided.
