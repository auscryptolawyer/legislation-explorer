Robinson and Commissioner of Taxation (Taxation and business) [2026] ARTA 50 (16 January 2026)

Applicant/s: Troy Robinson

TCR Group Pty Ltd

Respondent: Commissioner of Taxation

Tribunal Number: 2024/6566, 2024/6804

Tribunal: General Member C Willis

Place: Melbourne

Date: 16 January 2026

Decision: The Tribunal varies the objection decision issued to TCR dated 2 July 2024 such that the value of the taxable supply of the Property from TCR to Mr Robinson is to be determined under section 9-75 of the A New Tax System (Goods and Services Tax) Act 1999 (Cth) by reference to the amount of $2.25 million. The Tribunal otherwise affirms this objection decision.

The Tribunal sets aside the objection decision issued to Mr Robinson dated 2 July 2024 and in substitution makes a decision that Mr Robinson’s objection is allowed.

Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. Mr Troy Robinson (Mr Robinson) is the sole shareholder and director of TCR Group Pty Ltd (TCR). Mr Robinson and TCR are the Applicants in these two proceedings (together ‘the Applicants’). On the basis of the relationship between Mr Robinson and TCR, and the taxation issues in both proceedings arising from the same transactions, the Tribunal determined that it was appropriate for these proceedings to be case managed and heard together.
  2. Broadly, the income taxation and goods and services tax (GST) issues arise from the Applicants’ participation in transactions relating to the purchase, subdivision and sale of certain land in a coastal region of Victoria. The Applicants’ characterisation of these transactions and background events differs to that of the Commissioner of Taxation (the Respondent), giving rise to the dispute between the parties over the appropriate income taxation and GST treatment of the Applicants.

BACKGROUND

Timeline of key transactions and events

  1. The following key events and transactions are evidenced by documents filed with the Tribunal, including publicly available information, and provide context for the disputed matters:
    (a) A contract[1] dated on or around 19 March 2013 was entered into between ‘Mr Troy Charles Robinson and/or nominee’ as purchaser and a third party vendor for the sale of certain land in Victoria. This was referred to by the parties as the ‘Parent Block’ to distinguish it from the lots into which it was later subdivided. The purchase price of the Parent Block was $865,000, with a deposit of $86,500 having been paid on 19 March 2013 and the balance of $778,500 being payable at settlement.

    (b) TCR was registered on 20 May 2013 with Mr Robinson as sole director, company secretary and shareholder.[2]

    (c) Settlement of the sale of the Parent Block occurred on 28 June 2013. On that day a transfer of land relating to the Parent Block was lodged with Land Victoria.[3] The transferee was TCR, and the transfer form was signed by Mr Robinson as sole director and company secretary of TCR.

    (d) A building permit was issued in relation to the Parent Block on 18 July 2014 for the construction of a dwelling, pool house and carport.[4] The owner of the building and land subject to the permit was TCR.

    (e) A plan of subdivision was registered for the Parent Block on 27 January 2016, with the Parent Block being subdivided into four lots (and an area of common property).[5]

    (f) One lot within the Parent Block[6] was sold to a third party under a contract dated 6 January 2016 for a purchase price of $2.4 million. The vendor under this contract for sale was TCR. Settlement of this sale occurred in April 2016.

    (g) A second lot within the Parent Block was the subject of a contract of sale dated 7 December 2016 for a purchase price of $600,000. This lot was described as ‘vacant land.’[7] The vendor of this lot was TCR. Settlement of this sale occurred in January 2017.

    (h) A building contract dated 25 January 2017 for the construction of a home on a further lot (being the lot the subject of this dispute, the ‘Property’) was entered into by TCR as the ‘owner’ with Russell Constructions Pty Ltd (Russell Constructions).[8]

    (i) The Property was the subject of a contract of sale between Mr Robinson as vendor and a third party (T Co) as purchaser dated 14 July 2018, for a purchase price of $4.128 million.[9]

    (j) Title to the Property had been transferred from TCR to Mr Robinson on 9 October 2018 for a stated consideration of $2.25 million.[10]

    (k) Settlement of the sale of the Property to T Co occurred on 15 October 2018.[11]

  2. Surrounding these core transactions and events are a number of factual matters, the characterisation of which are the subject of significant disagreement between the parties, giving rise to the dispute over the taxation treatment of the Applicants. These matters, and the Tribunal’s view on particular factual points, are set out in greater detail below.

Purchase and subdivision of Parent Block and sale of lots including the Property

  1. A number of documents relating to the purchase and subdivision of the Parent Block, and in relation to building work on the Property and other lots, were filed with the Tribunal. Mr Robinson also gave evidence about his recollection of the circumstances surrounding the purchase and subdivision. Written witness statements were provided by certain individuals, some of whom attended the hearing and were cross-examined by the Respondent’s counsel.
  2. Mr Robinson told the Tribunal about the circumstances leading up to the purchase of the Parent Block. He said that the vendor of the Parent Block had previously tried to obtain approval to subdivide the Parent Block and having failed to do so decided to sell that land. However, Mr Robinson felt that development of this land was possible.
  3. It is not disputed that title to the Parent Block was transferred from the vendor to TCR in June 2013.
  4. Five versions of a plan of subdivision of the Parent Block were prepared by Speedie Development Consultants Pty Ltd (Speedie).[12] The versions were dated from May 2013 to July 2013. They show four lots together with a common property lot. Although the previous owner of the Parent Block had been unsuccessful in subdividing the Parent Block, approval for subdivision was obtained after the land was transferred to TCR. Mr Robinson said that the Applicants had to contend with a number of objections to his proposed subdivision and construction which delayed the relevant approvals.[13]
  5. From January 2014 to November 2015 Speedie undertook work relating to the subdivision of the Parent Block, together with surveying and engineering design, and supervision work relating to sewerage, water supply and drainage. Invoices for this work were addressed to TCR. [14]
  6. A contract was entered into around June 2014 with Niven Builders for construction of a new home on the Parent Block. An invoice dated 11 June 2014 for the deposit under the building contract was addressed to ‘TCR Developments Troy Robinson [Address]’.[15] A building permit was issued dated 18 July 2014 for the construction of a dwelling, pool house and carport at the Parent Block.[16] The building permit stated that an Occupancy Permit was required prior to occupation or use of the building. Further invoices were rendered by Niven Builders in October 2014, December 2014 and February 2015 in relation to the construction at the Parent Block.[17] These invoices were rendered to ‘TCR Developments Troy Robinson.’ Invoices were also rendered across 2014 and 2015 for various electrical and building supplies, flooring and household fixtures and appliances.[18] Some of these invoices are addressed to Mr Robinson, others to TCR and some addressed to both TCR and Mr Robinson.[19] Receipts for some of these invoices, including invoices addressed to TCR, appear to be for payments from accounts in Mr Robinson’s name. This residence appears to have been constructed on the lot sold to a third party in January 2016 (that is, not the Property).
  7. From February to August 2016, the Applicants indicated that design and planning was undertaken for the house to be built on the Property.[20]
  8. On 20 September 2016 Mr Robinson signed an agreement for a builder to apply for a swimming pool permit on the Property.[21] Mr Robinson identified himself in this document as the owner of the Property.
  9. On 20 January 2017 a policy for domestic building insurance in relation to the construction of a single dwelling on the Property was issued by QBE Insurance in Mr Robinson’s name as the ‘building owner’.[22] There is no reference to TCR in either the application for the insurance or the insurance policy as issued.
  10. However, when the building contract for construction of ‘new dwelling, pool, outbuildings, tennis court’ on the Property was entered into with Russell Construction on 25 January 2017, the ‘owner’ was stated to be TCR. TCR was also identified as the insured party under the QBE Insurance policy, contrary to what was stated in the policy.[23] The contract price for the construction was $1.4 million.
  11. A further building construction insurance policy taken out on 12 April 2017 refers to TCR and Russell Constructions being the insured parties.[24]
  12. Statements from April and May 2018 relating to a business bank account held by TCR with Bendigo Bank indicate payment of wages and purchases of what may be building or landscaping related materials with reference to ‘97E’ and ‘97c’, the latter being the Property.
  13. Mr Robinson said that the house at the Property was built by mid-February 2018 and he moved in during March 2018. Copies of utilities accounts and correspondence in support of his residing in the Property from March to October 2018 were provided.[25] This material included a request to Telstra in May 2018 for a broadband connection at the Property[26], a request for a new electrical connection to the Property dated 13 March 2018 and an electricity account dated November 2018 indicating a significant usage of electricity between May and October 2018. The Tribunal observes that each of these items[27] was requested by or addressed to TCR, rather than Mr Robinson. The Applicants’ former representative had previously told the Respondent in March 2020 that because the legal title to the Property was in TCR’s name, Mr Robinson was unable to have utility bills in his personal name.[28]
  14. Further, there was a delay in getting an electricity meter connected to the Property and Mr Robinson therefore used power from another lot within the subdivision until the meter was connected in May 2018. This is generally consistent with the evidence of Mr S, the electrical contractor for the subdivision, who said that he installed a temporary power pole to another lot in mid-2017 for use in construction at the Property, that he requested a meter connection for the Property in early March 2018, and that while waiting for the meter connection he set up a temporary line from the other lot to the Property.
  15. The request for the broadband connection and the subsequent connection by Telstra were made in May 2018, some months after the date Mr Robinson said that he moved into the Property. The account issued in May 2018 does not describe actual usage of the service but represents advance billing of a ‘bundle’ fee.
  16. The Applicants’ former representative also told the Respondent that there were no water accounts as a water meter was not installed at the Property until October 2018, although water had been connected to all of the lots and was available at the Property.
  17. An Occupancy Permit was issued for the Property on 21 June 2018.[29] This was issued to TCR as the ‘owner’ of the Property. Mr Robinson acknowledged that in a strict legal sense he may not have been permitted to live in the Property prior to the issue of the Occupancy Certificate. However, as a practical matter he said that he had in fact moved in before June 2018. He said that he did not want to leave the Property unoccupied for security reasons, being concerned about the potential risk of theft from the premises.[30]
  18. The Applicants said that there was a delay in having the Occupancy Permit issued due to an issue with windows being installed that were different to those specified in the planning permit, requiring an amended planning permit.[31] The Tribunal accepts that there was a delay in issuing the Occupancy Permit for this reason, consistent with correspondence from the relevant shire in June 2018 and the issuing of an amendment to the planning permit. An undated letter from Russell Constructions which was provided to the Respondent in March 2020 also referred to the delay due to windows but said that the Property was ‘finished to a certificate of occupancy’ standard in March 2018.[32] Mr Russell was unable to provide further evidence to the Tribunal due to ill health, and in the absence of other evidence corroborating the ‘completed’ status of Property, the Tribunal cannot make a finding as to whether construction at the Property was complete to this standard at that time.
  19. A statement provided by VicRoads of Mr Robinson’s address for the purposes of his Victorian driver’s licence records the Property as his address from 27 June 2018 to 28 May 2019.[33] Prior to and after that period his address given was that of his parents’ home.
  20. A valuation of the Property was prepared by Preston Rowe Paterson dated 24 May 2018 (the PRP Valuation).[34] An email requesting the valuation was sent by Mr Robinson’s father on 15 May 2018, apparently attaching ‘plans’ for the Property and including the message ‘Troys address is [address]. He is hoping to move into [Property] once the CofO is issued.’[35] The address given for Mr Robinson was that of his parents. It was not clear what the ‘plans’ referenced in the email were. Appendix C to the PRP Valuation contained the ‘Registered Plan’ being the plan of subdivision. The term CofO refers to a certificate of occupancy (presumably the Occupancy Permit). The date of the PRP Valuation reflected the date upon which PRP undertook a site inspection of the Property. The PRP Valuation gave a valuation of $2.25 million on the basis of ‘current market value’ and was exclusive of GST. ‘Market value’ was defined as per the International Valuation Standards 2017[36] for the purposes of the PRP Valuation as:
    ‘... the estimated amount for which an asset or liability should exchange on the valuation date between a willing buyer and a willing seller in an arm’s length transaction, after proper marketing and where the parties had each acted knowledgeably, prudently and without compulsion.’
  21. The particular reasons which lead to the Property being placed on the market are not entirely clear. Correspondence from Mr Vrahos, one of the Applicants’ former advisors, to the Respondent suggested that around May 2018 a previous advisor, DOA, had made Mr Robinson aware of significant taxation liabilities that were unexpected (at least to Mr Robinson)[37], and that the need to find funds to pay these taxation liabilities and other business debts meant that Mr Robinson was willing to consider listing the Property on an ‘opportunistic’ basis, but was not necessarily committed to selling.[38] The Respondent submitted to the Tribunal that the significant debt taken on by the Applicants was such that they could not have continued to hold the Property for any significant period of time, noting also that the refinancing that had been obtained from LaTrobe was for a very limited period and apparently given on the basis of a contemplated sale of the Property. This was disputed by Mr Robinson who believed that he would have been able to retain the Property long term.[39]
  22. On 27 June 2018 an Exclusive Sale Authority[40] for the sale of the Property was entered into between Mr Robinson as vendor and One Agency Peninsula as the agent. Mr Robinson’s father is named as the relevant representative of the agent. The agent’s stated estimate of the selling price was $3.9 million to $4.3 million.
  23. In relation to the process for the sale of the Property Mr Robinson told the Tribunal that:
    (a) Although the estimated selling price in the Exclusive Sale Authority was higher than the PRP Valuation that had been obtained, he and his father thought it was worth trying to ‘test the market’ and see if he could obtain $3.9 million.[41] He had spent more on the construction of the Property than the amount of the PRP Valuation.

    (b) Four parties inspected the Property in the first week it was on the market, with one offer being received from the ultimate purchaser who wanted Mr Robinson to take the Property off the market. Furniture already at the Property was included in the purchase.[42]

    (c) He had not expected to achieve a sale price of $4.128 million, based on the PRP Valuation and negative feedback from his finance broker about difficulties he would face in getting a further loan of $1.835 million.[43]

    (d) The Property was first advertised on 27 June 2018, and the contract for sale to T Co was signed on 14 July 2018.

  24. The purchase price paid by T Co was at the mid-point of the selling price estimate set out in the Exclusive Sale Authority.
  25. Mr P, the director of the purchaser T Co, provided a statutory declaration dated 25 February 2022 by way of a witness statement and attended the hearing by telephone for cross-examination by the Respondent’s counsel on his evidence. Mr P stated that:
    (a) He was the sole director of T Co which bought the Property as trustee of his family trust.

    (b) He was also the founding director of a residential property development and development advisory company. Although he worked in the property sector, he confirmed that he did not have any property valuation qualifications.[44]

    (c) In 2017 and 2018 he and his (now former) wife had been looking for a weekend retreat property. At that time they were experiencing marital difficulties and hoped that such a property might allow them to spend more time together and improve their relationship. They had looked at various properties and sites during this period, bidding unsuccessfully at one auction and encountering prospective planning control issues with another property. He said that there were not many properties on the market around that time.[45]

    (d) In July 2018 they inspected the Property, initially in the context of potentially purchasing a nearby vacant block and getting ideas for construction of a new build. However, they were impressed by the quality of the Property and decided they would prefer to buy that rather than spend years in constructing a new home or renovating an older home.

    (e) They had funds available following recent successful business ventures and wanted to have their new retreat available for the 2018/2019 summer season. His view was that they needed to make a ‘substantial offer above what was otherwise a benchmark considering what was available on the market at the same time and having regard to the fact that the Property lacked beach views and access’ in order to have the Property taken off the market so early in its sale campaign. He described himself as being in a ‘position of weakness’ in the negotiation for the sale, and that although he paid ‘a high price’ this was balanced against the convenience offered by the Property and the fact that they intended to hold it for the long term.[46]

    (f) The Property remained owned by T Co after Mr P and his wife separated and he hoped to retain ownership of it.

  26. Mr H, a real estate agent, also provided a witness statement in the form of a statutory declaration dated 13 December 2021 and appeared at the hearing by telephone for cross-examination by the Respondent’s counsel. Mr H told the Tribunal that:
    (a) He was the selling agent for the vendor of the Parent Block and for one of the lots subdivided from the Parent Block.

    (b) He had first met Mr Robinson in 2013 when Mr Robinson inspected the Parent Block. In his statement he said that prior to purchasing the Parent Block Mr Robinson had ‘shared his plans on building a principal place of residence in [suburb] on one of the lots once subdivided.’

    (c) He could not recall who was named as purchaser in relation to the sale of the Parent Block or into what name the Parent Block was transferred. His said that in the past it was not unusual for a sale contract to state a purchaser ‘and/or nominee.’ The role of the agent was to sign up the purchaser but during the settlement period it was more a matter for solicitors to deal with nominees.[47]

    (d) After Mr Robinson retained him in 2016 as the selling agent for the other lot, Mr H’s impression was that Mr Robinson was keen to start building his new home at the Property.

    (e) As a local real estate agent he was aware of and interested in properties in the area. He had visited the Property in early January 2018 when construction was still underway, and said that Mr Robinson was ‘looking forward to moving in.’ He confirmed that he was not sure when Mr Robinson actually moved in, although he believed Mr Robinson had done so because Mr H had been to the Property when Mr Robinson was there.[48]

    (f) The town where the Property is located is not large, hence as a local real estate agent he could remember property sales in that area.[49]

    (g) He had seen both the Property and the house built on another lot in the Parent Block subdivision and described the Property as ‘really, really impressive.’[50]

    (h) He confirmed that he had not provided any opinion on the market value of the Property or of the other lot which he had sold at the time those properties were sold. However, he suggested that because those two properties were next to each other and were of comparable size, he would have expected the Property to sell for a similar amount to the other lot and therefore the sale price of the Property was ‘exceptional.’[51]

    (i) He could not recall whether the other lot was sold furnished or whether it included an art installation similar to that which was found at the Property.

Financing arrangements for the subdivision and construction at the Property

  1. The purchase price of the Parent Block was $865,000. A deposit of $86,500 was paid in late March 2013 by way of a cheque drawn on a bank account held in the joint names of Mr Robinson and Ms D.[52]
  2. Copies of email correspondence indicate that after Mr Robinson’s offer on the Parent Block was accepted by the vendor, Mr Robinson contacted a bank manager at NAB about arranging finance for the purchase. A condition of the contract of sale was that NAB would approve a loan for $692,000 by a specified date.[53] The email correspondence suggests that in the first couple of weeks of April 2013 he was having to follow up the bank about the financing arrangements, and the finance condition in the contract had not been satisfied.
  3. In an email dated 16 April 2013[54], the bank manager at NAB confirmed details of a meeting between him and Mr Robinson about a home loan. The email noted that Mr Robinson had advised that the loan would be in Mr Robinson’s name (and not in another entity), that it would take three to four months to subdivide the land, and that Mr Robinson had a verbal offer from his father and brother for them to each buy a lot if he could not sell them. The email also noted that ‘Once subdivided land is sold, plan is to pay down this facility in full.’
  4. On 17 April 2013 Mr Robinson made a home loan application to NAB.[55] In the application Mr Robinson indicated his purchase of land (the Parent Block) for construction of a new home (or new homes). He was seeking a loan of 80% of the purchase price and associated costs and could make a personal contribution of $173,000. He described his occupation as a self-employed carpenter, but also stated that he received ‘consulting fees’ of $1,500 per week and rental income of at least $50,000 a year. He indicated that he had been living at his parents’ home for a month, prior to which he lived at an address in Blairgowrie for 18 months. He provided details of his income, outgoings, assets and debts. He stated that his assets included joint interests in two investment properties[56], cash in four bank accounts, two motor vehicles, a couple of trailers, tools, home contents, superannuation and a ‘loan’ of $120,000. He referred to goodwill of $600,000 to $800,000 in his business. His liabilities included three credit cards with NAB and two other financial institutions. He identified his accountant as David Osmond & Associates (DOA) and his lawyers as McGuiness & Hosking.
  5. Around 18 April 2013, an employee of NAB emailed McGuiness & Hosking to advise that ‘Based on the information our client Troy Robinson has provided NAB, we support his purchase for the property [Parent Block]’.[57]
  6. Although Mr Robinson had initially applied for a (personal) home loan, NAB finance to complete the purchase of the Parent Block ultimately came in the form of a business loan to TCR. Mr Robinson told the Tribunal, after he lodged the home loan application NAB told him that because he wanted to subdivide the Parent Block and sell lots, he was required to incorporate a company and apply for a business loan.[58] There is no contemporaneous documentation indicating what might have been discussed between 18 April 2013 (the date of the email from NAB to McGuiness & Hosking confirming support for Mr Robinson’s purchase) and 20 May 2013 (when TCR was incorporated).
  7. By letter dated 25 June 2013 to Mr Robinson, NAB advised that Mr Robinson’s application for a ‘NAB Business Markets – Flexible Rate Loan’ had been approved with a facility limit of $605,500.[59] This Letter of Offer was expressed as being made to ‘Troy Charles Robinson’, who was the ‘customer’ and the ‘you’ referred to in the attached terms and conditions. The required security was stated to be a registered mortgage over the Parent Block and a ‘T/D Letter of Set-Off’ to be provided by Mr Robinson in the amount of $36,500.
  8. This was followed by a further letter dated 27 June 2013, in similar terms to the above, but addressed to ‘The Director, TCR Group Pty Ltd’ and referring to TCR as the customer and ‘you’ referred to in the terms and conditions.[60] The required security was stated to be a registered mortgage over the Parent Block, a ‘Term Deposit Letter of Set Off’ over an unspecified term deposit, a security interest and charge over all present and future rights, property and undertaking of TCR, and a personal guarantee to be provided by Mr Robinson in the amount of $605,500.
  9. The reason for the change in the NAB correspondence from 25 June to 27 June is not clear, however the 25 June letter is unsigned whereas the 27 June letter is signed by an authorised officer of NAB on behalf of the bank, and Mr Robinson for TCR.[61] Similarly, there is a signed Term Deposit Letter of Set-Off dated 27 June 2013[62] which identifies TCR as the depositor (although again it does not identify the particular term deposit) and a signed ‘Letter of Instruction – Loan Drawdown’ for/to an account in the name of TCR.[63] Mr Robinson said that his solicitors (McGuiness & Hosking) received the 25 June version of the documentation[64] from NAB for review and advised him that the arrangements were complex and should be explained to him by NAB. Mr Robinson’s recollection was that an employee of NAB had met with Mr Robinson and his father for the purpose of signing the documentation, but the NAB employee realised that he ‘had brought the wrong paperwork’ and would need to get the documents amended and return.[65] Mr Robinson subsequently went to a branch of NAB to sign the documents, which he said he did without reading them because he assumed that they were correct and he was under pressure to finalise settlement as the vendor was threatening to withdraw from the sale.[66]
  10. A mortgage was registered on 27 June 2013 on the Victorian LANDATA system over the Parent Block with NAB as mortgagee.[67] The mortgagor was TCR, and the registration form was signed by Mr Robinson as sole director of TCR.
  11. The aggregate funds required upon settlement of the acquisition of the Parent Block on 28 June 2013 included the purchase price $865,000[68] (of which $86,500 had been paid by way of a deposit), stamp duty of $46,970 and legal fees of $1,266.53.[69]
  12. Mr Robinson said that the sources of funds for settlement were as follows:
    (a) $309,610.25 cash contribution from Mr Robinson personally, including the $86,500 previously paid by way of deposit, and $223,110.25 from another NAB bank account[70] in his personal name.

    (b) $603,523.74 from TCR funded by the business loan from NAB.

  13. Mr Robinson said that statements for his personal bank account were no longer available as NAB advised it could not reproduce bank statements for dates longer than 10 years. However he said that since TCR only borrowed $605,500 under the NAB business loan and NAB transferred only $605,500 from TCR’s business loan account to TCR’s business cheque account on 1 July 2013, it can be assumed that the remaining funds required for settlement were sourced from elsewhere.[71] A copy or extract of the statement for each of the TCR business loan and cheque accounts for the period late June/early July 2013 was provided to the Tribunal with the Applicant’s SFIC.
  14. Mr Robinson said that between July 2013 and March 2014 he made the NAB business loan repayments for TCR, transferring funds from his personal bank account to TCR’s business cheque account.[72] Copies of bank statements or other documentation evidencing these transfers were not provided to the Tribunal. In paragraph 52 of the Applicants’ SFIC the Applicants say that after March 2014 TCR ‘began making loan repayments itself by paying the loan funds advanced by NAB to construct the dwelling on [the Parent Block] back to the loan.’ The Tribunal is unclear as to how funds advanced under a loan were used to repay that loan. However later in the SFIC at paragraph 61, the Applicants say that TCR was not trading or earning any income until January 2016, and up until that date Mr Robinson was meeting all loan repayments himself.
  15. By a Business Letter of Offer dated 18 September 2014 NAB advised TCR that an overdraft facility of $200,000 had been approved. The securities to be provided in relation to the facilities comprised a registered mortgage over each of the four lots into which the Parent Block would be divided, a security interest and charge over all present and future property, rights and undertaking of TCR and a personal guarantee and indemnity by Mr Robinson for $2,312,500. An ‘additional covenant and undertaking’ for the provision of the overdraft facility was the sale of ‘Lot 4’, which was to be confirmed by the earlier of 30 June 2015 or within 3 months of the ‘practical completion of the unit.’[73]
  16. On 30 January 2015, Mr Robinson, in his capacity as sole director of TCR, sent a letter to NAB referring to three facilities provided (or possibly to be provided) by NAB to TCR in relation to the development of lots at the Parent Block, being a Business Markets Loan Facility of $1,808,500, a Business Overdraft Facility of $200,000 and a Market Rate Facility of $475,000. This letter contained an ‘undertaking’ acknowledging that no further increases would be supported by NAB prior to certain conditions being satisfied in relation to the sale of Lot 4 and two other properties in Rosebud.
  17. By a Business Letter of Offer dated 30 April 2015 NAB advised TCR that a market rate facility of $475,000 had been approved. The securities to be provided in relation to the facilities comprised a registered mortgage over each of the four lots into which the Parent Block would be dividend, a security interest and charge over all present and future property, rights and undertaking of TCR and a personal guarantee and indemnity by Mr Robinson for $2,787,500. As conditions and undertakings to the facility, NAB required a signed contract for the sale of Lot 4 by the end of November 2015 (a minimum of $1.7 million of the proceeds of sale of Lot 4 was to be applied in debt reduction) and the two lots at the property in Rosebud to be sold by the end of 2015 to reduce the debt by a further $304,000. [74] This was acknowledged and signed by Mr Robinson in his capacity as the director of TCR.[75]
  18. The Applicants said that around January 2017 NAB advised that it would not continue the business loan facility to TCR and gave TCR 90 days to refinance. A construction loan for $1.64 million was obtained from a different financier, LaTrobe Financial Asset Management (LaTrobe), with security to be taken over Property. The mortgage over the Parent Block in favour of NAB was discharged on 5 April 2017.

POSITIONS ON THE TAXATION TREATMENT OF TRANSACTIONS

  1. The Respondent’s position, as reflected in the amended assessments, is as follows:
    (a) Both legal and beneficial title to the Parent Block, and thus the Property, was acquired by TCR. TCR was not a trustee for Mr Robinson in relation to the Property.

    (b) When the title to the Property was transferred by TCR to Mr Robinson, the consideration received was income derived by TCR in the course of its property construction and development business, a profit making venture. TCR was therefore required to include an amount[121] in its assessable income for income tax purposes. TCR was also required to return an amount of GST payable on the sale of the Property.

    (c) Further, the $2.25 million consideration stated in the transfer from TCR to Mr Robinson was less than the market value of the Property at the time. The correct market value reflected the $4.128 million which T Co had agreed to pay in an open market sale. Therefore as (the sole) shareholder of TCR, Mr Robinson was required to include in his assessable income a ‘deemed dividend’ under Division 7A, Part III of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) (ITAA 1936). The amount of this deemed dividend was the difference between the (GST exclusive) market value of the Property at the time that title was transferred (being $3,752,727) and the (GST exclusive) consideration that Mr Robinson previously provided to TCR for the Property (being $2,045,454)

    (d) In relation to any sale by Mr Robinson of the Property to T Co, he was not entitled to the main residence exemption. The Respondent did not accept that the Property was Mr Robinson’s main residence at any time, or at least for the period required for exemption from CGT.

  2. However, the Applicants say that the proper taxation treatment of the transactions relating to the Property is as follows:
    (a) Although the legal title to the Parent Block and thus the Property may have been acquired and held by TCR, this was merely as nominee or trustee for Mr Robinson, or there was otherwise a trust in favour of Mr Robinson over the Property. The beneficial ownership of the Property was at all times with Mr Robinson.

    (b) Accordingly, there was no disposal or change in ownership of the Property (at least for taxation purposes) when the legal title was transferred from TCR to Mr Robinson on 9 October 2018, and no taxable amount arose to TCR, for income tax or GST purposes.

    (c) Nor did any taxable amount such as a deemed dividend arise to Mr Robinson as a result of this transfer of title from TCR to him.

    (d) A disposal of the Property by Mr Robinson to T Co may have given rise to a CGT event happening to Mr Robinson, however he had met the conditions for the ‘main residence’ exemption from CGT in relation to the Property, such that he was not required to pay tax on any capital gain arising.

    (e) The ‘market value’ of the Property at the time it was sold was as set out in the PRP Valuation (being $2.25 million). Even though T Co agreed to pay $4.128 million, this was not the market value.

    (f) In summary, neither of the Applicants was required to include any amount in its assessable income for income taxation purposes as a result of the transfer of title from TCR to Mr Robinson, or upon the disposal of the Property to T Co. Nor was TCR liable for any additional amount of GST in relation to these transactions.

  3. The parties’ positions relating to the imposition of administrative penalties reflect their positions on the proper taxation treatment as above. That is, the Applicants said that no penalties should be imposed as no additional tax was payable by either of them and therefore no tax shortfall arose. The Respondent said that both Applicants had made false or misleading statements which resulted in tax shortfalls, such that administrative penalties were to be imposed. Further, the Respondent’s view was that the tax shortfalls arose in circumstances where the Applicants were reckless as to the operation of taxation laws. The Applicants submitted to the extent that there was a tax shortfall, that was not due to recklessness and therefore the amount of any penalty should be reduced, or the penalties should otherwise be remitted.

CONSIDERATION OF ISSUES

  1. Certain key issues underlie the operation of the various provisions of the taxation law in this dispute. The Tribunal’s view on these issues is set out below, followed by the application of the provisions to the Applicants.

Was TCR a trustee for Mr Robinson in relation to the Property?

  1. The parties are broadly in agreement as to the legal principles and case authorities relating to the existence of bare or resulting trusts. However there is significant disagreement as to whether the evidence supports the elements of a trust (or trusts) being found in the Applicants’ circumstances.
  2. It is not in dispute that title to the Parent Block was transferred to TCR upon acquisition in March 2013, and that title to the Property upon subdivision was also in the name of TCR. There is no trust deed or other formal document setting out the terms of any trust over the Property.[122]
  3. It is also not in dispute that the Applicants bear the burden of proving that their respective Amended Assessments were excessive or otherwise incorrect, and what those assessments should have been. This means that the Applicants need to satisfy the Tribunal, on the balance of probabilities, that Mr Robinson was the beneficial owner of the Property (that is, in addition to the period between 9 October and 15 October 2018) and that TCR was merely a trustee or nominee for Mr Robinson in relation to the Property under a bare trust or resulting trust.
  4. The Applicants relied on Calverley v Green[123] (Calverley) in support of their argument that Mr Robinson’s personal financial contributions to the purchase of the Parent Block gave rise to a resulting trust in his favour. In relation to a purchase of property, Gibbs CJ said[124]:
    ‘... if the purchase money is provided by two or more persons jointly, and the property is put into the name of one only, there is, in the absence of [a relationship giving rise to a presumption of advancement], presumed to be a resulting trust in favour of the other or others.

    ... the general rule that in the situations mentioned it is presumed that a resulting trust arises in favour of the purchaser, or in favour of two purchasers in the proportions in which they contributed the purchase money, is subject to the exception created by the presumption of advancement.’

  5. In Calverley the purchase price of the property was $27,250, of which Mr Calverley personally paid a deposit of $9,000, and an amount of $18,000 was funded by a loan in the joint names of Mr Calverley and Ms Green. The extent of the beneficial interests of the parties in the property was to be determined at the time when the property was purchased and the trust created. The fact that mortgage debt was subsequently repaid by Mr Calverley did not determine the extent of the interests of the parties in the property, although such amounts might be relevant to any subsequent taking of accounts between the parties. It was not correct to treat the payment of mortgage instalments as payment of the purchase price, with the purchase price being what is paid in order to acquire the property and mortgage instalments being payments to the lender from whom all or part of the purchase price is borrowed. The High Court therefore recognised the joint obligations of Mr Calverley and Ms Green under the loan as indicative of their interests in the property.[125] An equitable presumption of a trust could be displaced by a counter-presumption or rebutted or qualified by evidence of the intention of the party paying the purchase price or of the common intention of the parties who contributed that price.[126]
  6. Both the Respondent and the Applicant referred to the decision of Gummow J in Herdegen v Commissioner of Taxation[127] where his Honour said:
    Today the usually accepted meaning of "bare" trust is a trust under which the trustee or trustees hold property without any interest therein, other than that existing by reason of the office and the legal title as trustee, and without any duty or further duty to perform, except to convey it upon demand to the beneficiary or beneficiaries or as directed by them, for example, on sale to a third party...

    Also, the term "bare trust" may be used fairly to describe the position occupied by a person holding the title to property under a resulting trust flowing from the provision by the beneficiary of the purchase money for the property.

  7. The Applicants submitted that administrative formalities should not take priority over the substance of the arrangements and point to the following matters in support of the existence of a trust arrangement or trust arrangements arising over time:
    (a) Mr Robinson’s intention from March 2013 was to subdivide the Parent Block and build his permanent home on one lot. At the time that the Parent Block was acquired Mr Robinson told Mr Jucevic, his then accountant and tax agent, that one of the blocks was intended to be for Mr Robinson’s ‘principal place of residence.’ In his witness statement Mr Jucevic said that he recalled a conversation where Mr Robinson said that ‘he was going to build his own house on one of the 4 lots in the subdivision that he was doing.’ Other witnesses gave statements that Mr Robinson had told them directly that he was going to build his home on one of the lots in the subdivision of the Parent Block.[128]

    (b) Mr Robinson signed the contract for the purchase of the Parent Block. TCR was not incorporated until two months after the contract was entered into. The signature block on the form of contract referred to the purchaser or their ‘nominee’ and TCR was Mr Robinson’s nominee in this context.

    (c) Mr Robinson made numerous and substantial financial contributions to the acquisition and development of the Property. This included payment of the deposit for the Parent Block from his personal bank account,[129] his personal contribution to the shortfall of funds at settlement, and his payment of the stamp duty on the acquisition of the Parent Block. Mr Robinson contributed 35.6% of the funds required for settlement of the Parent Block. All repayments on the construction loan for the Property were made by him personally.

    (d) Mr Robinson applied to the Bank for a home loan in April 2013 to fund the balance of the purchase price of the Parent Block. He said that the Bank told him that because a subdivision of more than two lots was involved, he needed to incorporate a company (which he did in May 2013) to obtain a business loan for funding the purchase (which it did in June 2013). Mr Robinson told the Tribunal that that TCR’s involvement was for lending purposes only, as a borrowing vehicle or conduit.

    (e) Mr Robinson signed the transfer and other documentation provided to him by the Bank and his then lawyers, under pressure and without fully understanding what he was signing.[130] The professional advice he received at various times was flawed.

    (f) Although TCR claimed the full amount of interest on the Bank loan as an income tax deduction, that loan related only to the three lots from the subdivision other than the Property. Mr Robinson had funded the Property (the fourth lot) himself by his 35.6% financial contribution and no interest was payable on that portion.

    (g) Mr Robinson, and not TCR, paid for the building plans and permits for the Property. Mr Robinson’s name was on the plans for subdivision prepared by Speedie.[131]

    (h) The stated provision of $2.25 million consideration for the transfer of the title to the Property from TCR to Mr Robinson, and the payment of stamp duty on such transfer, occurred as a result of incorrect advice from the former accountant Mr O to the effect that the transfer was necessary. The accountant (and conveyancer) did not identify the existence of a trust. This advice was provided after Mr Robinson had already moved into the Property.

  8. More generally, Mr Robinson told the Tribunal that he viewed himself and TCR as ‘the same’ entity.
  9. In relation to the legal effect of these matters the Applicants contended that the relevant trust arrangements changed over time, and this addressed the Respondent’s arguments about a bare trust not existing following the purchase of the Parent Block. First, a ‘resulting trust’ arose by operation of law[132] in relation to the Parent Block proportionate to Mr Robinson’s financial contribution to its purchase, such that Mr Robinson had a beneficial interest of 36.5% and TCR held the beneficial interest in the remaining 64.4% of the Parent Block. From the time that the Parent Block was acquired in 2013 until the time of subdivision in 2016, TCR was a resulting trustee with ‘active duties’ to manage the subdivision and separate the assets on behalf of all beneficiaries.
  10. The Applicants said that once the plan of subdivision for the Parent Block was registered, that resulting trust ‘crystallised’ and Mr Robinson’s 36.5% beneficial interest in the Parent Block became a 100% beneficial interest in the Property. In that sense the resulting trust ‘became’ a bare trust, and TCR’s only remaining duty in relation to the Property was to hold the legal title and convey it.
  11. The Respondent’s position was that TCR was the legal and beneficial owner of the Parent Block, and of the Property (and other lots) upon subdivision of the Parent Block. Mr Robinson was not absolutely entitled to the Property as against TCR. The Respondent highlighted the following matters for the attention of the Tribunal:
    (a) Although Mr Robinson signed the contract of sale for the Parent Block, title to the Parent Block was transferred to TCR upon settlement in 2013. The evidence indicated that Mr Robinson did not express dissatisfaction with this state of affairs until his former accountant Mr O advised him of a potential tax liability in early 2018, and did not address the issue of a different taxation treatment until after the Respondent had commenced its GST audit in May 2020.

    (b) The income tax returns and BAS of TCR were prepared on the basis that it owned the Parent Block. Following the subdivision of the Parent Block in 2016, other lots were sold in the name of TCR, and tax returns lodged accordingly.

    (c) Having regard to the statements of Gummow J in Hedergen, TCR did not merely hold title to the Property on the basis that its only duty was to transfer title to Mr Robinson on demand. TCR held the entirety of the Parent Block. TCR had borrowed funds from the Bank and provided security by way of a mortgage over the Parent Block. It was not possible for TCR to transfer the Property to Mr Robinson without the subdivision and a partial discharge of the mortgage having occurred.

    (d) If Mr Robinson personally contributed 35.6% of the funds required for settlement of the Parent Block, it is not clear how this relates to the Property which comprised either 21.65% of the total area of the Parent Block (including common property) or 25.3% of the total area excluding common property. Nor was it clear how a 35.6% interest in the Parent Block could ‘crystallise’ into a 100% interest in the Property as the time of subdivision, it was still subject to the mortgage given by TCR in favour of the Bank (which was not discharged until April 2017) and thereafter subject to a mortgage in favour of LaTrobe.

    (e) If Mr Robinson had been the beneficial owner of the Property at all times, it would not have been necessary for him to provide consideration of $2.25 million to TCR for the transfer of the title to him on 9 October 2018. Nor would it have been necessary for him to pay ad valorem stamp duty on this transfer.

  12. In relation to both the question of the intention of the Applicants in relation to the Parents Block and Property, as well as the financial contributions of Mr Robinson, the Tribunal observes that:
    (a) In February 2020 Mr Vrahos apparently asked McGuiness & Hosking for information about the arrangements between TCR and Mr Robinson in relation to the transfer of the Property. McGuiness & Hosking confirmed that a search of their file did not disclose any ‘formal contract’ but that a written contract would not have been required, with ‘verbal or basic written instructions’ from Mr Robinson being sufficient. In that regard they attached an email dated 6 May 2018 from Mr Robinson to McGuiness & Hosking which read[133]:

    ‘Can u put together what’s required to transfer the title from Tcr group to Troy Robinson for [the Property] Jill and get this transferred ASAP thanks’

    Other than Mr Robinson’s evidence of his recollection of events, this is the only direct evidence of how and why title to the Property was transferred from TCR to him in October 2018.

    (b) The Applicants have provided some limited documentation in support of amounts relating to the Parent Block and/or Property being paid by Mr Robinson personally. This includes the cheque for the deposit for the Parent Block and some invoices or receipts which are addressed to Mr Robinson.

    (c) There are also a handful of documents, such as the permit for the swimming pool at the Property, which are in Mr Robinson’s personal name.

    (d) However, the significant volume of documentary evidence refers to TCR as the owner of the Parent Block and the Property. This includes the documentation with legal force such as the transfer instruments, contracts of sale, loan and security documentation, insurance policy and the Occupancy Permit.

    (e) Other than perhaps the deposit for the Parent Block, the Tribunal is unable to be satisfied as to the Applicants’ contentions about the use of Mr Robinson’s personal funds to acquire and develop the Property. The Tribunal accepts that the Applicants may have encountered difficulties in obtaining historic bank statements, but the major obstacle to the Applicants’ submissions is the clear evidence of significant financing (and refinancing) provided by third parties, including NAB and LaTrobe Financial, to TCR and applied to the purchase of the Parent Block and development of the lots (including the Property). The Tribunal has been given no basis upon which it can identify or trace funds provided by Mr Robinson personally.

    (f) There may be confusion between the source of funds for the purchase price of the Parent Block, the funding of other amounts required for settlement of the purchase of the Parent Block and funding of construction on the Parent Block or subdivided lots. The Tribunal is willing to accept that Mr Robinson paid the deposit of $86,500 on the purchase of the Parent Block based on the copy of the cheque and bank account statement evidencing this payment. TCR obtained a loan from NAB for $605,500 which the evidence, including the loan documentation and bank statements of TCR, indicates was applied to the purchase of the Parent Block. The Tribunal agrees that this leaves a ‘gap’ of around $173,000 between the purchase price and the funding sources which can be identified. Mr Robinson referred to multiple accounts with NAB, including ‘a 605, a 200, a 400 and ... 860’ and a loan from NAB for $200,000 which did not come through until 2014.[134] Refinancing, including with LaTrobe, followed thereafter. Mr Robinson referred to having paid over $309,000 towards the settlement of the purchase of the Parent Block. If this is correct, it would include amounts other than those strictly referable to the Purchase Price. Mr Robinson acknowledges that he cannot provide copies of bank statements evidencing or clarifying what he paid from his personal bank accounts and/or how the balance of the purchase price of the Parent Block over the deposit and NAB loan amounts was funded.

    (g) Similarly there is no documentary evidence supporting Mr Robinson’s repayment of loans taken out by TCR. However the statements of the High Court in Calverley indicate that any repayment by him of amounts owing by TCR on loans taken out by TCR to fund the purchase would not of itself support the existence of a resulting trust.

    (h) Even if the Tribunal had been satisfied that there was evidence supporting Mr Robinson’s contribution of 36.5% of the purchase price of the Parent Block, the Tribunal shares the Respondent’s uncertainty as to how this translated to a bare trust in his favour over a particular lot arising post subdivision of the Parent Block. The process of ‘crystallisation’ was not explained by the Applicants to the satisfaction of the Tribunal. The entirety of the land within the Parent Block was subject to securities granted by TCR in favour of two different financiers from its purchase in 2013 until its sale to T Co in 2018. There was no evidence of any interest of Mr Robinson in this land being noted when these securities were given.

    (i) In relation to the witness statement of Mr Jucevic, his conversation with Mr Robinson was about Mr Robinson’s plans to build his home on a lot within the subdivided Parent Block. Although it says that ‘he’ (ie Mr Robinson) had ‘purchased some land’, it does not directly address the issue of ownership of the Parent Block. Mr Jucevic refers to the conversation being in ‘2013’, but it is not clear whether this was at the time the contract was signed by Mr Robinson in March 2013, after TCR was incorporated in May 2013 or upon settlement of the acquisition of the Parent Block in the name of TCR in June 2013. It is not clear whether Mr Jucevic was aware of TCR’s involvement. Mr Jucevic did not attend the hearing for examination on his evidence and the Tribunal therefore cannot be certain about the reliability or detail of his recollections.

    (j) Similarly, the witness statements and oral evidence of Mr H, Mr C and Mr S are anecdotal in nature and do not indicate any understanding by those witnesses of the ownership or financing arrangements of the Property. During cross-examination by the Respondent’s counsel they conceded that they did not have knowledge to this level of detail.

  13. The burden of proof is on the Applicants to demonstrate that the assessments issued by the Respondent were incorrect (and what they should have been). The Applicants have not been able to provide adequate documentary or other evidence of personal financial contributions by Mr Robinson which would support a trust having arisen in Mr Robinson’s favour over the Parent Block or the Property. There is a lack of reliable evidence of any intention of the Applicants for TCR to hold any interest in the Parent Block or the beneficial interest in the Property for Mr Robinson. Further, these contentions are inconsistent with the wider evidence that has been provided to the Tribunal of TCR’s ownership of the Property, at least prior to the formal transfer to Mr Robinson in early October 2018. The Tribunal cannot be satisfied on the balance of probabilities that TCR did not hold both legal and beneficial title to the Property prior to the formal transfer to Mr Robinson.
  14. The Tribunal appreciates that the Applicants may have faced challenges in obtaining supporting documentation from their lenders with several years having elapsed since the transactions in question. In making its finding of a lack of evidence in support of the Applicants’ case, the Tribunal is not suggesting that the Applicants were dishonest or misleading in their submissions to the Tribunal.

Was the Property the main residence of Mr Robinson or otherwise held for his personal use?

  1. The Tribunal has found that TCR held the legal and beneficial interests in the Property prior to the transfer to Mr Robinson, and that TCR did not hold the Property on trust for Mr Robinson.
  2. The Tribunal acknowledges that the Applicants made extensive submissions in support of their position that the Property was Mr Robinson’s main residence between March 2018, when the Property became ‘habitable’, and October 2018 when settlement of the sale to T Co occurred. In addition to Mr Robinson’s own evidence about his intention for the Property (at the time of purchase of the Parent Block and subsequently), reference was made to:
    (a) Mr P’s evidence about the quality of the home at the Property, suggesting that it was not typical of a developer’s stock in trade, as well as Mr S’s apparent belief that Mr Robinson was living at the Property when he and his wife inspected it.

    (b) Other witnesses’, including Mr S, Mr H and Mr C, recollection of Mr Robinson telling them that he intended to make the Property his personal and long-term residence.

    (c) Documentary evidence of Mr Robinson residing at the Property during this period, including bills for utilities.

  3. The Respondent said that the Tribunal should treat Mr Robinson’s statements about his intentions for the Property with some caution on the basis of his self-interest in the outcome of the Tribunal proceedings. The Respondent also queried why Mr Robinson repeatedly expressed his intention in terms of a ‘principal place of residence’ or similar terminology which had taxation law connotations, rather than a more general statement of wanting to make his home on the Property. Similarly, the Respondent suggested that while the evidence of Mr C, Mr H and Mr S might be accepted in terms of evidencing their recollection of certain discussions with Mr Robinson, the words that they recalled were only ‘vague statements’ and they otherwise had no knowledge about the ownership of the Parent Block or Property.
  4. The Respondent also submitted that[135]:
    (a) Mr Robinson’s financial circumstances were such that he would not have been able to obtain longer-term finance to allow him to retain the Property. He had to borrow $200,000 from his father and a friend in 2018 to complete the Property as his then lender LaTrobe Finance would not provide the additional funds.

    (b) Mr Robinson had acquired, renovated or developed and sold three other properties since 2006, living in each for a period prior to sale. This suggested business or profit-making purposes rather than personal or domestic ones.

    (c) All expenses relating to the acquisition, subdivision and development of the Parent Block were claimed for tax purposes, indicative of a ‘business’ rather than personal intention.

  5. In relation to the evidentiary matters, the Tribunal makes the following observations:
    (a) Had the Tribunal agreed with Mr Robinson’s argument that he held the beneficial interest in the Property under a bare Trust, such that he acquired an ‘ownership interest’ in January 2016 (or earlier), the Tribunal would not have been satisfied on the evidence that the Property became his ‘main residence’ as soon as possible after work on the dwelling was complete and continued to be his main residence for at least 3 months thereafter. On this point the evidence of the witnesses, other than Mr Robinson, was at a general level, essentially recollections of what Mr Robinson told them about his intentions and in some cases their awareness that he was staying at the Property (for example, hosting dinners for friends, using the swimming pool, having a cleaner). The evidence as to the timing of some of these matters, including when work on the Property was complete, was not clear.[136]

    (b) Nor did it demonstrate that the Property was Mr Robinson’s ‘main residence’ (or residence) at any particular time. Mr Robinson gave evidence about his concern that theft or damage might occur to the Property if it was left empty, however even if he was staying at the Property this did not necessarily mean that it was his main residence. Copies of accounts for utilities addressed to Mr Robinson at the Property are not necessarily probative of that Property being his main residence.

    (c) The email of instructions sent by Mr Robinson’s father on 15 May 2018 to Preston Rowe Paterson for the purposes of the PRP Valuation indicated that Mr Robinson’s address at that time was his parents’ address and stated that Mr Robinson was ‘hoping to move into’ the Property when a certificate of occupancy was issued for the Property. Mr Robinson’s father could be presumed to know whether his son was living in his parents’ home.

    (d) The Occupancy Permit for the Property was issued in June 2018. The Tribunal accepts Mr Robinson’s evidence that as a practical matter it was possible to live at a newly constructed home before a certificate of occupancy was issued, even if this should not be done from a strictly legal perspective. Nevertheless, the Occupancy Permit does have some bearing on when the Property could be said to be ‘complete’ and able to be treated as a residence. An email dated 15 May 2018 from Mr Robinson’s father to the Valuer states that Mr Robinson’s ‘address’ at that date was an address other than the Property[137], and that Mr Robinson hoped to move into the Property when the certificate of occupancy was issued. This appears inconsistent with Mr Robinson’s statements about when he moved into the Property. Mr Robinson’s father was not called to give evidence so this discrepancy could not be explained.

  6. The Tribunal is unable to make a finding on the evidence that Mr Robinson occupied the Property as his main residence for any particular period during 2018. Therefore had the Tribunal agreed with the Applicants’ submissions that the beneficial ownership of the Property had always been with Mr Robinson, it would not have accepted that he was entitled to the main residence exemption in Subdivision 118-B of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) (ITAA 1997) in relation to any capital gain he made upon disposal of the Property.
  7. Further, in relation to other criteria for the main residence exemption:
    (a) A taxpayer must ‘choose’ to apply the main residence exemption. Where the taxpayer is building, repairing or renovating the dwelling in question, they can only make the choice if the conditions in subsection 118-150(3) are taken to be met. For the reasons given above, the Tribunal cannot be satisfied on the balance of probabilities that those conditions were met. In any event, the Applicants acknowledged that no choice had been made, or had been purported to have been made, for the purposes of section 118-150.

    (b) Subsection 118-150(4) gives the Respondent some discretion to allow an alternative time period during which the choice can operate. The Applicants suggested that the Tribunal, standing in the shoes of the Respondent, should exercise the discretion. However, in circumstances where the Tribunal was unable to find that the Property was the Mr Robinson’s main residence at any particular time, it would not have been possible to exercise any discretion.

  8. The main residence exemption is only available to individuals, and only in relation to a capital gain arising to an individual. For the avoidance of doubt, it cannot operate in relation to any capital gain made by TCR in relation to the Property.
  9. On the basis of the factual matters set out above, the Tribunal is also unable to find that the Property was acquired or held for the personal or domestic use of Mr Robinson, as his primary home or otherwise. This is relevant to the characterisation of the disposal of the Property by TCR. It is not controversial that TCR conducted a business or enterprise which involved the acquisition, construction, development and sale of property. Both parties made submissions as to the definition of an ’enterprise’ for GST purposes and whether the transfer of the Property by TCR to Mr Robinson was done in the course or furtherance of an enterprise.[138] Similarly, an issue arose as to whether the Property was held on capital account for income tax purposes, being for the personal use of Mr Robinson as his residence, or otherwise not being held as part of a profit-making scheme.

Market value of the Property

  1. As with many valuation issues the parties are a considerable distance apart on what the appropriate market value for the Property was, and the weight to be given to particular items of evidence before the Tribunal in relation to that market value.
  2. The market value of the Property is relevant to this dispute in relation to:
    (a) The GST payable by TCR on the transfer or ‘supply’ of the Property to an associate (Mr Robinson)[139], and

    (b) Whether a deemed dividend should be included in the assessable income of Mr Robinson under Division 7A, Part III of the ITAA 1936 on the basis that Mr Robinson obtained a transfer of the Property from TCR, a private company in respect of which he was a shareholder, and the market value of the Property was more than the consideration he gave for its transfer.[140]

  3. Although the High Court in Spencer v The Commonwealth (Spencer) did not consider the particular term ‘market value’ and was determining a valuation question arising under compulsory acquisition legislation, courts and tribunals have subsequently adopted statements made in Spencer[141] in support of the proposition that market value should be ascertained by reference to a ‘hypothetical in which a willing but not anxious vendor deals at arm’s length with a willing but not anxious purchaser, each perfectly acquainted with the asset concerned, and asks what resultant sale price could reasonably be expected?’[142]
  4. The Respondent also referred the Tribunal to the decision of the High Court in Abrahams v Federal Commissioner of Taxation (Abrahams) where Williams J said in relation to statutory schemes which do not direct any particular method of estimating the value of assets that it would be proper to ascertain ‘the price which a willing but not anxious vendor could reasonably expect to obtain and a hypothetical willing but not anxious purchaser could reasonably have to expect to pay.’[143]
  5. In the Robinson Objection Decision the Respondent referred to the estimated selling price set out in the Exclusive Sale Authority as a ‘valuation’ and a ‘valuation by the real estate agent’ noting further that this ‘valuation’ was subject to REIV guidelines including those relating to overquoting and underquoting.[144] The Respondent also said that the fact that Mr Robinson accepted an offer of $4.128 million within a couple of weeks of putting the Property on the market indicated his awareness that this was ‘an accurate fair market value’ for the Property. The Respondent noted the existence of a sworn valuation (presumably the PRP Valuation) but did not otherwise address it in the Robinson Objection Decision.
  6. The Tribunal’s view is that although the estimated selling price put forward by a selling agent might generally be expected to reflect the market value of the property, the estimated selling price or price range is not a ‘valuation.’ Unlike PRP, Mr Robinson’s father was not a qualified valuer. The Respondent provided to the Tribunal a copy of provisions from the Estate Agents Act 1980 (Vic) relating to the obligations of Victorian real estate agents when determining an estimated selling price.[145] The estimate must be ‘reasonable’ and for properties located outside the Melbourne metropolitan area must also take into account the sale prices of the 3 comparable properties that the agent reasonably considers to be most comparable to the property being sold, such comparable properties having been sold within the preceding 18 months. It does not directly address the concept of ‘market value.’ The Tribunal is unable to form a view about the compliance by Mr Robinson Senior with these provisions, and it is not necessary to do so for the purposes of this decision.
  7. However, the PRP Valuation was addressed in the TCR Objection Decision, with the Respondent stating that the valuation ‘expired on 22 August 2018.’ This appears to be a reference to clause 21 of the terms and conditions attached to the PRP Valuation, which states:
    We do not assume responsibility or accept any liability where the valuation is relied upon after the expiration of ninety (90) days from the date of the valuation, or such earlier date if you become aware of any factors that have any effect on the valuation.
  8. In the TCR Objection Decision the Respondent referred to the ‘supervening open market sale’ of the Property as a factor that had an effect on the valuation. The Respondent also queried why the Property was valued by PRP for an amount less than the amount for which another lot sold in January 2016. The Respondent did not repeat its claim that the agent’s estimated selling range was a ‘valuation’ in and of itself.[146]
  9. The Respondent said that the market value of the Property should be determined as of 9 October 2018. The Tribunal agrees with this proposition. This was the date of the transfer from TCR to Mr Robinson, which was the date of the relevant ‘supply’ for GST purposes and also the date of the ‘transfer of property’ for section 109C purposes.
  10. In its submissions to the Tribunal the Respondent maintained its position that the market value of the Property as of 9 October 2018 was $4.128 million and clarified its contentions in this regard:
    (a) The amount of $4.128 million was the price obtained for the Property in an open market sale. The fact that T Co bought the Property on the open market meant that it was unnecessary to consider the question of what a hypothetical willing but not anxious purchase would have been willing to pay.[147]

    (b) The contract for that open market sale was signed on 14 July 2018.

    (c) The PRP Valuation was prepared as at 24 May 2018 and should otherwise be disregarded because it was obtained for stamp duty purposes only, did not take into account the previous sale of another lot in January 2016[148], gave a lower valuation for the Property than the ‘independent’ UPA Valuation prepared in February 2017 and mostly referred to comparable sales from 2016 and 2017. The Respondent said that the instructions from Mr Robinson’s father indicated that Mr Robinson wanted to move into the Property rather than sell it, which suggested they were seeking a lower valuation.

    (d) In relation to Mr P’s evidence that the price T Co paid was above market value, he was not an expert in property valuation.

  11. The Applicants’ position is that the market value was $2.25 million, reflecting the PRP Valuation. Mr Robinson reiterated his position that the estimated selling price he had agreed with his father was ‘opportunistic.’ He also said that it reflected the cost of the construction on the Property, as well as the time he had spent on it, and that if he had sold the Property for $2.5 million he would have lost money.[149] Mr Robinson’s father was not called to give evidence.
  12. The Respondent stated in the Robinson Objection Decision that it was unlikely that anyone would agree to pay above market value in an arm’s length arrangement. However, the Tribunal notes the case authorities, including Spencer and Abrahams, contemplate that an ‘anxious purchaser’ might pay an amount that an otherwise willing purchaser would not. Mr P told the Tribunal about personal circumstances which meant that T Co did not haggle with Mr Robinson but agreed to pay at the mid-point of the estimated selling price range to secure the Property quickly. Mr P said that his family trust had the money to do so.
  13. The Respondent also stated in Mr Robinson’s Objection Decision that it did not accept Mr P’s statutory declaration as sufficient evidence of itself, particularly as that declaration was not made contemporaneously.[150] The Tribunal is not clear as to why Mr P would have needed to prepare a written statement about his personal circumstances at the time of T Co’s purchase of the Property in 2018 when the issue of the value of the Property was not in dispute, but nevertheless agrees with the proposition that his evidence needed clarification or corroboration.
  14. In its SFIC and submissions to the Tribunal the Respondent questioned the reliability of the statutory declaration provided by Mr P and (properly) raised a concern about the ability to test Mr P’s evidence if he did not attend the hearing. However Mr P did appear at the hearing and was cross-examined by the Respondent’s Counsel. The Tribunal’s view is that Mr P’s evidence of his circumstances when T Co purchase the Property was clear, consistent and plausible. He acknowledged that he was not a valuation expert. The Tribunal accepts Mr P’s evidence as it relates to the circumstances of T Co, Mr P and his family at the time of their purchase of the Property. The fact that Mr P and his (then) wife are now separated is consistent with his account of marital difficulties in 2018. The fact that T Co made an offer for the Property and requested that it be taken off the market very shortly after it was listed, and signed a contract for the Property in less than a month of listing is also consistent with a level of anxiety on the part of the purchaser.
  15. The Respondent contended that even if T Co paid what might be regarded as a high price, this did not preclude that price from being the market value of the Property. The Respondent argued that the fact that Mr P had been looking at properties in the area over an extended period and had considered the pros and cons of purchasing an existing property as against a new build, indicated that he was a purchaser with full knowledge of the Property, who was ‘not overly anxious’ and bought the Property on the open market. Further, the Respondent said that to the extent that the Property may have had ‘special value’ to him, that was incorporated into the market value.
  16. Although the advantage of a turnkey property over a new build might be viewed as a matter of special value to some purchasers, Mr P described relationship circumstances and work commitments as reasons why he was especially keen to purchase a property as quickly as possible. This did not mean that the Property had any ‘special value’ to him. The payment of a price at the midpoint of an estimated selling range so early in the sale campaign, whether or not that might be considered advisable by others, is also consistent with an ‘anxious’ purchaser. Mr P wanted to acquire a coastal retreat, in time for the upcoming summer season and after seeing the Property decided that it met his family’s requirements. Where Mr P or his family trust had substantial funds at their disposal, it is a matter for them how they spent these funds.
  17. From Mr Robinson’s perspective it is completely plausible that having received an offer at the asking price so quickly, he accepted the offer promptly and did not wait to see if he could get a better price. There is no suggestion that T Co or Mr P and his then wife had any prior association or relationship with the Applicants, or that Mr Robinson as vendor and T Co as purchaser transacted other than at arm’s length. There is no reason for the Tribunal to assume that Mr P or T Co have any interest in the outcome of this proceeding.
  18. The Tribunal agrees with the Respondent’s submissions that the evidence of Mr H, insofar as it might purport to address the market value of the Property, is of limited assistance to the Tribunal. Mr H did not undertake any valuation of the Property and was not the selling agent for the Property. Mr H was the selling agent for another developed lot within the subdivision of the Parent Block and the Tribunal is willing to have regard to the actual sale price of that other lot as part of the overall factual matrix relating to the market value issue.
  19. However the Tribunal does not believe it is appropriate to disregard the PRP Valuation for the following reasons:
    (a) Although the PRP Valuation was prepared as of May 2018, this does not represent a substantial gap in time from October 2018.

    (b) The PRP Valuation set out at length the basis for the valuation given, including the matters taken into account, any assumptions made, and the methodology used. It was not suggested to the Tribunal that any of the assumptions or factual matters set out in the valuation report were incorrect or misleading. The PRP Valuation report was signed by a primary valuer and countersigned by a second valuer, both of whom identified their professional qualifications. PRP conducted a site inspection of the Property as well as reviewing title and planning documentation.

    (c) The definition of ‘market value’ adopted by PRP aligns to the principles set out in case law such as Spencer and Abrahams.[151]

    (d) There are five ‘comparable sales’ set out in the PRP Valuation with sale dates ranging from November 2016 to February 2018. The basis for comparison with the Property is described for each property. It is entirely plausible that a limited range of comparable sales is explicable by fewer properties being for sale in a regional location. The Tribunal does not necessarily accept the Respondent’s argument that the ‘most comparable property’ would be the lot sold by TCR in January 2016, more than two years prior to the date of the PRP Valuation. Even if the Tribunal were to accept that prior lot sale as ‘more comparable’ than other properties sold more recently in the area, the price of $2.4 million that was paid for the other lot is considerably closer to the PRP Valuation of $2.25 million than the estimated selling range and actual price for the Property.

    (e) Although the PRP Valuation is for a lesser amount than the previous UPA Valuation, they are broadly consistent ($2.4 million for the UPA Valuation which was prepared prior to construction of the home compared to $2.25 million for the PRP Valuation based on the nearly completed site). There is a substantial difference between the price paid by T Co and each of these sworn valuations.

    (f) The PRP Valuation was not stated to be limited to use for stamp duty purposes only, nor is the Tribunal willing to place this limitation on it, by reference to the brief email of instructions or otherwise. The Tribunal does not believe there is evidence in support of the Respondent’s claim that Mr Robinson and his father influenced PRP to provide a lower valuation. Again, the UPA Valuation, which was undertaken on the instructions of LaTrobe, is closer to the PRP Valuation.

  20. There was a significant disparity between the market values contended for by the parties to this proceeding. This dictated further inquiry by the Tribunal as to why such a disparity existed. The Tribunal appreciates that it is not for the Respondent to prove its position on the valuation of the Property in context of section 14ZZK of the TAA 1953. However in the present case the Tribunal is satisfied that the weight of evidence is more consistent with the Applicants’ position, and in determining the appropriate market value of the Property in October 2018, a market value of $2.5 million, reflecting the PRP Valuation, is to be preferred. The Tribunal believes that the circumstances of this matter are unusual, particularly as they relate to the purchaser T Co. The Tribunal acknowledges that in more straightforward circumstances the actual price paid in an open market is likely to be compelling evidence of market value.

TAXATION TREATMENT OF TCR

  1. Having regard to the findings above, the Tribunal’s view of the correct treatment of TCR is as follows:
    (a) TCR was the legal and beneficial owner of the Property prior to the transfer, it was registered for GST purposes and the transfer was made in the course or furtherance of an enterprise it carried on, such that the transfer was a ‘taxable supply’ made by TCR for the purposes of section 9-5 of the A New Tax System (Goods and Services Tax) Act 1999 (Cth) (GST Act). The Tribunal agrees with the Respondent’s position that TCR should have disclosed GST payable on that taxable supply in its BAS for the quarter ended 31 December 2018.

    (b) However, the Tribunal does not agree with the Respondent’s position on the amount of GST that was payable. Section 9-70 of the GST Act provides that the amount of GST on a taxable supply is 10% of the ‘value’ of the taxable supply. Subsection 9-75(1) states that the value of a taxable supply is determined by reference to the ‘price’ and where the consideration for the supply is expressed as an amount of money, the price is that amount. However, this general provision is subject to special rules, including those applying to the ‘sale of freehold interests etc’ found in Division 75 of the GST Act[152] as well as the provisions of Division 72 which apply to supplies between associates.

    (c) Subdivision 72-C applies to supplies between associates for ‘inadequate consideration.’ Section 72-20 applies where a taxable supply is made to an associate for consideration that is less than the ‘GST inclusive market value.’ In those circumstances the ‘value’ of the supply for GST purposes is taken to be the ‘GST exclusive market value’ (overriding section 9-75). Mr Robinson is an associate of TCR in his capacity as TCR’s sole director and shareholder.[153] The term ‘GST inclusive market value’ is defined in section 195-1 by reference to the ‘market value’ of the consideration or thing in respect of the supply. The Respondent’s position was that section 72-20 applied to the supply of the Property by TCR to Mr Robinson as the consideration of $2.25 million provided by Mr Robinson was less than the market value of the Property at the time of the transfer. The Respondent therefore determined the GST payable by TCR by reference to the GST exclusive market value of the Property, and thus the ‘value’ of the taxable supply being based on a market value of $4.128 million. For the reasons given by the Tribunal above, the Tribunal’s view is that the consideration provided by Mr Robinson was not less than the market value of the Property. Therefore, the Tribunal finds that section 72-70 does not apply and the value of the taxable supply by TCR to Mr Robinson is to be determined under section 9-75 by reference to the $2.25 million price. The GST to be disclosed by TCR for the quarter ended 31 December 2018 is to be calculated under section 9-70 accordingly.

    (d) Division 75 of the GST Act contains provisions relating to the use of the ‘margin scheme’ which broadly determines GST on the basis of a portion of the margin between sale and purchase consideration of the relevant property. Access to the margin scheme is conditional on the existence of a written agreement between the supplier and recipient of the supply that the margin scheme is to apply, and that written agreement must be entered into on or before the time of the supply, or within such further period as the Commissioner of Taxation allows.[154] It is not disputed that no such written agreement was entered into between TCR and Mr Robinson on or before 9 October 2018. The Tribunal understood the Applicants’ position to be that they were not told by their professional advisers of the existence of the margin scheme and/or how they could access it. The Tribunal is unwilling to exercise the discretion to extend the period for entering into a written agreement having regard to the considerable time that has elapsed since the transfer and the reason for the failure to enter into an agreement being a high-level reference to a misunderstanding on the part of advisers. The Tribunal therefore agrees with the Respondent that the margin scheme provisions of Division 75 will not apply.

    (e) The transfer of the Property by TCR to Mr Robinson was made for consideration and in the course of TCR’s business of property development. The Tribunal agrees with the Respondent’s position that an amount referable to the $2.25 million consideration paid by Mr Robinson was assessable income of TCR for the 2019 year for income tax purposes.[155] The Tribunal also agrees with the Respondent’s view that the consideration paid by Mr Robinson should be regarded as inclusive of GST, such that the amount to be included in TCR’s assessable income under section 6-5 of the ITAA 1997 is the ‘GST exclusive’ amount of $2,045,454.[156]

TAXATION TREATMENT OF MR ROBINSON

  1. In relation to the taxation treatment of Mr Robinson, the Respondent treated the difference between what it contended was the GST exclusive market value of the Property (at the time of transfer from TCR to Mr Robinson, or $3,752,727 based on a market value of $4.128 million) and the GST exclusive consideration stated to have been paid by Mr Robinson upon the transfer ($2,045,454 based on an amount of $2.25 million) as a deemed dividend under section 109C of the ITAA 1936.
  2. The Tribunal agrees with the Respondent’s analysis of section 109C to the extent that:
    (a) TCR is a ‘private company’ and Mr Robinson was an entity who was a shareholder in that private company during the 2019 year. Therefore, where TCR paid an amount to Mr Robinson during the 2019 year, it was prima facie deemed to have paid a dividend to Mr Robinson at the end of that year of income: see paragraph 109C(1)(a).

    (b) Such a deemed dividend would be included in the assessable income of Mr Robinson for the 2019 year.

    (c) The transfer of the Property by TCR to Mr Robinson would be taken to be a ‘payment’ by TCR to Mr Robinson for these purposes: paragraph 109C(3)(c).

  3. Subsection 109C(4) provides that:
    The amount of a payment consisting of a transfer of property is the amount that would have been paid for the transfer by parties dealing at arm’s length less any consideration given by the transferee for the transfer. (The amount of a payment is nil if the consideration given by the transferee equals or exceeds the amount that would have been paid at arm’s length for the transfer.)
  4. The Respondent submitted that subsection 109C(4) provided that ‘the amount of a payment consisting of a transfer of property is the market value of the property less any consideration given by the transferee.’[157] The Respondent provided its submissions on section 109C on the same basis as its contentions on ‘market value’ as discussed above. The Applicants therefore made their submission on section 109C in response to the Respondent’s contentions.
  5. The Respondent’s paraphrasing of subsection 109C(4) in its written submissions did not fully or directly address the wording of that provision, which unlike the GST Act does not refer to ‘market value’ specifically. Towards the end of the hearing the Tribunal asked the Respondent’s Counsel if she would assist the Tribunal and Mr Robinson by explaining in more detail the Respondent’s position on section 109C and how this fitted into the assessments that had been issued to the Applicants, as the Tribunal wanted to be sure that the Applicants were aware of the deemed dividend issue.[158] The Tribunal appreciates Ms Wilson’s assistance in this regard as she drew Mr Robinson’s attention to the specific wording of subsection 109C(4) and the concept of arm’s length consideration. Ms Wilson explained the Respondent’s view that the arm’s length consideration for the transfer of the Property was $4.128 million, whereas Mr Robinson had paid TCR $2.25 million for the Property and the deemed dividend reflected the difference between those two amounts.[159]
  6. Having said that subsection 109C(4) does not use the term ‘market value’, the Tribunal believes that many of the factors it set out above in relation to its findings on market value are relevant. The Tribunal has stated the reasons why it prefers the PRP Valuation over the amount that was subsequently actually paid by T Co, even in circumstances where it is acknowledged that T Co (and its officers) were unrelated to and dealt at arm’s length with Mr Robinson. Subsection 109C(4) looks to the amount that ‘would have been paid ... by parties dealing at arm’s length’ in the sense of hypothetical parties in the circumstances of the transfer. Even where the language of subsection 109C(4) does not, for example, expressly draw on concepts of the anxious or imprudent purchaser, the Tribunal remains of the view that the matters set out in the PRP Valuation, and the circumstances in which it was obtained, provide a better indication of the amount that ‘would have’ been paid by parties dealing at arm’s length rather than that which was subsequently paid by a particular purchaser.
  7. This would mean that the value of the payment consisting of the transfer of the Property would be nil, as the consideration given by Mr Robinson as transferee equalled the amount that would have been paid at arm’s length for the transfer. No deemed dividend would therefore arise in the hands of Mr Robinson under section 109C in the 2019 year.
  8. The Tribunal finds that Mr Robinson has met his burden of proof in relation to the Robinson Amended Assessment being excessive or otherwise incorrect, and that he should only have been assessed on the amount of assessable income he previously returned and not including the deemed dividend.

PENALTIES

  1. The Respondent imposed administrative penalties on the basis of both Applicants having made false or misleading statements as follows:
    (a) TCR made a false or misleading statement by not including the proceeds of sale of the Property in its assessable income in the 2019 year (that is, the TCR Income Tax Penalty Assessment).

    (b) TCR also made a false or misleading statement by not including a liability for GST relating to the sale of the Property in its BAS for the quarter ending 31 December 2018 (that is, the TCR GST Penalty Assessment).

    (c) Mr Robinson made a false or misleading statement by not including an amount in his assessable income in the 2019 year by way of a deemed dividend arising from the transfer to him of the Property for less than its market value (that is, the Robinson Penalty Assessment).

Relevant law

  1. Division 284, Schedule 1 to the TAA 1953 provides for the imposition of penalties for false or misleading statements.
  2. A person:
    (a) Will be liable to an administrative penalty if they make a statement to the Respondent that is false or misleading in a material particular because of things in it or omitted from it: subsection 284-75(1).

    (b) Will not be liable for a penalty if they took ‘reasonable care’ in connection with the making of the statement: subsection 284-75(5).

    (c) Will not be liable for an administrative penalty where they engaged a registered tax agent or BAS agent and gave the agent all relevant taxation information, the registered tax agent or BAS agent made the false or misleading statement, and the false or misleading nature of the statement did not result from intentional disregard by the agent of a taxation law or recklessness by the agent as to the operation of a taxation law: subsection 284-75(6). The person has the burden of proving that this exception applies: subsection 284-75(7).

  3. In determining the amount of any penalty to be imposed:
    (a) A ‘base penalty amount’ is determined by applying a percentage to a ‘shortfall amount’: sections 284-80, 284-85 and 284-90. A shortfall amount will arise to the extent that a tax-related liability, as worked out on the basis of a false or misleading statement, is less than it would be if the statement were not false or misleading.

    (b) The type of behaviour that resulted in the shortfall amount determines the percentage to be applied. It will be 50% of the shortfall amount if the shortfall resulted from ‘recklessness’ by the taxpayer or their agent as to the operation of a taxation law[160]: see item 2 in the Table in subsection 284-90(1).

    (c) The base penalty amount may be increased by 20% in certain circumstances, including where a base penalty amount has previously been worked out for the taxpayer in relation to the making of a false or misleading statement: section 284-220.

  4. Terms such as ‘reasonable care’ and ‘reckless’ are not further defined in the TAA 1953. The Explanatory Memorandum to the Bill which introduced the penalty provisions stated that:[161]
    Reasonable care requires a taxpayer to make a reasonable attempt to comply with the provisions of the ITAA and regulations. The effort required is one commensurate with all the taxpayer’s circumstances, including the taxpayer’s knowledge, education, experience and skill.
  5. Courts and tribunals have also addressed the question of what constitutes reckless behaviour, with the Federal Court stating:[162]
    Recklessness in this context means to include in a tax statement material upon which the Act or regulations are to operate, knowing that there is a real, as opposed to fanciful, risk that the material may be incorrect, or be grossly indifferent as to whether or not the material is true and correct, and that a reasonable person in the position of the statement-maker would see there was a real risk that the Act and regulations may not operate correctly to lead to the assessment of the proper tax payable because of the content of the tax statement. So understood, the proscribed conduct is more than mere negligence and must amount to a gross carelessness.

And[163]:

‘the context makes it clear that recklessness means something more than failure to exercise reasonable care ... but less than an intentional disregard of the Act.’

  1. The Respondent has a discretion to remit all or part of an administrative penalty that has been imposed: section 298-20 of Schedule 1 to the TAA 1953. The TAA 1953 does not prescribe specific matters to be considered for the purposes of exercising this discretion. Case authority indicates that the decision maker must consider whether it is appropriate in ‘all the circumstances’ to remit the penalty[164] and that the power to remit must be exercised for a proper purpose, in accordance with the objects of the TAA 1953.[165]

Should penalties have been imposed on the Applicants?

  1. Although separate penalty assessments were issued to Mr Robinson and TCR, in respect of false or misleading statements for different taxation items, both parties argued their positions on these administrative penalties by reference to common underlying factual matters and contentions. [166] The Tribunal will therefore consider these common matters before applying its findings to particular administrative penalties.
  2. The Respondent submitted that the administrative penalties were correctly imposed on the Applicants, and correctly imposed on the basis of the ‘recklessness’ of each Applicant, for the following reasons:
    (a) The Applicants had not included amounts in their assessable income and TCR had failed to disclose any GST liability in relation to the transfer of the Property which they should have done. The Applicants had shown a disregard or indifference to these tax liabilities.

    (b) Mr Robinson had significant experience and skill in the purchase, development or subdivision and sale of real property. He was not new to business. The Respondent referred to previous property acquisitions and development undertaken by the Applicants, as well as evidence given by Mr Robinson of his assessment of the suitability of the Parent Block for subdivision prior to TCR’s acquisition of that land. The Respondent also referred to Mr Robinson’s father being an experienced real estate agent who assisted the Applicants in their property development activities.

    (c) Certain actions of the Applicants were inconsistent with the manner in which their taxation returns were lodged. For example, the Property was the subject of a transfer from TCR to Mr Robinson whereas the BAS and income tax returns did not treat this as a taxable transaction on the basis that Mr Robinson was already the beneficial owner of the Property.

    (d) Mr Robinson directed his solicitors to transfer the title to the Property into his personal name, having previously directed that the Parent Block be purchased in the name of TCR, so that he could claim the ‘main residence’ exemption from CGT, only holding the Property for a few days.

    (e) Mr Robinson and his father had colluded to obtain an intentionally low valuation for the Property, possibly to minimise stamp duty payable upon the transfer from TCR to Mr Robinson.

    (f) The ‘safe harbour’ exclusion from liability for administrative penalties where a registered tax or BAS agent has been engaged did not apply here. The Applicants had not discharged their onus to demonstrate that they provided their tax agents with all relevant information. The various advisors retained by the Applicants prepared the relevant BAS and income tax returns for the Applicants in accordance with the instructions of Mr Robinson and the information provided by him.

  3. The Applicants:
    (a) Disagreed with the Respondent’s characterisation of Mr Robinson as an experienced or sophisticated businessman. Mr Robinson said that his background is that of a carpenter, working as a sole trader. He submitted that the evidence provided of his confusion with the legal paperwork surrounding the transfer of the Property demonstrated his lack of sophistication in business matters.

    (b) Also disagreed with the Respondent’s characterisation of the Applicants’ prior property related activities. They said that the properties identified by the Respondent were either Mr Robinson’s own homes which he had renovated, or those of family.

    (c) Did not necessarily understand that TCR had obtained both legal and beneficial title or ‘the whole thing.’[167] NAB required a company (TCR) to be established. Mr Robinson signed the loan documentation under pressure, being at risk of losing his deposit if the contract for the Parent Block had failed to proceed.

    (d) Believed that they had placed reasonable reliance on professional advisers. Mr Robinson said that he realised he was ‘out of his depth’ with legal and taxation matters and retained three different accounting firms to assist him. He pointed to evidence of issues with his accountants which he said indicated he had been provided with conflicting and incorrect advice, which caused the errors that the Respondent relied upon. This was not ‘recklessness’ on the part of the Applicants. At worst it was ‘carelessness in failing to identify’ the accountants’ error or lack of understanding.[168]

  4. The Tribunal accepts the evidence given orally by Mr Robinson at the hearing and in the Applicants’ written submissions and SFIC that the Applicants sought and received advice from multiple professional advisers over a period of some years in relation to the legal and taxation treatment of the acquisition and subdivision of the Parent Block and sale of subdivided lots, including the Property. There is a lack of documentary or other corroborative evidence detailing the specific content of that advice, the information that was provided by the Applicants to the advisors and in some instances when particular advice was received. Mr Jucevic provided a brief statutory declaration, albeit one which did not address the advice that he may have provided and he apparently declined to appear before the Tribunal. The other advisers did not provide written statements or appear at the hearing.
  5. The Tribunal has some indication of the views of Mr Vlahos from the records of his communications with the Respondent when he was representing the Applicants, although these communications obviously occurred after the Respondent had commenced its review of the Applicants’ tax affairs. The various revisions to the relevant BAS were also made some time after the relevant transactions occurred. However, when focussed on the advice that was obtained around the time of the transactions involving the Property, the Tribunal believes the evidence is consistent with more than one advisor being retained during the course of 2018 and the Applicants’ long-term advisor (Mr Jucevic) having resigned or retired. Three different versions of the BAS for the quarter ended 31 December 2018 were ultimately lodged by different advisors.
  6. The Tribunal is also mindful that due to financial constraints the Applicants lost their professional legal representation shortly after making their applications to the Tribunal, and therefore, may have experienced challenges in obtaining evidence from or about their former advisers. For example, as described above the Respondent queried the Applicants’ filing of witness statements in the form of statutory declarations rather than affidavits. This was in addition to the significant volume of evidence that Mr Robinson was managing in relation to the other substantive issues, as the sole representative for himself and TCR, and presumably while also continuing to operate his business.
  7. The Tribunal accepts the submissions of the Respondent that Mr Robinson had been involved in other property developments. However, the Tribunal does not believe it is appropriate to characterise Mr Robinson, or the Applicants, as sophisticated or highly experienced, particularly in relation to legal or taxation matters that arise in this context. The property developments identified by the Respondent are limited in number and appear to be smaller in scale. As set out above, the Tribunal accepts the evidence of Mr Robinson that the Rosebud property involved a renovation and sale of a property owned by his grandmother.[169] The Tribunal also accepts that the first Blairgowrie property was the home of Mr Robinson and his partner for two years. The Tribunal’s impression is that the subdivision of the Parent Block was the most significant development undertaken by the Applicants by that time, both in terms of physical size and financial impact.
  8. None of the above should be taken as saying that the Applicants are to be absolved of taking responsibility for compliance with taxation laws or ensuring that accurate and complete taxation returns are lodged with the Respondent. However, it is relevant to the Respondent’s penalty submissions as they relate to the Applicants’ ‘knowledge, education, experience and skill.’ Recent observations of the Federal Court also draw attention to the need to recognise some level of informality in the affairs of small businesses, including in relation to documentary evidence and particularly where the small business is closely held.[170]
  9. The Tribunal is not willing to make a finding that Mr Robinson and his father ‘worked together’ to obtain an incorrect valuation for the Property.[171] The Tribunal was provided with a copy of the PRP Valuation dated 24 May 2018, which was prepared by a director of PRP and countersigned by a second qualified valuer, both of whom were subject to professional standards scheme obligations. The PRP Valuation sets out at length the matters taken into account in preparing the valuation, information relied upon, comparable sales and an explanation of the valuation methodology. It also contains a copy of an email from Mr Robinson instructing PRP to conduct the valuation. The PRP Valuation is also consistent with the earlier UPA Valuation prepared in February 2017 for, and on the instructions of, LaTrobe for the purposes of their assessing the security to be provided for a substantial loan from LaTrobe. The Tribunal suggests that caution should be exercised before any allegation is made that at least one, if not two, qualified valuers intentionally provided an inaccurate valuation in breach of their professional obligations.
  10. The Tribunal has found that no trust existed over the Parent Block or the Property in favour of Mr Robinson, and therefore the transfer of title to the Property from TCR to Mr Robinson in early October 2018 is recognised for taxation purposes. The Tribunal agrees with the Respondent’s position that this transfer was made in the course of, or furtherance of, TCR’s enterprise or business. The Tribunal has also found that evidence of Mr Robinson’s use of the Property is considerably lacking such that the Tribunal cannot be satisfied that the Property was Mr Robinson’s main residence, or otherwise his home, at any particular time. This means that to the extent that either of the Applicants’ tax returns were filed on a basis that did not reflect these matters, or either of the Applicants did not file tax returns which reflected these matters, that Applicant has made a false or misleading statement to the Respondent. The Tribunal is not satisfied that the Applicants took reasonable care in this context. The Applicants acknowledged the possibility that they had been ‘careless’ in their closing submissions.
  11. However, the Tribunal has also made a finding that it prefers the market value of the Property proposed by the Applicants over that proposed by the Respondent. This is based largely on the PRP Valuation and the consistent evidence given by Mr P on behalf of the T Co. The Tribunal views the reasonableness of the Applicants’ behaviour differently in relation to the market value issue.
  12. Accordingly, the Tribunal finds that:
    (a) TCR made a false or misleading statement to the Respondent when it omitted the consideration it received from Mr Robinson for the transfer of the Property in October 2018 from its assessable income, as disclosed in its income tax return for the 2019 year. A shortfall amount arose to TCR as a result of that statement: subsections 284-75(1), 284-80(1).

    (b) TCR similarly made a false or misleading statement to the Respondent when it did not include any GST liability in its BAS for the quarter ended 31 December 2018 in relation to that transfer (sale) of the Property, such that a shortfall arose.

  13. However, the Tribunal has found that it prefers the PRP Valuation as indicative of the market value of the Property at the time of the transfer from TCR to Mr Robinson and consequently:
    (a) The quantum of the GST shortfall amount, the subject of the TCR GST Penalty Assessment, which is based on the GST exclusive market value of the Property, must be determined by reference to the market value set out in the PRP Valuation.[172]

    (b) No deemed dividend arises for inclusion in Mr Robinson’s assessable income for the purposes of Division 7A of the ITAA 1936 in the 2019 year. The corollary of this is that no false or misleading statement was made by Mr Robinson giving rise to an income tax shortfall amount in this regard in the 2019 year for the purposes of the Robinson Penalty Assessment.

  14. In relation to the Robinson Amended Assessment, the Robinson Penalty Assessment and the Robinson Objection Decision, the Respondent’s assessment of Mr Robinson for income tax in the 2019 year was based on the inclusion of a deemed dividend under Division 7A. It did not address the inclusion in Mr Robinson’s assessable income of any capital gain arising from his disposal of the Property to T Co in circumstances where he was not entitled to the ‘main residence’ exemption. The decision under review by the Tribunal is the Robinson Objection Decision.
  15. In relation to whether either, or both of the Applicants, were ‘reckless’ having regard to the Tribunal’s previous findings:
    (a) The Tribunal believes that the Applicants, or at least their tax agents, knew or were grossly indifferent to a real and not fanciful risk that the Applicants’ position relating to the beneficial ownership of the Property was incorrect. The overwhelming weight of the documentary and circumstantial evidence was inconsistent with the existence of a trust in favour of Mr Robinson. The Tribunal has also referred to the lack of evidence relating to Mr Robinson’s occupation of the Property as his residence. This forms a basis for a finding of recklessness in relation to the failure to recognise the transfer of the Property from TCR to Mr Robinson in the income tax and GST lodgements of TCR.

    (b) Even if it is accepted that the Applicants provided all relevant information to their tax agent(s), the Tribunal’s view is that the tax agent(s) demonstrated recklessness as to the operation of taxation laws (both income tax and GST) in this regard. Therefore the ‘safe harbour’ exclusion from administrative penalties under subsection 284-75(6) is not available to TCR in this regard.

    (c) However the quantum of the penalty imposed in the TCR GST Penalty Assessment, which was subsequently confirmed in the TCR Objection Decision is affected by the Tribunal’s finding in relation to the market value of the Property.

    (d) The Tribunal has found that Mr Robinson did not make a false or misleading statement in relation to the non-inclusion of a deemed dividend in his income tax return for the 2019 year. Therefore, no issue relating to recklessness arises in this regard for the purposes of the Robinson Penalty Assessment as subsequently confirmed by the Robinson Objection Decision.

  16. There remains the question of whether the Tribunal should exercise the discretion under section 298-20 to remit all or part of the administrative penalty that has been imposed on TCR. As stated above, the Tribunal does not characterise Mr Robinson, the sole director and shareholder of TCR, as having the level of experience and knowledge contended by the Respondent, particularly in relation to taxation and legal issues. The Tribunal also accepts the Applicants’ evidence, supported by documents provided to the Tribunal, that Mr Robinson engaged multiple professional advisors to assist him, including replacing advisors when he became concerned about the quality of the advice that he had received. However, the penalties imposed on TCR arise as a consequence of the Applicants’ insistence that a trust arose over the Property (and previously the Parent Block), in circumstances where evidence of relevant facts is contrary to this conclusion or was apparently not recorded. Although the Tribunal is mindful of the Applicants’ status as a small business and their limited resources, the Tribunal does not believe that there are circumstances for remission of the penalties imposed on TCR.[173]
  17. The Tribunal observes that had it formed a view that Mr Robinson was liable under Division 284 for a penalty in relation to the non-inclusion of a deemed dividend in his assessable income, arising from the disputed valuation of the Property, the Tribunal would have exercised its discretion to remit that penalty, at least to the level of 25% for ‘failure to take reasonable care’ (rather than recklessness), on the basis that it was not unreasonable for him to rely on the conclusion reached in the PRP Valuation, particularly having regard to the possibly unusual circumstances of the purchase by T Co, his limited level of knowledge of taxation and legal matters, and his documented efforts to obtain correct and complete advice from several advisors qualified in these matters.

CONCLUSION

  1. Counsel for the Respondent told the Tribunal that the Respondent’s position was fair, and possibly event lenient, on the basis that the combined effect of the amended assessments issued to the Applicants was simply to levy tax on the amount that was actually received for the Property, being the $4.128 million, with approximately $2 million being taxed in the hands of TCR and $1.7 million being taxed in the hands of Mr Robinson and an amount of GST being levied on the price. This was despite the fact that the Property had been transferred twice in quick succession and tax therefore might have been levied on the full market value of the Property twice. However the Respondent chose not to do this and was not seeking to further increase the assessments issued. In the Respondent’s view the Applicants were seeking an outcome where no tax at all was paid on the disposal of the Property.[174]
  2. The Applicants’ view, as expressed by Mr Robinson, was that the intentions of the Applicants were clear and the Respondent’s treatment was a ‘money grab.’[175]
  3. For the reasons given above, the Tribunal does not fully agree with either position. Although the Tribunal recognises the potential economic merit of the Respondent’s position, practically it does represent the taxation of both parties to the same transaction on effectively the same amount. Counsel for the Respondent alluded to the two separate transfers of the Property (that is, from TCR to Mr Robinson and then from Mr Robinson to T Co) and the potential for the taxable amounts to arise from each of these disposal transactions. Where the Respondent had accepted the PRP Valuation as the market value of the Property, although an assessable dividend might not have arisen to Mr Robinson under section 109C (upon the transfer to him of the Property), it is likely that he would have made a capital gain of a similar amount upon the disposal of the Property by him to T Co. On the basis of the Tribunal’s findings above, that capital gain would not have been excluded from taxation by the main residence exemption.[176] However this is not the basis on which the Robinson Amended Assessment or Objection Decision was made.[177] The Tribunal is tasked with reviewing the Robinson Objection Decision and determining whether the Applicant (Mr Robinson) has discharged his burden of proving that the Robinson Amended Assessment is excessive or incorrect and what it should have been. The Tribunal has found that Mr Robinson has done so. Therefore the Tribunal must set aside the Robinson Objection Decision.
  4. Also for the reasons given above, the Tribunal does not believe that the Applicants have provided sufficient evidence in support of their contention that TCR held the legal title to the Property on trust for Mr Robinson at any time, or that the Property was Mr Robinson’s personal home or main residence. Therefore the Tribunal believes that the transfer of the Property from TCR to Mr Robinson was recognised for income taxation and GST purposes, such that an assessable amount arose to TCR as the entity disposing the Property and an amount of GST was payable on that disposal as a taxable supply made by TCR. Subject to the value to be used in determining these amounts, the Tribunal therefore does not believe that TCR has discharged its burden of proof in relation to the assessments underlying the TCR Objection Decision. Therefore the Tribunal will vary the TCR Objection Decision to reflect its view on the issue of market value, but otherwise affirm the TCR Objection Decision.
