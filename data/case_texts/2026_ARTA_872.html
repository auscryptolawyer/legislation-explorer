Statement of Reasons

INTRODUCTION

  1. The issue in this case is whether the Applicant, Ms Alexandra Peta Ward, was a resident of Australia for income tax purposes for the income years ended 30 June 2021, 30 June 2022, and 30 June 2023 (Relevant Period). Ms Ward’s tax residence is relevant to the income tax treatment of the capital gain she made on the disposal of her house located in St Ives, NSW (House).[1]
  2. The Respondent issued assessments for the Relevant Period (Assessments) on the basis of audits in which the Respondent determined that Ms Ward was not a resident of Australia during the Relevant Period, and calculated the amount of capital gain to be included in her taxable income resulting from the sale of the House.
  3. On 22 March 2024, Ms Ward objected to the Assessments and the Respondent’s determination of her residency status during the Relevant Period and calculation of capital gain on the sale of the House (Objection).[2]
  4. On 8 November 2024, the Respondent disallowed Ms Ward’s Objection with regard to her residency status, and allowed in part her Objection with regard to the calculation of capital gain on the sale of the House (Objection Decision).
  5. On 19 December 2024, Ms Ward applied for review of the Objection Decision’s disallowance of her Objection to the determination of her residency status during the Relevant Period.
  6. As the taxpayer, Ms Ward bears the burden of proving that the Assessments are excessive or otherwise incorrect and what the assessments should have been.[3]

WAS MS WARD A TAX RESIDENT OF AUSTRALIA DURING THE RELEVANT PERIOD?

Law

  1. Relevantly for this case, the statutory definition of ‘resident’ includes a person ‘whose domicile is in Australia, unless the Commissioner is satisfied that the person’s permanent place of abode is outside Australia’.[4] As the parties agree that Ms Ward’s domicile is Australia, the sole substantive issue for determination in this case is whether Ms Ward’s ‘permanent place of abode’ was outside Australia during the Relevant Period.
  2. ‘Permanent place of abode’ is not statutorily defined. Case law establishes that a ‘permanent place of abode’ is a town or country in which the taxpayer is physically residing in a permanent way, having ‘definitely abandoned’ residence in Australia.[5] However, a ‘permanent place of abode’ in this context does not necessarily mean a place where someone is going to live for the rest of their life.[6] The inquiry is an annual one, and ‘permanent’ does not necessarily mean ‘indefinite’; rather, it is to be contrasted with ‘temporary or transitory’.[7] The inquiry is based on the totality of a taxpayer’s circumstances, and relevant factors include the length of the taxpayer’s stay overseas, the nature of their accommodation, and the nature and durability of their economic and personal connections overseas and in Australia.[8]

Facts and Contentions

  1. Ms Ward was born in Australia and is an Australian citizen. She is married with two children.
  2. She purchased the House in January 2005 for $815,000.
  3. Ms. Ward lived in the House until 1 May 2009, when she left Australia with her family to move to the United States for her husband’s overseas work assignment. Shortly thereafter, the House was leased out as a rental property and remained so until it was sold in February 2023 for $2.3 million, resulting in the capital gain that is (indirectly) at issue in this case.
  4. Ms Ward entered the United States on an E3 Dependent visa, which is for dependents of Australians in specialty occupations. The visa is valid for two years at a time, does not allow the holder to work, is contingent upon the validity of the primary visa holder’s E3 visa (in this case, that of Ms. Ward’s husband), and upon its expiry must be applied for anew from outside of the United States.
  5. From May 2009 until November 2014, Ms Ward lived in rental accommodation in Foster City, California. In November 2014, Ms. Ward bought a house (with her husband) in Foster City, and lived there until February 2021, when the house was sold and Ms Ward moved with her family to the Netherlands.[9]
  6. Ms Ward entered the Netherlands in February 2021 on a tourist visa and since that time has been residing there in the same leased accommodation on a temporary residence permit.
  7. In her submissions and at the hearing (which Ms Ward did not attend), Ms Ward (via her representative) placed heavy emphasis on the contingent and temporary nature of her presence overseas based on her visa conditions in both the United States and the Netherlands, which gave her no right of permanent residence or legal capacity to remain lawfully present overseas indefinitely, and did not allow her to work (she has earned no foreign income while living overseas).
  8. During her time living in the United States, Ms Ward’s children attended local public schools and Ms Ward joined the Parent-Teacher Association; she owned two second-hand cars (which she sold before departing the United States); and she filed joint U.S. income tax returns with her spouse. During her time living in the Netherlands, Ms Ward’s children attended the International School of The Hague; and Ms Ward became a member of community groups for parents and women ex-pats. Ms Ward was living in the Netherlands at the time the House was sold.
  9. Between 1 July 2009 and 30 June 2023, Ms Ward returned to Australia six times to visit and stay with her mother and check in on the management and arrangements for the rental of the House; during this period, she spent a total of 145 days in Australia. Ms Ward did not return to Australia at all between 5 November 2019 and 30 June 2023 (a period which included the Covid pandemic).[10]
  10. Ms Ward contends that she intended to move back to Australia from the United States in 2021, and had applied for and been offered spots for her children to enrol in a private school in Sydney’s northern beaches in anticipation thereof, but decided against moving back because of the difficulties and expense of doing so caused by the Covid pandemic. While Ms Ward has no fixed return date, she contends that it has always been her intent to return to Australia, in part because of the need to take care of her aging mother.
  11. Ms Ward also contends that she has retained meaningful connections to Australia during her years living overseas: she has maintained ongoing relationships with her mother, extended family, and friends; has stored her household belongings at a relative’s property in Budgewoi; has maintained her Australian bank accounts and driver’s license; and is a beneficiary of an Australian family trust.
  12. Respondent contends that Ms Ward’s ‘conduct, actions and objective manifestations of her intention are overwhelmingly consistent with her living permanently overseas’.[11]

Consideration and Decision

  1. I consider a primary factor in this case to be physical presence: the relative amount of time Ms Ward has spent living overseas compared to the amount of time she has spent in Australia. I give considerable weight to the sheer length of time Ms Ward has spent overseas – at the time of writing, in excess of 17 years (and at the time of the relevant CGT event occurring with respect to the House, almost 14 years). During this lengthy period of living overseas in a settled way (as discussed below), Ms Ward’s return visits have been infrequent and short (with the exception of her return visit in 2011). I note that over time Ms Ward’s visits to Australia became more infrequent and shorter, and that she did not return to Australia at all during the Relevant Period. I consider that living overseas for such a long period of time, with such minimal visitation back to Australia, is indicative of permanent residence rather than a temporary or transitory stay overseas.
  2. I acknowledge the impact that the Covid pandemic had on the ease and cost of international travel, but I do not consider that returning to Australia was impossible during the Relevant Period, and it certainly did not transform Ms Ward’s already settled residency in the United States, where she had lived for a decade before Covid began (and in a house that she owned for more than five of the latter of those years), into a temporary or transitory stay. When Ms Ward and her husband sold their home in the United States after living there in a settled way for well over a decade, they did not return to Australia, but rather moved to the Netherlands and set up family and professional life there in a permanent way. I consider that the foregoing indicates that Ms Ward had definitely abandoned her Australian residence well before the Relevant Period.
  3. In addition to the length of Ms Ward’s overseas residency (which is ongoing), I consider that the nature of her living situation overseas was settled and permanent (in the relevant sense) rather than temporary and transitory. Ms Ward and her family well and truly settled in Foster City, California: her husband worked there to earn the income that supported the family there, her kids spent their formative school years there, she bought a family home there and lived in it for many years, and she engaged with community life there. Thereafter, she and her family moved to the Netherlands and similarly settled in by sending the kids to school there and living in the same rented accommodation for many years. In both the United States and the Netherlands, Ms Ward lived with her family and supported her husband’s work and her children’s education.
  4. During the Relevant Period, Ms. Ward also took actions that indicate a definitive abandonment of her prior Australian residency: most notably, selling the House, her most significant asset and tangible connection to Australia. Even during the period that she maintained ownership of the House, it was rented out and was not available for her to live in or stay in during her brief and infrequent visits. That the House was sold after Ms Ward moved from the United States to the Netherlands does nothing to support Ms Ward’s contention that her intent was always to live overseas only temporarily. Ms Ward also took her name off of Australia’s electoral roll, a practically and symbolically significant act indicating Ms Ward’s sense of political belonging (or the lack thereof).
  5. That Ms Ward did not work or earn any foreign income while living overseas does not indicate that her stay overseas was temporary or transitory in nature; to the contrary, her lengthy residency overseas in support of her husband and his career, and her children and their schooling, is an objective manifestation of a settled and ongoing mode of living and an indication that she intended to live overseas with her family in a permanent way before and during the Relevant Period.
  6. I accept that Ms Ward maintained some social and economic ties to Australia, but I consider them to pale in comparison to the reality of her overseas life. She spent the great bulk of the Relevant Period and the preceding years physically present overseas with her husband and children, the family’s income (via Ms Ward’s husband) was earned overseas, and her children’s education took place overseas. While Ms Ward’s extended family and friends remained in Australia, she had limited contact with them, and her economic ties dwindled (as she sold the House and does not appear to have kept much money in her Australian bank accounts). I place little weight on Ms Ward’s contention that she is the beneficiary of an Australian family trust; there is not sufficient information before me regarding the trust to conclude that it represents a significant economic tie to Australia. I have mentioned above the civic significance of Ms Ward removing her name from Australia’s electoral roll.
  7. Despite Ms Ward’s emphasis on the technicalities of her visa conditions while living overseas, I do not give much weight to the contention that, because her visas were ‘temporary’, her residence overseas was also temporary. The practical reality is that Ms Ward was able to continue to get ‘temporary’ visas in succession such that she has been able to live overseas continuously for more than 15 years. Putting substance over form, it is clear that Ms Ward’s physical presence overseas, her objectively manifested intentions to set up her and her family’s life overseas, and her family and economic ties are indicative of a stay overseas that is permanent in nature with regard to the years in question rather than temporary or transitory. That she was able and willing to buy a house in the United States is indicative that she was confident that she would be able to live in the United States in a prolonged and settled way, which is what she did. That she did not declare the sale of her United States house in her Australian income tax return for the relevant year is indicative that she did not consider herself to be a resident of Australia (despite declaring herself as an Australian resident in the relevant income tax returns).
  8. I have considered the cases mentioned in Ms Ward’s Amended Statement of Facts and Contentions, but they are factually inapposite to this case and I consider that they provide little guidance other than identifying the general factors that are to be considered when making a determination regarding a taxpayer’s permanent place of abode (and I have considered those factors above).
  9. For the reasons set out above, on the totality of the circumstances, I conclude that Ms Ward was not a resident of Australia during the Relevant Period because she had abandoned her Australian residency to live overseas in a permanent way during the Relevant Period, establishing a permanent place of abode first in the United States and then in the Netherlands. Ms Ward has not discharged the burden of proving that her permanent place of abode was not outside of Australia during the Relevant Period and that the Assessments are thus excessive or otherwise incorrect.
  10. It follows that the decision under review must be affirmed.

[1] As a general matter, under Division 118 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1997 (Cth) (ITAA 1997), the capital gain realised on the disposal of a taxpayer’s main residence is disregarded. However, this exemption is not available if the taxpayer is an ‘excluded foreign resident’. (The exemption may be only partially available if the dwelling was used as the taxpayer’s main residence for only part of the ownership period or if it was used to produce assessable income during the ownership period. A capital gain discount was available to foreign residents on the disposal of their main residence up until 8 May 2012). An ‘excluded foreign resident’ is one who has been a foreign resident for 6 years or more. ITAA 1997 ss 118-110(3)(a), (4). A ‘foreign resident’ is one who is not a resident of Australia for the purposes of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (Cth) (ITAA 1936). ITAA 1997 s 995-1.

[2] The Objection also covered multiple prior years, but is only valid and relevant for the years in which there was a tax liability (that is, the Relevant Period).
