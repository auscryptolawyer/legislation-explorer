BACKGROUND AND ISSUES

Issues

  1. The claimed deductions relate to expenses incurred by the applicants as franchisees of IPF Finance Corporation Pty Ltd (IPF Finance) and claimed as income tax deductions (the claimed deductions). Those expenses as well as the tax savings and cash benefits obtained by each of the applicants is set out in Annexure A to these reasons.
  2. The issues to be determined in each application in respect of the claimed deductions are:
    • (a) whether the claimed deductions are allowable under section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 (ITAA 1936);
    • (b) if the claimed deductions were allowable under that section, whether the applicants obtained tax benefits in connection with a scheme to which Part IVA of the ITAA 1936 applies.

The IPF 97 Project

  1. In these Reasons the “IPF 97 Project” refers to the proposal contained in the IPF 97 Information Memorandum (IM) and the arrangements effected by the Licence Agreements, Franchise Agreement, Management Agreement, Loan Deed and Indemnity Agreement summarised below and matters incidental to those arrangements.
  2. It is common cause that the various companies involved in the franchise arrangements for the IPF 97 Project (IPF Finance; Retail Management Australia (RMA); Commercial Credit Finance (CCF), Commercial Insurance Australia (CIA); Insurance Premium Finance (IPF); Easy Rent Pty Ltd (Easy Rent); Mortgages Direct Pty Ltd (Mortgages Direct) and Chambers Capital Group) were associates.
  3. IPF Finance (the Franchisor) and RMA (the Manager) were also trustees for the IPF Unit Trust and the Retail Management Services Trust respectively. Denby Vale Pty Ltd (Denby Vale) was the unit holder in those trusts. Denby Vale is associated with Mr Stephen Wharton. A Heads of Agreement was executed by IPF and Denby Vale which, among other things, governed the manner of distributions to Denby Vale by the issue of units and cash distributions: T101 and 102/V1/1-67 and T902/V5/1345-1347.
  4. IPF, Easy Rent and Mortgages Direct provided the financial products referred to in the IPF 97 Project documents. Those entities are collectively referred to in this decision as the “Financial Providers”.

The IPF product

  1. An annexure to the ATO audit report contained in the “T” documents before the Tribunal summarises the accounts for IPF for the years ending 30 June 1995 to 1998: T820/V5/1297 and T821/V5/1298.
  2. The accounts for the year ended 30 June 1996 disclosed that IPF made a net profit of $31,887 on income of $92,306. The income comprised premiums $37,000), other revenue ($7,115) and the interest received ($48,191). Mr Leunig who was one of the principal witnesses for the applicants produced the accounts for IPF for the year ended 30 June 1997 which showed the comparative figures for the previous year (annexure “KBL 1” to his affidavit sworn 22 July 2005 – Ex A3; that affidavit is referred to in these Reasons as Leunig Ex A3).
  3. Mr Leunig was cross-examined about the source of IPF’s income for each of the years ended 30 June 1996 and 1997. He was unable to state the source of its income.
  4. For the year ended 30 June 1997, IPF made a net loss of $117,765. Its income for that year was $102,680 comprising interest received ($96,800) and other revenue ($5,880). Its expenses in the year ended 30 June 1997 included $124,000 paid in royalties. Mr Leunig was unable to advise as to the reason for those royalty payments.
  5. The balance sheet for IPF for the year ended 30 June 1996 showed net assets of $29,461. Its principal asset comprised trade debtors of $321,423. The balance sheet for IPF for the year ended 30 June 1997 showed a deficiency of assets over liabilities of $98,873. The company’s principal assets comprised trade debtors ($286,371) and “loan accounts” ($86,669). Its principal liabilities comprised loans of $274,800 and trade creditors of $171,365. Mr Leunig was unable to identify the trade debtors or trade creditors or the loans made to and by IPF.
  6. The accounts for the period 1994 to 1998 summarised in the ATO audit report and the accounts annexed to the affidavit of Mr Leunig indicate that at no time was IPF a profitable company or a company that had, as claimed in the IM, a “successful track record”. In the Tribunal’s view it is clear that any prospective investor who had asked for the past accounts of IPF would have discovered that this statement in the IM was misleading, as was the general impression created in the memorandum that IPF was an established business successfully marketing its product.
  7. In Leunig Ex A3 at [15], Mr Leunig referred to relationships being established with insurance brokers and insurance intermediaries in Melbourne and Sydney. He referred to Gow-Gates Financial Services Pty Ltd but could not demonstrate whether any contract had been entered into with that company: see Leunig Ex A3, “KBL 6”/84 and “KBL 8”/100, 109, 114. Nor was there any evidence of executed copies of consultancy agreements.
  8. In relation to projected fee income for franchisees from IPF, it should be noted that the agreement to be entered into between IPF and insurance brokers provided that a broker received 35% of the gross interest payments made by borrowers – see the schedule to the consultancy agreement forming part of “KBL18” to Leunig Ex A3 (at p 536).
  9. The Franchise Agreement provided that franchisees would receive 60% of the net profit (defined to mean the annual net profit as determined by IPF and Easy Rent in respect of finance provided by them) “derived” by IPF from the insurance premium finance by it (cl 1.1.15 of the Franchise Agreement). That is, the calculation of the franchisees’ income was net of the amount paid to brokers; franchisees received 60% of the net profit earned by IPF from receiving 65% of the gross interest payments made by clients.

The Easy Rent product

  1. T909/V5/1385 is a historical company extract for Easy Rent. The extract discloses that Easy Rent was first registered on 30 June 1989 as HID Insurance Services (WA) Pty Ltd; the company changed its name on 16 April 1997 to Easy Rent; Mr Jones was appointed director of Easy Rent on 15 February 1990; Mr Leunig was appointed a director on 1 June 1997; and the ultimate holding company of Easy Rent was Chambers Capital Group Ltd.
  2. According to Mr Leunig, it was not until early 1997 that the Easy Rent product was first “publicly” marketed (Leunig Ex A3 at [11] and [12]). Presumably, that coincided with the change in the company’s name.
  3. The accounts of Easy Rent for the years ended 30 June 1994 to 1998 are summarised in attachments to the audit report: T822/V5/1299 and T823/V5/1300. For the year ended 30 June 1997, the accounts show that the principal source of income was “contract income”. Mr Leunig was unable to give an explanation as to the source of that income.
  4. The profit and loss statement for the year ended 30 June 1997 further shows that the company made a net loss of $38,421. It paid $80,000 in management fees and $40,000 in royalties – again, Mr Leunig was unable to give an explanation as to the reasons for those payments.
  5. The balance sheet for Easy Rent for the year ended 30 June 1997 disclosed a net deficiency of assets of $50,073. The company’s principal assets comprised loans made to Jaymark Holdings (a company associated with Mr Jones) and Mortgages Direct. Its principal liability was a loan from CCF.
  6. The profit and loss statement for the year ended 30 June 1998 disclosed that Easy Rent received $205,528 in rental income but the company made a net loss of $4,457. The company’s principal expense was rental equipment depreciation. Other major expenses comprised commissions paid ($36,669), franchisee commissions ($29,373) and interest payments ($57,082).
  7. Easy Rent’s balance sheet for the year ended 30 June 1998 disclosed a net deficiency of assets of $54,530. The company’s principal assets comprised the rental equipment that it had financed (under the terms of its rent agreements, Easy Rent became the owner of the equipment). The company also had a significant receivable for commissions. The company’s principal liability was a debt of $1,150,558 owing to CCF.
  8. The IM at p 9 is clearly intended to convey the impression that the business of Easy Rent was established, at least in Western Australia. Had prospective investors enquired, they would have found that Easy Rent had, at best, barely commenced trading by 30 June 1997. It is clear that at the time of the distribution of the IM there would have been no meaningful trading results on which the projections for franchisee income could be based.

The Mortgages Direct product

  1. T910/V5/1393 is an historical company extract for Mortgages Direct. The historical company extract disclosed that:
    • (a) The company was first registered on 23 March 1998 as Noredale Nominees Pty Ltd. On 7 March 1990 the company changed its name to Noredale Financial Services Pty Ltd. The company changed its name again on 6 May 1991 to Chambers Financial Services Pty Ltd and yet again, on 20 August 1992 to Noredale Financial Services Pty Ltd. On 28 February 1996 the company changed its name to Mortgages Direct.
    • (b) Mr Jones was appointed a director of the company on 4 August 1988. Mr Leunig was appointed a director on 13 November 1996.
    • (c) The ultimate holding company of Mortgages Direct was Chambers Capital Group Ltd.
  2. The accounts for Mortgages Direct for the years ended 30 June 1994 to 1998 were summarised in an attachment to the audit report – T824/V5/1301 and T825/V5/1302. The profit and loss statements disclosed no income or expenditure for the years ended 30 June 1994 to 1996. The profit and loss statement for the year ended 30 June 1997 disclosed that the company received $103,480 in income, of which $92,000 was described as being “National Mutual”. Mr Leunig was not able to identify the source of that income. Further, the company made a net loss of $43,354. The company’s principal expenditure was $80,000 in management fees and $40,000 in royalties – again, Mr Leunig was unable to provide any explanation for those payments
  3. The profit and loss statement for the year ended 30 June 1998 showed that Mortgages Direct received $239,966 in income, nearly all of which was commissions; the company made a net loss of $4,933 and its principal expense was franchisee commissions.
  4. The balance sheets for Mortgages Direct disclosed that in every year from 30 June 1994 there was a deficiency of net assets. In the year ended 30 June 1997 the deficiency was $124,256 and in the year ended 30 June 1998, it increased to $129,189. In the year ended 30 June 1997 the company’s principal asset comprised a loan to Jaymark Holdings. Its principal liabilities comprised loans from “Finkelstein” and Easy Rent. In the year ended 30 June 1998 the company’s principal assets comprised commissions receivable - $219,000. That amount, in the form of a liability to pay franchisee commissions, also represented the company’s principal liability.
  5. According to Mr Leunig, the Mortgages Direct product began in the early 1990s and was a mortgage broking service. While there was no direct evidence to the contrary, it should be noted from the account summary in the audit report that for the years ended 1994 to 1996 Mortgages Direct received no income and incurred no expenses. There was little evidence of any business activity by Mortgages Direct during 1997 to 2000.
  6. There was much uncertainty as to the nature of the business operated by Mortgages Direct (if any). The IM stated that the franchises to be offered were for the operation of a marketing business within the finance industry that provided “confirmed sources of finance” for “securitised home mortgages to the community for new home loans and the refinancing of existing home loans, together with innovative home loan reduction programmes” (p 7). The memorandum further stated that each of the financial “services” had a common denominator – “the lending of money to credit-worthy clients”. Those statements suggest that the business of Mortgages Direct was the provision of home loans. Mr Weir, one of the applicant’s witness, read the IM that way. At [10] of his affidavit (Weir Ex A14), he referred to the statements contained in the IM but noted that he had been instructed that Mortgages Direct in fact took the role “as purely a broker of mortgages”.
  7. The IM contained a further description of the business of Mortgages Direct at p 9. It stated that:

“Mortgages Direct does not operate in the market as just another home loan provider or an extension of a finance broking arm, but was formed primarily to develop ‘Finance Consultants’ Australia-wide who wish to provide financial services to their existing client bases.

The concept has the capacity to significantly increase the potential earnings of these finance consultancy firms. Mortgages Direct has targeted in excess of 100 such intermediaries and established contractual relationships with many of these firms.” (emphasis added)

  1. The business described by those statements is far from clear. The promotional material prepared by IPF Finance and/or RMA after June 1997 was also equivocal. The promotional material was directed primarily to the “Wealth Pro” product which was not referred to in the IM.
  2. “KBL14” annexed to Leunig Ex A3 is a document that Mr Leunig described as being an “early development” of the franchise business (at [22]). The document described the “Franchisor” as operating as a finance company specialising in, among other things, providing residential finance via mortgage originators to home buyers and refinancing existing home loans and providing homesaver insurance through a product known as “Home Saver Insurance”
  3. That accorded with references in the Licence Agreement made between IPF Finance and Mortgages Direct: see recital A and cl 5 where it was stated that Mortgages Direct provided residential finance and associated loan protection policies. Significantly, that statement was repeated in the description of the Products in item 6 to the Schedule to the Franchise Agreement.
  4. No agreements involving Mortgages Direct were produced in evidence – that is, agreements between Mortgages Direct and lenders pursuant to which Mortgages Direct was appointed as agents for the lenders or between Mortgages Direct and brokers. There was no evidence that Mortgages Direct ever entered into any arrangements with lenders that enabled them to act as a mortgage broker.
  5. Having regard to the above analysis of the documentary evidence Mr Leunig’s evidence that the Financial Providers were profitable prior to the commencement of the IPF 97 Project is rejected by the Tribunal. It is inconsistent with the accounts of the companies concerned as disclosed by the audit undertaken by the ATO. It was apparent in cross-examination that Mr Leunig had no understanding and little knowledge of the accounts of the companies. He had not reviewed the accounts prior to making his affidavits and witness statement or giving evidence.

THE AGREEMENTS FOR THE IPF 97 PROJECT

Licence Agreements (T103/V1/68-87)

  1. By Licence Agreements dated 1 June 1997, IPF, Easy Rent and Mortgages Direct, as Licensors, each granted IPF Finance, as Licensee, a licence for 10 years from the Commencement Date to market the Products and to franchise the marketing of the Products to third party Franchisees “in accordance with a system developed by the Licensee to enable Franchisees to locate and recruit new Clients for the Licensor for the Products in the Territory and further for the Licensee (as franchisor) to enter into Franchise Agreements with Franchisees for that purpose and to receive royalty fees from Franchisees under the Franchise Agreements” (cl 1) (emphasis added). The Franchisees were not parties to the Licences.
  2. Pursuant to the Licences:
    • (a) the Licensors were to pay direct to the Franchisees the Franchisee’s Entitlement (cl 4);
    • (b) the Licensors were to deliver annually to the Licensee at the Licensee’s request a report specifying the Clients who had been referred by Franchisees to the Licensor and to whom the Licensor had supplied any Product (IPF and Easy Rent) or with whom the Licensor has entered into a mortgage (Mortgages Direct) (cl 5). There was no evidence that any such reports were requested by the applicants and/or provided.
  3. Clause 4.3 of the Licence Agreements with IPF and Easy Rent provided for the Licensee to direct the Licensor to withhold and deduct from the Franchisee Entitlements the amounts set out in cll 4.2, 4.3 and 4.4 of the Franchisee Agreement. Those amounts were interest, management fees, annual royalty and loan principal payable under the various agreements entered into by Franchisees. The Licence Agreement with Mortgages Direct made no such provision.

The IM

  1. In about May 1997 IPF Finance issued a “Business Opportunity Information Memorandum” and began marketing the franchises through Chambers Investment Planners. Each applicant produced the IM (Mr Player’s affidavit, Ex A9, “JRP 4”; Mr Fyson’s affidavit, Ex A1. “CHF 6” and Mr Leunig’s affidavit sworn 20 May 2005 Ex A2, “KBL 8”) (Mr Player’s affidavit sworn 14 May 2005 is referred to in this decision as Player Ex A9; Mr Fyson’s affidavit sworn 13 May 2005 is referred to as Fyson Ex A1 and Mr Leunig’s affidavit sworn 20 May 2005 is referred to as Leunig Ex A2).
  2. The front sheet of the IM described the content as a “Business Opportunity”, and a “Finance Business Franchise”. “The Franchise Offer” was described at p 8 of the IM as follows:-

“IPF Finance Corporation Ltd (“the Franchisor”) is granting up to nine hundred and fifty (950) franchises, each for a ten year period.

Each Franchise will be granted an exclusive geographic area and will operate in the marketing of financial services within the badged products and services of the Franchisor.

The Franchisor will provide a full service to the Franchisees to assist them to develop and maintain their business.

These include, but are not limited to, computer software, licensing and representative requirements, accounting and financial control, training and marketing. These are provided as a service to benefit and assist the Franchisee.”

  1. The IM further stated at p 5 that:

“ IPF Finance Corporation Ltd currently provides finance under the badged product names of Insurance Premium Finance, Easy Rent (Rental Finance) and Mortgages Direct.”

  1. The “Summary of Franchise Offer” contained in the IM at p 4 stated:
    • “IPF Finance Corporation Ltd has identified and operates in three strategic and profitable financial service markets and is now granting exclusive Franchise rights to market these financial services to both businesses and individuals based on specific geographic locations throughout Australia.
  • The Franchises have been structured so that the Franchisees have the opportunity to elect to either have their Franchise area managed by a Professional Manager or manage it themselves.
  • The first year’s expense is $32,000, followed by annual fees, based on the gross revenue of each Franchise.
  • A business loan of up to $20,000 at an interest rate of 7% per annum, secured by the Franchise and repayable from the net profits of the Franchise business is available for Franchisees.
  • The Loan Insurance Fee provides the Franchisee with an insurance policy which will repay the business loan and any accrued interest, in full, should the Franchise not make sufficient net profit to repay the loan.
  • The loan is repayable from the net profit of the Franchise business and loan insurance is available.
  • If the business loan option is used, the Franchisee is required to pay $12,000 of the first year’s Franchise expenses at the time of application. (This includes $375 for legal documentation and stamp duty and $625 Loan Insurance Fee).
  • A short term credit facility of $10,000 may also be made to approved applicants.
  • Applicants wishing to proceed with this Business Opportunity, should read this Information Memorandum and then complete the Application Form attached and forward it to the Franchisor.”
  1. The IM at pp12 and 13 contained “Estimated Returns to Franchisee” on a pre-tax basis from a franchise operated for a 10 year period. No explanation of how the projections were derived, or any assumptions on which they were based was included. The estimated returns were expressly disclaimed.
  2. The various experts who gave evidence agreed that the only information in relation to the estimated returns to the franchises was stated at p 11 of the IM as follows:

“The Income and Expenditure estimates for the Franchisee have been based on the business being operated for 10 years.

The estimates are based on the development of a network of intermediaries and mortgage originators to market and distribute the products and services.

The Franchisee receives income from each financial transaction completed in their geographic franchise area.”

(Dawson Ex R8, Report [20]; Acheson Ex R6, Report [2(k)] pp 5-6; 1st and 7th paras p 14; [8] pp 28-29)

  1. At p 21 the IM contained information as to how franchises were to be allocated. It detailed the availability of the 950 franchises by State or Territory franchise areas, beginning with New South Wales, as follows: -

“1. FRANCHISE AREA NSW (NEW SOUTH WALES)

IPF Finance Corporation Ltd is granting up to a maximum of 242 Franchises in this area based on postcodes of appropriate size. The first Franchise allocated will be numbered NSW001. The last will be numbered NSW242”..........

and so on for Victoria, Queensland, South Australia, Australian Capital Territory, Tasmania, Northern Territory and Western Australia.

The projections in the IM

  1. “KBL 14” to Leunig Ex A3 contained projections for IPF and Mortgages Direct said to have been given to Mr Leunig in the “early development” of the franchising proposal ([22]). The document projected total franchisee income over ten years at $103,250. A loan was to be granted to franchisees which, together with interest, would amount to $23,000 over ten years. Franchisees would earn $81,250 over ten years after deducting the loan and interest.
  2. The projections were based on 100 brokers agreeing to exclusively market the insurance premium product with each broker funding $3 million on an interest charge of 7% and with 10% of the interest charged being allocated to two franchisees; a further 100 representatives marketing the insurance premium funding product independently with each representative funding $1 million with interest charges at 7% and 20% of the interest charge being allocated to franchisees; and 100 originators marketing the Mortgages Direct product and each originator writing 60 home loans per annum generating a total of $6,000 home loans with trailing commission.
  3. It is to be noted that the projected franchisee income on those assumptions was significantly less than that projected in the IM.
  4. There was no evidence as to the circumstances in which the document “KBL 14” was prepared or, indeed, who prepared the document. The significant increase in projected franchisee income in the IM was not explained.
  5. The IM contained no information about the derivation of the projections for franchisee income. Further, there was little evidence as to how the projections were made. Consequently, the only means by which the applicants were able to obtain any evidence concerning the reasonableness of the projections was to instruct persons who were supposedly experts in the areas of business conducted by IPF, Easy Rent and Mortgages Direct. Significantly, it was necessary for each of those persons to make their own assumptions so as to assess the projections contained in the IM.
  6. In his report (Dawson Ex R8) at paragraph [20], Mr Dawson, a chartered accountant, and independent expert called by the respondent stated that:

“(a) ... In these circumstances:-

(i) it would not have been possible for an investor to properly assess whether the returns projected in the IM would adequately compensate for the risk associated with the investment; and

(ii) any calculated measure of return, including an IRR, would have been meaningless.

(b) The figures in the column headed “Total” were arguably misleading because those figures do not include the outgoings in the column headed “Base year”. The figures showing in the “Total” column in the “Net Position” line did not represent the projected net return at the end of the ten year life of the franchise.”

  1. In his report (Acheson Ex R6, pp 5-6 and 14) Mr Acheson, a franchise expert and chartered accountant, also called by the respondent refers to mistakes in these estimates and to inconsistencies between the estimates and the Franchise and Management Agreements. Further he states at pp14 and 28-29:

“... there is no apparent attempt to conform to any of the requirements of either the Franchising Code of Practice or the much discussed new Franchising Code of Conduct relating to providing the base/assumptions of the representations and other information. These projections would appear to apply irrespective of the location of the franchise territory with no mention of any variation of projection of estimated net income.” (page 14)

“The projected returns for the franchisees were overstated in the projections as the first year negative figures have not been deducted from the total return. The initial investment of $32,000 should be deducted since the Franchise Agreement unusually has no provision for renewal therefore there is no value left at the end of ten years, capital or income. This contrasts to an investment in say shares where the shares will still exist at the end of ten years with a capital value and a regular income. ...”

“... To have the projected earnings of $40,364 over ten years in nominal dollars for an investment of $32,000 is not a great return, for an investment in a new, untested business.” (pages 28-29).

  1. The experts called by the applicants, Messrs Harvey, Janeczko, and Weir also agreed that the assumptions they drew to provide their opinions on the projected income figures in the IM were assumptions they would expect to see identified in the IM. It is apparent from their reports that they were not provided with earlier projections contained in “KBL 14” to Leunig Ex A3.
  2. The Tribunal notes that the projections bore no resemblance to, and cannot be justified on the basis of, the past financial performance of the Financial Providers. The past financial performance of the Financial Providers would, of course, ordinarily be the starting point for any assessment of the projections. Significantly:
    • (a) none of the applicants made any inquiries concerning the past financial performance of the Financial Providers prior to entering into the IPF 97 Project;
    • (b) none of the applicants’ experts sought information about the past financial performance of the Financial Providers prior to expressing their opinions on the reasonableness of the projections.
  3. The Tribunal also notes that the projections in the IM indicated that the applicants could not expect to make a net gain over the term of the franchise until year 8 and that the total gain would only be approximately $58,000 (under option B). That represented a very modest projected gain for the amount outlayed given the ten year term of the Franchise Agreement. That is particularly so as most of the gain was projected to be made in the last year – a gain to be made in the tenth year was obviously a highly speculative and uncertain forecast.

The Franchise Agreement (“EJRP 7”, “CHF 9” and “KBL 9”)

  1. Recitals A to E of the Franchise Agreement recited that:
    1. The Financial Providers have granted the Franchisor the exclusive right under licence to market the following financial services and products:
      • (1) Insurance Premium Finance;
      • (2) Easy Rent;
      • (3) Mortgages Direct.
    2. The Franchisor has developed a system for the identification and recruitment of potential clients for the Products.
    1. The Franchisor has agreed to grant an exclusive franchise within the Territory to the Franchisee, whereby the Franchisee operates the Franchised Business on the basis of finding new clients for the Products in the Territory in accordance with the System, and upon the terms and conditions contained in this Agreement.
    1. The System requires franchisees to strictly comply with the standards, specifications, procedures and policies of the Franchisor for the uniform conduct and operation of the Franchised Business.
    2. The success of the System depends upon Franchisees’ compliance with this Agreement.
  2. The System was defined in cl 1.1.33 of the Franchise Agreement as follows:

“System means the system developed by the Franchisor involving the identification and recruitment of Clients for the Products through marketing and contact with registered insurance brokers, finance and mortgage originators operating throughout Australia for the purpose of introducing those Clients to the Financial Providers who will:

1.1.33.1 provide finance to those Clients for the purpose of allowing those Clients to pay insurance premiums, obtain the use of plant and equipment and purchase residential property;

THE FRANCHISE BUSINESSES

The System

  1. It is apparent from the Licence Agreement, Franchise Agreement and the Management Agreement that the purpose of the Franchised Businesses was to utilise “the System” (note in particular, the grant of right by IPF Finance as Franchisor in cl 2.1.2.1 of the Franchise Agreement). Consequently, the System was central to the rights granted by IPF Finance to Franchisees. The System was the essence of the Franchise as formulated by the Franchise Agreement.
  2. As noted above, the System supposedly involved marketing to and contact with insurance brokers and mortgage originators (see cl 1.1.3.3 of the Franchise Agreement). Importantly, there was no reference in the Franchise Agreement to marketing the Easy Rent product although the description of the product in the IM suggested it was to be marketed to wholesale and retail equipment suppliers.
  3. Despite its central role, there was no evidence of what constituted the System, how it was devised and by whom or how it was to be used. Mr Leunig produced a document which he described as a “Franchise Business Manual” ( Leunig Ex A3 at [46]). However, the “manual” was a diverse collection of documents for use by insurance brokers in their dealings with IPF, equipment suppliers in promoting the Easy Rent product and promotional material for “Wealthpro”. It was not a manual that related to the conduct of the Franchised Business or the use of the System. It contained no information on how to operate the Franchise Business or how to “identify and recruit” clients. It simply comprised promotional material that was self-evidently prepared for other purposes.
  4. Mr Leunig had no reasonable explanation for why he sought to characterise the documents as being a manual for franchisees having regard to their contents: see Tp150 – 151 and Tp161 – 164. Further, Mr Leunig’s evidence on the “manual” reflected a profound lack of understanding of the difference between the businesses supposedly to be conducted by Franchisees and the businesses of the Financial Providers and the Products. His evidence on this matter was one of many instances of him mis-describing documents in a way that he thought would advance his case and that of the other applicants.
  5. The Management Plan for the first six months did not refer to the System (which would be expected to govern the contents of any management plan) nor could it be described as a proprietary system.
  6. The definition of the System referred to computer software. The software identified by Mr Leunig (Leunig Ex A3 at [27] and [28]) comprised the “Ironbark” system and the “Real Time and Decision Systems” programme. The only documents provided were in respect of the Ironbark system (“KBL 20”). That system was for reporting on all IPF business and was not directed to recruiting or identifying clients or to monitoring the Franchise Businesses.
  7. Similarly, the procedure manuals for IPF and Easy Rent produced by Mr Leunig (“KBL 18” and “KBL 19” to Leunig Ex A3) were not manuals for franchisees but for brokers and equipment suppliers dealing with IPF and Easy Rent.
  8. Further:
    • (a) there was no explanation of the System in the IM;
    • (b) there was no evidence that “the System” was ever used by any of the applicants or by RMA;
    • (c) none of the applicants (including Mr Leunig) could give any description of the System;
    • (d) none of the applicants (including Mr Leunig) had made any enquiries about the System or its use by RMA;
    • (e) Mr Fyson and Mr Player made no inquiries about any franchise manual or computer software;
    • (f) although RMA was to manage the Franchise Business of each Franchisee, the System was only referred to in the recitals to the Management Agreement; there was no obligation expressly imposed on RMA to use the System.
  9. The only available inference is that no “System” existed and that the references in the Franchise Agreement and the other agreements to the System were merely a means of giving the appearance of a franchise by adopting the language of franchising (as evidenced by the definition of a franchise contained in the Franchising Code – see the evidence of Mr Hillard at Ex A17).

Allocation of Territories

  1. There is no evidence as to the basis upon which the total number of Territories to be offered was determined; in particular, there is no evidence that 950 Territories represented a number that prior investigation had disclosed was viable or which could be properly managed by RMA.
  2. Indeed, the evidence concerning RMA and IPF Finance disclosed that as at 30 June 1997 they had no capacity to manage any of the Franchise Businesses and had made no preparations for implementing the Management Plans in each Territory as required by the Management Agreement.
  3. The allocation of Territories to the applicants indicates that no attempt was made to match the Franchisee’s location with the site of their Territory. According to Mr Leunig, the allocation of territories was made by Mr Wodovich (Tp210/46) on signing the Franchise Agreements under the franchisees’ powers of attorney and after the applications for franchise were signed: Tp100/26-33 and Tp143/23-41. The franchise territory numbers were processed clerically by “[allocating] a franchise number from the Franchise Codes file” when processing the application: see Ex Leunig A3 at [19], “KBL 11”/165 and Tp140/31-39.
  4. Although Mr Fyson and Mr Player gave evidence about wanting to be in a position to introduce clients to their franchises, there was no realistic prospect of that occurring as Vier’s territories were in Victoria and Mr Player’s territory was in New South Wales, and both of them reside in Western Australia.
  5. Mr Leunig claimed franchisees could market anywhere notwithstanding the allocated territories: Tp133/31-35. He stated “there was a contingency within the business management to allow other referral fees to pass across to franchise”: Tp134/13-14, and “if you receive income from an introduction from somewhere else, then its shared”: Tp134/26-27. He also stated that it was not uncommon “for franchisees to meet at the annual franchise gathering and they could cross-pollinate”: Tp134/46-48.
  6. However, those “cross-pollination” opportunities were once a year. Further, there was no agreement setting out the basis for sharing commissions between franchises: Tp135/6-10. There was no document recording what franchises could expect to earn by making a referral or what records would be kept of the referrals: Tp137/6-29. Finally, the notion that franchisees could market where they wished was inconsistent with the franchise arrangements and the concept of a franchise system; in particular, with the notion that franchisees would pay a royalty for the use of the System in their Territory. In the Tribunal’s view Mr Leunig’s evidence either overstates the true position or is an indication that the franchise arrangements were seen as merely being a means of raising funds by offering a tax benefit.
  7. The method of allocation of Territories also meant that there was little or no prospect of the applicants (or other Franchisees) supervising in any meaningful way what actually occurred in their Franchise Business; in particular, monitoring the implementation of the Management Plan, the provision of the Franchisor’s Services, the adoption of the Franchisor’s Procedures Plan and ensuring that there was a proper accounting by the Financial Providers for income payable to Franchisees.
  8. Mr Acheson commented on this aspect in his report (Ex R6) Mr Leunig sought to challenge Mr Acheson’s observation at [7] of his witness statement dated 6 March 2007 (Ex A4) by referring to the use of the internet for marketing. Mr Leunig’s statement:
    • (a) was argument not evidence and is accorded no weight by the Tribunal;
    • (b) is also rejected as being irrelevant as there is no evidence that in 1997 business was routinely conducted by the internet – as evidenced by the fact that there was no reference to internet marketing of any of the Products in any of the contemporaneous documents for the IPF 97 Project (the fact that Mr Leunig gave such evidence indicated his willingness to make any statement he thought might advance his case and that of the other applicants. Further reference to this appears below).
  9. However, Mr Leunig’s statement at [7] of Ex A4 does contain one revealing disclosure namely the reference to his territories comprising post boxes and “therefore it would be difficult for me or any other Franchisee, to visit the Territory”.
  10. The purpose of the Franchise Business was purportedly to market the Products to persons such as insurance brokers, mortgage brokers and equipment dealers who could, in turn, sell the Products to their clients and customers. The allocation of territories by post office box numbers was clearly not a business like way of establishing a franchise network for such a business – obviously, there could be significant differences between the number of insurance brokers, mortgage brokers and equipment dealers in each Territory (including the real possibility that there would be no person operating any of those businesses in a particular Territory).
  11. It is to be inferred from Mr Leunig’s scant evidence about the allocation of territories that no investigation was made as to whether there were insurance brokers, mortgage brokers and equipment dealers for each of the Territories offered and if so, how many.
  12. There is a critical difference between the businesses of the Financial Providers and Franchise Businesses that must also be kept in mind. It is obvious that the allocation of Territories by postal delivery points makes no commercial sense having regard to the business supposedly conducted by the Franchisees.
  13. According to Mr Leunig, the promoters contemplated the franchises as a source of referral business from the franchisees: Leunig Ex A3 at [18] and Tp132/35 to Tp133/4. However, there was no process by which franchisees were assessed to ascertain whether they had a network for referrals and there was no process to match the allocation of a franchise territory with the residential location of Franchisees: Tp135/40-46 and Tp136/15-22. The Franchisees were also anticipated to be a source of working capital for the operations of the Financial Providers: Leunig Ex A3 at [31]. In substance they were treated as passive investors and the relationship was managed by the directors and the general manager of IPF Finance.

The Management Plan

  1. The first Management Plan required RMA to undertake in each Territory a letter drop to at least 2,000 residents; two community promotions; place a newspaper advertisement and contact all insurance brokers, accountancy firms, law firms, computer stores and outlets and real estate agencies located in the Territory. To undertake those activities, RMA committed to provide sufficient staff to complete 50 hours of direct canvassing and 200 hours of national promotion in each Territory. A total of 950 franchises were offered so that RMA was supposedly to have the capacity to conduct 1,900,000 letter drops, 1900 community promotions and 47,500 man hours of canvassing (without considering the commitments in respect of the national promotion). It would have taken approximately 46 persons working 26 x 40 hour weeks to complete only the canvassing commitment.
  2. The ATO audit report (T806/V5/1198-9) discloses that as at 1 July 1997 IPF Finance/RMA may have had the following staff available – Mr Jones (the managing director of the Chambers Group), Mr Gilbert and Mr Baker (a consultant). One person (a “secretary/loan processor”) was engaged in 1997. As at September 1999, three employees were involved in business development and sales in WA; four were involved in NSW and Victoria; there was a State manager for Queensland and there was a representative for SA, ACT, Tasmania and NT. A further 17 persons were said to have been previously employed by IPF Finance/RMA (of which ten were office staff).
  3. In Leunig Ex A2, Mr Leunig asserts that RMA had the capacity as at 30 June 1997 to “service potential clients” (at [39]). That statement is merely a conclusion not supported by the evidence and is rejected by the Tribunal. In any event:
    • (a) The Management Plan was concerned with advertising and marketing programmes and not about servicing clients.
    • (b) The evidence of marketing given by Mr Leunig demonstrated that RMA did not fulfil, nor did it make any real attempt to fulfil, its obligations under the Management Plan. There was no evidence of marketing in each Territory, including the Territories of the applicants – distributing 30,000 flyers in late 1997/early 1998 and some brochures and placing some advertisements in trade journals and newspapers (Leunig Ex A3 at [43] to [45]; “KBL 28”, “KBL29” and “KBL 31”) may have promoted the Products but did not in any way accord with the requirements of the Management Plan in each Franchise Agreement. The same observations apply to activities referred to by Mr Leunig at Leunig Ex A3 [48] to [62]. Those activities were clearly sporadic, appear to have been primarily undertaken in 1998 and were not specific to Territories.
  4. The Tribunal finds that RMA never had the capacity to discharge its obligations under the Management Plan for each Franchise Agreement. It is also clear that RMA made no attempt to fulfil its obligations under the plan. That was not disputed by Mr Leunig. His explanation was that the task proved too great. However, the application of funds raised by IPF 97 Project indicates that RMA never had the money available that would have been required to discharge its obligations under each Management Plan.
  5. The evidence disclosed that RMA took no steps prior to 30 June 1997 to enable it to discharge its obligations under the Management Plan in each Franchise Agreement. The activities undertaken by RMA after 30 June 1997 primarily involved appointing State managers who did not market the Products separately in each Territory but rather, pursued a very limited number of brokers and equipment suppliers with whom they thought they could develop a relationship.
  6. The failure of RMA to give effect to the Management Plan is confirmed by the second plan that was produced in mid – 1998 (Leunig Ex A3 at [47], “KBL 33”). Although Mr Leunig stated that the plan was produced in accordance with the Management Agreement, plainly that was not correct. Apart from the timing of the plan (more than six months after 30 June 1997), there was no reference to marketing the Products to brokers and others Territory by Territory.
  7. Further, the general statements made by Mr Leunig at [51] of Leunig Ex A3 concerning discussions to ensure that RMA complied with its obligations under the Management Plan are not credible having regard to the evidence that no attempt was made to comply with the Management Plan for each Franchise. Moreover, Mr Leunig was not shown as having attended any meetings for which he annexed minutes; there was no evidence that meetings continued after November 1997 and any suggestion that RMA held regular meetings to discuss what was required to fulfil the Management Plan for each Franchise Business is inconsistent with all the evidence regarding the marketing of the Products.
  8. On the evidence none of the applicants made any inquiries prior to entering into the IPF 97 Project about how marketing activities would be undertaken in respect of their Franchises.
  9. Each applicant knew from the IM that 950 franchises had been offered as part of the IPF 97 Project. It was, therefore, obvious from the Franchise Agreement that RMA was obliged to undertake an extraordinary level of marketing activity involving a considerable expenditure of money, yet none of the applicants made any inquiry about the capacity of RMA to fulfil its obligations. Further, they made no enquiries as to whether the stipulated marketing activities had been undertaken nor did they seek any explanation as to why RMA did not adhere to the terms of the Management Agreement in relation to the preparation of further marketing plans after the first six months. That was so, notwithstanding that the income received by franchisees was significantly below the amounts projected in the IM in the second and third years and RMA never provided any report on its marketing activities to the relevant franchisee in each Territory.
  10. The System and the Management Plan constituted the core of the Franchise Businesses according to the relevant agreements. The Tribunal finds that the applicants’ lack of any knowledge of the System and disinterest in the Management Plan is entirely inconsistent with any assertion that they were conducting a business.

THE ORAL EVIDENCE OF THE APPLICANTS

The oral evidence of Mr Fyson

  1. In his oral evidence at Tp11/28-45 and Tp12/4 to Tp13/15, Mr Fyson stated that as a real estate agent he had experience in selling and purchasing businesses. He had also purchased (and sold) a restaurant business as an investment. He did not mention and apparently did not consider the acquisition of three IPF franchises as the purchase of a business.
  2. He referred to an initial meeting with Mr Clements at which he was given the IM. He reconstructed subsequent discussions with Mr Clements which were not disclosed in his affidavit: Tp14/4 to Tp16/3. He was not interviewed by anyone as to his suitability to be a franchisee: Tp61/1-2.
  3. When asked how he assessed the proposal, Mr Fyson confined himself to:

Mr Acheson observed that in reality the Franchisor is dealing with its related entity the Manager. The Manager was not set up to manage the franchisees as individual businesses. The financial products were marketed as if they were one business not some 900 separate businesses. SECTION 51(1)

Tribunal’s Findings

  1. The Tribunal finds that the claimed deductions claimed by the applicants are not allowable under section 51(1) of the ITAA 1936 for the reasons which follow.
  2. In deciding whether the claimed deductions were allowable, the Tribunal has had regard to what the outgoings were really for. The relevant outgoings were styled Annual Royalty and Management Fees and purportedly paid for the use of the System for the Term in the allotted franchise Territory. However, the question is answered by an analysis of all the rights and obligations of the applicants and not by the way in which the outgoings were styled: Vincent v FCT [2002] FCAFC 291; (2002) 124 FCR 350 at [64], [65] and [67]. From the analysis set out above the Tribunal has concluded that the claimed deductions were capital outgoings.
  3. Further, the Tribunal finds that the applicants were passive investors sharing in the profits of such business activities as the Financial Providers conducted. No distinct business of the applicants was ever established. This is clear from:
    • (a) An examination of the Licence Agreements, the Franchise Agreement and the Management Agreement.
    • (b) The evidence of the applicants. Each clearly treated the claimed outgoings as outgoings for an investment, as did their expert witnesses. Further, the applicants were unable to describe their business, the Products, the System, the activities to be undertaken by franchisees, how income was to be generated or the role of the manager.
    • (c) The conduct of the Franchisor, Manager and Financial Providers as a whole. They conducted a single business without regard to any franchise network, treating the Franchisees as merely passive investors. Evidently, that conduct was consistent with the understanding and intentions of the applicants.
  4. In the absence of any System and business formed by the applicants, their outgoings are capital outgoings: Clowes v FCT [1954] HCA 10; (1954) 91 CLR 209 at 218; Milne v FCT [1976] HCA 2; (1976) 133 CLR 526 at 535; and Enviro Systems Renewable Resources Pty Ltd v ASIC [2001] SASC 11; (2001) 80 SASR 1 at [36], [43], [44].
  5. Even if, contrary to the view of the Tribunal, activities to be carried out by or for an individual franchisee could be discerned, the necessary indicia of a business as outlined in Ferguson v FCT [1979] FCA 29; (1979) 26 ALR 307 at 311 and Hope v The Council of the City of Bathurst [1980] HCA 16; (1980) 144 CLR 1 are not found here (and see the discussion of Hill and Carr, JJ. in Puzey v Commissioner of Taxation [2003] FCAFC 197; (2003) 131 FCR 244 at [46]- [49] and of Hill, J. (with whom Hely, J. agreed) in Commissioner of Taxation v Sleight [2004] FCAFC 94; (2004) 136 FCR 211 at [47]- [50]):
    • (a) Beyond their initial entry into the agreements, the applicants engaged in no activity despite receiving vague and general financial statements and other reports which called for their intervention.
    • (b) The arrangements presented to and entered into by the applicants lacked the System by which they were to conduct their businesses and derive their Franchisee Entitlements. The Management Plan clearly was not and never could be implemented. The arrangements lacked any business-like structure with a profit-making purpose.
  6. The effect of the evidence as analysed above is that the applicants are not assisted by cases to which they referred which suggest that the appointment of a manager is not inconsistent with the conduct of business:
    • (a) the question of whether the applicants were in a business at all must first be answered;
    • (b) ultimately, the nature of these purported businesses precludes the applicants’ reliance on Puzey v Commissioner of Taxation [2003] FCAFC 197; (2003) 131 FCR 244 at [46]- [49] and Commissioner of Taxation v Sleight [2004] FCAFC 94; (2004) 136 FCR 211 which, in turn, relied upon principles expressed in Commissioner of Taxation v Lau [1984] FCA 401; (1984) 6 FCR 202 and Commissioner of Taxation v Emmakell [1989] FCA 34; (1988) 22 FCR 157; and
    • (c) as well, the different legislative contexts in which questions of management were considered in Madison Pacific Property Management & Ors v Australian Securities Commission [1999] FCA 62; (1999) 30 ACSR 218 and the Disktravel litigation, Australian Trade Commission v Disktravel [1999] FCA 48 and Travel Vision International Pty Ltd and Ors v Australian Trade Commission [1998] AATA 11 preclude reliance on those decisions to which further reference is made below in more detail.
  7. In view of the findings by the Tribunal that:
    • (a) the applicants were not in a business (as to which see further below); and
    • (b) the claimed deductions were outgoings of capital;

it is not strictly speaking necessary to consider whether the claimed deductions were incurred for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income. However, in case the Tribunal’s finding as to the capital nature of the outgoings (as to which also see below) is incorrect the Tribunal has considered this issue.

  1. Having regard to the evidence and in light of the principles set out in Fletcher v Commissioner of Taxation [1991] HCA 42; (1991) 173 CLR 1 AT 17.1-18.2 and 18.7-19.4 and Ure v Commissioner of Taxation [1981] FCA 9; (1981) 34 ALR 237 per Brennan, J. at 241, lines 20-31 and Deane and Sheppard, JJ. at 248-249, line 25 and 249 line 35 – 250, the Tribunal finds that the claimed deductions were incurred by the applicants, not for the purpose of gaining or producing assessable income, but for the purpose of obtaining the tax deductions which facilitated their investment. The Tribunal is of the view that the obtaining of the tax deductions provides a more satisfactory explanation for the outgoings in circumstances where the applicants entered into arrangements which made no business sense and were clearly indifferent to the nature or conduct of business activities it was said would be carried out on their behalf.
  2. Mr Acheson concluded in his report: “the IPF Scheme does not present itself or indeed appear to be a thought through, properly prepared normal franchise or commercially viable venture”[10] at page 29. In the Summary to his Report he stated: “I believe that the IPF Finance franchises were very different in nature from what is normal in franchising and that they were more in the nature of a complex financial engineering exercise. ... This was not a franchise in reality: ... the franchisee/franchisor relationship was not a business one but a highly leveraged investment with other benefits. I believe the whole complicated structure was unnecessary for the purported purpose of running a franchise and was a device designed to yield other benefits”. (See also Mr Dawson’s view, Ex R8 at [18](b) and (c) at [33].) Those benefits were the clearly the tax benefits.
  3. The evidence amply substantiated the views expressed by Mr Acheson and the Tribunal’s findings agree with his conclusions as set out above.

Capital outgoing

  1. The overall result of the arrangements was that by the prepaid first year so called Annual Royalty Fee and Management Fee, the franchises paid for the carrying out of activities for the development of the business of the Financial Providers: Acheson Report, [1] pp 26-28; Leunig A3 at [31]
  2. At best, the Franchise Agreement contemplated that the franchisee introduce clients to the Financial Providers by the use of the System. In return, the Financial Providers were to pay the franchisees a share of the net profit of the Financial Providers. The franchisee was, in effect, to be nothing more than an investor and a referrer of business. Apart from the cash funds introduced, the franchisees provided nothing more than referrals. The Tribunal is of the opinion that the applicants cannot realistically have contemplated the payment of the Annual Royalties and Management Fees as being for the right to introduce or refer Clients to the Financial Providers: and see Leunig Ex A3 at [18]. The payments were in the Tribunal’s view of a capital nature.

The assertion that the businesses were “serious businesses”



  1. In their opening address to the Tribunal, the applicants claimed that the businesses of the Financial Providers were “real” and/or “serious” businesses. The businesses were said to be operating in sectors of the finance market that had become significant since the mid-1990s. The cross-examination of Mr Acheson demonstrated that this contention remained an important part of the applicants’ cases.
  2. Whether the businesses of the Financial Providers were real or serious businesses is not really to the point once the respondent’s allegation of sham was dropped. The relevance of the assertion is limited to a tenuous connection with the dominant purpose of the Scheme. That is, the question of whether the businesses of the Financial Providers were real or serious businesses has some bearing on the question of whether the Scheme had a dominant commercial purpose. However, the relevance is limited as the Scheme concerns the businesses of the Franchisees and not those conducted by the Financial Providers. The absence of any Product to be marketed would of course be compelling evidence of a dominant tax benefit purpose. However, the fact that there were Products (at least in the case of IPF and Easy Rent) does not necessarily lead to a conclusion that the dominant purpose of the Scheme was a commercial and not a tax benefit purpose.
  3. In the Tribunal’s view, the evidence concerning the Financial Providers and the conduct of their businesses supports the following findings:
    • (a) As at June 1997, the IPF product had been marketed to clients of the Chambers Group in Western Australia but not elsewhere; no real attempt had been made to market the Easy Rent product and no attempt had been made to develop any product for Mortgages Direct.
    • (b) Attempts to market the Products after 30 June 1997 were only sporadic and did not accord with the marketing proposals contained in the Management Plan.
    • (c) There was no evidence of any business activity by Mortgages Direct apart from very limited attempts to market the “Wealth Pro” product – that product was not the subject of any licence agreement between IPF Finance and Mortgages Direct and was not, therefore, part of the IPF 97 Project.
    • (d) The manner in which the businesses of the Financial Providers were conducted and in particular, the way in which the Products were marketed after 30 June 1997 demonstrated that the franchise network purportedly created by the IPF 97 Project was in fact irrelevant – that is, that there was no commercial purpose for the franchise network.
  4. The embryonic nature of the businesses when the Project was marketed and what occurred after 30 June 1997 is also, of course, relevant to the reasonableness of the projections contained in the IM. The Tribunal finds that the projections were not reasonable as:
    • (a) there was no evidence of any investigation or research ever having been undertaken to substantiate the projections prior to their inclusion in the IM – indeed, as mentioned earlier, the only evidence of a prior attempt to forecast income for the franchisees projected significantly less income than the IM forecasts (and no reason was given for the increase in the IM projections);
      • (e) there was no evidence as to the assumptions upon which the IM projections had been made so that the experts called by the applicants had to make their own assumptions based on their industry experience;
      • (f) the evidence of the experts called by the applicants showed that the projections in the IM were unrealistic (especially, in the absence of any plan to, for example, secure a major international broker for the IPF product or to capture by year 2 a very substantial proportion of the equipment leasing market for the Easy Rent product).

The franchise arrangements were not commercial

  1. The Tribunal is of the view that the circumstances of these applications are entirely different to the circumstances considered by French J in Lenzo v Commissioner of Taxation [2007] FCA 1402 (7 September 2007). In these applications, as already stated the Tribunal finds that there was no commercial purpose for the franchising arrangements constituted by the IPF 97 Project. The Tribunal’s finding as set out above is that the sole or dominant purpose of the Project was to create a structure which would generate tax benefits to assist the Chambers Group in raising funds. That emerges clearly from a consideration of the s 177D factors as set out below. The lack of any commercial purpose is highlighted by the evidence of Mr Acheson on the many unusual features of the so-called franchise arrangements – there was no franchise in reality. This is also readily apparent from the very inception of the applicants’ participation in the project: they became bound to the franchise arrangements by signing an application form and power of attorney and without having sighted any document that recorded the agreements upon which they were to conduct their franchise businesses.
  2. The difference between the circumstances in Lenzo and in these applications becomes stark when the evidence, or more accurately, the absence of any evidence about the System is compared to the evidence about the Sandalwood plantation in which the taxpayer in Lenzo was directly investing.
  3. The only explanation proffered for the so-called franchise network was Mr Leunig’s notion that it provided the “arms and legs” for marketing the Products. He suggested that the franchise network represented, in effect, a collection of franchisees who had a direct interest in pursuing customers for the Products and in generating “word of mouth” referrals. That suggestion is rejected by the Tribunal having regard to:
    • (a) The geographic divide between the Franchisees’ Territories and their residential locations. No explanation for this geographic divide consistent with the proper development of a genuine franchise network was provided by any of the applicants or their witnesses.
    • (b) The use of a manager to undertake the marketing activities that were apparently to be performed by Franchisees. The supposed advantages of many “arms and legs” were immediately negated by the use of a single manager.
    • (c) The fact that the Franchise Businesses did not consist of marketing to the end consumers. The system was about identifying and recruiting clients through marketing and contact with registered insurance brokers, finance and mortgage originators.
    • (d) The manner in which RMA purportedly managed the Franchise Businesses. As Mr Acheson rightly observed at p 11 of Ex R6, “It appears that the Manager was not set up to manage the individual Franchise Territories or even the individual multiple franchisees as individual businesses or give individual comments or advice”. The Chambers Group/IPF Group clearly continued to conduct its business after 30 June 1997 without any substantive regard to the establishment of the purported franchise network. The evidence shows overwhelmingly that the so-called Franchisees were treated merely as passive investors in the businesses of the Financial Providers.

No business

  1. The evidence shows clearly that there was no franchise business established; almost total control of the business remained with IPF Finance and the Financial Providers under the terms of the IPF 97 Project agreements.
  2. Furthermore, no discernible business was defined by the Franchise Agreement. The business activity to be undertaken by franchisees was said to be to introduce Clients to the Financial Providers by use of the System. The business was not defined adequately to permit a franchisee to know the System, how to use the System to introduce Clients and the Products to which clients were being introduced.
  3. Even if some business activity was capable of being identified, it was the business activity of IPF Finance and/or RMA by which the franchisee’s income was derived (and, in the event, derived irrespective of any business activities carried out by or for the franchisee).
  4. The Franchise Agreement provided that franchisees were to receive “Franchise Entitlements” generated by and to be paid to IPF Finance by the Financial Providers from their net profit from business written with Clients introduced to them. The “Franchise Entitlements” were to be reduced by amounts owing to IPF Finance, RMA and CCF.
  5. The evidence is clear that activities undertaken by the Financial Providers and not the franchisee were to generate revenue for the Financial Provider from which the franchisee was to receive a share. Accordingly, the franchisee’s income was to be derived, indirectly, from the Financial Providers’ business activities which were the ultimate, and only, source of revenue. All aspects of the business were conducted by those entities. It is abundantly clear that the franchisee had no business of its own distinct from that of the Financial Providers.
  6. The overall conclusion to be drawn from the evidence as a whole is not that the applicants were undertaking a business of their own but that they were passive investors of capital in a project from which they were hoping for a commercial return.
  7. No discernible business, its product, the activities to be undertaken by franchisees or how income was to be generated emerged from the applicants’ evidence.
  8. In their oral evidence, the applicants demonstrated a profound lack of understanding of what they had invested in. In particular, they did not understand what service was to be provided or that income was to be derived from the sale of financial products by third parties. They did not understand the System by which they were required to introduce Clients to the Financial Providers and derive their Franchisee Entitlements.
  9. It was plain from their evidence that the applicants themselves considered that they were investing in the businesses operated by the Financial Providers and the Products. They did not consider that they were operating their own business in any meaningful way and they conducted themselves accordingly. The business of IPF Finance was conducted as a whole (and not in a Territory or Franchise Business basis) without complaint from the applicants. Evidently, that was consistent with their understandings and intentions.
  10. As noted above, the only feature which purportedly distinguished the business of one franchisee from another was an allocation of Clients enlisted by suburb and franchise area or “territory”. There was no evidence that any of the applicants were ever allocated any clients. Annual franchise financial statements provided to the applicants contained no explanation of income and franchisor expenses figures given, how they were derived, from what activities or from whom.
  11. Mr Fyson (through his company) and Mr Leunig both had multiple franchises. In each case, the income shown was the same. It must have been obvious to them that they had been allocated a proportion of total income without reference to “amounts received from the Financial Providers in consideration for introducing Clients to the Financial Providers ... in accordance with the System”: see Franchise Agreement clauses 4 and 1.1.15 – Franchisee’s Entitlement. However, they made no inquiry.
  12. Newsletters provided to franchisees were in general terms. They were not even confined to material in respect of “financial services franchisees” but covered IPF’s whole range of business activities. Some contained no information about IPF’s financial services at all. Consistent with the applicants’ own evidence, the newsletters were expressed in terms of an investment.

The necessary indicia of a business are absent

  1. According to the authorities, the indicia of a business are:
    • (a) the activities undertaken by the taxpayer;
    • (b) the repetition and regularity of those activities;
    • (c) whether the activities are organized in a business-like manner;
    • (d) the structure through which those activities are conducted;
    • (e) whether the activities are carried out for a profit-making purpose –

Ferguson v FCT [1979] FCA 29; (1979) 26 ALR 307 at 311; Hope v The Council of the City of Bathurst [1980] HCA 16; (1980) 144 CLR 1 (and see the discussion of Hill and Carr, JJ. in Puzey v Commissioner of Taxation [2003] FCAFC 197; (2003) 131 FCR 244 at [46]- [49] and of Hill, J. (with whom Hely, J. agreed) in Commissioner of Taxation v Sleight [2004] FCAFC 94; (2004) 136 FCR 211 at [47]- [50].

  1. The applicants’ purported business activities are set out above. The applicants undertook no additional activities with regard to their “businesses”. Further activity would have been apparent if, in fact, the applicants were each conducting a business. The further activity would have:
    • (a) been directed to ensuring compliance with the rights and obligations contained in the Franchise Agreement and the Management Agreement;
    • (b) arisen out of the paucity of information provided to the applicants, none of which indicated that a business was being carried out on any individual applicant’s behalf.
  2. None of the applicants took any steps to inform themselves of, or to ensure that, the obligations they had undertaken to IPF Finance were met by RMA (in accordance with its adoption of those obligations by cl 5.1 of the Management Agreement). In this respect, particular regard ought to have been had to the franchisee’s covenants in cl 14 of the Franchise Agreement as to the keeping of accounts for the Franchise Business.
  3. Neither did the applicants seek to inform themselves of or to ensure the franchisor’s compliance with its obligations: -
    • (a) “to provide the System in a proper and professional manner during the Term” (Franchise Agreement, cl 15.1);
    • (b) to produce the Manual and provide a copy of it to the Franchisee and keep it up to date (cl 9 of the Franchise Agreement). A breach of this clause was expressly stated in clause 9.7 to be a fundamental breach of the Franchise Agreement.
  4. By cl 9 of the Management Agreement, RMA was required to separately identify its activity in respect of the Franchised Business “including without limitation providing a record of all Clients introduced to the Financial Providers by the Franchisee”.
  5. As noted earlier the franchise financial statements provided to the applicants contained no explanation of income and expense figures given or how they were derived, let alone whether or how income was derived from utilisation of the System as a result of any and what activities.
  6. Also as already noted the applicants sought no information by which they could assess whether or how their purported businesses were being conducted, such as schedules of Clients introduced, reports of the volume of transactions conducted in different periods or proper financial statements.
  7. Having regard to the indicia of a business, the arrangements presented to and entered into by the applicants lacked a business-like structure. Indeed, they were, in the Tribunal’s opinion in essence, a commercial nonsense:
    • (a) Prospective participants were to enter into a franchise arrangement that lacked the System that was the subject matter of the Franchise Agreement and which they were required to use in meeting their obligations under that Agreement. (Franchise Agreement, cll 20, 5.1.4).
    • (b) Their appointed manager, RMA was an associate of the Franchisor and the Financial Providers.
    • (c) RMA was purportedly to undertake all the work of each franchisee for payment in the second and following years in the expectation of payment of its fees from the Franchisees Entitlements.
    • (d) RMA was lender of its own fees. The applicants borrowed most of the First Year Management Fee from CCF by an advance which was effected by round robin of cheques and book entries and so, manifestly, without cash. In the result, the applicants agreed to pay fees which had no commercial function.
  8. The IM presented no business-like structure for the prospective franchisees. It defined no discernible business or the System and raised more questions than it answered:
    • (a) Did the Financial Providers have financial services to sell?
    • (b) What was the System?
    • (c) What was the experience of IPF Finance, RMA and the Financial Providers with respect to the marketing or provision of financial services?
    • (d) How did the utilization of the System assist in the provision of financial services?
    • (e) What activities were to be undertaken by the Franchisee?
    • (f) How would those activities generate income?
  9. The latter question was not answered by the projected returns. They give no indication of how the gross profit figures used were derived; from what activities they were derived; how much business they represented; from what products or services that gross profit was derived; or the amount of revenue expected to be generated from each sale.
  10. The arrangements for Territories lacked a business-like allocation and application. There was no system for determining when a given franchisee might be said to have introduced a client. The allocation of Territories interstate from the applicants’ home state restricted the financial return of franchisees and, critically, their ability to meaningfully participate in or control their franchises.
  11. There was no place for a franchise structure in the businesses of the Financial Providers. There was no activity that could be sensibly franchised:
    • (a) The purported Franchisee could not provide the financial services. He was not authorised to do so.
    • (b) The Franchisee could not receive the request from a Client for a particular service. That would go to the Financial Providers.
    • (c) Once a Client request was received it had to be dealt with by an authorised agent who would accept the referral and be willing to share the commission.
  12. The marketing activity proposed for Franchisees could not sensibly be isolated from the activities of the Financial Providers.
  13. In terms of the indicia of a business, the applicants did not engage in activities with the required profit-making motive. As outlined above:
    • (a) They did not intervene in their purported businesses to ensure reporting and other obligations were complied with.
    • (b) They did not intervene in their purported businesses although information provided to them indicated that business was not being conducted on their behalf and, indeed, suggested that business was not being carried on at all.
    • (c) At the outset:
      • (i) they agreed to pay large fees, without question, for undefined services;
      • (ii) they did not consider the true nature of the purported arrangements which were, manifestly, a commercial nonsense;
      • (iii) they made no inquiry about the true nature of their purported business or how it was to be conducted, despite its lack of definition and obvious practical difficulties;
      • (iv) they made no inquiry about the capacity of IPF Finance, RMA or the Financial Providers to deliver the services and products, at best, vaguely outlined in the IM, or their respective capacities to carry out their obligations to the franchisee.

The applicants are not assisted by cases, decided in quite different circumstances, which have held that the appointment of a manager is consistent with a finding of business

  1. As noted earlier the Tribunal is of the opinion that comparison with cases that suggest that the appointment of a manager was not inconsistent with the conduct of business are of no assistance to the applicants.
  2. If the applicants were not in a business at all, there was no requirement to consider the role of the Manager. Accordingly, the question of whether the applicants were in a business at all must first be answered.
  3. The Tribunal, as already indicated, finds that, the nature of these so called businesses precludes the applicants’ reliance on Puzey v Commissioner of Taxation [2003] FCAFC 197; (2003) 131 FCR 244 at [46]- [49] and Commissioner of Taxation v Sleight [2004] FCAFC 94; (2004) 136 FCR 211 which, in turn, relied upon principles expressed in Commissioner of Taxation v Lau [1984] FCA 401; (1984) 6 FCR 202 and Commissioner of Taxation v Emmakell [1989] FCA 34; (1988) 22 FCR 157. Conceptually, the businesses found in Puzey and Sleight are quite different from any business which might be found here. As Hill and Carr JJ pointed out in Puzey v Commissioner of Taxation [2003] FCAFC 197; (2003) 131 FCR 244 at [50]: “It is not possible to argue from the decision in a particular case that a person is carrying on business unless there is a complete identity of facts and that is seldom the case.” And see Commissioner of Taxation v Sleight [2004] FCAFC 94; (2004) 136 FCR 211 at [51], per Hill J (with whom Hely J agreed).
  4. In those cases, the Court, relying on the reasoning in Commissioner of Taxation v Lau [1984] FCA 401; (1984) 6 FCR 202, found a business of the investor growing and harvesting tree products. That business was a distinct business of the investor because the investor was granted a specific area of land and so an identifiable interest in specific trees. See Commissioner of Taxation v Sleight [2004] FCAFC 94; (2004) 136 FCR 211 per Hill J (with whom Hely J agreed) at [51]-[61] and per Carr J at [164]-[188]; Puzey v Commissioner of Taxation 2003 ATC 4782, per Hill and Carr JJ [2003] FCAFC 197; (2003) 131 FCR 244 at [51]- [54]; and see Lee J at first instance, Puzey v Commissioner of Taxation (2002) 194 ALR 615 at [51]-[54].
  5. Further, the forestry investor in those cases could conveniently appoint and rely on the services of a common manager, co-ordinated with the provision of services to other investors: Commissioner of Taxation v Sleight [2004] FCAFC 94; (2004) 136 FCR 211 per Carr J at [184] and, at first instance Sleight v Commissioner of Taxation 2003 ATC 4801, Nicholson J at [29]. That is because the forestry business consists of producing product from growing, maintaining and harvesting a particular number of trees (that is, the number of trees which can be grown on the investors’ identifiable plot). The needs of the individual investor’s business, and so the manager’s activities, are limited by those activities required for the production of the product from those trees. Moreover, the needs of the individual investor’s business are the same as the needs of other investors. They are the needs of properly maintaining the trees. Accordingly, the manager carries out the business of each individual investor when managing the plantation as a whole. For example, when harvesting the whole plantation, the manager, necessarily and identifiably, also harvests the individual investor’s trees.
  6. Here, by contrast, an IPF franchisee such as the applicants had no identifiable business of its own. There was no System, no discernible means of marketing to Clients and introducing Clients to the Financial Providers who were the only entities purportedly conducting any business.
  7. Moreover, where all franchisees were engaged in the purported use of the System through a common manager, only evidence of activities directed to the purported identifiable territory of the particular franchise could establish the conduct of that franchisee’s business and distinguish the individual franchisee’s business from all other franchises using the System to introduce Clients to the Financial Providers. There is no such evidence. The evidence that the manager, common to all franchisees, was engaged in marketing for the Financial Providers does not show the conduct of any individual franchisee’s business, and there was clearly no such business.
  8. Unlike the forestry investor’s business, the nature and requirements of the IPF franchisee’s business are not satisfied by the co-ordinated activities of a common manager. The forestry investor’s business is to produce product. The IPF franchisee’s business was supposed to be to introduce Clients to the Financial Providers. The income and success of the IPF’s franchisee’s purported business depended on the extent of the efforts of Financial Providers made to convert the Clients into customers for their financial services.
  9. Neither does the decision in Madison Pacific Property Management & Ors v Australian Securities Commission [1999] FCA 62; (1999) 30 ACSR 218 assist the applicants’ contention that they are carrying on a business in their own right. That decision dealt with different legislation, Chapter 7 of the Corporations Law dealing with “prescribed interests” and the exemption from the requirements of that Chapter for franchises. More particularly, it dealt with whether the components of the definition of franchise were met. The aspect of the definition that caused concern was whether the proposal for management by an approved manager was inconsistent with the franchisor/franchisee relationship contemplated in the definition. That is, whether the franchisee would be substantially dependent on services supplied by the franchisor. The Court held that the analysis of the legal rights and duties of the manager and the franchisee created by the management proposal were not inconsistent with the scheme of the documents which otherwise satisfied the definition of “franchise”: see French J at 231-233 and Carr J at 250-251.
  10. Here, the answer to the question whether or not each applicant was carrying on a business, depends on the evidence and an exhaustive survey of the facts and circumstances relating to each applicant. It cannot be arrived at by comparing the approach to, and the decision on, structures under consideration in other cases, (particularly where the authority relates to points of narrow compass in the application of distinctly different legislation). To approach the question in this way is, in the view of the Tribunal, to misconceive the task of determining whether each of the applicants carried on a business as a franchisee. For the same reasons, the Tribunal does not find the decision in the Disktravel litigation: Australian Trade Commission v Disktravel [1999] FCA 48 and Travel Vision International Pty Ltd and Ors v Australian Trade Commission [1998] AATA 11 to be relevant to this matter.

Fletcher’s case – first limb of section 51(1)

  1. The determination of whether outgoings are, generally, deductible for the purposes of section 51(1) is a matter of characterization by reference to the advantage the outgoings seek to achieve. The outgoing must be incidental and relevant to gaining or producing assessable income. Put another way, the production of assessable income is the occasion of the outgoing: Amalgamated Zinc (de Bavay’s) Ltd v FCT (1935) CLR 295 at 309 and Ronpibon Tin NL v FCT [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 CLR 47.
  2. The relationship between the outgoing and the assessable income must be such as to impart to the outgoing a character of an outgoing of the relevant kind. Where there is a disproportion between the outgoings incurred and the amount of assessable income, the question of the character of the outgoings must be answered by:

“a common sense appreciation of the overall factual context in which the outgoings were incurred. It necessarily involves a consideration of the contents and implications of the overall contractual arrangements ... pursuant to which the outgoings ... became payable. ... it also encompasses a consideration of the purpose which the members of the partnership ... had in incurring the outgoings.”

Fletcher v FCT (1991) [1991] HCA 42; 173 CLR 1 at 20-21

  1. This ”commonsense” or “practical” weighing of all the factors ought to indicate that the relationship between the whole of the expenditure and the production of assessable income is "genuine and not colourable" for amounts to be deductible. The occasion of the loss or outgoing is not the earning of assessable income where the circumstances point to another purpose: see Fletcher at 17.1-18.2, 18.7-19.4 and Ure v FCT [1981] FCA 9; (1981) 34 ALR 237 per Brennan J at 241, lines 20-31 and per Deane and Sheppard JJ at 248-249, line 25 and 249, line 35 -250.
  2. As already noted the Tribunal finds that the occasion of the applicants’ outgoings was not the production of assessable income but the income tax deductions which facilitated their participation, as follows:
    • (a) In Mr Player’s case, he saved tax of $12,839.39 and after making the cash payments required of him, had a small net cash deficit of $160.61.
    • (b) In the case of Mr Fyson’s company Lost Gold Pty Ltd, the resulting tax savings for the 1997 year of $87,840 covered the cash payments required and left it with a substantial net cash surplus of $39,840.
    • (c) In Mr Leunig’s case, his tax savings from his investment in the 1997 year were realised over the years he could carry forward the loss to offset against income in the following year. His tax saving was $8,987.52 in the 1997 year, $7,716.05 in the 1998 year and $1,233.20 in the 1999 year which resulted in total tax saved of $17,936.77. His net deficit to cash payments of $36,000 was $18,063.23.

(See the detailed calculations in annexure A to these reasons).

  1. The tax savings met (or, substantially met) the applicants’ required cash contributions. By reason of the indemnity, the applicants took and retained the benefit of the tax savings regardless of the outcome of their investment.
  2. In the terms used in Fletcher’s case (set out above), having regard to the “contents and implications of the overall contractual arrangements”, the applicants’ outgoings made no business sense. They are more satisfactorily explained, in the Tribunal’s opinion, by the more certain and immediate tax deductions they secured, facilitating their investment.
  3. The applicants sought to support the wisdom of the investment by the evidence of Mr Simpson. However, that evidence amounted to no more than an uncritical endorsement of an unsupported rate of return, that is, the rate shown by a page of income projections behind which there was no demonstrated reasoning. The investor’s money was to be risked on the attractiveness of the business idea with very little more.
  4. Mr Simpson supposed an investor seeking to diversify his or her portfolio by the investment. Ultimately, however, one cannot avoid the implications of the tax advantages the investment offered, combined with the indemnity. They meant that the investment was offered with no investment risk. The only risk to the investors’ funds was that the tax deductions which funded, or substantially funded, their investments might not be allowed.
  5. The significance of Mr Simpson’s evidence was entirely undermined by the concessions he made in cross-examination as noted above.
  6. Similarly, the evidence of Messrs Weir, Harvey, Jancezko and O’Brien is of no assistance to the applicants. As noted earlier the supporting material which formed the basis of their views was not available to the applicants and not sought at the time they made their investments.
  7. Further, the evidence of Messrs Weir, Harvey, Jancezko and O’Brien is in the Tribunal’s view of no assistance to the Tribunal given that it entirely reflected the assumptions they made – assumptions that on the evidence before the Tribunal had no foundation in the past performance or future plans or activities actually undertaken by the Financial Providers and/or IPF Finance and RMA.

PART IVA

  1. While the Tribunal’s primary finding is that the claimed deductions are not allowable under section 51(1) of the ITAA 1936, in case that finding is not correct the Tribunal has considered whether the applicants obtained tax benefits in connection with a scheme to which Part IVA of the ITAA 1936 applied, so that the deductions should in any event be disallowed.

Part IVA - relevant provisions

  1. Section 177F of the ITAA 1936 provides, inter alia, for the disallowance of a tax benefit in the form of a deduction otherwise allowable to a taxpayer in connection with a scheme to which Part IVA applies.
  2. Section 177C(1), relevantly, defines a “tax benefit” in the following terms:

“Subject to this section, a reference in this Part to the obtaining by the taxpayer of a tax benefit in connection with a scheme shall be read as a reference to –

...

(b) a deduction being allowable to the taxpayer in relation to a year of income where the whole or a part of that deduction would not have been allowable, or might reasonably be expected not to have been allowable, to the taxpayer in relation to that year of income if the scheme had not been entered into or carried out;

...

and, for the purposes of this Part, the amount of the tax benefit shall be taken to be -

...

(d) in a case to which paragraph (b) applies – the amount of the whole of the deduction or the part of the deduction, as the case may be, referred to in that paragraph.”

  1. With section 177F, section 177D is the provision about which Part IVA pivots: FCT v Hart [2004] HCA 26; (2004) 217 CLR 216 per Gummow and Hayne JJ at [37]. It provides that a scheme must be entered into or carried out by a person for a purpose of the kind identified in section 177D(b) as follows:

“This Part applies to any scheme that has been or is entered into after 27 May 1981, and to any scheme that has been or is carried out or commenced to be carried out after that date ... where -

(a) a taxpayer (in this section referred to as the “relevant taxpayer”) has obtained, or would but for section 177F obtain, a tax benefit in connection with the scheme; and
  • (b) having regard to -
    • (i) the manner in which the scheme was entered into or carried out;
    • (ii) the form and substance of the scheme;
    • (iii) the time at which the scheme was entered into and the length of the period during which the scheme was carried out;
    • (iv) the result in relation to the operation of this Act that, but for this Part, would be achieved by the scheme;
    • (v) any change in the financial position of the relevant taxpayer that has resulted, will result, or may reasonably be expected to result, from the scheme;
    • (vi) any change in the financial position of any person who has, or has had, any connection (whether of a business, family or other nature) with the relevant taxpayer, being a change that has resulted, will result or may reasonably be expected to result, from the scheme;
    • (vii) any other consequence for the relevant taxpayer, or for any person referred to in subparagraph (vi), of the scheme having been entered into or carried out; and
    • (viii) the nature of any connection (whether of a business, family or other nature) between the relevant taxpayer and any person referred to in subparagraph (vi),

it would be concluded that the person, or one of the persons, who entered into or carried out the scheme or any part of the scheme did so for the purpose of enabling the relevant taxpayer to obtain a tax benefit in connection with the scheme or of enabling the relevant taxpayer and another taxpayer or other taxpayers each to obtain a tax benefit in connection with the scheme (whether or not that person who entered into or carried out the scheme or any part of the scheme is the relevant taxpayer or is the other taxpayer or one of the other taxpayers).”

  1. According to section 177A(5), the “purpose” for which a person entered into or carried out a scheme may be the sole purpose or the dominant purpose of doing so.
  2. A “scheme” for the purposes of Part IVA is defined in section 177A(1) in the following terms: -

“”scheme” means –

(a) any agreement, arrangement, understanding, promise or undertaking, whether express or implied and whether or not enforceable, or intended to be enforceable, by legal proceedings; and

(b) any scheme, plan, proposal, action, course of action or course of conduct.”

  1. As has frequently been observed, the definition of “scheme” is very broad. See, for example, FCT v Spotless Services Ltd [1996] HCA 34; (1996) 186 CLR 404 at 425 per McHugh J; FCT v Peabody [1994] HCA 43; (1994) 181 CLR 359 at 383. See further FCT v Hart at [9] per Gleeson CJ and McHugh J, [43] per Gummow and Hayne JJ, [85], [87] per Callinan J and Eastern Nitrogen Ltd v FCT [2001] FCA 366; (2001) 108 FCR 27 at [71] per Carr J (Lee and Sundberg JJ agreeing).

Section 177D(b) factors

  1. The test posited by section 177D is an objective one: FCT v Spotless Services Ltd at 421-423, 424; FCT v Consolidated Press Holdings [2001] HCA 32; (2001) 207 CLR 235 [95]; Eastern Nitrogen Ltd v FCT per Carr J at [80]-[84]; FCT v Sleight [2004] FCAFC 94; (2004) 136 FCR 211 per Hill J (Hely J agreeing) at [67] and per Carr J at [205]; Calder v FCT (2005) 61 ATR 267 at [91]; FCT v Cooke [2004] FCAFC 75; (2004) 55 ATR 183 at [88]. The test does not require, or even permit, any inquiry into the subjective motives of the taxpayer or others who entered into or carried out the scheme or any part of it: FCT v Hart at [65].
  2. By way of example, in Vincent v FCT (2002) ATC 4490 French J (at first instance) found, in relation to section 51(1), that the obtaining of a tax deduction was not Ms Vincent’s dominant purpose. In relation to Part IVA, however:

“whatever the subjective purpose of Ms Vincent and her state of knowledge about the true nature of the scheme into which she entered, a reasonable person would conclude, having regard to the eight listed factors, that those taxpayers who entered into the project did so with the dominant purpose of obtaining a tax benefit in connection with it”: at [142]; and see [133].

  1. Similarly, in Calder v FCT (2005) 61 ATR 267 at [114] a taxpayer’s unawareness of the financial structuring of the project was held to be immaterial.
  2. As the terms of section 177D make clear, the reference to “purpose”, objectively determined, includes not only the applicant’s purpose in entering the scheme, but also the purpose of the scheme’s promoters in enabling the applicant and others to obtain tax benefits in connection with the scheme. In this respect, see Vincent v FCT [2002] FCAFC 291; (2002) 124 FCR 350 at [100] per Hill, Tamberlin and Hely JJ; Puzey v FCT (2002) 194 ALR 615 per Lee J [105]; FCT v Sleight per Hill, J (Hely J agreeing) at [65]-[66], [67.3], [96] and per Carr J at [239]-[240].
  3. Each of the factors set out in section 177D(b) must be considered. However, as Hill J pointed out in Peabody v FCT [1993] FCA 74; (1993) 40 FCR 531 at 543:

“This does not mean that each of those matters must point to the necessary purpose referred to in s 177D(b). Some of the matters may point in one direction and others may point in another direction. It is the evaluation of these matters, alone or in combination, some for, some against, that s 177D requires in order to reach the conclusion to which s 177D refers.”

See also FCT v Sleight per Hill J at [67].

  1. Nevertheless, the relevant purpose may be so apparent on the evidence taken as a whole that consideration of the factors can be collapsed into a global assessment of purpose: FCT v Consolidated Press Holdings at [94] and FCT v Sleight per Hill J at [67]. Echoing the statement in FCT v Consolidated Press Holdings Ltd per the Court at [94], the Full Court in Calder v FCT (2005) 61 ATR 267 at [79]-[80] stated:-

“It is important, however, to bear in mind that the ultimate judgment as to purpose under s177D is holistic, albeit it requires that regard be paid to each of the eight factors listed in s177D(b). Indeed it can be expressed as a global or overall judgment provided that it is apparent that those factors have been considered.”

  1. It does not follow from the requirement that the purpose of a person who has entered into or carried out the scheme must be determined objectively, that this refers to what a reasonable person would have done. As the words of paragraph 177D(b) make clear, the conclusion as to the purpose of a person who has entered into or carried out the scheme is to be drawn from a consideration of the eight factors there set out. What a reasonable person may or may not have done in entering into or carrying out the scheme is irrelevant to the conclusion that is required under paragraph 177D(b).
  2. Critically, the pursuit of a commercial objective is not inconsistent with the existence of a dominant purpose of enabling the taxpayer to obtain a tax benefit. This has been pointed out:
    • (a) from the earliest authority, FCT v Spotless Services Ltd at 415, 416 (and see FCT v Consolidated Press Holdings at [96]);
    • (b) through the authorities concerning agricultural investments: FCT v Sleight per Hill J at [67.6] (Hely J agreeing) and per Carr J [206]; Calder v FCT at [92]; FCT v Cooke at [93];
    • (c) and, ultimately, confirmed by the High Court in FCT v Hart per Gleeson CJ and McHugh J at [10]-[12], [16]-[18] per Gummow and Hayne JJ at [52], [68], [71]; per Callinan J at [93]-[96].

In both FCT v Spotless Services Ltd and FCT v Hart, the High Court emphasised the shape or form taken by the scheme as pointing to its purpose.

  1. The evidence of the applicants was aimed at establishing that their decisions to invest in a IPF franchise were based upon commercial considerations and that IPF was a genuinely commercial operation. As Hart indicates, all of that begs the question. There, Mr and Mrs Hart borrowed funds for, inter alia, an investment property. There was no question that the funds were borrowed for a commercial purpose or that they would secure an acceptable commercial return. Nevertheless, the form the loan took and the consequent tax advantages it secured founded the conclusion that the dominant purpose of the Harts in entering into and carrying out the scheme was to obtain tax benefits.
  2. Here, each of the applicants:
    • (a) obtained large tax deductions in respect of obligations for annual royalty and management fees;
    • (b) those obligations were, largely, to be met from indemnified loans to be repaid from any income earned by exploitation of the franchise;
    • (c) any fee obligations after the first year were to be met only from such income as might be earned by exploitation of the franchise.
  3. Moreover, the loans providing $20,000 of the $30,000 first year franchise fees were not supported by cash. No actual funds were advanced by CCF in respect of any of the loans. Instead, the loan funding was effected by cheque round robins with IPF Finance, RMA and CCF making entries in its books of account for the sums lent to the applicants.
  4. More importantly, the means by which the loans were effected meant that there were no funds for the conduct of any commercial activities by IPF Finance and RMA except the cash payments made by the franchisees. The large up-front fees had no commercial function. Their only possible function was to gear up the available deductions.
  5. The structure described above resulted in tax savings for each applicant. The cash benefits that accrued to each applicant were:
    • (a) in the case of Mr Player - $12,839.39
    • (b) in the case of Lost Gold Pty Ltd - $87,840 (after taking into account cash payments required, those tax savings left Lost Gold Pty Ltd with a significant cash surplus of $39,840);
    • (c) in the case of Mr Leunig - $17,936.77 as at the 1999 income year.

The calculations appear in Annexure A.

  1. The indemnity for the loans and the fact that all obligations with respect to those loans and further franchise fees were to be met only from such income as might be earned by exploitation of the franchises, meant that the applicants were free to undertake significant obligations without regard to how the fees charged were to be applied or whether they were likely to recoup them. As a result of the tax advantages their investment offered, the applicants covered their initial payment obligations and could retain any cash advantages they derived without further risk and regardless of the performance of the investment in IPF.
  2. When the whole circumstances of the scheme are examined below as section 177D(b) requires, the relevant prevailing purpose of obtaining tax benefits is clear.

Section 177D(b)(i) – the manner in which the scheme was entered into or carried out

  1. The analysis above describes:
    • (a) the manner in which the Scheme was presented to intending participants;
    • (b) the making of the formal documents by which it was carried into effect in each year.
  2. The Scheme was presented by the IM which provided little information or expert review about the nature of the business and the income projections being offered intending franchisees. Prospective investors received two tax opinions. Correspondence and worksheets demonstrating the tax effect of the investment were also used in its marketing.
  3. Each applicant entered into the Scheme by signing an application form which formed part of the IM. By this application form each applicant agreed to have entered into a binding agreement with IPF. Each applicant also signed a Power of Attorney and Appointment of Agent for IPF to enter into and execute on his behalf the Franchise Agreement, the Management Agreement and the Loan Deed and any other ancillary documents, (including, presumably, the Indemnity Agreement).
  4. The manner in which each applicant entered into the Scheme is set out above.
  5. The effect of the Indemnity Agreement was that the loans to fund the fees were, effectively, limited recourse as liabilities were to be satisfied only out of the Franchisee Entitlements.
  6. Loans to the applicants and other investors were effected without actual cash.
  7. IPF Finance, RMA, CF and CIA were associates.
  8. In the result, the applicants were relieved of all obligations except the making of the agreements and limited cash payments:
    • (a) they signed the Franchise Applications to agree to be bound by the various agreements;
    • (b) the terms of the various agreements required nothing of participants:
      • (i) all activities were to be undertaken by IPF finance and RMA;
      • (ii) save for the initial prepaid first year fees, some of which was required to be in cash, all payments in respect of ongoing franchise fees, repayment of loan, and of interest on the loan, were to be met from the Franchisee Entitlements;
      • (iii) the Financial Providers and IPF Finance were authorised to deal with any Franchisee Entitlements under the franchises and to deduct payments due.
  9. In the result, little effort was required of the applicants to secure tax deductions which by reason of the Indemnity Agreement provided an enduring financial benefit. They were free to undertake significant obligations without regard to how the fees charged were to be applied or recouped. As a result of the tax savings, the applicants covered their initial payment obligations in whole or part and retained any cash benefits without further risk and regardless of the performance of their investment.
  10. The applicants’ entry into the scheme followed, at best, a desultory approach to the information with which they were presented as set out above.
  11. While there was some focus on whether the stated tax deduction position was reliable, no inquiry was made regarding the basis of the income projections in the IM. There was no information given in the IM as to the key assumptions supporting the projections and minimal information regarding risks, cash flows and the business.
  12. Fees were incurred without making any/adequate attempts to understand or consider the effect of the arrangements. Indeed, the applicants did not even have to seek access to IPF 97 Project agreements – the agreements by which their businesses were supposedly constituted prior to becoming bound to participate and pay their fees.
  13. The overall conclusion to be drawn from the evidence is overwhelmingly that the applicants were passive investors in a Project from which they were hoping for a commercial return, but the dominant motivation for which was the tax benefit.
  14. Neither did the applicants give consideration to the conduct of the purported business. The IM and agreements were, effectively silent on how the Franchised Business was to be conducted.
  15. There were obvious difficulties in the practical operation of the supposed business. However, the applicants made no complaint or inquiry about the conduct of the Franchise Businesses despite those difficulties and their poor financial performance.
  16. It is clear that IPF Finance and RMA did not have the infrastructure to cope with 950 franchises. As it turned out, the Franchise Businesses were not carried out in accordance with the terms of the Franchise Agreement and the Management Agreement. The Tribunal finds on the evidence that this was because IPF Finance and RMA were never able, or intended to be able, to discharge their obligations.

Section 177D(b)(ii) – the form and substance of the scheme

  1. In respect of the scheme under consideration in Commissioner of Taxation v Sleight [2004] FCAFC 94; (2004) 136 FCR 211, Hill, J. stated as follows (at [80]): -

“Finally, it should be noted that the financial structure that the management agreement, loan agreement and indemnity agreement created was not necessary to the success of a tea tree project. Presumably, the promoters, for example, could still have received the same amount of return by limiting the first year management fee to the actual cash outlay of the investor, and then adjusting the management fee in subsequent years to achieve this result. Arguably, an investor would thus have a legitimate, albeit significantly reduced, tax deduction for his cash outlay because it was actually a necessary cost of the project. This fact points towards a dominant tax incentive purpose because it could be objectively determined or concluded that an investor, who had a dominant commercial purpose, would prefer the project with a normal structure, rather than one which was so structured that it maximized the deductions available by the use of a somewhat artificial structure.”

And see at [82] and Calder v FCT at [69] and [117]. Investors in those schemes and the applicants here were, in substance, passive investors in what, once the tax features are removed, is a managed fund where no deduction would be available...” (Hill J in Sleight at [82]).

  1. The evidence shows clearly that in these applications, the form of the arrangements similarly took an artificial form not necessary to its commercial outcomes:
    • (a) they were carried out by related parties and IPF Finance, RMA, CCF and CIA were established in the months leading up to the implementation of the Scheme;
    • (b) those parties provided the franchises, and indemnified obligations, none of which was necessary to the carrying on of the purported underlying commercial activity of developing and conducting the System;
    • (c) the franchisee did nothing more than take a franchise and then appoint a manager to conduct all the franchised activities. The underlying commercial activities could as well have been carried out without the creation of the franchise network;
    • (d) the business that existed was, in substance, conducted by the Finance Providers or IPF Finance/RMA without regard to the franchise network;.
    • (e) There was no System and the System as described in the IPF 97 Project agreements was in any event unnecessary – the Financial Providers or an entity acting on their behalf could have marketed the Products to insurance and mortgage brokers and equipment vendors (as subsequently occurred);
    • (f) The manner of allocation of territories and the appointment of a single manager defeated any legitimate commercial role the franchise network might have played.
  2. The IPF 97 Project arrangements secured deductions for revenue expenses – franchise fees. Further, loans to meet those revenue expenses were made on an indemnified (effectively, limited recourse) basis and were not the subject of cash transactions in any event. As a result the deductions were in excess of:
    • (a) the cash amounts participants were required to pay;
    • (b) any amount participants would ever be liable to pay, owing to the indemnity;
    • (c) any amount that was ever available to IPF Finance/RMA to conduct the Franchise Businesses.
  3. The arrangements lacked any commercial substance:
    • (a) The fees were purportedly incurred in establishing a business the nature and operation of which was barely defined. The obligations of IPF Finance and RMA were, largely, unspecified. They were not accompanied by business plans, marketing agreements or other detailed proposals for the application of the fees payable.
    • (b) There seemed little prospect of recouping these fees:
      • (i) the purported business described by the IM lacked any real substance.
      • (ii) the assumptions upon which the projected cashflows were based were not disclosed. Neither was there any discussion of risks, business variations, business development, competitors, possible market changes or the like;
      • (iii) even if the projections were met, the investment of $32,000 would not be recovered for more than 8 years. As the projections might be viewed as no more than unreliable predictions at this point, franchisees were at risk of not recouping the initial investment at all or, at least, not for a considerable period;
      • (iv) the restriction on franchisee territory would limit the franchisees’ financial return and not make it possible to achieve rapid sales growth and significant net profit.
  4. Despite the apparent lack of substance, the applicants made no inquiries about the projected cashflows or the capacity of the Franchisor and the Manager to service their proposed customers or the 950 it was proposing to manage.
  5. Ultimately, the large up-front fees payable make no commercial sense and there was no explanation for that aspect of the arrangements. Mr Acheson rightly observed that IPF Finance was supposed to receive very substantial funds from the Project but RMA was only obliged to engage in relatively cheap forms of marketing such as letter drops. The irresistible explanation for the large first year fee was the tax benefit it generated for participants.

Conclusion with respect to section 177D(b)

  1. It is the Tribunal’s finding that, having regard to the eight factors set out in section 177D(b), it would be concluded that each of the applicants entered into or carried out the Scheme for the dominant purpose of obtaining tax benefits, in the form of the deductions they claimed in the 1997 year.
  2. Similarly, it would be concluded that the promoter entities entered into or carried out the Scheme for the dominant purpose of obtaining tax benefits for the applicants. Their purpose in entering into or carrying out the Scheme, was to obtain a tax benefit for participants in IPF franchises, including the applicants. It is not to the point that the overall commercial objective of the promoter entities was to make money. They achieved their commercial purpose by creating a structure to which the attractiveness of the tax advantages it secured was central. Obiter comments made in the Full Court decisions in Vincent at [100] and Sleight at [95]-[96] must now be read in light of the High Court decision in Hart, where it was pointed out that the pursuit of a commercial objective is not inconsistent with the existence of a dominant purpose of enabling the taxpayer to obtain a tax benefit particularly where the shape or form of the scheme entered into by the promoter entities points to the purpose of obtaining tax benefits.
  3. The agreements by which the scheme was implemented “took such a form that there is a particular scheme in respect of which a conclusion of the kind described in s177D is required, even though the particular scheme also advances a wider commercial objective”: FCT v Hart [2004] HCA 26; (2004) 217 CLR 216 per Gleeson CJ and McHugh J at [16]. That form enabled the applicants to obtain large deductions in respect of obligations largely funded by loans covered by an indemnity. The consequent tax savings were to the applicants’ advantage, with or without the commercial success of their investment. But for the tax savings, the scheme might have taken the more usual form of an investment scheme, as Hill, J. suggested at [80] of his judgment in Sleight, quoted above. In doing so, he articulated the relevant “alternative postulate” contemplated by Gummow and Hayne JJ in Commissioner of Taxation v Hart [2004] HCA 26; (2004) 217 CLR 216 at [66].
  4. In the result, the potential commercial performance of the applicants’ investments, and even their nature, were irrelevant. Additionally, however, as the foregoing examination of the scheme by reference to each of the eight factors set out in section 177D(b) demonstrates, the scheme lacked commercial substance and, in many respects, defies rational commercial explanation.
  5. The Tribunal finds that that if, contrary to its finding the claimed deductions were allowable to the applicants under section 51(1), then it was appropriate for the respondent to make the determinations under section 177(1) of the ITAA 1936 in respect of the claimed deductions.

ADDITIONAL TAX

  1. As the Tribunal finds that the claimed deductions were not allowable to the applicants under section 51(1), the Tribunal also finds that the elements of section 226L of the ITAA 1936 are satisfied, and accordingly, that section applies to impose the penalties by way of additional tax assessed to each applicant.
  2. Alternatively, if Part IVA applies to disallow the claimed deductions then the Tribunal also finds that sub-sections 226(1) and (2) of the ITAA 1936 apply to impose the penalties by way of additional tax that were assessed to each applicant.
