Re Adele Laura Carr and Commissioner of Superannuation [1985] AATA 107 (3 May 1985)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Re: ADELE LAURA CARR

And: COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. N84/412

Superannuation

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

R.K. Todd (Deputy President)

Dr A.H. Marsh (Member)

D.R. Steele Craik (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - extension of period for requesting reconsideration of decision to accept as contributor to Superannuation Fund - relevant factors

Superannuation Act 1922, ss.5, 79, 141

Superannuation Act 1976, ss.16, 66, 154, 184

Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Act 1973, s.99

Re Gardner and Commissioner for Superannuation (1978) 2 ALN 164

Re Schmack and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1981) 3 ALN N77

HEARING

CANBERRA

3:5:1985

ORDER

The Tribunal decides to affirm the decision under review, being the decision of the respondent to confirm on reconsideration a decision of his delegate not to extend pursuant to s.154(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 beyond 30 days the prescribed period within which the applicant might request reconsideration of the decision of a delegate of the Superannuation Board pursuant to s.5 of the Superannuation Act 1922 and dated 8 June 1973.

DECISION

In this matter the applicant initially lodged an application (No.A83/45) for the review of a reconsidered decision, made as long ago as February 1979, that the benefit entitlement of the applicant upon her ceasing to be an eligible employee on 1 September 1977 by reason of retirement on the ground of invalidity should be determined under s.66(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the 1976 Act"). An extension of time within which to lodge such application for review was granted by the Tribunal on 1 August 1983.

2. The stated reasons for the decision on reconsideration were as follows:

"(a) I am satisfied that the Superannuation Board did

not accept Mrs Carr as a contributor under Part III

of the Superannuation Act 1922 in 1973 by reason of

the condition, 'anxiety-depression'.

(b) Mrs Carr was a contributor to the Provident Account

under the Superannuation Act 1922 immediately

before the commencement of the Superannuation Act

1976 on 1 Jul 1976.

(c) Mrs Carr was a contributor on 1 Jul 1976 and under

sub-section 184(2) of the Superannuation Act 1976

there was deemed to be in force in respect of Mrs

Carr on that date a benefit classification

certificate specifying the condition, 'anxiety-

depression'.

(d) The report, dated 29 Aug 1977, of the Commonwealth

Medical Officer who examined Mrs Carr to determine

her fitness for continued employment stated that

she was suffering from 'Depression/Anxiety Neurosis

- exacerbation' and considered that she was unfit

for further service and should be retired. He also

noted that 'this condition was pre-employment

causing her to be classified as fit for Provident

only'.

(e) In an advice dated 19 Jan 1979 the Director, (NSW

Division), Department of Health, Sydney, stated:-

'... There is definite evidence that Mrs Carr

did suffer from a nervous disorder that

required medical attention before her

appointment in 1971. Since that time she has

required treatment by a Neurosurgeon and

Gynaecologist but, according to both of these

Specialists, neither of these conditions should

prevent her working.

The Commonwealth Medical Officer is still of

the opinion that Mrs Carr's retirement was due

to a pre-appointment condition, namely Anxiety

Depression which could have been aggravated by

the above neurosurgical and gynaecological

condition.'

(f) I am of the opinion that the incapacity which was

the ground for retirement of Mrs Carr was caused,

or was substantially contributed to by the

condition, 'anxiety-depression' or by a physical or

mental condition or conditions connected with that

condition."

3. When the application for review came on for hearing before a differently constituted Tribunal, it appeared that the applicant wished to contest the original decision of the Superannuation Board made under the Superannuation Act 1922 ("the 1922 Act") not to accept the applicant as a contributor under Part III of the 1922 Act but rather to accept her as a contributor to the Provident Account under Part V of that Act. It became apparent however that the applicant wished to challenge that decision directly, and not simply to overturn it as part of the process of challenge to the 1977 decision set out in paragraph 2 above. For that reason the applicant, as in effect required by the Tribunal, made application to the respondent, pursuant to s.154(2) of the 1976 Act, to extend beyond 30 days the period within which the applicant might request reconsideration of the original decision made by the Superannuation Board. Having regard to when the notice was in fact given, in the circumstances mentioned above, the length of the extension sought is some 10 years and 10 months.

4. To appreciate the problem before the Tribunal, it is necessary to set out the somewhat complex history of the applicant's membership of the Australian Public Service. She first commenced with the Public Service in 1964, but she resigned therefrom in 1966 upon her marriage to one J. During this time she contributed to the Superannuation Fund. Following legislative amendments permitting married women to be appointed permanently to the Public Service, she applied in late 1966 to be re-appointed, and this eventuated in February 1967. In March 1971 she separated from her husband and was subsequently divorced. In about October 1971 she commenced an association with her present husband, an association which, she said, caused her considerable mental strain having regard to her religious upbringing and beliefs. She was also having difficulties at her place of employment and on 26 August 1971 she in fact handed in her resignation. Her evidence was that her intention in resigning was to attract attention to her desire to bring about a hearing, outside her immediate section, of her request for a transfer to another section. In September 1971 she was asked to go in for an "exit interview" and this she did, but what followed was a continuing effort to be re-appointed to the Public Service, which did not in fact take place until May 1972.

5. As with the first period of the applicant's membership of the Public Service, she had during the second period contributed to the Superannuation Fund. But on the occasion of her third entry she was told informally, after a couple of weeks, that she had been accepted as a member of the Provident Account. She was aware that there was a difference between the Superannuation Fund and the Provident Account, and she had known of two persons having the experience of being accepted for the Provident Account, one because she was overweight and the other because she was underweight. She said that she was advised that it would cost her slightly more but that she would eventually receive interest on her contributions. She said that at the time she had seen what had happened as "a benefit. It meant saving - forced type of saving .... It was coming out of your pay before you could have a chance of spending it." She assumed that she had been assigned to the Provident Account because of an adverse report that had been made.

6. The report in question was from a Dr Burke. It was a certificate dated 6 October 1971 which was noted on 16 November 1971 in the report of the applicant's Medical Examination for Permanent Appointment as follows:

"16.11.71 Certif. from Dr B. Burke (Psychiatrist) dated

6.10.71 'under my care since 4.6.71 suffering

from a depressive illness, made an

unsuccessful suicidal gesture on 4.10.71

etc'."

The report itself added that after "discussing the situation with her de facto husband ... stationed at ... I consider it advisable that he remains at home with her for the following three days inclusive - 6th, 7th, 8th October, 1971".

7. This report apparently led to the rejection of the applicant's application for permanent employment. A further report was obtained from Dr Burke dated 15 February 1972 as follows:

"This is to certify that (the applicant) has been

consulting me regarding domestic and marital

problems rather than seeking psychiatric treatment

as such. I consider her to be quite fit and

capable of carrying out any of the positions for

which she has applied in the Public Service."

A favourable report was also obtained from a Dr Greenberg, and, as previously related, she was ultimately accepted for permanent appointment. But clearly the reports in question led to the applicant's being accepted as a contributor to the Provident Account.

8. It would be undesirable, having regard to the fact that the whole matter will have to be traversed again in the course of the hearing of the applicant's principal application for review, to say more than the bare minimum here about the circumstances of her retirement in 1977. It is enough to refer, without indicating agreement or otherwise with them, to the reasons set out in paragraph 2 above. In a nutshell, the applicant, having been retired on invalidity grounds was treated, and her rights were determined, on the footing that the required connection existed between the condition causing the incapacity that was the ground for her retirement ("Depression/Anxiety Neurosis - exacerbation") and what may be called her "pre-employment" condition, said to be "anxiety-depression".

9. As stated in the reasons given by the respondent for confirming, upon reconsideration, his delegate's decision not to extend the prescribed period within which the applicant might request reconsideration under s.154(2) of the 1976 Act, the applicant had two avenues of review under the 1922 Act had she wished to challenge the decision taken under s.5 of that Act to admit her as a contributor to the Provident Account only. First, she could have disputed that decision by invoking s.141 of the 1922 Act. If successful on such an application, she would have been accepted as a contributor to the pension scheme from the outset. Second, s.79 of the 1922 Act could have been invoked, under which however the effect would have been to accept her as a contributor to the pension scheme as from the date of a favourable decision of the Superannuation Board. The first of these avenues of review has been open to her since the decision that was made to admit her to the Provident Account only. The second was open only up to 12 February 1976.

10. From 1 July 1976 there was deemed to be in force in respect of the applicant, as a contributor to the Provident Account, a benefit classification certificate under s.184(2) of the 1976 Act, specifying the physical or mental condition or conditions by reason of which the Superannuation Board did not accept the applicant as a contributor to the Fund. From 1 July 1976 the applicant had under s.16 of the 1976 Act the right to seek variation or revocation of the benefit classification certificate. Except therefore for the period 13 February 1976 to 30 June 1976 the applicant had until the date of her retirement, 1 September 1977, either the right to apply to the Superannuation Board for admission to the pension scheme or the right to apply to the respondent for variation or revocation of the benefit classification certificate that was deemed to be in force in respect of her from 1 July 1976.

11. The argument for the applicant and for the respondent tended to concentrate on what have apparently come to be known as "Schmack factors", which, without it being suggested that they were in any way exclusive of other factors, were considered as being relevant by Deputy President A.N. Hall in Re Schmack and Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority (1981) 3 ALN N77. The issue there was a question of a similar nature arising under s.99 of the Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Act 1973. Before returning to those factors, though, it is desirable to point to what seem to us to be fundamental contentions that were put for applicant and respondent respectively.

12. First, it was strongly argued for the applicant that she should never have been confined to the Provident Account, that she had been unaware until much later of the consequences of her being so confined, and that as it would be necessary to traverse her medical and employment history from 1971 onwards anyway, it would be idle if not unjust not to permit her the extension which would allow her to raise now an argument that she should not have been admitted as a contributor to the Provident Account only.

13. For the respondent, on the other hand, it was equally strongly put that the applicant was really asserting a right to elect, many years later and after retirement from the Public Service, to opt to receive a pension through the Superannuation Fund on the footing that, invalidity retirement having occurred, a pension would be more beneficial than the benefits under the Provident Account which had, it appeared from the evidence, seemed eminently suitable to the applicant when she had been contemplating early retirement as a matter of choice.

14. As between these two major contentions, the Tribunal is impressed by the respondent's argument. The fact is that, as Mr Whithear for the respondent pointed out, in practical terms the applicant should be under no disadvantage should the extension not be granted. In the same way as it has been found not possible to find a connection between a condition contained in a benefit classification certificate and a condition made the basis of invalidity retirement but found not in fact to have existed (see Re Gardner and Commissioner for Superannuation (1978) 2 ALN N164), a condition found, as the applicant would assert to be the case, not to have in fact existed when the applicant was not accepted as a contributor to the Superannuation scheme, could not be connected with a condition in a benefit classification certificate. Thus while there is undoubtedly a conceptual difference between the remedy now available to the applicant and the remedy which would be available should the extension of time be granted, the effect would be the same, should the applicant's contentions as to her entry condition be sustained on the merits.

15. Leaving these broader considerations for the moment, we now refer to the criteria laid down in Re Schmack as they appear to us to apply in this case. Of the eight factors there referred to, six may for the purposes of this case be grouped in pairs:

(i) (a) The reasons for the failure to lodge the request for

reconsideration within the 30 days allowed by

Parliament for making that request as of right; and

(b) The diligence shown by the applicant in lodging her

request for reconsideration.

The applicant showed no diligence at all in lodging the

request. It was only when the matter came before the AAT

that it became apparent that this was necessary if the

point were to be taken. She did not lodge the request

earlier because she had no notion that it was necessary to

do so if the original decision were to be challenged, and

because she had not, it would seem, even formed any

intention to challenge the original decision. This is

understandable enough, as it seems that the applicant had

at an earlier stage received unsatisfactory advice from a

solicitor, not being her present legal adviser.

(ii) (a) The adequacy of the information conveyed to the

applicant at the time when the original decision was

notified to her, both as to the basis of the decision

and of her entitlement to request reconsideration; and

(b) The extent of the applicant's knowledge of the relevant

provisions of the legislation and of her awareness that

the decision in question involved a discretionary

element the exercise or non-exercise of which might

affect the ultimate decision.

We have little doubt that the applicant had a quite

imperfect understanding of the implications of her having

been admitted to the Provident Account only, that she was

not given the kind of information and assistance that

hopefully would now be given, that she was not told what

rights were open to her to challenge the decision, and that

in general terms she was more or less in the dark about

what had happened and about what might be done. Yet she

seemed content with the results and regarded herself as the

member of a useful scheme of enforced saving.

(iii) (a) The possible prejudice to the benefits scheme

established by the Act if the time for reconsideration

be extended as requested; and

(b) The administrative difficulties in reconsidering the

decision due to the absence or inadequacy of relevant

evidentiary material after such a lapse of time.

In this particular case it would appear that most of the

evidence remains available and that it would be possible to

adduce the necessary evidence of the reasons for the

Superannuation Board, which was not under any obligation to

furnish reasons for its decision under the 1922 Act,

deciding as it did. In terms of the general administration

of the legislation, however, there can be little doubt that

reconsideration of decisions taken so long ago would be

likely to create, in the circumstances of this legislation,

an administrative nightmare.

(iv) The nature of the decision of which reconsideration is

requested and the consequence of the decision upon the

applicant's rights or obligations under the legislation.

This problem has already been adverted to above. The

applicant's rights are most unlikely to be affected if the

extension not be granted.

(v) The length of the further time sought to be allowed.

In relation to this criterion, the circumstances are in our

opinion overwhelmingly against the grant of the extension

of time. On any basis the grant of an extension of time

amounting to nearly 11 years is, we consider, so extreme a

step that we would not countenance it unless the

circumstances in support of such grant were so extreme as

virtually to require it, with all other circumstances being

in the applicant's favour and with her rights not capable

of vindication in any other way.

16. In our opinion the circumstances are not such as to justify the grant of the extension sought. The primary consideration affecting this conclusion is the length of time that has elapsed. The next is that the applicant retains a remedy adequate to enable the assertion of her rights. While the applicant argues that the extension should be granted because the relevant facts in any event will have to be traversed, and a conclusion reached, as to the justification for the original decision, in our opinion the contrary conclusion should be drawn. It is because they will be so traversed, and a conclusion so reached, that the remedy sought to be invoked by granting the extension is unnecessary. Finally, we agree that to grant the extension in the present circumstances would have the effect of conferring an election on the applicant, years after the event, to overturn a decision made and accepted during her term of service, as to her rights under the legislation.

17. In all the circumstances the Tribunal decides to affirm the decision under review, namely the decision of the respondent to confirm on reconsideration a decision of his delegate not to extend pursuant to s.154(2) of the 1976 Act beyond 30 days the prescribed period within which the applicant might request reconsideration of the decision of a delegate of the Superannuation Board pursuant to s.5 of the 1922 Act and dated 8 June 1973.
