ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION



RESPONDENT



REASONS FOR DECISION



Before: Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)



Date: 9 May 1990



Place: Brisbane



The question to be decided is whether the applicant is liable for



vendors' recoupment tax in respect of unpaid tax and undistributed



profits tax in respect of a company which I will call B Ltd.



2. At the hearing before the Tribunal the applicant's case relied



upon a decision of Tribunal reported as Case U204 87 ATC 1138. That



decision was subject to an appeal to the Federal Court at the time of



the hearing before me. The Court's decision and reasons are now



reported as FCT v Swift and Ors 89 ATC 5101. In coming to the



decision in this matter I have taken into account the judgment of the



Federal Court (French J).



3. The Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax - Vendors) Act 1982 provides in



sections 4 and 5 that where at any time a vendor's taxable amount



exists under the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax) Assessment Act 1982



("the Act") tax, being an amount equal to the vendors taxable amount,



is imposed and shall be levied and paid on the vendor's taxable



amount.



4. Vendors taxable amount is defined by sub-section 3(1) of the Act



to mean (unless the contrary intention appears) in relation to a



person, an amount that is a primary taxable amount or a secondary



taxable amount in relation to the person. Primary taxable amount is



also defined to mean, in relation to a person, an amount that is a



primary taxable amount in relation to the person under section 5 of



the Act.



5. Sub-section 5(1) provides the basis upon which it may be held



that a primary taxable amount shall be taken to exist in relation to



the applicant in respect of sale of the shares in B Ltd. In summary,



only these are:



(a) some or all of the shares were sold under a scheme which occurred



before the commencement of the Act;



(b) the vendors were capable of controlling more than 90% of the



voting power immediately before the first sale time;



(c) the consideration on sale was more than the difference between



the total asset value of the company and the total liabilities of



the company including liability for company tax (which by



definition includes undistributed profits tax);



(d) an assessment of company tax has been made in relation to the



year of income in which the sale of shares took place and that



assessment is either not the subject of an objection or any



objection has been finalised;



(e) an amount of company tax so assessed remains unpaid;



(f) an arrangement or transaction was entered into that achieved the



result that the company was unable to pay the unpaid company tax



or the company ceased to exist and had been unable to pay the



company tax;



(g) the company did not continue to carry on its previous business.



6. If the conditions in sub-section 5(1) apply, then a primary



taxable amount shall be taken to exist at the relevant time in



relation to each person who, as the holder or one of the joint holders



of the shares, sold the shares under the scheme. The quantum of the



primary taxable amount is determined by a straight apportionment of



the overdue company tax.



7. The vendors primary taxable amount becomes, by definition, the



vendors taxable amount and makes the vendor liable to pay tax by



virtue of sub-section 8(1) of the Act which creates the liability to



pay tax imposed by the Taxation (Unpaid Company Tax - Vendors) Act



1982.



8. The basis of the applicant's case really revolves around



sub-section 5(4) of the Act. That sub-section provides:



"Where a primary taxable amount exists or existed in



relation to a person in relation to an amount of company tax



payable by a company and -



(a) the Commissioner, having regard to -



(i) circumstances relating to the sale of the shares



or the interest in shares to which the primary



taxable amount relates;



(ii) circumstances, whether occurring before or after



that sale, that caused or contributed to the



failure of the company to pay that company tax;



and



(iii) such other circumstances as the Commissioner



considers relevant,



considers it unreasonable that the primary taxable



amount should be taken to exist or to have existed in



relation to the person; or



(b) if the primary taxable amount is less than $100 - the



Commissioner considers that the primary taxable amount



should not be taken to exist or to have existed in



relation to the person,



the primary taxable amount shall not be taken to exist or to



have existed in relation to the person."



9. Sub-section 43(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975



provides, inter alia, that for the purpose of reviewing a decision, the



Tribunal may exercise all the powers and discretions that are conferred



by any relevant enactment on the person who made the decision. This



means that the Tribunal has the same powers as the respondent under



section 186 of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 which applies, mutatis



mutandis, for the relevant purposes of the Act by virtue of sub-section



4(1) of the Act. In this regard see Fletcher and Ors v. FCT 88 ATC 4834



at 4845-6.



10. I turn now to the facts of this case. The applicant is the former



wife of a chartered accountant now deceased. I shall call him H. The



applicant and H were divorced in 1981 (the applicant has remarried) well



after the events with which this application is directly concerned.



H died several years after the divorce.



11. Exhibit G establishes that the applicant was allotted 1,999 shares



in B Ltd. on 30 June 1972. These shares were allotted to the applicant



in her own right. The exhibit also establishes that the applicant was



allotted 7,996 shares in B Ltd. on 1 June 1972. These shares were held



jointly with one C as trustees for the applicant's four children in four



holdings of 1,999. The exhibit establishes a further allotment of shares



in B Ltd. to the applicant and the four trusts of 1,334 shares each on 27



April 1973. That return of allotment also discloses the allotment of



3,334 shares to H. Exhibit F, which is a poor copy of the register of



Directors, Manager and Secretaries of B Ltd. in 1971, shows the applicant



as one of the directors of B Ltd. The other directors were shown as



being H and C. C gave evidence before the Tribunal.



12. Exhibit H is a copy of "Particulars and Changes of Particulars in



Register of Directors, Managers and Secretaries of B Ltd." dated 6th June



1978. The copy form 43 shows that the applicant, H and C resigned as



directors on 22 June 1977 when new directors were appointed. H also



resigned as secretary.



13. None of the above documents are signed by the applicant except the



return of allotments of shares dated 27 April 1973. In evidence the



applicant stated that she did not know of these allotments of shares but



did not deny that she had signed documents in relation to B Ltd.



14. On the evidence before me I have no doubt and so find that the



applicant was a shareholder in B Ltd. and that she was aware of that



fact. It is irrelevant to such a finding that the applicant relied upon



H for the conduct of the affairs of B Ltd.



15. There was no dispute before me as to whether the elements of



sub-section 5(1) had been satisfied. I find that a primary taxable



amount exists in relation to the applicant in terms of sub-section 5(1).



16. The issue argued before the Tribunal is whether sub-section 5(4)



should be applied by a favourable exercise of the discretion.



17. The evidence before the Tribunal established that H was very much



the controlling mind in respect of B Ltd. only occasionally consulting



with his wife and then only on a conclusive basis. The decisions were



his to make and he did not invite assistance or interference in the



making of those decisions. As I understand the applicant's evidence, she



readily accepted H's domination in business matters, seeing her station



in life as a maternal one rather than the business side of the family's



affairs. In the result she signed documents at the request of her



husband, sometimes being given an explanation, but on most occasions



having little idea of the nature of the document being signed. Although



it was suggested that some documents purporting to have been signed by



the applicant were in fact forgeries, there is no basis for making such a



finding.



18. Exhibit 2 is a document described as "Power of Attorney" and signed



by the applicant. In evidence-in-chief the following evidence was given



by the applicant in relation to this document at pages 88-9 of the



transcript:



"What was your understanding of the affairs and operations of



"B Limited"? --- I had no idea what "B" did.



Did your ex-husband ever speak to you or report to you about



the affairs and operations of "B Limited"? --- No.



Did you ever discuss "B Limited", or its affairs and operations



with your co-director "C"? --- No, never.



But you saw "C" quite often? --- Yes, often, but we did not



ever talk of anything about business.



Why did you not talk to "C" anything about business? ---



Because I did not know anything to talk to him about.



Do you recall signing any documents regarding "B Limited"? ---



I cannot say I specifically recall signing a document that had



"B" on it, but I can recall signing documents.



Do you recall ever resigning as a director of "B Limited"? ---



No.



When did you first become aware of the sale of shares in "B



Limited"? --- I never. I mean I did not know whether the



shares were sold. I did not even - well, I did not know



anything abut it, so I would not know when the shares were



sold.



So the first notice you had presumably was when these notices



came from the Taxation Office in '83/'84? --- Yes. Well, in



fact it was - I remember the day - April '83, and my accountant



- I have got - because my relationship with "H" at this stage



had deteriorated of course, I got an accountant to do my tax



return and he phoned me up and he said to me, 'did you ever



have anything to do with any of "H's" business?' And I said,



'No, never; I had nothing to do with it.' And he said, 'Well,



I am sorry to tell you but I have an assessment here,' and that



was for some - a company that I had never even heard of.



Do you recall ever signing any documentation in relation to the



sale of your shares in "B"? --- No, I do not recall



specifically.



Do you recall signing a Power of Attorney in favour of a Mr



Allan Charles Garroway to execute documentation effecting the



sale of your shares in "B"? --- No, I do not. I have never



heard of Mr Garroway.



So you have never even heard of Mr Garroway? --- No.



If you were shown a Power of Attorney or some documents which



purported to bear your signature in relation to these share



sales, or in relation to documentation regarding these share



sales, would that change anything you said? I mean I am



putting it to you that there is a document or a Power of



Attorney in favour of Mr Garroway which actually has a



signature which purports to be yours. Have you got anything to



say to that at all? --- I find - is that some time in '77 - in



June '77?



That is right? --- I cannot say that I did not do that. The



only thing I can say is that in June '77 I had already, as was



said earlier today, been to see "C" about the fact that I felt



that I wanted to get out of my marriage. So I find it amazing



that I would have - I mean I know what a Power of Attorney -



even I know that - what a Power of Attorney is. But once again



it could easily have been given to me in a pile of other



documents. The strange thing is that even though I felt like I



did I still probably would have - I mean "H" ran everything. I



mean without him I did not have any money, I did not have



anything.



Were you ever aware that your ex-husband forged your signature



on any documents? --- No. I have seen some signatures that I



find doubtful but I could not - you know, I am not aware. But



I have seen some signatures that I find doubtful.



Do you recall at any time holding discussions with a person



called Don Lockyer? --- I have never met Don Lockyer but I have



spoken to him on the telephone.



Do you remember what you spoke to him about? --- Yes. It was



mainly asking him for money when "H" was in jail because that



is how my money came."



19. I found the applicant to be a witness of truth. Her evidence was



understandably vague on business matters for the reason stated above.



20. I am unable to find that the signature on the power of attorney is a



forgery. It must therefore be presumed to be the signature of the



applicant. That document clearly authorises the Attorney to "execute



sign and seal and deliver in the name of the Donor (the applicant) as



vendor the agreement and relevant share transfers for the sale and



purchase of shares in B Ltd. as between the Donor as Vendor and W Pty.



Ltd. as purchaser". I find that the shares in B Ltd. were sold by the



applicant on her express instructions.



21. The purchasers of the shares was a company I have called W Pty. Ltd.



The transfer of shares was approved at a meeting of the directors of



B Ltd. said to have been held at Brisbane on 22 June 1977 and attended by



C, H and the applicant. The document before the Tribunal is not signed



and I have no doubt was not accurate in that it asserted a meeting had



taken place. C said in his evidence that he had not attended any



meetings of B Ltd. and it may be inferred from the applicant's evidence



that she was not present at the meeting said to have been held on 22 June



1977. Such a finding is consistent with the evidence of C and the



applicant that they were passive, in the sense of quiescent, in the



affairs of the company, H ran the company for the benefit of himself and



his family without assistance from the other directors. The diary of C



for 22 June 1977 makes no mention of any appointment relevant to the sale



of the shares. C was however able to recall details of meetings with B



and the applicant shown in the diary at around that time.



22. The issue is therefore whether the passive acquiescence of the



applicant in the affairs of B Ltd., including the sale of her shares,



should be a basis for exercise of the dispensing power in sub-section



5(4) of the Act. As French J points out in Swift the sub-section is, in



a sense, in conflict with the overall intent of the Act to collect tax.



The discretion is one that must be exercised with care (as must all



discretionary powers) and with due regard to the clear intent of the Act.



23. Sub-section 5(4) requires that regard must be had to the



circumstances relating to the sale of shares, the circumstances that



contributed to the company's failure to pay the company tax and such



other circumstances as the Tribunal considers relevant.



24. Firstly, I should say that there is no conclusive evidence of the



circumstances which contributed to the failure of B Ltd. to pay the tax.



I can only assume that some device was used after the relevant sale of



shares to strip the company of its assets (cf. Exhibit J which discloses



a significant change in shareholdings over a very short period of time).



25. As to the circumstances of the sale of the shares, I accept the



evidence that although the applicant signed the power of attorney she had



no real knowledge of the circumstances of the sale and in particular did



not understand that the company was to be placed in such a position that



it was unable to pay the company tax. In this sense she was not



therefore a party to the company strip.



26. The other circumstances which I consider relevant in this matter are



the total control of B Ltd. by H, the evident possibility that H was well



aware of the arrangements to ensure that the company would be unable to



pay its company tax liability and the applicant's passive acquiescence in



H's direction and control of the affairs of the company. I do not



consider that this passive acquiescence forms the basis for a favourable



exercise of the discretion. The applicant was content to adopt the



actions of H. The actions of H, so far as I can perceive them, on the



evidence, such as it is, do not ground a basis for the exercise of the



discretion. That being so, the applicant has no better case for



dispensation from the liability imposed by the Act. I do not consider



that the simple expedient of washing one's hands of the company's



affairs, including the sale of shares in the company, thereby leaving H



to attend to those matters, is a basis for a favourable exercise of the



discretion (cf. per Kirby P in Metal Manufacturers v Lewis (1988) 13



NSWLR 315 at 321). I can find nothing in Swift to suggest that this is



not the right conclusion on the facts of this case. That Swift was



decided in favour of the taxpayer does not create a binding precedent in



this case, which must be decided having regard to its own facts. On the



evidence, there is no basis for a favourable exercise of the discretion



in sub-section 5(4).



27. The objection decision under review will be affirmed.

the Decision and Reasons of Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member) in the matters

of QT86/3802-3803.
