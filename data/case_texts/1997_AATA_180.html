DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION NO 11912

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No VT96/214-218

TAXATION APPEALS DIVISION )



Re      

Applicant



And COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

Respondent



DECISION



Tribunal Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member

Date 30 May 1997

Place Melbourne

Decision The Tribunal sets aside the decisions under review and allows the applicant’s objections in full.



.....(Sgd) Mr B. H. Pascoe.......

Senior Member



CATCHWORDS

FRINGE BENEFITS TAX - housing benefit - residence included in leased land - whether provided to employees - whether provided in respect of employment - whether amount of recipients rent

Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986

REASONS FOR DECISION

30 May 1997 Mr B. H. Pascoe, Senior Member



  1. These are applications for review of a decision of the respondent to disallow objections against Fringe Benefits Tax Assessments in respect to the years ended 31 March 1989 to 31 March 1993 inclusive. The applicant company is the trustee of the A Family Trust. Following on an audit of the taxation affairs of the trust, the respondent determined that a fringe benefits tax liability in the form of a housing benefit had been provided by the trust to Mr and Mrs A. Assessments were issued on 18 April 1994 to assess the value of the housing fringe benefit as follows:

Year ended 31 March 1989 9,680

Year ended 31 March 1990 10,720

Year ended 31 March 1991 8,640

Year ended 31 March 1992 5,564

Year ended 31 March 1993 5,512

  1. At the hearing the applicant was represented by Mr Roberts, a solicitor, and the respondent by Ms Batrouney of counsel. Evidence was given by Mrs A. The Tribunal had before it the documents provided by the respondent pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (T1-T88) together with a report by the Australian Valuation Office and a letter from a hospital which is the current landlord of the property in question.
  2. The basic facts of this case were not in dispute. The applicant company in its capacity as trustee carries on a business of an equestrian centre on leased rural land. The lease was entered into on 28 February 1981 with a Mr and Mrs P and related to approximately 227 hectares of land. Mrs P died on 14 July 1988 and bequeathed her interest as the surviving lessor of the land to the hospital. The original lease was for a period of six years with three ten year options. The lease is still current and the only amendment was executed in June 1981, giving the lessee the right of first refusal of a new lease at the expiration of the lease or for the purchase of the leased property. Within the leased land is a residence of some 130 square metres within a fenced land area of some 2000 square metres. Whilst it had been the understanding of the applicant and Mr and Mrs A that the residence was not included within the land which was the subject of the lease, it is now conceded that it is so included. The residence has been used solely by Mr and/or Mrs A over the period. The amounts assessed by the respondent were those provided by the Australian Valuation Office in its report as being the market rental value of the residence in each of the relevant years. For the years ended 31 March 1989, 1990 and 1991 a market rental value assuming the owner or lessor pays expenses relating to the property was used. For the year ended 31 March 1992 and 1993 a value assuming the tenant meets the property expenses was used.
  3. Mrs A gave evidence that the business was acquired from Mrs P who was ill and unable to care for the animals on the property or afford any renovations. She said that the residence in question was dilapidated and uninhabitable and that a verbal agreement had been entered into that the residence could be occupied for no rental provided that she and he husband met the cost of improving and maintaining it. She had understood prior to the issue of the assessments now in dispute that the area on which the residence stood was not included in the lease of the equestrian centre and surrounding land. Mrs A said that, in 1985, she borrowed money from her father and spent some $44,000 to renovate the residence so as to make it habitable. She said that she returned to work at a city hospital at that time so as to earn money to repay the loan. She said that she has had no direct involvement in the business from that time. Her husband, Mr A, had ceased residing at the residence at the end of 1991 and had continued to live overseas since January 1992. She accepted that both she and her husband had remained directors of the trustee company but she was unsure whether she had received any benefit from the trust. Mrs A said that discussions had been held with the hospital after the death of Mrs P regarding the possible purchase of the residence and/or a new lease. However, no changes were made and the hospital agreed to continue the then existing lease. Mrs A said that she travels to the property and stays for two nights each week to finalise salaries and sign cheques. She said that the business is managed by an employed lady with support staff. She was firm that the residence was separate from the business, was not covered by the rental paid for the land and the agreement with Mrs P was that the home could be used in consideration of Mr and Mrs A meeting the costs of renovation and maintenance.
  4. It was submitted for the applicant that the use of the residence was not a fringe benefit under the Fringe Benefits Tax Assessment Act 1986 ("the Act"). Firstly, it was said, that the residence was not provided by the applicant in respect of the current employment or past employment of Mr or Mrs A. It was submitted that Mrs A had received no wages from the applicant and Mr A had received wages between 1987 and 1989 but not thereafter. It was further submitted that any benefit was provided in the capacity as a beneficiary of the trust not as a consequence of employment. It was argued that, in any event, the true position was that the applicant had not paid rent for the residence, it being the position that, by agreement with the original landlord, no part of the rent paid related to the residence. Secondly, it was submitted that, if it could be said that the right to use the residence had been provided by the applicant, what was provided was an uninhabitable building which had no market rental value. The third argument was that consideration had been paid in the provision by Mr and Mrs A of some $44,000 for renovations in 1985 and the responsibility for ongoing care and maintenance. The fourth ground relied upon was that the residence was provided by the landlord, not the applicant, and that the landlord could not be regarded as an "arranger" within the meaning of the Act. Finally, it was submitted the lease was capable of rectification by exclusion of the area containing the residence so as to correct a mistake where the lease did not reflect the true intention of the parties to the lease. In a letter dated 14 February 1987 addressed to the applicant's solicitors, the Director of Financial Services for the hospital stated:
"I was of the understanding that the House had been substantially renovated and repaired by the tenant or by Mr & Mrs A, from its original state as an uninhabitable shell to a comfortable residential dwelling. Further, I understood that, accordingly, pursuant to an agreement between the original landlord and the tenant, no rental was to be payable under the lease in relation to the House, provided that the tenant continued to repair and maintain the House. The rental payable under the lease related solely to the remainder of the property and no monetary rental was imposed in relation to the House due to the substantial sums incurred by the tenant on a dwelling which it did not own.

This position was accepted by the Hospital and had been continued in relation to the lease since the Hospital became landlord."

In an earlier letter from the executive director of the hospital dated 17 June 1991 regarding the possibility of a new lease he stated that the existing lease did not cover "the house in which Mr A lives - its cost and management" and "that the house occupied by Mr A should be maintained by him. If he wishes repairs on the house to be paid by the hospital, then he should pay a separate rental on the residence".

  1. It was accepted by Mr Roberts that, in returns of income lodged by the trust, some expenses which could be regarded as being relevant to the private residence had been claimed as a deduction. Apart from a proportion of rent and rates which could be regarded as relevant to the residential part of the leased land there were amounts of $418 and $600 in 1989 and 1990 respectively for electricity, $130 in each of 1989 and 1990 for telephone and $300 for insurance in 1991. By letters dated 6 April 1994, the applicant's tax agents had requested that the returns of income for the years ended 30 June 1989 to 1992 inclusive be amended to exclude the private portion of expenses as deductions. The respondent had declined to amend the returns. It was submitted by Mr Roberts that these expenses had been claimed in error.
  2. It was submitted for the respondent that the residence constituted a housing right granted by the applicant to Mr and/or Mrs A and as such was a Housing Fringe Benefit under the Act. It was argued that Mr L was an employee in that he received wages in 1987, 1988 and 1989 and is now a former employee. In subsequent years he made drawings of $250 per week and was "entitled" to salary and wages. It was argued that, in any event both Mr and Mrs A were directors of the applicant and, as such, entitled to remuneration. It was said that Mr and Mrs A were provided with a benefit either by the applicant or by the hospital as an "arranger" and that such benefit was provided in respect of employment. The fact that the applicant claimed deductions for expenses in relation to the residence was said to demonstrate that the applicant regarded the residence as a business residence not a family arrangement. It was submitted that there was no clear evidence of any separate agreement for occupation of the residence. It was argued further that, even if it could be said that no rental was paid by the applicant for the residence, the residence was still demised to the applicant as tenant and, in turn, provided to Mr and Mrs A as sub-tenants. The benefit to be assessed under the Act is market rental value unrelated to any actual rent paid by the provider. It was submitted that the value of work done by Mr and Mrs A could not be regarded as "recipients rent" as it did not represent consideration paid to the applicant and was not paid "during the year of tax". In regard to the applicant's argument that the lease agreement could be rectified, it was said it had not been established that there had been a mistake that should be rectified and, in any event, this Tribunal has no power to rectify the lease.
  3. Section 136(1) of the Act contains the definition of "fringe benefit". As far as is relevant to this case, the definition provides that:
" 'fringe benefit', in relation to an employee, in relation to the employer of the employee, in relation to a year of tax, means a benefit:

(a) provided at any time during the year of tax; or

(b) provided in respect of the year of tax;

being a benefit provided to the employee or to an associate of the employee by:

(c) the employer;

(d) an associate of the employer; or

(e) a person (in this paragraph referred to as the 'arranger') other than the employer or an associate of the employer under an arrangement between:

(i) the employer or an associate of the employer; and

(ii) the arranger or another person;

in respect of the employment of the employee, ..."

There are exclusions provided for which are not relevant here. A "housing fringe benefit" is defined as a fringe benefit that is a "housing benefit" which, in turn is defined as a benefit referred to in section 25 of the Act. Section 25 states:

"25 The subsistence during the whole or a part of a year of tax of a housing right granted by a person (in this section referred to as the 'provider') to another person (in this section referred to as the 'recipient') shall be taken to constitute a benefit provided by the provider to the recipient in respect of the year of tax."

A return to section 136 provides the definition of a "housing right" in these terms:

" 'housing right' in relation to a person, means a lease or licence granted to the person to occupy or use a unit of accommodation, insofar as that lease or licence subsists at a time when the unit of accommodation is the persons usual place of residence".

Section 26 of the Act sets out the basis of calculation of the taxable value of a housing fringe benefit. It is a lengthy and difficult section but, for the purposes of this case it is sufficient to say that the value is the market value of the housing right reduced by the "recipients rent". The relevant part of the definition of "recipients rent" states that it is "the amount of any rent or other consideration paid to the provider or the employer by the recipient or the employee in respect of the subsistence, during the year of tax, of the recipients housing right".

  1. It is appropriate, in considering this matter, to determine a number of questions and in the following order:
  • was the right of occupancy of the residence a benefit
  • were Mr and/or Mrs A employees
  • was the residence provided by the employer or an "arranger"
  • was it provided in respect of the employment of Mr and/or Mrs L
  • what was the market value of the housing right
  • was there an amount of "recipients rent"
  1. There is no doubt that the right of occupancy of a residence is a benefit. Given the specific provisions of the definition of a "housing right", however, it would not appear that such right of occupation necessarily satisfies the definition for each of the years in question. The evidence of Mrs A was that Mr A ceased to reside there at the end of 1991. It was her evidence also that she commenced working at a hospital in the city and, currently, attends the property two nights per week. It is not clear when she commenced to be away from the property for five days per week but it is doubtful whether it can be said that, since late 1991, the residence has been "the persons usual place of residence".
  2. Undoubtedly, Mr A was an employee of the applicant at least until some time in 1989. As such he is still a former employee. It would appear that he continued to provide services to the applicant until late 1991 and possibly later, as he appeared to be present at an interview in December 1992 and wrote a letter to the hospital in May 1993. Nevertheless he did not receive salary or wages. The definition of employee under the Act uses the definitions in section 221A of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 which states that an "employee" "means a person who receives, or is entitled to receive, salary or wages". It was submitted for the respondent that both Mr and Mrs A were "entitled" to receive salary and wages as directors of the applicant and for provision of services to the applicant. Mrs A has clearly continued to have an ongoing role in the business, spending two nights a week there with responsibility for payroll and other expenses. If the final answer to this whole matter depended on a finding that Mr and Mrs A were employees during the years in question, I would be inclined to the view that they were. However, it is appropriate to consider the remaining matters before deciding whether this question requires a definitive answer.
  3. A less difficult question is whether the benefit was provided by the applicant. I have no difficulty in finding that it was. The simple facts are that the applicant was the lessor of the whole of the land which included the area occupied by the residence and its immediate surrounds. Whether or not the calculation of rental took the residence into account and whether or not the parties recognized that the residence was included in the 1981 lease is irrelevant. The residence was subject to that lease and, in turn, was available by way of sub-lease or licence to Mr and Mrs A. It was not provided by the hospital which was solely the lessor by succession of the whole of the land to the applicant.
  4. The question of whether the benefit was provided in respect of the employment of Mr and Mrs A is a more difficult one. Here the only substantive evidence was that of Mrs A. Prior to dealing with this evidence, it is appropriate to say that the fact that Mrs A was to give evidence and some of the content of that evidence was a surprise to the respondent. At the commencement of the hearing, Mr Roberts proceeded to outline the facts of the case and presented his submissions. He then stated that Mrs A was present and available to answer any questions from the Tribunal. I then indicated that, as this matter would require findings of fact, it was a more appropriate approach for the applicant to have Mrs A give sworn evidence and be available for cross-examination. I found Mrs A to be a credible witness. Based on her evidence, I am of the view that the use of the residence was not provided by the applicant in respect of the employment of Mr or Mrs A. The negotiations for the use of the house were conducted by Mr and Mrs A directly with Mrs P. At the time the house was uninhabitable and funds were borrowed personally to enable it to be renovated to enable its use as a residence. While Mr and Mrs A were also directors of the applicants, I am satisfied that the negotiations were carried out in their personal capacities and for their own private purposes. I accept also that Mr and Mrs A did not regard the area in which the residence was located as being part of the land used in the business of the applicant and for which the rental was paid. It is most unlikely that funds would be borrowed personally and Mrs A recommence a full-time job to provide an income to repay the borrowing if the provision of the residence was related to employment within the applicant's business. In cases such as these where the relevant parties are directors of the trustee company, beneficiaries of the trust and employees in the business activities of the trust, it is difficult to separate benefits of employment and benefits as beneficiaries or family members. On balance, here I accept that the residence was not a benefit provided in respect of employment.
  5. The Australian Valuation Office on instructions from the respondent provided two estimates of market rental value for each of the years in question. The lower value was based on an assumption that the tenant was responsible for meeting expenses of rates and water, gas and electricity, telephone and insurance applicable to the property. The higher value was based on the assumption that these costs were met by the owner or provider of the property. As indicated in paragraph 6 above, the applicant paid certain expenses relevant to the residence in 1989, 1990 and 1991 and claimed them in the return of income lodged. Given that they appeared to be round amounts, it is not clear whether they were reimbursements of part or all of the expenditure to Mr and/or Mrs A or were direct payments to suppliers. The applicant now says that these expenses were claimed in error. There appears little doubt that this view arose after the commencement of the audit. However, I doubt that whether or not they were intended to be claimed is relevant. Clearly such amounts were incurred by the applicant. Nevertheless, I am of the view that it is inappropriate to regard the applicant as having met all expenses of the nature listed applicable to the property. Consequently, it would seem that the higher level of market rental value used in the assessments for the years ended 31 March 1989, 1990 and 1991 was not justified. The lower level would appear to be the more appropriate assessment of rental value. This is further indicated when it is recognized that all maintenance costs were met by the occupiers.
  6. It was submitted for the applicant that an amount of "recipients rent" should be allowed for. Given my earlier findings, it is unnecessary to determine this question. However, if I am wrong in such findings, I would characterise the payment of $44,000 by Mr and Mrs A as recipients rent. This question only arises if it can be said that the applicant provided the right to Mr and/or Mrs A to occupy the residence. The granting of such a right would have required the applicant to have expended money to make the property habitable and acceptable as a residence. The payment of the occupiers of the amount required to render the property habitable could be said to have discharged the liability of the applicant and, consequently, "consideration paid to the provider or the employer by the recipient or the employee in respect of the subsistence, during the year of tax, of the recipients housing right". The reference to "during the year of tax" is a reference to the year of tax in which the housing right subsists not a reference to the year of tax in which the consideration is paid. Consequently, a lump sum consideration paid in 1985 can be said to be consideration in respect of the subsistence, in each future year, of a housing right. An appropriate calculation of the value of the consideration in each year of tax is the annual sum which would be provided by way of an annuity by the capital sum of $44,000 invested in 1985 for the period of the right of occupancy. Alternatively, and producing the same answer, the value of the annual consideration could be taken as the amount which would be required to repay a loan of $44,000 made in 1985 over the period of the right of occupancy. The original lease expired in 1987 with three ten year options exercisable in 1987, 1997 and 2007, giving, in 1985, a potential right of occupancy of the residence of somewhere between two and thirty-two years. As the first option had been exercised at the time of these particular years of assessment, the right of occupancy could be from twelve to thirty two years. A mid-point of twenty two years could be seen as appropriate. Hindsight might suggest twelve years to 1997 as the evidence of Mrs A is that the residence has not been used by Mr A since the end of 1991 and is used only two nights per week by her. In her submission, Ms Batrouney used a period of twenty-two years but simply divided the $44,000 by 22 to produce $2,000 per annum. Such a calculation ignores the money value of the lump sum paid in advance. If the notional interest rate pursuant to section 18 of the Act relating to Loan Fringe Benefits is used, the interest rate for a fixed term loan made in June 1985 would be 12%. Adopting that rate the annual consideration based on twelve years would be $7,103 and based on twenty-two years, $5,756. Given the volatility in interest rates in the relevant period moving from 12% at 30 June 1985 to 14.75% between 1986 and 1988, and reducing to 9.25% by 31 March 1993, the use of a variable rate calculation would, in all likelihood, approximate the lower level market rental valuer used by the Australian Valuation Office.
  7. The consequence of all of these findings is that the residence in question was not provided to Mr and/or Mrs A in respect of their employment but, in any event, the recipients rent reduced any taxable value to nil. This means that the decision under review should be set aside and the applicant's objections to the relevant assessments allowed in full.
