Last Updated: 25 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

INCOME TAX - allowable deductions - university lecturer on leave without pay carrying out research study overseas - travelling and living expenses - whether deductible as incurred in gaining or producing assessable income.

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 s. 51(1)

Lunney v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1958) 100 C.L.R. 458.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 C.L.R. 60.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Forsyth [1981] HCA 15; (1980-1981) 148 C.L.R. 203.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Hatchett [1971] HCA 47; (1971) 125 C.L.R. 494.

Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. White (1975) A.T.C. 4018.

REASONS FOR DECISION

Mr. J.R. Gibson (Senior Member)

  1. The decision on this application was given on 15th April 1988 and reasons for the decision were given orally at that time. Following a request by the applicant pursuant to s. 43(2A) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act, written reasons for the decision are now provided.
  2. The application was for review of a decision of the Commissioner of Taxation disallowing an objection by the applicant to the assessment of income tax on his income for the year of income ended 30 June 1984. The objection, so far as is relevant, was to the disallowance of a deduction of $5,637 claimed by the applicant for expenditure incurred in the course of a study project carried out overseas. The question for determination by the Tribunal was whether the amount claimed was deductible under s.51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 as having been earned in gaining or producing the assessable income of the applicant.
  3. The amount of $5637 comprised the applicant's fare to the United Kingdom, the cost of his passport, the proportion applicable to him of the cost of accommodation and food for the family in England and motor vehicle expenses. The Commissioner did not dispute that the amount claimed had been expended.
  4. The evidence before the Tribunal consisted of the documents furnished by the Commissioner to the Tribunal, a copy of the relevant return of income, a copy of a letter from the Head of School of Behavioural Studies at the University at which the applicant is a lecturer and the oral evidence of the applicant. He impressed as a frank and truthful witness and the Tribunal accepts his evidence completely.
  5. The applicant, who holds a Doctorate in Psychology, had for some years prior to the year of income been a Senior Lecturer at an Australian University and he has continued to hold that office. He has reached the top of the salary scale for Senior Lecturers and can only attain a higher salary scale if promoted to Associate Professor. It was open to him at any time to apply to be appointed an Associate Professor but as he regarded it as important that any application which he made should succeed, he felt that he should establish a very solid basis for it. Promotion would depend not only on what he had achieved in his teaching work, but also on contributions made by him in the field of psychology. He had had articles published and had been a co-author of one book and a co-editor of another, both published by an international publisher of scientific work. The applicant said that he regarded those publications as good building blocks towards an application for an Association Professorship and he felt that he needed to have published a book of which he was the sole author dealing in an original way with a topic of interest in the field of behavioural science.

6. The applicant had not decided upon a suitable topic until, following some conversation with a colleague, it came into his mind that an original study on role reversal would be of interest and of value; the information which he would derive if he and his wife changed roles would be at least as valuable, probably more valuable, than information on the subject obtained from surveys and questionnaires.

8. The applicant's wife is a qualified psychologist. They have two children now aged 12 and 10 years. The wife, who, like the applicant, came to this country from England, had been in full employment in England as a clinical psychologist but in Australia, because of the need to care for the children, had only worked on a part time basis. She was agreeable to taking part in the proposed role reversal study, which required that she should return to full time employment. The applicant felt that the experiment would be difficult to carry out in the context of the social life of the family in the city in which they live in Australia and that it would be better if the study were undertaken far from there. From enquiries made on a previous visit to England it appeared to the applicant and his wife that she would have good prospects of obtaining employment in England at a salary which, taking into account the lower cost of living in England, would be better than a higher salary which she could earn in full time employment in Australia. The applicant considered that the English midlands would have advantages in that he would be able to make arrangements to participate to some extent in activities at Warwick University and to use the resources of that University. Because he had recently had study leave with pay he considered that he did not have good prospects of being granted further paid study leave so soon. He therefore applied for and was granted twelve months leave without pay. He discussed his project with the Vice Chancellor of the University, who approved of the idea.

9. The applicant and his family travelled to England in December 1983 and the role reversal and the applicant's observations of it were carried out as planned. He kept notes of his experiences and impressions and had discussions with his wife on the subject. On about three days each week, when the children were at school, he spent some time at Warwick University doing some research and he took part also in academic discussions and seminars.

10, The applicant and his family returned to Australia in due course and he resumed his duties as Senior Lecturer in Psychology. He has been working hard to convert the material gathered to the experiment into book form but is not yet near completion of that part of the project. He said, and it seems quite right, that it is much more difficult to get a scientific work into such a form that when published it will have an impact and receive approval in the scientific world than it would be to complete a popular work such as a novel. He has not applied for an Associate Professorship. He expects to obtain comparatively little financial reward from the book when it is published.

  1. In the letter which is in evidence the Head of the School of Behavioural Sciences at the University confirmed that to succeed in obtaining an Associate Professorship a candidate must satisfy a selection committee that he or she has made a substantial contribution in his or her chosen field and that the assessment is usually made on the basis of academic publications. Papers in journals are valued, books are more valued. The Head of the School also confirmed that at the time of the experiment role reversal was an active area of interest in psychology and that publication and a good reception of the proposed work would increase the likelihood of the applicant being promoted.
  2. The question whether expenditure is deductible under s. 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act, 1936, as incurred in gaining or producing the assessable income is to be answered by determining the essential character of the expenditure. (Lunney v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1958) 100 C.L.R. 458). In every case it is question of fact or degree whether the outgoing has the necessary relation to the gaining of assessable income. (Commissioner of Taxation v. Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 C.L.R. 60, per Windeyer J. at 70; Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Forsyth [1981] HCA 15; (1980-1981) 148 C.L.R. 203, per Wilson J. at 213). There have been two High Court decisions as to the deductibility of expenses incurred by a taxpayer obtaining education or improving knowledge in his field, Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 C.L.R. 60 and Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. Hatchett [1971] HCA 47; (1971) 125 C.L.R. 494 The reasons for judgment in those cases were analysed by Helsham J. of the Supreme Court of New South Wales in Federal Commissioner of Taxation v. White 1975 A.T.C. 4018 and, having made the analysis, the learned Judge said:-

"As the result of the decision in the two cases it seems to me possible to say that expenses incurred in pursuing studies associated with employment will qualify as allowable deductions under sec. 51 when it can be said that those studies are part and parcel of the employment, which means that the expenditure is incurred in the process of carrying out the employee's duties, or, even if they are not such, they can be seen to have a direct effect on income. Where that cannot be seen, I think it will be difficult to establish the necessary connection between the study activity and the employment so as to give study expenses the character of outgoings incurred in gaining or producing the income derived from the employment.

In my view there is no sufficient association in the present case. It is not enough, as was said by Menzies J. in Hatchett's case, that the course of study relevant to his employment pursued by an employee should be likely to enable him the better to carry out his work and hence to obtain promotion, even if encouraged by his employer. It is not saying anything different if the situation be that without undertaking a course of study encouraged by his employer a person may not be able to improve his position in his employment."

13. The Tribunal accepts the applicant's evidence and accepts that the purpose of his journey to England was to carry out a study, the results of which when published would improve his prospects of promotion. As he pointed out he gave up a substantial income and incurred expense in carrying out the studies. But, although the decisions of the Courts show that the purpose of expenditure is a factor relevant to be considered when ascertaining the essential nature of expenditure claimed to be deductible under s. 51(1), there is no decision which is authority for the proposition that purpose of itself is sufficient. It is unfortunate that it will be some time before the applicant's project is completed by publication of the book and it can bear fruit. The applicant therefore cannot point to it having resulted to date in any assessable income. It was not contended, nor can it be found, that in carrying out his study, the applicant was carrying out the duties of his position at the University at which he is Senior Lecturer and, on the facts before the Tribunal, I do not think it can be found that there is such a connection between the applicant's project and the production of his assessable income as to satisfy the requirements of s. 51(1) and to entitle him to the deduction sought. The objection decision of the Commissioner must therefore be affirmed.

14. The decision of the Tribunal is that the objection decision under review be affirmed.
