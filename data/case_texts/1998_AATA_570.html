Dominic Lawrence Oliveri and Michael Gregory Jones as Trustee of the Bankrupt Estate of DL Oliveri [1998] AATA 570 (29 July 1998)

Last Updated: 31 July 1998

Administrative

Appeals

Tribunal

DECISION AND REASONS FOR DECISION

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No N94/1165

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION )

Re DOMINIC LAWRENCE OLIVERI

Applicant

And MICHAEL GREGORY JONES AS TRUSTEE OF THE BANKRUPT ESTATE OF DOMINIC LAWRENCE OLIVERI

Respondent

DECISION NO 13139

DECISION

Tribunal Mr BJ McMahon (Deputy President)

Date 29 July 1998

Place Sydney

Decision The decision under review is affirmed.

(Sgd) BJ McMahon

..............................................

Deputy President

CATCHWORDS

BANKRUPTCY - administration of property - contribution by bankrupt - bankrupt working during contribution assessment period - remuneration less than reasonable - no award or industrial agreement - whether remuneration less than reasonable amount expected by person engaged in similar employment at arms length

Bankruptcy Act 1966 ss 139J, 139K, 139Y

Re Nelson and Inspector General in Bankruptcy (1994) 35 ALD 113

Bond v Trustee of Property of Bond (A Bankrupt) [1994] FCA 1411; (1994) 34 ALD 385

REASONS FOR DECISION

29 July 1998 Deputy President BJ McMahon

1. This is an application brought pursuant to section 139ZF of the Bankruptcy Act 1966 , to review a decision of the respondent of 5 October 1994. The hearing of this review has been delayed by some appeals and other processes. The decision under review was made pursuant to section 139Y and was based upon an estimate of reasonable remuneration of $65,000 per year.

2. Section 139J sets out the objects of Division 4B. Among these objects is an intention to require a bankrupt who derives income during the bankruptcy to pay contributions towards the bankrupt's estate. The Division defines income and imposes a liability upon the bankrupt's trustee to make an assessment in relation to a contribution assessment period. Section 139K defines the latter term to mean a period beginning with the commencement of the bankruptcy, and ending annually on the anniversary of that commencement. The assessment under review is made in relation to the first contribution assessment period. No further assessments have, at this stage, been made.

3. In 1991, the applicant became involved in litigation with a Mrs Ferreri which occupied five days of a contested hearing before the Supreme Court of NSW. At the conclusion of that hearing, judgement was entered against the applicant by consent. As the judgement was not satisfied, Mrs Ferreri petitioned in the Federal Court for the sequestration of the applicant's estate in bankruptcy. In proceedings heard before Lockhart J on 23 December 1993, the applicant sought to have the hearing of that petition adjourned or stayed because of an allegation that the consensual judgement had been obtained by the fraud of Mrs Ferreri.

4. His Honour refused the application. In the course of his judgement he said:

`Having considered all the evidence to which I have referred, and indeed, the whole of the evidence before the court, I regret to say that I cannot accept the evidence of the debtor as being truthful.'

Again (at page 14) he said in relation to certain evidence concerning the alleged fraud:

`I am persuaded that his evidence is not truthful.'

5. These observations, of course, are not judicial authority for the proposition that the applicant should not be believed in any proceedings. Indeed, they have no binding effect in the proceedings before this Tribunal and, except incidentally, would not be relevant. I refer to these observations of His Honour only because they played a small part in the applicant's subsequent history and were referred to in cross examination.

6. The bankruptcy proceedings resulted in a sequestration order being made on 23 December 1993, thus fixing the commencement of the contribution assessment period. There was an interim stay granted to enable an appeal to be filed. This stay was not renewed at a later hearing on 11 January 1994.

7. The applicant is a solicitor who was admitted to the Supreme Court of NSW as such in July 1982. At the time of the sequestration order, therefore, he had held practising certificates for 11 years. Since January 1989, he had carried on a practice at 161 Palmer Street, East Sydney in a building owned by himself, two siblings and a friend, as tenants in common from offices on the ground and first floors. He also lived in this building at the time.

8. The official trustee was originally appointed to manage the bankruptcy. Shortly after the original order was made, a representative of the official trustee attended at the applicant's premises to discuss his situation. It was proposed that his legal practice be sold. An inventory was made of various files that were then current, together with furniture and other chattels attached to the practice. The trustee's representative asked the applicant to assist in finding a suitable purchaser for the practice.

9. The applicant had known Mr Di Lizio since the early 1970s when they were law students at Sydney University together. Mr Di Lizio was then a solicitor carrying on practice at Parramatta and Hurstville. It was arranged that he should buy the applicant's practice from the trustee. After some negotiation, this arrangement was concluded.

10. Mr Oliveri said in evidence that, at the time, he did not know where his future lay. He had had many conversations with Mr Di Lizio then and subsequently. Between the time that Mr Di Lizio took over the practice in February or March 1994, and the time when their association was regularised, Mr Oliveri continued to take part in the running of the practice.

11. He was aware that he would be involved in litigation in the future and considered that he could not give all of his time to productive work. He said "I was filling in time". A number of examinations under section 81 were pending. In fact, Mr Oliveri attended on each of the ten days when these examinations took place, although his own examination was only for a brief period in August. His father had commenced proceedings against the trustee, claiming to be entitled to three properties standing in Mr Oliveri's name. This litigation led to a hearing before Bryson J. Mr Oliveri knew that he would be required to participate and give evidence. None of the section 81 examinations, nor the litigation by the applicant's father, commenced until late 1994.

12. Between February or March 1994, when Mr Di Lizio took over the practice, and 1 July 1994, Mr Oliveri attended at the office regularly when he was not required by other considerations to be elsewhere. During that time, he was not paid any money by Mr Di Lizio. His evidence was that he also had no other source of income.

13. Much was made in cross examination of the fact that the applicant alleged he was without an income for some 3 or 4 months in 1994. I have no reason to doubt his word that his needs were provided for by his personal partner and by friends. Considerably greater sums were accepted as "benefactions" from family and friends in Bond v Trustee of Property of Bond (A Bankrupt) (1994)34 ALD 385. The issues in that case were concerned with whether such benefactions were income for the purposes of section 139L. There was no such issue in this case. The cross examination in the present proceedings merely sought to cast doubt upon the veracity of the applicant's account of the way in which he lived for those three or four months.

14. The assessment in the present case was not made under section 139X. Under that section, if the trustee considers that any information provided by the bankrupt is incorrect, the trustee may disregard that information and may make an assessment on the basis of what the trustee considers to be the correct information. Had the assessment been made under that section it would have been relevant to establish the truth or falsity of the evidence which Mr Oliveri gave. The assessment in question, however, is made under section 139Y which raises entirely different issues.

15. The relevant parts of that section are as follows:

`139Y (1) If:

(a) the bankrupt is engaging or has engaged during a contribution assessment period in employment or other work or in activities that resemble employment or other work; and

(b) the bankrupt does not receive or did not receive any remuneration in respect of the employment, work or activities or receives or received remuneration that is less than the remuneration (in this subsection called the "reasonable remuneration") that:

(i) in the case of employment where an industrial award or agreement prescribes rates or minimum rates of salary or wages for the employment - might reasonably be expected to be or to have been received by the bankrupt in respect of the employment by virtue of that award or agreement; or

(ii) in any other case - might reasonably be expected to be or to have been received by a person who engaged in similar employment, work or activities where there was no relationship or other connection between that person and the person for whom the employment, work or activities were carried out;

then, for the purpose of making an assessment, the trustee may determine that the bankrupt receives or received the reasonable remuneration in respect of the employment, work or activities.'

16. There was evidence before me (although not given with a great deal of confidence) that there was no industrial award or agreement prescribing rates or minimum rates of salary to be paid to an employed solicitor who had had 11 years experience. The assessment (and this review) therefore proceeded on the basis that subparagraph (b)(ii) contained the appropriate standard to be applied in arriving at an assessment of reasonable remuneration.

17. Shortly before 1 July 1994, it appeared to both Mr Oliveri and Mr Di Lizio that the situation between them had, to some extent, stabilised. Mr Oliveri gave evidence that he had never considered applying for a job with someone else. He considered that he did not have enough hours available to take on full time employment. He, therefore, asked Mr Di Lizio whether he could have a "consulting job" for $300 per week. He said that he told Mr Di Lizio "I can't do much work" because of the pending litigation and because of a number of complaints that had been made about him to the Law Society with which he felt he had to concern himself. Mr Di Lizio's recollection of their conversations was that Mr Oliveri had said words to the effect "I'd probably like to work but not that much. I'll be here - I'll help you out but don't count on me being here all the time. I'll do it for $300 per week." Mr Di Lizio remembered saying "All right we'll see how it goes".

18. The arrangement began on this basis and continued for some two years. Mr Oliveri had a full practising certificate as a sole practitioner which expired on 30 June 1994. In view of his arrangement with Mr Di Lizio, and in view of the fact that an employee's practising certificate was cheaper, he renewed it on that basis. The restrictions on his practising certificate were the standard ones, namely that he could practise as a solicitor only in the employ of someone else, and that he could not operate a trust account. The nature of the complaints that had been made to the Law Society was not made clear to me. Whatever they were, apparently no disciplinary action followed their existence nor, so far as the evidence goes, was there any objection to the renewal of the applicant's practising certificate in 1994, or for that matter, in subsequent years.

19. Throughout the whole of 1994, the applicant performed legal work appropriate to his seniority. He made court appearances in the Local, District and Supreme Courts. He conducted criminal matters, he interviewed clients, gave them advice, drafted documents, instructed counsel and supervised the work of two junior solicitors who were engaged by Mr Di Lizio and who worked in the Palmer Street office.

20. He said in evidence that he did not ask for more money because he did not think he would be available. There was no written agreement between the two solicitors. The arrangement was based on trust. Although it was generally agreed that he did not physically attend the office more than on an average of 20 hours per week, he was shown as a full time employee in the wages book. The figure of $300 was arrived at, Mr Oliveri said, as an estimate of the amount which he needed for day to day living expenses. It was not calculated with reference to his limited availability or to the value of the work he performed.

21. Although I am concerned only with the events in the first contribution assessment period, it is relevant to note that after their arrangement concluded in 1996, Mr Oliveri applied for admission as a barrister. That application was rejected in October 1996. According to Mr Oliveri, the reasons were the existence of the complaints to the Law Society and the reflections on his credit by Lockhart J. There was no objection taken by the Bar Council to his professional qualifications and capabilities. Since that rejection, he has resumed practice as a solicitor at the Palmer Street address.

22. There is no doubt that the applicant engaged in work during the contribution assessment period and that the preconditions of paragraph 139Y(a) have, therefore, been met. The only question to be determined is whether, during that period, he received remuneration which was less than the amount that might reasonably be expected to have been received by "a person who engaged in similar employment" in an arms length situation. If it was, then the amount which could reasonably be expected to have been received by that person may be regarded by the trustee as reasonable remuneration and may form the basis of the relevant assessment.

23. In Re Nelson and Inspector General in Bankruptcy 35 ALD 113 at 117 I said:

`Section 139Y(1)(b) draws a distinction between what might reasonably be expected to be or to have been received by "the bankrupt" in subpara (i), and what might reasonably be expected to be or to have been received by "a person" in subpara (ii). The second subparagraph therefore does not necessarily refer to the bankrupt but refers to a hypothetical person engaged in similar employment work, or activities in an arm's length situation. In my view, however, it is necessary to pay some regard to the particular circumstances of the bankrupt in order that the expectation can be seen to be reasonable.'

24. Following the above passage, I identified certain factors personal to Mr Nelson which inhibited his capacity to engage in employment. He was aged 58, an undischarged bankrupt, had unsuccessfully sought other employment and, most importantly, had restrictions imposed on his practising certificate limiting the sort of work that would be available to him.

25. Mr Oliveri was born in 1956 and was thus aged 37 at the commencement of the contribution assessment period. He held a full practising certificate for the first six months of that period and an ordinary employee's practising certificate for the second half which was not subject to any extraordinary limitation or condition. He had never applied for any other position and therefore could not say whether he would have been successful in his application. The only unusual feature pointed to by the applicant as impacting on his ability to earn was the demands which he anticipated would be made upon him by litigation in the contribution assessment period so that he felt he was unable to attend the office more than on an average of 20 hours per week.

26. Evidence was given as to the state of the market for employed solicitors at the time. An affidavit of Ms Warnecke was tendered in evidence. She was not required for cross examination. She was the principal of a recruitment agency dealing in all levels of placement for Australian lawyers in the Sydney region. In July 1995 she produced a salary survey which related to incomes of lawyers in NSW. This survey was based upon information provided to her from over 250 solicitors whom she had interviewed and who were registered with her firm during the period July 1994 to June 1995, or who were placed by her firm during the same period irrespective of the date of their registration. Only solicitors then working in private practice in the Sydney CBD were included.

27. The range of salaries varied with the size of firms and with the length of experience of the interviewed solicitors. For those of seven or more years of experience, the lowest salary was $71,500, the highest $100,000 and the average salary $83,000 in large firms. Similar figures for medium firms were $54,000, $92,000 and $76,500. For small firms, the range was $52,000, $104,000 and $78,000.

28. Evidence was also given of a survey compiled by Mahlab Recruitment during the period July 1994 to December 1994. For solicitors with 10 or more years experience, the evidence from this survey was that a salary between $65,000 and $140,000 could be expected from a small size firm and a medium sized firm, and from $80,000 to $150,000 in a large firm.

29. Evidence was given by way of affidavit and orally by Mr Brown. He had been a partner in the firm which acted for Mrs Ferreri and which subsequently acted for the trustee. Mr Jones was appointed trustee on 15 April 1994. At the time he gave his evidence, however, and for some time previously, Mr Brown was not a partner in that firm but in an entirely different firm. Although reference was made to his background in cross examination, I accept his present status and past experience as qualifying him to give disinterested opinion evidence on the matters relevant to the present issues. He was admitted to practice in 1972 and was a partner of the city law firm of Law and Milne from 1974 to approximately 1981. From that date until 1985 he was the principal of the firm Alan Brown and Company which merged in 1985 to form Cohen Brown, which became in 1981 the firm of McCabe Brown. He had been managing partner since 1982 and part of his responsibilities was concerned with the employment of solicitors, law clerks and paralegals. He was involved in those responsibilities during the whole of the contribution assessment period. He was asked to give his opinion as to the reasonable remuneration as defined by section 139Y.

30. He commenced from the objective evidence provided by Ms Warnecke's firm and from Mahlab. This evidence established an appropriate range which he took to be between $52,000 and $104,000 per year. This was based upon the applicant's seniority and capacity. Mr Brown said in evidence that he also took into account matters that were personal to Mr Oliveri. He took into account the fact that he was an undischarged bankrupt, that there were complaints that had been made about him to the Law Society and that reflections on his truthfulness had been contained in the judgement of Lockhart J. A hypothetical employer would have been concerned with these matters and would have taken them into account. In Mr Brown's opinion, however, they would not have been determinative of the applicant's eligibility for employment, although they may have had a dampening effect on the level of salary. For these reasons Mr Brown considered a reasonable remuneration to be $65,000 per annum which is below the middle of the range which he had adopted as the standard.

31. When the negotiations for the sale of the practice took place, a list of current files was prepared. This indicates the range of matters with which Mr Oliveri was then dealing as a sole practitioner and indicates the level of his expertise. Among the criminal matters in which he was acting for the defendant were charges of a conspiracy to murder, procuring a benefit by deception, indecent assault, importing heroin, drug trafficking, driving in a manner dangerous and so on. The list of matters in civil litigation contained a fair number of industrial accident cases and motor vehicle accident cases as well as a partnership dispute, an insurance property claim, a breach of contract and family law matters. There were also matters involving conveyancing and financing transactions and matters carried out as a city agent. Mr Oliveri was also a notary public and charged for notarial certification in certain cases.

32. To Mr Brown, this list indicated a range of experience and competence that would have been valuable in the employment market. Experience in industrial accident cases, in particular, he said, opens up valuable opportunities in that area. He was not concerned with amounts which the applicant had previously earned as shown in his income tax returns. In my view, this is the correct approach. Mr Brown said he focussed more on what an employer would consider relevant in the market. I see no reason why I should not accept this evidence. No evidence was called by the applicant to refute it. Mr Brown's evidence was tested in cross examination.

33. I do not accept that the applicant was limited to 20 hours of attendance at the office per week. No amount of litigation in that first contribution assessment period could have accounted for such absences. In my view, it is not relevant to consider the applicant as a potential part time employee in applying the criterion of subparagraph 139Y(b)(ii) to the hypothetical person contemplated by that paragraph. Although, as I said in Nelson, it is necessary to pay some regard to the circumstances of the bankrupt, the test ultimately is not to determine what the bankrupt would (or even could) have earned. The test to be applied is an objective one, tempered only by such considerations that might render an expectation reasonable.

34. In my view, the evidence of the respondent clearly supports the assessment. No evidence from the applicant seriously attempted to dispute it. Accordingly the decision under review is affirmed.
