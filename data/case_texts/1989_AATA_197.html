QT86/149-150 and Commissioner for Taxation [1989] AATA 197; (1989) 20 ATR 3966; 89 ATC 758 (25 August 1989)

Last Updated: 16 June 2009

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

Income Tax - Allowable deductions - loan taken out to facilitate payment of a transfer value - effect of ensuring eligibility for retirement benefits - whether interest on loan was outgoing incurred in course of gaining assessable income - whether the payments were of a capital or revenue nature.

Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Act 1973 - s.69

Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 - s.51(1)

FCT v. Smith 81 ATC 4114

Case J23 77 ATC 220

Case P130 82 ATC 651

Case K52 78 ATC 511

Case 018 83 ATC 63

FCT v. Markey 89 ATC 4600

FCT v. Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153

Re :

APPLICANT

And: COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION

RESPONDENT

REASONS FOR DECISION

Before: Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member)

Date: 25 August 1989

Place: Brisbane

The question at issue is whether interest incurred on a loan taken out to facilitate certain amounts paid by the applicant under the Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Act 1973 ("the DFRB Act") is an allowable deduction within the terms of sub-section 51(1) of the Income Tax Assessment Act 1936 ("the Act"). A second issue concerns a claim for a deduction of $121 in respect of a track suit and running shoes. The respondent's representative conceded the claim before the Tribunal.

2. Essentially, and so far as is relevant, sub-section 51(1) provides for a deduction of losses and outgoings incurred in the course of gaining or producing assessable income but excludes from deduction outgoings of capital or of a capital nature.

  1. The applicant is an officer of the Australian Army, born in 1940 and therefore will turn 50 years of age in 1990. However, the applicant has not always been in the Australian Army. He resigned from the British Army in 1977 and immediately joined the Australian Army. Certain payments were made to the applicant by the British Army on the termination of his commission in that Army.
  2. Upon joining the Australian Army the applicant was concerned to ensure that he had full rights under the DFRB Act. Without going into the detailed workings of that Act it is sufficient to note section 69 which provides for a transfer value to be paid where a contributing member so elects. Such a payment has the effect of increasing the effective service of a contributing member in respect of whom a transfer value has been paid. The applicant was concerned to make such a payment so as to ensure he was eligible for retirement benefits under the DFRB Act when he attained 50 years of age (assuming he was a Lieut. Colonel at the time) or 47 years of age (Captain or Major).

5. Initially it seems it was thought that the payments made by the British Army would be sufficient in amount to constitute the necessary payment of transfer value but that proved not to be the case. The matter was not resolved expeditiously and substantial delays began to occur in the applicant's dealings with the Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority ("the Authority") established under the DFRB Act. The authority appears to have had considerable difficulty in dealing with the applicant's request that it determine the transfer value and the amount of service to be credited. The resulting substantial delays which occurred were detrimental to the applicant's financial position.

  1. That deterioration in his financial position caused the applicant to seek to make the payment of the transfer value by instalments. That proposal was rejected by the Authority. The applicant then applied to this Tribunal for review of the Authority's refusal to allow him time to pay the transfer value. That application was unsuccessful insofar as the applicant sought to pay the transfer value by instalments but was successful in that the Tribunal directed that the period for electing to pay the transfer value be extended to 1 July 1981. The Tribunal handed down its decision in July 1980.
  2. After three years the Authority determined that the transfer value was an amount of $18,973 rather than an amount of approximately $8,000 as had previously been intimated.
  3. The transfer value determined by the Authority was paid by the applicant. Payment was facilitated by a loan obtained by the applicant from the Commonwealth Bank for that purpose. The payment of the transfer value achieved 6 years 120 days prior service credit for the purposes of the DFRB Act and thereby created the pension entitlements under that Act in the event of normal service retirement.

9. The applicant's claim is for a deduction in respect of interest charges incurred by the applicant to the bank in respect of the loan used to pay the transfer value. He does not seek a deduction in respect of the transfer value itself – a fact which seems to have been misunderstood by the respondent. For the years ended 30 June 1982 and 30 June 1983 the amount claimed is $1,275 in respect of each year.

  1. The applicant's case is quite straightforward. He submitted that the transfer value was paid to ensure that he had the right to receive an annual pension when he retired from the Army in 1990. The bank interest was therefore incurred for the purpose of producing assessable income.
  2. The respondent's representative submitted that the interest outgoings were not incurred in the course of gaining assessable income and in any event were payments of a capital nature. Those submissions are in essence the same as the submissions made by the respondent in FCT v. Smith 81 ATC 4114 and which were rejected by the High Court at page 4117 in these words:

"In our opinion both of these submissions must fail in the light of the principles established by the earlier decisions of this Court: W. Nevill and Co. Ltd. v. F.C. of T. [1937] HCA 9; (1937) 56 C.L.R. 290 at p.305; Ronpibon Tin N.L. & Tongkah Compound N.L. v. F.C. of T. [1949] HCA 15; (1949) 78 C.L.R. 47 at pp.56-57; Charles Moore & Co. (W.A.) Pty. Ltd. v. F.C. of T. [1956] HCA 77; (1956) 95 C.L.R. 344 at p.351; F.C. of T. v. Finn [1961] HCA 61; (1961) 106 C.L.R. 60. In Charles Moore which was a case where employees of the taxpayer were robbed at gunpoint as they took the previous day's takings to the bank, the judgment of the Court contained these words:

'But unfortunately it [i.e. the robbery] is still a familiar and recognised hazard and there could be little doubt that if it had been insured against the premium would have formed an allowable deduction. Phrases like the foregoing or the phrase "incidental and relevant" when used in relation to the allowability of losses as deductions do not refer to the frequency, expectedness or likelihood of their occurrence or the antecedent risk of their being incurred, but to their nature or character. What matters is their connection with the operations which more directly gain or produce the assessable income.' (At p.351.)

The section does not require that the purpose of the expenditure shall be the gaining of the income of that year, so long as it was made in the given year and is incidental and relevant to the operations or activities regularly carried on for the production of income. What is incidental and relevant in the sense mentioned falls to be determined not by reference to the certainty or likelihood of the outgoing resulting in the generation of income but to its nature and character and generally to its connection with the operations which more directly gain or produce the assessable income. It is true that the payment of the premium in June 1978 did not result in the generation of any income in that year, but there is a sufficient connection between the purchase of the cover against the loss of ability to earn and the consequent earning of assessable income to bring the premium within the first limb of sec. 51(1). Likewise the periodic nature of the payment and other provisions in the policy which contemplate its renewal from year to year militate against its characterisation as an outgoing of a capital nature. Nor is there any reason whereby it could assume a domestic or private nature."

12. The respondent also relied upon decisions of Taxation Boards of Review reported as:

Case J23 77 ATC 220

Case P130 82 ATC 651

Case K52 78 ATC 511 to which can be added:

Case 018 83 ATC 63

FCT v. Markey 89 ATC 4600.

  1. It is the essence of each of these decisions that they deal with claims for deduction of contributions to a superannuation fund. In each case the decision was that such contributions are not deductible within the terms of sub-section 51(1). None of the decisions deals with the deductibility of interest charges incurred in the circumstances of the present case.
  2. More relevantly the respondent's representative relied upon the decision of the High Court in FCT v. Munro [1926] HCA 58; (1926) 38 CLR 153 where the High Court had to consider whether interest incurred in respect of borrowed moneys was an allowable deduction. The borrowed moneys although secured on a rent producing property were used as to €20,000 for 20,000 shares in a company and as to £10,000 as an interest free loan to the same company. Of the 20,000 shares only 2,000 were retained by the taxpayer, the balance being gifted to his sons. The Court held that the interest had not been wholly and exclusively laid out or expended for the purpose of producing assessable income having been laid out in large part for the purpose of benefiting the taxpayer's sons. As will be apparent the statutory provision applied a stricter test than that applied by sub-section 51(1).
  3. It was somewhat faintly suggested that because the applicant's widow or children could benefit under the DFRB Act in the event of the applicant's premature death that the claim was not attributable solely to his future assessable income. I think that suggestion overlooks the fact that the transfer value was paid for the purpose of ensuring that the applicant became eligible for an annual pension when he turned 50 years of age and was not paid for and did not need to be paid for any other purpose. It was not suggested that such an annual pension was other than assessable income.
  4. As Munro's Case shows it is essential to determine the purpose for which the borrowed moneys are applied. In the present case the borrowed moneys were applied for one purpose only. That was to ensure the applicant was entitled to the annual pension when he retired. In essence then to finance the purchase of an annuity. The borrowed funds were applied to purchase a right to future income payable subject to certain contingencies. Although the moneys applied to pay the transfer value were paid on capital account the interest charges incurred in borrowing the funds were on revenue account.
  5. I am satisfied that the interest charges incurred by the applicant were incurred for the purpose of gaining or producing his assessable income and were not of a capital nature.
  6. The objection decisions will be set aside and the objections allowed.

This and the preceding seven (7) pages are a true and correct copy of the Decision and Reasons of Mr K L Beddoe (Senior Member) in the matters of QT88/149-150.

Secretary 25/8/89
