The Tribunal:

(a) refuses to grant leave for Sandoz to rely on the Settlement Agreement and associated confidential documents;

(b) refuses to authorise Sandoz to disclose the confidential annexures to the Sharkey affidavit;

(c) grants the issue of the summons requested by the Applicants excluding category (f);

(d) authorises the issue of summons requested by Lundbeck amended in accordance with these reasons.

CATCHWORDS

PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE – proceedings – application for leave to rely on Settlement Agreement and associated confidential documents – applications for issue of summons –obligation of confidentiality – orders made

CASES

H Lundbeck A/S v Sandoz Pty Ltd (2011) 124 ALD 219; [2011] NSWSC 1297

Richards v Kadian (2005) 64 NSWLR 204; [2005] NSWCA 328

Science Research Council v Nasse [1979] UKHL 9; [1980] AC 1028



REASONS FOR DECISION



The Hon Brian Tamberlin, QC, Deputy President and Dr T Nicoletti, Member





10 May 2012

  1. The Applicants seek review of a decision of 1 June 2011 made by a delegate of the Commissioner of Patents (the delegate) in relation to an application by H. Lundbeck A/S (Lundbeck) for an extension of time to file an application for an extension of the term of patent number 623144 (the patent) (Lundbeck Application) pursuant to s 223(2)(a) of the Patents Act 1990 (the Act).
  2. The delegate gave reasons for the decision which permitted the patentee to file an application under s 70 for an extension of term for a pharmaceutical substance which was based on the marketing approval of CIPRAMIL. In her reasons for decision at [63] and [64] the delegate said:
[63] Based on this evidence, I accept that the patentee genuinely believed on 26 July 1999 that a section 70 extension of term application could not be based on the racemate, CIPRAMIL. Further, the patentee’s involvement in the long and complex court proceedings is clear evidence that they continued to hold this belief until at least 11 June 2009 when the Full Federal Court handed down their decision.

[64] The patentee’s misunderstanding of the law clearly caused them to decide not to file an extension of term application based on CIPRAMIL. I am satisfied that this is a causative error which would enliven the provisions of section 223.

(Emphasis Added)
  1. The Applicants seek to challenge the findings that Lundbeck genuinely believed in July 1999 that a s 70 extension of term application could not be based on the racemate, CIPRAMIL, and that the patentee continued to hold the belief until at least 11 June 2009, when the Full Federal Court handed down a decision on the matter, and it disputes that Lundbeck misunderstood the relevant law.
  2. The Applicants have also applied to the Tribunal for the issue of a summons for the production of documents by Lundbeck. The documents sought to be produced are set out in Attachment A to the summons. Documents covered under categories (a) to (e) have been produced by Lundeck to the Applicants’ solicitors. Lundbeck says that there are no documents answerable to category (g). The Applicants press that the summons should be issued retaining category (g) in the terms that appear in Attachment A and this is not opposed by Lundbeck. The issue in dispute is category (f) which seeks the production of:
any agreements between Lundbeck and any other party in relation to:

(i) the importation or sale in Australia, or possible importation or sale in Australia (by a person other than Lundbeck or Lundbeck Australia Pty Ltd) of a product containing escitalopram, including the settlement agreement between Lundbeck and Sandoz Pty; and

(ii) AU Patent 623144.

  1. The Settlement Agreement referred to in category (f) which resulted in Federal Court proceedings commenced by Sandoz seeking revocation of the patent being discontinued, is also the subject of a separate application made by Sandoz. Sandoz seeks leave of the Tribunal for disclosure and use in the proceedings of material of a confidential nature set out in a Settlement Agreement between itself and Lundbeck together with other documents annexed to an affidavit dated 22 February 2012 made by James Alexander Sharkey (the Sharkey affidavit). Mr Sharkey is the current Legal Director Australia and New Zealand for AstraZeneca Pty Ltd and was formerly employed by Sandoz in the capacity of Head of Business Development & Licensing, Asia Pacific Region.
  2. Sandoz seeks an order from the Tribunal granting it leave to rely on the confidential annexures to that affidavit in the Tribunal proceedings for a review of the decision to extend time. In the alternative, Sandoz seeks an order granting leave to disclose the confidential annexures to that affidavit to such persons and on such terms as may be authorised by the Tribunal.
  3. Sandoz and the other applicants contend that the information contained in the documents could support their contention by providing affirmative evidence and will assist in their review application.
  4. In the Settlement Agreement between Lundbeck and Sandoz there are clauses requiring the parties to keep confidential the terms of the agreement.
  5. In proceedings brought in the Supreme Court of NSW in H Lundbeck A/S v Sandoz Pty Ltd (2011) 124 ALD 219; [2011] NSWSC 1297 Brereton J decided that the Settlement Agreement of February 2007 was the subject of enforceable confidentiality provisions and he restrained Sandoz from disclosing any of the terms or any part of the settlement terms to any person whatsoever, but expressly stated that the order did not prohibit the disclosure of the Settlement Agreement to this Tribunal or such persons as it may authorise.
  6. In his judgment at [15] and [16] His Honour said:
[15] Sandoz has issued a summons for production in the AAT proceedings. The summons calls for the product of, inter alia, the settlement deed. No doubt when the deed is produced to the AAT in answer to that summons – assuming that the summons is not in the meantime set aside – the AAT will decide, having heard argument, whether access should be permitted to it pending the determination of the AAT proceedings. Lundbeck agrees that this court ought not interfere with that process and, accordingly, such order as I may make will be limited in a way which leaves open to the AAT to determine how the deed may be used for the purposes of the proceedings before it.

[16] The perceived risk of disclosure which primarily concerns Lundbeck is to the firm of solicitors Mallesons Stephen Jacques (Mallesons) and to the Commissioner for Patents, who is a party in the AAT proceedings, in which Mallesons act not only for Sandoz, but also for Alphapham and the other generic pharmaceutical manufacturers. At one stage it seems to have been contemplated that some undertaking might have been proffered on behalf of Mallesons not to use the deed, if provided to them, other than in the interests of Sandoz, nor further to disclose it. Ultimately no such undertaking was proffered, but in any event it seems to me that it would have left Mallesons in a most awkward position. With the best intentions in the world, it is impossible to see how, armed with the information contained in that deed, in the interests of Sandoz – while acting concurrently for Alphapharm and the other generics – Mallesons could have avoided determining strategies, negotiations and tactics, without at least inadvertently taking into account, on behalf of and or the benefit of all their clients, what they took into account on behalf of and for the benefit of Sandoz. The observations of Drummond J in Carindale Country Club Estate Pty Ltd v Astill and Others [1993] FCA 218; (1993) 42 FCR 307 at 313; [1993] FCA 218; 115 ALR 112 at 119 are pertinent on this point:

... it has long been recognised that a solicitor who, with the best will in the world, is determined not to make use of one client’s confidential information for the benefit of another client may still subconsciously draw on that information to the disadvantage of the former.

  1. In Science Research Council v Nasse [1979] UKHL 9; [1980] AC 1028, the House of Lords considered the relevant principles. That was a discovery case. At 1066 Lord Wilberforce noted that the relevant test is whether disclosure of confidential terms is necessary for disposing fairly of the proceedings. If it is, then discovery must be ordered notwithstanding confidentiality. Where the court finds that there is a need to preserve confidentiality it will consider carefully whether the necessary information has been or can be obtained by some other means not involving a breach of confidence. He said that a court or tribunal faced with such an issue should inspect the documents. He emphasised that there is a broad discretion. Lord Salmon agreed that the question is whether disclosure is necessary for fairly disposing of the proceedings. If the answer to that question is in the affirmative then disclosure should be ordered notwithstanding the documents’ confidentiality. His Lordship considered at 1072 that in any event it was essential for the tribunal to examine the documents in question to determine this issue.
  2. Their Lordships stated that the question must be approached with an open mind without any preconceived notion or predisposition to preserving confidentiality.
  3. A similar approach was taken by the NSW Court of Appeal in Richards v Kadian (2005) 64 NSWLR 204; [2005] NSWCA 328 at [86] – [87]. In that case the Court agreed that there was necessarily a balancing exercise involved in determining whether an obligation of confidence can be insisted upon in circumstances where there is an interference with the administration of justice. The balancing exercise will depend on the nature of the confidential information, the public interest said to be affected and whether there is any other public interests consideration.
  4. In this application there are two competing public interests. There is a public interest in ensuring the proper administration of justice and there is also a powerful public interest in ensuring that provisions in settlement agreements as to confidentiality are duly enforced in the interest of encouraging the efficient settlement of disputes.

SUBMISSIONS

  1. Sandoz says it is necessary and should be permitted to employ in the proceedings in this Tribunal, particular provision of the Settlement Agreement in order to dispute the delegate’s conclusion that Lundbeck did not realise, until the Full Federal Court handed down its decision on 11 June 2009, that it ought to have made its application for an extension of patent term based on the CIPRAMIL product rather than on the product known as LEXAPRO. Sandoz says that certain terms of the Settlement Agreement will assist it to establish that Lundbeck was aware at a much earlier date and that the delegate’s decision was procured on evidence which did not accurately reflect Lundbeck’s actual state of knowledge. The delegate’s decision to grant an extension of time was a matter of discretion. The absence of candour on the part of Lundbeck, according to Sandoz, is an important consideration in the review as to whether or not an extension of time should be granted. The crux of this evidence they say relates directly to the due administration of justice by the Tribunal on this issue. The Applicants refer to Lundbeck seeking to use a “cloak” of confidentiality to shield itself from having raised against it actual evidence of its state of knowledge and bearing on its understanding of the true position concerning the necessity or desirability of making an extension application at an earlier point in time.

REASONING

  1. The Tribunal has examined the terms of the Settlement Agreement and associated confidential documents. It has heard submissions from the Applicants and Lundbeck (some of the oral submissions took place in the absence of the Mr Murray and his instructing solicitor, and the Respondent was excused from attendance).
  2. This is not a case in which confidential documents are the only evidence before the Tribunal to point to an argument that Lundbeck was aware of the availability or possibility of making a different application at an earlier period of time.
  3. There is evidence before the Tribunal in the form of a witness statement by Ms Kim O’Connell of 8 March 2012 to the effect that Lundbeck had been advised to file an extension of time application in 2005 by its patent attorneys, Watermark Patent and Trade Mark Attorneys (Watermark), who stated that an appropriate defensive strategy was to file an application based on the registration of CIPRAMIL.
  4. In the light of this material, and giving due weight to the first and second covenants in the Settlement Agreement, the Tribunal does not consider that the necessity or desirability of disclosure in the present case carries sufficient weight to warrant the authorisation of a breach of the confidentiality provisions of the Settlement Agreement and this conclusion extends to other documents referring to the terms of the settlement.
  5. This is a case in which the ground as to the belief and understanding of Lundbeck can be ventilated without the necessity of disclosing the material.
  6. The Tribunal is not persuaded that leave should be granted or that disclosure should be made at this stage of the proceeding. It may, however, be that at some more advanced stage of the proceedings an attempt may be made to reconsider this question and that can be dealt with at the appropriate time. On the material presently available to it the Tribunal does not grant leave to permit use of or reliance on the confidential provisions.
  7. As to the alternative orders sought for disclosure of the documents to parties other than Sandoz the application is refused for the reasons given above.
  8. In relation to the summons the Applicants seek to have issued to Lundbeck, category (f) was characterised by Lundbeck as essentially an application for the Settlement Agreement. For the reasons given above the Tribunal grants the issue of the summons excluding category (f).
  9. Also during the interlocutory hearing questions were raised as to the issue of several summonses at the request of Lundbeck. The Tribunal notes that as to one of these concerning Gary Baxter Cox of Wrays – Patent and Trade Mark Attorneys, the application to issue the summons was withdrawn. In the case of the other summonses addressed to each of the Applicants, more limited time periods were agreed upon in relation to the documents limiting the range to the period between 1 July 2005 and 16 June 2009. Leave is granted to issue the summonses amended in accordance with the above on the basis that any claims to privilege be considered at a later date.

I certify that the preceding 24 (twenty four) paragraphs are a true copy of the reasons for the decision herein of The Hon Brian Tamberlin, QC, Deputy President and Dr T Nicoletti, Member.
