Copperart Pty Ltd v the Commissioner of Taxation of the Commonwealth of Australia [1994] FCA 1038; (1994) 94 Atc 4259, (1994) Aust Sales Tax Cases 85-186 (22 April 1994)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

COPPERART PTY LTD v. THE COMMISSIONER OF TAXATION OF THE COMMONWEALTH OF

AUSTRALIA

Nos. G777 and 778 of 1993

FED No. 216/94

Number of pages - 16

Sales Tax - Practice

(1994) 94 ATC 4259, (1994) Aust Sales Tax Cases 85-186

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

NEW SOUTH WALES DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

DAVIES(1), GUMMOW(2) AND FRENCH(3) JJ

CATCHWORDS

Sales Tax - liability to taxation - operation of Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 6) (1930)(Cth), ss.4 and 4A - whether the Commissioner gave notice "specifying that an amount of tax is payable" in Sales Tax Procedure Act 1934 (Cth), s.12B(3) - approach to assessment of value where importer and purchaser not at arm's length - whether two assessments were inconsistent - effect of inconsistency.

Practice - Administrative Appeals Tribunal - duty of AAT to decide material facts and issues.

Stevenson v. Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 29 FCR 282

Krew v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 71 ATC 4213

Case X45 (1990) 90 ATC 364

SYDNEY, 16 February 1994

22:4:1994

Counsel for the appellant: D.H. Bloom QC

with J de Wijn

Solicitors for the appellant: Mallesons Stephen Jaques

Counsel for the respondent: G. Nettle QC

Solicitor for the respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

1. Leave be granted for the filing of the amended notices of appeal.

2. The appeals be allowed.

3. The orders of the trial Judge be set aside and that there be substituted
therefor the following orders:

(ii) That the matter be remitted to the

Administrative

either with or without the hearing of further

that such part of the sales tax assessed under

s.4(2) and s.4A of the Sales Tax Assessment Act

(No. 6) 1930 as related to transactions, acts

or operations effected or done in relation to

any goods before 9 November 1984 must be

remitted by the Commissioner of Taxation

pursuant to s.12B(1) of the Sales Tax Procedure

(iii) The Respondent pay the Applicant's costs of the

DECISION

DAVIES J: These are appeals from the decision of a Judge of this Court (Hill J). Before his Honour were two appeals brought by Copperart Pty Limited ("Copperart") from decisions of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal ("the Tribunal").

2. The issues concern the amount of sales tax payable by Copperart in respect of copper and brass goods imported by it into Australia and sold by it during the period 1 April 1982 to 30 November 1986. The liability arose under the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 6) (1930) (Cth) ("the Act)". Copperart sold goods as a wholesaler, principally to its franchisees. Copperart also had a wholly owned subsidiary, Laddeton Nominees Pty Limited ("Laddeton"), which purchased goods from Copperart and sold them at retail outlets.

3. The original notices of appeal were drawn as appeals from so much of the judgment of the primary Judge as contained a certain finding. Section 24 of the Federal Court of Australia Act 1976 ("the Federal Court Act") limits the appellate jurisdiction of this Court to hearing and determining, so far as is presently relevant, an appeal from a judgment. That term is defined in s.4 of the Federal Court Act to mean "a judgment, decree or order ... or a sentence." In this connection the word "judgment" refers only to operative judicial acts and is not used as a convenient abbreviation for reasons for judgment: Landsal Pty Ltd (In Liq.) v. REI Building Society [1993] FCA 121; (1993) 41 FCR 421 at 424-5. The reason for the expression of the notices of appeal in this way perhaps was that, on the face of the orders made by the primary Judge, Copperart, the present appellant, was successful in having the matters remitted to the Tribunal. The complaint was with certain of the grounds supporting the orders made and with some of the consequences thereof. Leave should therefore be granted for the filing of the amended notices of appeal which counsel has proposed in which the appeals are, in effect, brought against so much of the orders made by the Court on 17 September 1993 as depend upon the finding that, by letter dated 19 April 1985, the respondent ("the Commissioner") was entitled to require payment of sales tax by Copperart for the purposes of the Sales Tax Procedure Act 1934 ("the Procedure Act"); cf Driclad Pty Ltd v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation [1968] HCA 91; (1968) 121 CLR 45 at 64-65.

4. In accordance with ss.7 and 9 of the Act, Copperart lodged monthly returns setting out the required information as to the sale values of goods sold by it and it paid tax upon those sale values. The sale values returned were those specified in s.4(1) of the Act, namely "the amount for which those goods are sold". Section 9 imposed an obligation upon Copperart to "pay sales tax upon that sale value".

5. In 1985, the Commissioner took the view that the sale values should be altered under ss.4(2) or 4A of the Act. Firstly, the Commissioner took the view that, as the goods sold to Laddeton were sold at lower prices than the goods sold to the franchisees, their sale values should be amended to reflect the amount for which the goods could "reasonably be expected to have been sold if the importer and the purchaser had been dealing with each other at arms length". Copperart, for its part, alleged that the differences in prices were due to such factors as greater volume and lower handling costs, factors which were ordinarily reflected in the price in an open market situation.

6. A second issue arose from the fact that the franchisees paid to Copperart sums based upon the turnover of each franchisee's store or stores. The first payment was called "an advertising contribution" and was agreed to be 7% of monthly turnover. In consideration of this sum, Copperart arranged for the delivery of catalogues to householders in the vicinity of stores and conducted all advertising, including advertising in the localities where retail stores existed. A second payment was a fee of 4% of turnover payable pursuant to an arrangement whereby Copperart was to implement a program to ensure a consistent physical image in the franchised stores, to develop uniform marketing strategies, to achieve a better training of staff and to maintain proper store supervision. The 4% of turnover was alleged by Copperart to represent its realistic costs on an annual basis of providing the additional services which it provided. The Commissioner considered that these payments, calculated by reference to turnover, should be included in the sale values.

7. Under the Act, the Commissioner had a duty to issue an assessment when he considered that the sale values of the goods should be altered in accordance with s.4(1), s.4(2) or s.4A. The Commissioner was satisfied that Copperart had returned, in its monthly returns, the amounts for which it had sold its goods. However, the Commissioner was of the view that the sale values should be altered under s.4(2) or s.4A.

8. It is not in dispute that Copperart and Laddeton were not at arm's length. Section 4(2) provided that, where the importer and the purchaser were not dealing with each other at arm's length, then if the Commissioner was of the opinion that the goods could reasonably be expected to have been sold at a higher price, the Commissioner should alter the sale values of the goods to accord with the price at which the goods could reasonably be expected to have been sold had the parties been dealing at arm's length. Section 4(3) provided that "the sale value so altered shall be the sale value of the goods for the purposes of this Act."

9. Section 4A dealt with the following circumstance:-

"(1) Where -

...

(b) under an agreement entered into for the

purpose, or for purposes that included the

purpose, of securing that the amount of the

sale value of the relevant goods would be

less than the amount that could reasonably

be expected to be the amount of the sale

value of the relevant goods if the

agreement had not been entered into,

valuable consideration (in this section

referred to as the `relevant

consideration') has been given, directly or

indirectly, by the purchaser, or by another

person, to the importer or another person

for, or in connection with, any of, or any

2 or more of, the following acts:

(i) the grant of a right or option to purchase goods;

(ii) the exercise, in whole or in part, of a

right or option to purchase goods;

(iii) the surrender or other termination, in whole or in

part, of a right or option to purchase goods;

(iv) allowing a right or option to

purchase goods to lapse in whole or

in part;

(v) the assignment, in whole or in part, of a

right or option to purchase goods;

(vi) the provision of, or procuring the

provision of, services in connection

with the relevant goods ...

..."

Where such a circumstance occurred and the Commissioner was satisfied that the goods were sold at a price less than that for which the goods could reasonably be expected to have been sold if the parties had been dealing with each other at arm's length, then the Commissioner was required by s.4A(1) and (5) to alter the sale value to an amount equal to the arm's length price and, under s.4A(5), "the sale value so altered shall be the sale value of the relevant goods for the purposes of this Act".

10. It can be seen that s.4(2) and s.4A empowered the Commissioner to substitute for the actual price for which the goods had been sold the value which, in the Commissioner's opinion, represented a price at which the goods could reasonably be expected to have been sold if the importer and the purchaser had been dealing at arm's length. I think that is a sufficient description of the sections for present purposes.

11. On 19 April 1985, the Commissioner wrote to Copperart in these terms, inter alia:-

"With regard to arms-length wholesale sales to franchisees,

tax is payable on the amount for which the goods are sold. In

this connection, a promotional fee and a franchise service

fee, both of which relate to the sale of specific goods and

which are components of the price that the franchisee is

required to pay for those goods, form part of the taxable sale

value on which sales tax is payable.

In relation to non-arms length wholesale sales to Laddeton

Nominees Pty Ltd, tax is also payable on the price for which

the goods are sold, subject to the formation of an opinion by

the Commissioner that such amount is lower than the arms-length

price, ie the price for which similar goods are sold in

similar circumstances to an arms-length customer (eg a

franchisee).

You are required to lodge a supplementary return for the

period 1 April 1982 to 28 February 1985 and make payment of

the tax due thereon in respect of:

a. promotional fees and franchise service fees charged to

franchisees/and omitted from the taxable sale value of

the goods; and

b. the difference between the price charged to Laddeton

Nominees Pty Ltd for goods sold and the arms-length

price of those goods having regard to prices charged to

independent franchisees, including the promotional fees

and franchise service fees.

The return together with a remittance for the tax payable

thereon should be lodged at this office within 21 days."

12. Thereafter, there was correspondence and discussions between the parties. On 7 November 1987, two assessments issued. One was an assessment under s.4(2) which dealt with the goods which had been sold to Laddeton during the period 1 April 1982 to 2 August 1985 and which increased the sale values of the goods sold to accord with the prices at which similar goods had been sold to the franchisees. The other assessment dealt with all the goods sold to Laddeton and to the franchisees during the period 1 April 1982 to 30 November 1986 and was expressed as an assessment under s.4A. Its effect was to add the 7% and 4% turnover fees to the price at which the goods had been sold. The result, as set out in a letter which incorporated the two assessments, was that an aggregate amount of $937,303.39 became payable.

13. Needless to say, the assessments were misconceived. Both s.4(2) and s.4A required the Commissioner to form an opinion as to the prices at which the goods could reasonably have been expected to have been sold if the parties had been dealing at arm's length. It was an error for the Commissioner to make an assessment under s.4(2) arriving at one arm's length price and, at the same time, to make an assessment under s.4A arriving at a different arm's length price.

14. Moreover, neither s.4(2) nor s.4A empowered the Commissioner simply to add in an element of which he was aware. If a percentage fee should be added in because it truly formed part of the price, then a sale price could be adjusted to accord with s.4(1). However, both s.4(2) and s.4A required the Commissioner to form a view as to the arm's length price and it was that price which became the sale value. That price would not necessarily have a direct relationship to the constituents of the price or other financial arrangements agreed to by the importer and the purchaser, though those matters were relevant.

15. The first issue arises under s.12B of the Procedure Act, which provided inter alia:-

"(1) Where tax (not being tax paid prior to the commencement

of this section) in respect of any transaction, act or

operation, effected or done in relation to any goods has not

been paid at the expiration of a period of three years from -

(a) where the tax is payable in respect of the importation

of those goods - the date of the entry of those goods

for home consumption; or

(b) in any other case - the close of the month in which the

transaction, act or operation was effected or done,

the Commissioner may remit that tax unless he -

(c) has required payment of the tax prior to the expiration

of that period; or

(d) is satisfied that the payment of the tax was avoided by

fraud or evasion.

...

(3) For the purposes of this section the Commissioner shall

be deemed to have required payment of tax if he, or an officer

acting on his behalf, has served upon any person a notice in

writing specifying that an amount of tax is payable by that

person in respect of -

(a) the transaction, act or operation specified in sub-section

(1) of this section; or

(b) any transactions, acts or operations which include the

transaction, act or operation specified in that

subsection.

(4) For the purposes of this section `tax' includes any

further tax payable under any Sales Tax Assessment Act and any

additional tax for which the person is liable under this Act

or under any Sales Tax Assessment Act."

16. The issue is whether the Commissioner's letter of 19 April 1985 amounted to a notice in writing as specified in s.12B(3). The learned trial Judge held that s.12B(1) was a mandatory provision requiring the Commissioner to remit the tax not paid at the expiration of a three year period unless paragraphs (c) or (d) were satisfied. There is no appeal from his Honour's ruling on that point, which accords with my own impression of the sub-section.

17. The trial Judge held that the notice satisfied s.12B(3). His Honour said:-

"The word `amount' appearing in s.12B(3) is perhaps ambiguous.

It can refer to a money figure or it may be taken to refer

merely to the fact that there is sales tax payable in some

amount not yet quantified. Of the two alternatives I prefer

the latter. To require the Commissioner to stipulate the

precise figure of sales tax payable when that information may

well be known only to the taxpayer and not disclosed to the

Commissioner by the taxpayer, imposes in my view an

unreasonable burden upon the Commissioner. It is also

inconsistent with the explanatory memorandum to which I have

already referred.

The second submission may be disposed of more quickly. There

is nothing in s.12B(3) which requires the Commissioner in the

notice to indicate the Assessment Act under which the sales

tax is payable. It is true that the tax in question may be

tax under any of the various sales tax assessments acts (see

s.12B(4)), but that of itself does not seem to me to require

the particular sales tax act to be nominated.

Finally, I do not think the reference to `tax payable'

requires that the notice under s.12B(3) can only be given

where tax arises without assessment under, for example, s.4(1)

of the Act, but not to a case where tax becomes payable as a

result of the alteration of a sale value. The words `tax

payable' in s.12B(3) have the same meaning as they in

s.12B(1), that is to say, they refer to tax ultimately payable

even though not yet payable, so long as that tax relates to a

transaction, act or operation effected or done in the three

year period."

18. I have given careful attention to his Honour's views but think that the Commissioner could not for the purposes of s.12B(1) require "payment of that tax" and that a notice in writing for the purposes of s.12B(3) could not specify "that an amount of tax is payable" unless there was at that time an amount of tax which was payable.

19. If an importer did not lodge returns when required to do so or did not complete its returns by including all the goods or "the amount for which those goods are sold" in accordance with s.4(1), there could be an amount of tax which was payable and which had not been paid. That was because sections 7 and 9 of the Act, when read together, so provided. But Copperart had fulfilled its obligations in that respect, hence no tax was payable.

20. No further tax became payable by Copperart until the Commissioner had formed an opinion as to the arm's length prices of the goods and had adjusted the sale values accordingly. Until the Commissioner exercised his discretion - and the calculation of an arm's length price involved the making of a discretionary or value judgment as to that price - the sale values of the goods as fixed by the Act were the sale values as fixed by s.4(1). On 19 April 1985, no further tax was payable.

21. It should be noted that, at the time of the letter of 19 April 1985, s.10 of the Act read, inter alia:-

"10(2) Where, under sub-section (1) or (2) of

section 4 or sub-section (5) of section 4A

of this Act, the sale value of any goods

has been altered, the Commissioner shall

calculate the further tax (if any) payable

in consequence of that alteration.

...

(3) As soon as conveniently may be after an

assessment is made or a sale value is altered,

the Commissioner shall cause notice in writing

of the assessment or alteration and of the tax

or further tax to be given to the person liable

to pay the tax or further tax." ...

At least in a case where the importer had complied with its requirements under ss.7 and 9, no further tax was payable until an assessment had issued or a sale value had been altered. When additional liability arose by the making of an assessment or by the adjustment of a sale value, the Commissioner was required to give notice in writing in accordance with s.10(3). Such a notice would have constituted a requirement to pay the tax for the purposes of s.12B(1) and a notice "specifying that an amount of tax is payable" for the purposes of s.12B(3). However, the letter of 19 April 1985 did not do so.

22. The letter of 19 April 1985 was misconceived. Copperart had duly lodged returns of the goods sold and of their sale value. If the sale values were to be adjusted in accord with arm's length prices, it was the duty of the Commissioner to determine those prices and to adjust the values. It was not the duty of Copperart to lodge returns and to pay tax upon a basis inconsistent with s.4(1).

23. It follows that the appeals must succeed and that the orders made by the trial Judge should be varied accordingly.

24. Several matters were raised under a notice of contention.

25. The trial Judge discussed the point mentioned above that there should not be two assessments, one under s.4(2) and the other under s.4A of the Act, issued at the same time, but arriving at different arm's length prices. Having concluded for other reasons that the decision of the Tribunal should be set aside and that the matter should be remitted to the Tribunal for rehearing, the trial Judge expressed the view that the two assessments could not stand together and that if, for example, it was shown that the assessment under s.4(2) had been made first, then the Tribunal would have to set aside the assessment under s.4A as incompatible with the s.4(2) assessment and therefore excessive.

26. Section 67 of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 1) 1930 (Cth) which is incorporated into the Act by s.12 thereof, provided that, except in proceedings on a review or appeal relating to the decision, a notice of assessment was conclusive evidence that the amounts and all particulars of the assessment were correct. Accordingly, when the matter goes back to the Tribunal, the Tribunal will have the task of reviewing the objection decisions with respect to both assessments.

27. On the review, both assessments cannot stand, for there will be only one issue of substance before the Tribunal and that is the arm's length prices of the goods. Once the Tribunal has dealt with the objections to one assessment and has either affirmed or set aside or varied the Commissioner's assessment of the arm's length price, the Tribunal will have completed its task and the other assessment should be set aside. But which assessment should be set aside as needless will be a matter for the Tribunal. If the Tribunal forms the view that the Commissioner was correct in the total figure arrived at, the Tribunal may have to consider whether, under its power to review the Commissioner's decision on an objection, it is open to the Tribunal to increase either of the assessments. Counsel for the Commissioner has cited Stevenson v. Commissioner of Taxation (1991) 29 FCR 282 as authority for that proposition; but it would not be appropriate for the Court to consider the issue in this appeal.

28. The notice of contention also raised issues with respect to two remarks made by the trial Judge in the course of his reasons. However, the issues which counsel sought to discuss arise only if his Honour's remarks are read out of the context of the case. I do not propose to discuss these matters, as it appears to me that his Honour's remarks are readily understood in their context and that his Honour did not intend the meanings which counsel for the Commissioner seeks to attribute to them.

29. However, I take the opportunity to express my agreement with an observation of his Honour that:-

"It is not for the taxpayer first to show some error before

the Commissioner in the exercise of the discretion before the

Tribunal commences itself to exercise that discretion afresh."

His Honour also said:-

"The Tribunal must consider all the facts before it".

30. It is the duty of the Tribunal, as it is of the Court when an objection decision is on appeal, to consider the facts for itself, to make findings of fact and to come to its own conclusions on the issues before it. As Walsh J said in Krew v. Federal Commissioner of Taxation (1971) 71 ATC 4213 at 4216:

"I have a duty to reach my own conclusions on the questions of

fact which have to be decided and to give effect to those

conclusions. .... I must make my own decisions as to the

facts."

31. The trial Judge was troubled by the fact that the Tribunal, in a long decision, recited the evidence but appeared to make few findings of fact. For example, the Tribunal set out the evidence of an accountant who expressed the view that the differences between the prices of the goods sold to Laddeton and of those sold to the franchisees were commercially realistic and reasonable having regard to the trading conditions. These included the fact that Laddeton purchased much greater volumes than the franchisees and delivery was made in unbroken boxes. But the Tribunal did not make a finding as to arm's length prices of sales on those terms. Nor did the Tribunal discuss whether the marketing, advertising and like arrangements into which Copperart entered impacted on the sale values of the goods sold. Whilst general advertising of goods such as Rolex watches and Renault motor vehicles will increase the value of those goods, the Copperart advertising may have enhanced only the Copperart name and not the arm's length value of the individual copper and brass wares which it sold. Similar issues in the context of value for customs duty were considered in LNC (Wholesale) Pty Ltd v. Collector of Customs (1988) 17 FCR 154. Nor did the Tribunal make any finding of fact flowing from the evidence called on behalf of Copperart that it sold goods to non-franchisees at the same prices as it sold to franchisees.

32. The failure of the Tribunal to deal with the facts and issues, as one would have expected it to do, led the trial Judge to conclude that the Tribunal may have approached the matter in accordance with comments in Case X45 (1990) 90 ATC 364, where at 375 a differently constituted Administrative Appeals Tribunal said:-

"The applicant is required to demonstrate that the

Commissioner's exercise of discretion was vitiated by some

error, or that there was not before the Commissioner material

which is now before the Tribunal, and which would inexorably

have led the Commissioner to a different result."

As the trial Judge pointed out, such an approach is incorrect. It is the duty of the Tribunal to form its own view on the material before it.

33. Although the onus is on a taxpayer to show that the assessment was excessive, an assessment is not, in a review, itself evidence of the facts on which it was founded. It is the Tribunal's duty to come to its own conclusions of fact and to give effect to them. In this context, the trial Judge referred to s.43(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth) which provides, inter alia:-

"... for the purpose of reviewing a decision, the Tribunal may

exercise all the powers and discretions that are conferred by

any relevant enactment on the person who made the decision

..."

34. In the result, I am of the opinion that leave should be granted for the filing of the amended notices of appeal, that the appeals should be allowed, that the orders of the trial Judge should be set aside and that there should be substituted therefor the following orders:

1. That the decisions under appeal be set aside.

2. That the matter be remitted to the Administrative Appeals

Tribunal to be heard and decided again, either with or

without the hearing of further evidence, but in accordance

with the direction that such part of the sales tax assessed

under s.4(2) and s.4A of the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No.

6) 1930 as related to transactions, acts or operations

effected or done in relation to any goods before 9 November

1984 must be remitted by the Commissioner of Taxation

pursuant to s.12B(1) of the Sales Tax Procedure Act 1934.

3. That the Respondent pay the Applicant's costs of the

application to this Court.

The Respondent should pay the Appellant's costs of the appeals.

GUMMOW J: I agree with the orders proposed by Davies J in his reasons for judgment. I agree with the reasons his Honour gives for that course. For myself I wish to add some comments only upon the issue of whether the letter of 19 April 1985 was a notice answering the description in para. 12B (1) (c) of the Sales Tax Procedure Act 1934 ("the Procedure Act").

2. Section 12B was introduced into the Procedure Act by the Sales Tax Amendment Act 1936. The following passage appears in the second reading speech in the House of Representatives (Hansard, 2 December 1936, p. 2128):

"The general effect of the provision is that sales taxpayers

will not be called upon to pay tax more than three years

after the close of the month in which a taxable transaction

took place, unless they receive a specific notice from the

department to pay tax on those sales, etc., within that

period."

Section 12B, so far as is relevant, provides:

"12B(1) Where tax (not being tax paid prior to the commencement of

this section) in respect of any transaction, act or

operation, effected or done in relation to any goods has not

been paid at the expiration of a period of three years

from -

(a) where the tax is payable in respect of the importation

of those goods - the date of entry of those goods for home

consumption; or

(b) in any other case - the close of the month in which the

transaction, act or operation was effected or done,

the Commissioner may remit that tax unless he -

(c) has required payment of the tax prior to the expiration

of that period; or

(d) is satisfied that the payment of the tax was avoided by

fraud or evasion.

(2). . .

(3) For the purposes of this section the Commissioner shall be

deemed to have required payment of tax if he, or an officer

acting on his behalf, has served upon any person a notice in

writing specifying that an amount of tax is payable by that

person in respect of -

(a) the transaction, act or operation specified in

sub-section (1) of this section; or

(b) any transactions, acts or operations which include the

transaction, act or operation specified in that

sub-section."

3. Both parties accept that in the phrase in sub-s. 12B (1) "the Commissioner may remit . . ." the word "may" is used in a sense which is mandatory rather than permissive. Both parties also seemed to accept the proposition that sub-s. 12B (3) in specifying the steps by which the Commissioner shall be deemed to have required payment for the purposes of sub-s. 12B (1) did so exhaustively. For myself, I would not readily accept that the Commissioner may require payment only by using the means specified in sub- s. (3). It is unnecessary to resolve this issue of construction because the Commissioner did not suggest that if the facts fell outside sub-s. (3) they could nevertheless amount to a requirement by him of payment within the meaning of para. 12B (1) (c).

4. The Procedure Act operated as part of a structure which included the Sales Tax Assessment Act (No. 6) 1930 ("the No. 6 Act"). Broadly, under the No. 6 Act sales tax was levied and paid on the sale value of goods imported into Australia and sold by the taxpayer or applied to his own use (s. 3); the "sale value" of goods sold by the importer by wholesale was the amount by which the goods were sold by a registered person (sub-s. 4 (1)); by the operation of ss. 7 and 9 the taxpayer was obliged within 21 days of the close of a month to furnish to the Commissioner a return in a form approved by him and to pay sales tax upon the sale value of the goods sold by the taxpayer or applied to his own use during that month; sales tax was due and payable at the end of that period of 21 days (sub-s. 9 (2)). The Commissioner might make an assessment in relation to a taxpayer if he found in any case that tax or further tax was payable by that person (sub-s. 10 (1)), but in the ordinary course the effect of the provisions above described was that the tax was due and payable without an assessment having issued.

5. However, a significant change in the structure of the No. 6 Act was effected by the Sales Tax Assessment (No. 6) Amendment Act 1978. This included the new sub-s. 4 (2) and s. 4A which provided for the alteration by the Commissioner of the sale value of the relevant goods. Moreover, the legislation dealing with the alteration of sale value also provided for the calculation by the Commissioner of any further tax payable in consequence of the alteration and notification thereof to the person liable to pay, as steps in the making of that further tax payable.

6. At the time of the letter of 19 April 1985 this process was dealt with as follows in s. 10 of the No. 6 Act:

"10(1) Where the Commissioner finds in any case that tax or further

tax is payable by any person, the Commissioner may -

(a) assess the sale value upon which tax should be or should

have been paid; and

(b) calculate the tax or further tax which is payable.

(2) Where, under sub-section (1) or (2) of section 4 or

sub-section (5) of section 4A, the sale value of any goods

has been altered, the Commissioner shall calculate the

further tax (if any) payable in consequence of that

alteration.

(2A) Where -

(a) any person makes default in furnishing any return; or

(b) the Commissioner is not satisfied with the return made by

any person; or

(c) the Commissioner has reason to believe or suspect that

any person (though he may not have furnished any return) is

liable to pay sales tax, the Commissioner may cause an

assessment to be made of the amount upon which, in his

judgment, sales tax ought to be levied, and the person shall

be liable to sales tax thereon, excepting so far as he

establishes on objection that the assessment is excessive.

(3) As soon as conveniently may be after an assessment is made or

a sale value is altered, the Commissioner shall cause notice

in writing of the assessment or alteration and of the tax or

further tax to be given to the person liable to pay the tax

or further tax.

(4) The amount of tax or further tax specified in the notice

shall be payable on or before the date specified in the

notice, together with any other amount which may be payable

in accordance with any other provision of this Act.

(5) The omission to give any such notice shall not invalidate the

assessment and calculation made by the Commissioner."

7. It follows from these provisions, as in force at the date of the letter of 19 April 1985, that in a case where the sale value of goods was altered, the Commissioner was obliged (by sub-s. (2)) to calculate the further tax if any which was payable in consequence of that alteration and to cause notice in writing of the alteration and of the tax or further tax to be given to the person liable to pay the tax or further tax (sub-s. (3)). Furthermore, the amount of the tax or further tax specified in that notice would be payable on or before the date specified therein (sub-s. (4)). If those steps had been taken under s. 10 of the No. 6 Act, then, clearly enough, the Commissioner would have thereby, within the meaning of sub-s. 12B (3) of the Procedure Act, "served upon (the taxpayer) a notice in writing specifying that an amount of tax is payable by that person" in respect of a transaction, act or operation specified in sub-s. 12B (1). The alteration of the sale value would have been the first in a series of steps involving calculation and notification to the taxpayer of the tax or further tax to be payable on or before a date specified in the notice given to the taxpayer.

8. It is common ground that none of these steps had been taken before 19 April 1985. Until those steps had been taken there could be no amount of tax payable, within the meaning of sub-s. 12B (3), as a consequence of the alteration of the sale value. The terms of the letter of 19 April 1985 could not operate to anticipate the implementation of the necessary procedures to achieve the result that any amount of such tax was so payable.

9. The present litigation was triggered by notices of objection to what the Commissioner described as notices of alteration, enclosed with his letter to the taxpayer of 9 November 1987. If the classification of the letter of 19 April 1985 for the purposes of sub-s. 12B (3) of the Procedure Act be approached by having regard to the provisions of the No. 6 Act as they stood at this later date, 9 November 1987, no different result follows from that indicated above. Section 10 had been amended, in the interim, by s. 164 of the Taxation Boards of Review (Transfer of Jurisdiction) Act 1986, with effect 1 July 1986. Section 10 now read:

"10(1) Where the Commissioner finds in any case that tax or

further tax is payable by a person, the Commissioner may

make an assessment in relation to the person.

(2) Where, under sub-section 4 (1) or (2) or 4A (5), the sale

value of any goods has been altered, the Commissioner

shall make an assessment in relation to those goods.

(2A) Where -

(a) a person makes default in furnishing a return;

(b) the Commissioner is not satisfied with a return

furnished by a person; or

(c) the Commissioner has reason to believe or suspect that

a person (although not having furnished a return) is

liable to pay sales tax, the Commissioner may determine an

amount to be the amount upon which, in the opinion of the

Commissioner, sales tax should be paid and may make an

assessment in relation to the person.

(3) As soon as conveniently may be after an assessment has

been made, the Commissioner shall cause notice in writing

of the assessment to be served on the person liable to pay

the tax or further tax.

(4) (omitted)

(5) The omission to give any such notice shall not invalidate

the assessment made by the Commissioner."

The effect of these provisions was to require the Commissioner, upon the alteration of the sale value, to make an assessment (sub-s. (2)) and to cause notice in writing thereof to be served on the person liable to pay the tax or further tax (sub-s. (3)). No doubt a notice in writing of the assessment for the purposes of the amended sub-s. 10 (3) of the No. 6 Act would answer the description in sub-s. 12B (3) of the Procedure Act of a notice in writing specifying that an amount of tax was payable by the taxpayer. The letter of 19 April 1985 was not such a notice. Even if the operation of sub-s. 12B (3) upon the letter of 19 April 1985 is to be considered with reference to the operation of the No. 6 Act as it stood at the later date, the result is no different.

10. However, in my view, the correct approach to the matter is to consider the operation of sub-s. 12B (3) in respect of the letter, by looking to the terms of the No. 6 Act as they stood at the date of the letter itself.

FRENCH J

I agree with the orders proposed for the reasons given by Davies J and subject to the additional observations of Gummow J.
