Statement of Reasons

APPLICATION FOR REVIEW

  1. This is a review of an objection decision of the respondent dated 20 March 2025 refusing to exercise his discretion to release the applicant from a tax liability under Division 340 of Schedule 1 to the Taxation Administration Act 1953.

BACKGROUND

  1. The applicant seeks to be released from income tax liabilities and associated general interest charges in the amount of $148,582.05. (as of 16 May 2026)
  2. These liabilities arise in respect of payments received during the income years ended 30 June 2021 and 30 June 2022 by the applicant as a consultant engaged by the Asian Development Bank to consult to the National Airports Corporation of Papua New Guinea.
  3. On 17 December 2019, the applicant made an early engagement request for advice regarding the application of the Inturnational Organisations (Privileges and Immunities) Act 1963 in respect of potential payments from the Asian Development Bank.
  4. The respondent confirmed that the payments would be exempt from taxation provided that the services were performed outside of Australia in line with Taxation Ruling TR 92/14.
  5. The applicant subsequently tendered for a contract with the Asian Development Bank to provide consulting services to the National Airports Corporation in Papua New Guinea.
  6. On 16 March 2020, the Applicant received confirmation that his bid was successful.
  7. Subsequent to entering into the agreement to perform the service, but prior to the first receipt of payment for those services, the Australian Government prohibited outbound travel from Australia as part of quarantine measures during the COVID-19 Pandemic.
  8. The applicant thereafter entered into negotiations with the Asian Development Bank culminating in an agreement to vary the contract to permit the applicant to provide the services remotely from Australia. The applicant had further sought to vary the contract in respect of the fee to account for tax but was unable to do so.
  9. On 6 May 2020, the applicant sought confirmation from the respondent as to whether the payments from the Asian Development Bank would be exempt from taxation in circumstances where he performed services while working from home in Australia but would otherwise have performed the services overseas but for the border closures.
  10. On 11 May 2020, the respondent advised that the income earned working from home in Australia would not be exempt, even if the income would have been exempt if the same services were performed overseas in line with Taxation Ruling TR 92/14.
  11. The applicant commenced consulting services to the National Airports Corporation of Papua New Guinea in May 2020.
  12. On 15 July 2021, the applicant lodged a private ruling application seeking advice on whether the income received by the applicant from the Asian Development Bank would be exempt from taxation in circumstances where he performed services while working from home in Australia but would otherwise have performed the services overseas but for the border closures.
  13. On 22 October 2021, the respondent issued a private ruling, confirming that payments from the Asian Development Bank would not be exempt from taxation where the work was preformed inside of Australia, and that the respondent did not have a discretion to treat the work as having been carried out outside of Australia.
  14. On 25 November 2023 the Applicant lodged his overdue income tax returns for the years ended 30 June 2021 and 30 June 2022, reporting the following amounts from Asian Development Bank as personal services income:
    1. in the year ended 30 June 2021, $156,259
    2. in the year ended 30 June 2022, $226,232.
  15. These assessments resulted in a cumulative income tax debt of $124,045.96, which was subject to general interest charges from the relevant due dates for payment.
  16. Refunds due to the taxpayer in subsequent income years have been withheld and applied against this debt, with general interest charges continuing to accrue, resulting in the amount stated above at paragraph two (2).
  17. On 30 July 2024, the Applicant made an application to be released from this taxation liability.
  18. On 20 November 2024, the respondent made a decision not to release the applicant from this taxation liability and provided reasons for this decision.
  19. On 3 December 2024, the applicant lodged an objection to this decision.
  20. On 20 March 2025, the respondent disallowed the objection and provided reasons for this decision.
  21. On 10 April 2025 the Applicant made an application to this the Administrative Appeals Tribunal (AAT) for a review of this objection decision.
  22. On 14 October 2024, the AAT ceased to have effect, and the Administrative Review Tribunal (the Tribunal) came into effect. Under the transitional provisions in the Administrative Review Tribunal (Consequential and Transitional Provisions No 1.) Act 2024, applications made to the AAT that were not finalised prior to 14 October 2024 are taken to be applications made to the Tribunal.
  23. Anything done by the AAT in relation to the proceeding before 14 October 2024 is taken to have been done by the Tribunal and the Tribunal is authorised to continue and finalise such proceedings in accordance with the Administrative Review Tribunal Act 2024 (Cth).
  24. Pursuant to orders dated 4 August 2025, on 18 August 2025, the applicant filed a statement of financial circumstances. The respondent, on 1 September 2025, filed his statement of facts, issues and contentions and evidence. The applicant in turn filed his statement of facts, issues and contentions and evidence on 6 October 2025.
  25. Pursuant to orders dated 9 November 2025, on 5 December 2025, the Respondent provided to the Tribunal and the Applicant his analysis of the applicant’s expenditure. The applicant in turn filed, on 2 January 2026, an amended statement of facts, issues and contentions and the respondent, in turn, filed a statement of facts, issues and contentions in reply on 23 January 2026.
  26. The matter was called for hearing on 19 March 2026. Post hearing the applicant provided further submissions to wit the respondent provided submissions in reply.
  27. Having had due regard to parties submissions and, for the following reasons, I have determined that the decision under review should be set aside and substituted with a decision that the applicant be released from his outstanding income tax liabilities for the income years ended 30 June 2021 and 30 June 2022, and the associated general interest charges, under Division 340 of Schedule 1 to the Taxation Administration Act 1953.

RELEVANT LAW

  1. Subsection 340-5(3) of Schedule 1 to the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth) provides that the commissioner may, at his discretion, release an individual from a liability to taxation if satisfied that the individual would suffer serious hardship if required to satisfy that liability.
  2. There is no statutory definition of ‘serious hardship’ in Division 340 of Schedule 1 to the Taxation Administration Act 1953 (Cth). Nor does the Division set out any guiding factors or principles that may be relevant in assessing serious hardship.[1]
  3. It is, however, well established by the various authority on Subsection 340-5(3) and its predecessor[2] that the hardship referred to is financial hardship,[3] and that, in assessing whether serious financial hardship is established, consideration must be given to whether the taxpayer would, if required to satisfy the liability, experience financial difficulties which are serious, though not necessarily at the level of causing destitution.[4]
  4. The respondent sets out his view of what constitutes serious financial hardship in Practice Law Administration Statement PS LA 2011/17. While PS LA 2011/17 is not binding on this Tribunal, the consideration therein have been endorsed by the Tribunal[5] and the Federal Court[6] as largely reflecting those identified in relevant case authority.
  5. PS LA 2011/17[7] states that serious financial hardship will exist where an applicant is left without means to achieve the reasonable acquisitions of food, clothing, medical supplies, accommodation, or education.[8] What constitutes a reasonable acquisition is referenced by normal community standards,[9] and the Tribunal may draw on its own knowledge in determining normal community standards.[10]
  6. PSLA 2011/17 further states that serious financial hardship may be established by assessing an applicant’s capacity to repay in a reasonable timeframe on the basis of their income and outgoings, or the applicant’s equity in, or access to, assets which may be realised in satisfaction of the debt.
  7. Relevantly, the resources of the household, and not just of the applicant are to be considered in determining the relevant assets and income as against liabilities and expenditure,[11] and the Tribunal is to consider the position of the applicant’s household at the time of its decision.[12]
  8. PSLA 2011/17 further sets out a number of other relevant factors which may be considered when deciding whether it is appropriate to exercise the discretion to grant a release from taxation liabilities, however, those consideration are contingent on first being satisfied that the applicant would suffer serious hardship if required to satisfy the liability.[13]

CONSIDERATIONS OF CLAIMS AND EVIDENCE

  1. The respondent concedes that the applicant does not have sufficient equity in, or access to, assets which may be realised in satisfaction of the debt beyond those regarded as normal and reasonable possessions not expected to be surrendered in order to satisfy the debt.
  2. The respondent, however, submits that the applicant maintains capacity to pay in a reasonable timeframe on the basis of his income and outgoings and that it would otherwise be inappropriate, having regard to the other relevant factors in PSLA 2011/17 to release the applicant from this liability.
  3. The respondent relies on section 14ZZK of the Taxation Administration Act 1953 and submits that the applicant has failed to discharge his burden of proof that the taxation decision concerned should not have been made or should have been made differently.
  4. I have accordingly confined my consideration to whether the applicant has demonstrated that he lacks the capacity to repay the debt in a reasonable timeframe on the basis of his income and outgoings and that it is otherwise appropriate, having regard to the other relevant factors in PSLA 2011/1 to release the applicant from this liability.
  5. The applicant does not contest the respondent’s assertion that there is an amount of discretionary expenditure above his reasonable household expenditure; however, the applicant asserts a lower quantum and submits that it is insufficient to discharge the debt within a reasonable timeframe. The applicant therefore submits that discharging the debt within a reasonable timeframe would cause the applicant to suffer serious financial hardship.
  6. Both the respondent and the applicant assert different basis for ascertaining the applicant’s income and outgoings.
  7. Pursuant to orders dated 4 August 2025, on 18 August 2025, the applicant provided the Tribunal and the respondent with a statement of financial circumstances.
  8. On 1 November 2025, the applicant further provided bank and credit card statements for the months of May 2025 to September 2025.
  9. In accordance with orders dated 9 November 2025, the respondent prepared an Excel Spreadsheet containing his analysis of the applicant’s income and expenditure, and a summary thereof.
  10. The respondent’s analysis of the applicant’s income and expenditure identified a number of inconsistences between the amounts stated in the applicant’s statement of financial circumstances, and the amounts recorded in the bank and credit card statements for the months of May 2025 to September 2025.
  11. The respondent contends that the applicant’s statement of financial circumstances is, therefore, not a sufficiently accurate reflection of the applicant’s household income or expenditure and should not be relied upon by the Tribunal.
  12. The respondent does not, however, contest that the applicant’s actual household income and expenditure can be ascertained for the months of May 2025 to September 2025 from the bank and credit card statements provided for that period.
  13. Further, while the applicant disputes the respondent’s categorisation of certain items of income and expenditure, he accepts the figures contained therein as an accurate transcription of items contained in the bank and credit card statements provided.
  14. I have had regard to the inconsistences identified by the respondent between the amounts stated in the applicant’s statement of financial circumstances, and the amounts recorded in the bank and credit card statements for the months of May 2025 to September 2025; I find that the applicant’s statement of financial circumstances is not a sufficiently accurate reflection of the applicant’s household income or expenditure.
  15. However, I find that the applicant’s actual income and expenditure, as identified from the bank and credit card statements for the months of May 2025 to September 2025, and other material provided, can be averaged and applied as a reasonable estimate of household income and expenditure.

EDUCATION

  1. The applicant’s bank and credit card statements record total monthly expenditure on education of $1,722.22 in May, $1,902.89 in June, $1,565.31, $1,687.08 in August, and $1,558.93 in September.
  2. The majority of which consists of regular payments to Siena Catholic Primary School and Siena Catholic College in the amount of $315.10 per fortnight and $446.58 per fortnight.
  3. The respondent submits that, per Dua v Federal Commissioner of Taxation [2022] AATA 1520, private school education is generally considered to be discretionary in nature. This overstates Dua in my view. Relevantly, in Dua the applicant expended in excess of $4,000 per month on private school fees for his children.[15] The Tribunal found that an annual expenditure by the Applicant of almost $50,000 on private school fees was a discretionary amount that could be reduced or eliminated if the children were to transfer to public school.[16]
  4. The present case is distinguishable as the amount of expenditure is considerably less than in Dua; namely, $18,285.60 for two children. Further, the applicant testified that he and his family are Catholic and that a religious education for their children is important to he and his wife. The applicant testified that he and his wife had waitlisted their children for a place in the Catholic education system upon moving to Queensland from the Norther Territory and that they enrolled their children immediately upon receiving a place for them. The respondent did not challenge this evidence, and I note that it is consistent with Mrs Prasser’s employment as a Campus Minister.
  5. I therefore consider expenditure on a religious education for the applicant’s children to be reasonable and, having regard to the tuition fees, consider the amount to be reasonable expenditure on education.
  6. I note that, as observed by the respondent, the published primary and secondary college fee schedules indicate that the base tuition, year-level levies and vocational educational/subject levies accord with the $11,952 in tuition declared by the applicant and that this is less than the actual expenditure evidenced by the applicant’s bank statements.
  7. The applicant testified that he incurs additional charges for extracurricular activities; this is reflected in the account statement for Siena Catholic Primary School provided by the applicant.
  8. I therefore consider that the difference between the $11,952 in tuition declared by the applicant and the $18,285.60, recorded in the applicant’s bank and credit card statements, namely $6,333.60, is representative of expenditure on extracurricular activities. While this amount is not in my view excessive, and notwithstanding the apparent benefit to the children’s prospects, it nevertheless represents a level of discretionary spending above that necessary to acquire a reasonable education.
  9. The respondent does not contest that the other education expenses, namely the ‘Wearrito’ expenses which appears to be associated with school uniforms and ‘Flexischool’ canteen payments, are reasonable. These cost average at $30.44 per month and $40.18 per month respectively.
  10. The applicant’s bank and credit card statements also disclose payments to averaging $27.4 per month to Chancellor State College. The applicant has not provided an account of what this expenditure comprises. I note that the individual amounts are irregular, ad hoc, and low value. I note also that the Chancellor State College is part of a larger campus incorporating the grounds of the University of the Sunshine Cost, Siena Catholic Primary School, and Siena Catholic College. These payments may therefore be incidental to either Mrs Prasser’s employment or the children’s education, however, without more I am unable to determine if this is reasonable expenditure and accordingly have considered these amounts as discretionary expenditure.
  11. I determine that $1,066.62 per month represents reasonable expenditure on education.

  3. This leaves $519.55 per month as discretionary expenditure.

WILL THE APPLICANT SUFFER SERIOUS FINANCIAL HARDSHIP?

  1. I note that the respondent has proposed a payment plan dated 11 March 2026 which remains open to the applicant. The respondent notes correctly that the test of serious financial hardship is altered where a payment plan is proposed in that regard must be had to whether the applicant would experience serious financial hardship in meeting his or her taxation liabilities via the Payment Plan offered.[19]
  2. The payment plan proposed by the respondent identified discretionary spending of approximately $3,000 per month and proposed that this amount be applied to satisfaction of the tax debt (with general interest charges capitalised as at the date of entering into the payment plan). This would see the applicant meeting the debt in approximately 2031.
  3. Having determined there to be discretionary expenditure of $519.55 per month, meeting this payment plan would require the taxpayer to forgo approximately $2,500 of other expenditure such that, in my view, he would be left without means to achieve the reasonable acquisitions of food, clothing, medical supplies, accommodation, or education.
  4. I note further that, were the applicant to meet this liability on the same terms at $519.55 per month, the debt would not be meet until approximately 2050 when the applicant would be 78 years of age.
  5. I note that the applicant has submitted a quote for car repair in the amount of $2,682.50 and submits that an allowance should be made for this forthcoming expenditure. The respondent submits that this should not be regarded as reasonable expenditure as it is to repair the vehicles air conditioning only.
  6. While I accept that repairs to certain fitments designed to enhance the use of a vehicle (such as, a sunroof, radio, or satellite navigation unit) may be properly regarded as discretionary expenditure, I regard air-conditioning as a standard fitment to any vehicle sold in Australia and necessary to maintain proper operation of a vehicle.
  7. The applicant also submits dental and orthodontic quotes for himself and one of his minor daughters in the amounts of $6,435 and $8,980 respectively. The respondent does not contest that these are reasonable medical expenses but submitted that the applicant and his family are covered by private heath cover and, prima face, nothing in the documents indicates that these expenses would not be covered, at least in part, by this insurance. The applicant subsequently proved evidence from his insurers that a benefit of only $773.50 would be paid in respect of his dental expenses and $650 in respect of his minor daughter.
  8. The respondent maintains that it is not immediately clear on the face of the documents provided by the applicant that these expenses are immediately payable; or in the alturnative, ongoing expenses that are significant enough to prohibit the applicant from satisfying the taxation obligations through the payment plan proposed by the respondent.
  9. I find that it is plain on the face of the documents that the applicant has forthcoming dental expenditure of $13,991.50 which must be met either immediately or by instalment.
  10. I consider these expenses to be reasonable and that they will absorb the $519.55 per month of discretionary expenditure such that satisfying the liability would cause the applicant to suffer significant financial hardship.
  11. Having due regard to the matters outlined above, I am not satisfied that the applicant has capacity to pay in a reasonable timeframe on the basis of his income and outgoings and, accordingly, that satisfying the liability would cause the applicant serious financial hardship.

WHETHER IT IS APPROPRIATE TO RELEASE THE APPLICANT FORM THE LIABILITY.

  1. Having determined that the applicant has demonstrated that he lacks the capacity to repay the debt in a reasonable timeframe on the basis of his income and outgoings it is necessary to determine whether the applicant has demonstrated that it is otherwise appropriate, having regard to the other relevant factors in PSLA 2011/1 to release the applicant from this liability.
  2. PSLA 2011/1 sets out a non-exhaustive list of factors that are appropriate to have regard to in exercising the discretion to grant a release from taxation liabilities. Namely,
(a) whether release would not alleviate hardship, such as where the person has other liabilities or creditors;

(b) whether it was reasonable for the taxpayer to acquire assets ahead of meeting their tax liabilities;

(c) whether a taxpayer has made plans to address their tax liabilities prior to disposing of funds or assets;

(d) whether a taxpayer has paid other debts (either business or private) in preference to their tax debt;

(e) whether the taxpayer has pursued debts owed to them or provided reasons as to why they have not;

(f) whether serious hardship is likely only to be short term;

(g) whether there was an event that led to a change in the taxpayer's compliance;

(h) the taxpayer's past compliance history, including lodgment of returns, payment of tax debts and the outcome of any audit or enforcement activity taken to enforce compliance;

(i) whether the taxpayer has made plans to pay future debts;

(j) how the taxpayer has structured their affairs and whether they have placed themselves in a position of hardship (for example, placing all assets in trusts or related entities over which they have control); and

(k) whether there are other factors contributing to the taxpayer's serious hardship that are outside their control – this includes experiences of vulnerability such as family violence, financial coercion, sudden homelessness or serious mental health challenges.

  1. The respondent considers that the following factors militate against the exercise of the discretion to release the applicant.
  2. The respondent submits that, at all material times, the applicant was aware that the income received would not be exempt from income tax. The respondent notes that, on 6 May 2020, the applicant wrote to the respondent seeking confirmation whether income received from the Asian Development Bank would be tax exempt if the services were performed in Australia.
  3. The respondent notes further that the applicant indicated in said correspondence that it was his understanding is that work he undertook for the Asian Development Bank in Australia would not be exempt from Australian income tax.
  4. The respondent notes that, on 11 May 2020, the respondent confirmed that the income would not be tax exempt. The respondent notes that the applicant did not derive income under this contract until May 2020.
  5. The respondent therefore submits that the applicant, in knowledge of this, failed to make provision for the future income tax payable on those amounts.
  6. The applicant testified that money was set aside from the Asian Development Bank payments for the payment of tax liability, but that this money was then put to use in maintenance of household expenditure when his income ceased. The applicant testified that he submitted a tender to the Asian Development Bank when his contract came due for renewal and that this tender was rejected as he had accounted for the tax payable which made his subsequent tender uncompetitive. The applicant further testified that he was unable to obtain employment after this time as his profession is in the aviation industry which was significantly affected by Covid-19 restrictions during the relevant period. The applicant testified that it was this that lead him to establish Scuvvers Pty. Ltd. as a means of deriving income after his savings (including the monies set aside for the payment of tax) had been depleted to the maintenance of household expenditure.
  7. The applicant’s testimony was not challenged by the respondent and I accept his testimony.
  8. I therefore do not accept that the applicant has deliberately failed to make provision for income tax payable on amounts received from the Asian Development Bank but rather, that irregular circumstances beyond the applicant’s control led to his applying these funds to the maintenance of household expenditure.
  9. I consider that it was reasonable for the applicant to do so in the circumstances and that this does not, therefore, militate against the exercise of the discretion to release the applicant.
  10. The respondent notes that the applicant lodged income tax returns for financial years ending 30 June 2021 and 30 June 2022 concurrently and late on 25 November 2023. The respondent contends that the applicant was aware that income tax was payable in the aforementioned financial years and that his failure to lodge the returns on time caused him to be liable for general interest charges.
  11. The applicant submitted that, apart from these years, he has not failed to meet his tax obligations on time and that his failure to do so in these years was due to his difficulties in finding income following the rejection of his Asian Development Bank tender.
  12. While the applicant’s failure to meet his tax obligations on time does militates against the exercise of the discretion to release the applicant, I note that this occurred during a global pandemic and period of significant financial strain for the applicant. I therefore accord this only minimal weight.
  13. The respondent notes that the applicant is a shareholder of Scuvvers Pty. Ltd. and commenced this business venture on or about 30 August 2021. The respondent suggest that the applicant may have contributed capital to support the venture which could have otherwise been put aside and remitted to the respondent once the income tax returns for the 2021 and 2022 financial years were lodged and assessments raised.
  14. The applicant testified that he did not contribute any capital to the establishment of Scuvvers Pty. Ltd. and that this business is capitalised by its other shareholders who contributed capital in lieu of running the business operations. The respondent noted that the applicant and his wife each own approximately 2.5 million shares in Scuvvers Pty. Ltd. which represents about 84% of the business. The respondent submitted that it was, therefore, implausible that the other shareholders (who own about 18% of the company) would have contributed all the capital. The applicant submitted that such funding arrangements are ordinary in the industry.
  15. I do not find the capital structure submitted by the applicant to be inherently implausible and there is no material presently before me to indicate that the applicant, or his spouse, contributed any capital to the establishment of Scuvvers Pty. Ltd. Absent any such material I accept the applicant’s testimony that neither he or his spouse contributed any capital to the establishment of Scuvvers Pty. Ltd.
  16. The respondent further submits that the applicant’s discretionary spending in preference to meeting taxation obligations and his having made no voluntary payments towards the outstanding taxation liabilities militates against the exercise of the discretion to release the applicant.
  17. I note my finding above that the applicant does maintain an amount of discretionary spending which could have otherwise been put toward that outstanding taxation liabilities and find that this militates against the exercise of the discretion to release the applicant.
  18. However, I note that the amount of discretionary spending is only minimal and I accord this only minimal weight.
  19. Having weighed all of these factors, and with due regard to this debt having arisen in the unique circumstances of a global pandemic where the applicant’s industry was significantly impacted, I find that it is appropriate to release the applicant from this liability.

DECISION

  1. I find that the decision under review should be set aside and substituted with a decision that the applicant be released from his outstanding income tax liabilities for the income years ended 30 June 2021 and 30 June 2022, and associated general interest charges, under Division 340 of Schedule 1 to the Taxation Administration Act 1953.
