Evelyn Frances Howarth and Commissioner of Superannuation [1997] AATA 110 (2 April 1997)

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

EVELYN FRANCES HOWARTH v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. V96/486

AAT No. 11743

Number of pages - 22

Superannuation - Statutory Interpretation - Practice and Procedure

COURT

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

J. DWYER (Senior Member), I L CAMPBELL (Member) AND F SMITH (Member)

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - Benefit Classification Certificate (BCC) - s 16(6) of Superannuation Act - whether s 16(6) allows eligible employee to apply for revocation of BCC at any time - whether correspondence from applicant could be construed as such a request - s 154(2) - whether BCC had "come to notice of" applicant - whether actual knowledge required - difference between document being served on person and "coming to notice of" person - decision set aside

Statutory Interpretation - interaction between s 16(6) and s 154(2) of Superannuation Act - rule that more specific provision to prevail - interpretation of beneficial legislation

Practice and Procedure - Commissioner of Superannuation to review requests for revocation of BCCs under s 16(6) of Superannuation Act

Words and Phrases - "come to notice of"

Commissioner for Superannuation v Benham (1989) 86 ALR 239 Khoury (M & S) v Government Insurance Office of New South Wales [1984] HCA 55; (1984) 165 CLR 622 Comcare v Willems (1996) 43 ALD 253 Downey v Trans Waste Pty Ltd (1991) 99 ALR 402 Eagle Trust plc v SBC Securities Ltd [1992] 4 All ER 488 Re Montagu's Settlement Trusts [1987] Ch 264 McDonald v Director-General of Social Security [1984] FCA 57; (1983) 6 ALD 6 Repatriation Commission v Gordon [1990] FCA 417; (1990) 100 ALR 255 Re A L and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 12 ALD 308 Re Brian Lawlor Automotive Pty Ltd and Collector of Customs (New South Wales) (1978) 1 ALD 167

HEARING

MELBOURNE, 11 November 1996 2:4:1997

Counsel for the Applicant: Mr T Keely

Solicitor for the Applicant: Ryan Carlisle Thomas

Solicitor for the Respondent: Ms T Munoz Comsuper

ORDER

1. The Tribunal sets aside the decision under review.

2. In substitution, the Tribunal remits the matter to the Commissioner for Superannuation for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the Commissioner deal with Mrs Howarth's request of 25 June 1995 and her solicitor's request of 16 January 1996 as provided for in s 16(7) and s 16(8) of the Superannuation Act 1976 by deciding whether to:

(a) revoke the Benefit Classification Certificate;

(b) revoke the Benefit Classification Certificate and issue in substitution a new Benefit Classification Certificate; or

(c) refuse the requests under s 16(6) of the Act.

DECISION

J. DWYER, I L CAMPBELL AND F SMITH

1. This is an application under s 154(6) of the Superannuation Act 1976 ("the Act") for review of a decision made 23 January 1996 by a delegate of the Commissioner for Superannuation ("the Commissioner"). The decision was confirmed on reconsideration on 22 March 1996. The delegate decided not to allow Mrs Howarth a period further to the thirty day period specified under s 154(2) of the Act in which to request reconsideration of the decision of a delegate of the Commissioner dated 2 July 1981, to issue, in respect of her, a benefit classification certificate ("BCC") under s 16(4) of the Act.

2. Mr T Keely of Counsel appeared for Mrs Howarth. Mrs Howarth gave evidence. Miss T Munoz, an advocate with Commonwealth Superannuation Administration ("ComSuper"), appeared for the respondent. The Tribunal received into evidence six volumes of documents ("the T documents") lodged pursuant to s37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. The Tribunal also received into evidence the exhibits tendered by the parties. Exhibit R4 was a comprehensive twenty-four page chronology setting out Mrs Howarth's medical and work history which also served as an index to the relevant documents in the six volumes of T documents. The Tribunal found it to be a very useful document.

3. The respondent's statements of facts and contentions set out the following background facts as to which there was no dispute: "1. The applicant was born on 8 January 1950. 2. The Applicant became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1976 (the "1976 Act") on 17 April 1979. 3. The Applicant underwent an entry medical examination on 23 August 1979. 4. On 2 July 1981 a delegate of the Respondent Commissioner for Superannuation (the "Commissioner") decided to issue in respect of the Applicant a Benefit Classification Certificate ("BCC") pursuant to section 16(4) of the 1976 Act, specifying the following conditions: (i) post traumatic spondylitis of the lumbar thoracic and cervical regions with disorganised pelvis (ii) post traumatic arthritis left knee (iii) hypo pituitarism with thyroid deficiency. 5. On 3 July 1981, a letter was sent by the Respondent to advise the Applicant that a BCC had been issued in respect of her."

4. As set out in her statement of facts and contentions, Mrs Howarth claims that she never received the letter sent to her by the respondent. Paragraphs 4-9 of the applicant's statement of facts and contentions provide as follows: "4. The Respondents allegedly posted the B.C.C. to the Applicants parents' address at 32 Thomson Street, Belmont, on or abouts the 3rd July, 1981. 5. The Applicant had been residing at 25 Gatehouse Drive, Parkville at the time of the B.C.C. having been allegedly mailed at or abouts 3rd July, 1981. 6. The Applicant denies having ever received the B.C.C. as alleged by the Respondent in or abouts July, 1981. 7. The Applicant left Australia in approximately August, 1981 for the United Kingdom as she was awarded a Rotary International Scholarship to study for the British Academic year 1981 - 1982. 8. The Applicant received correspondence whilst studying in the UK from her family, including any letters received from any other party addressed to her, forwarded by her family. 9. The Applicant did not receive any correspondence from the Respondent whilst on Study Leave in the UK during 1981 - 1982."

5. The evidence established that Mrs Howarth joined Telecom (a predecessor of Telstra) as an occupational health nurse in April 1979. She returned to Telecom at the conclusion of her scholarship year in the United Kingdom. Mrs Howarth remained with Telstra until 17 April 1996 when she retired due to invalidity. She had been on continuous sick leave since 30 June 1992. On or about 17 June 1995 Mrs Howarth received by post a copy of a decision made 7 June 1995 by a delegate of the Superannuation Board which stated that the delegate was of the opinion that the conditions which were the grounds for her retirement were caused or substantially connected with the conditions specified in her BCC. The consequence of that decision if it remains in force is to reduce the superannuation pension payable to Mrs Howarth.

6. The relevant legislative provisions governing the issue of a BCC are found in s 16(2), (3) and (4) of the Act. Those provisions were in a somewhat different form as at the date when Mrs Howarth attended her medical examination and the date when the BCC was issued. They were amended by s 6(1) of the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1986 (No 80 of 1986) but, by s 2(1) of that Act, those amendments are deemed to have come into operation on 1 July 1976. This Tribunal must therefore have regard to the provisions as amended. Section 16, so far as relevant, reads as follows: "16. (2) The Commissioner may, for the purposes of this section, require a person (other than a person to whom section 184 applies) who proposes to become or becomes an eligible employee to undergo, within such period as the Commissioner specifies, such medical examinations or examinations by an approved medical practitioner or practitioners as the Commissioner determines. (3) A report or reports of the result or results of the medical examination or examinations shall be furnished to the Commissioner. (4) The Commissioner shall consider the report or reports, and such other matters (if any) as the Commissioner considers relevant, and, if he is of the opinion that there is a real risk that the person, by reason of or for a reason connected with a physical or mental condition or conditions referred to in the report or reports, will not continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age, the Commissioner shall issue a benefit classification certificate to that effect, being a certificate in which the relevant condition or conditions is or are specified. . . . (6) Where a benefit classification certificate is in force in respect of an eligible employee, he may, on grounds specified in the request, request the Commissioner to revoke the certificate or to revoke the certificate and issue in substitution for the certificate a new benefit classification certificate and, where an eligible employee makes such a request, he shall, within such period as the Commissioner requires, undergo such further medical examination (if any) or examinations by an approved medical practitioner or practitioners as the Commissioner requires. (7) A report or reports of the result or results of the further medical examination or examinations shall be furnished to the Commissioner. (8) The Commissioner shall consider the report or reports (if any), and such other matters as he considers relevant, and: (a) where there is not more than 1 physical or mental condition specified in the benefit classification certificate and the Commissioner is of the opinion that there is not a real risk that the person, by reason of or for a reason connected with that condition, will not continue to be an eligible employee until he attains his maximum retiring age - the Commissioner shall revoke the certificate; (b) where there is more than 1 physical or mental condition specified in the benefit classification certificate: (i) if the Commissioner is of the opinion that there is not a real risk that the person, by reason of or for a reason connected with those conditions, will not continue to be an eligible employee until the person attains his maximum retiring age - the Commissioner shall revoke the certificate; or (ii) if the Commissioner is of the opinion that there is a real risk that the person, by reason of or for a reason connected with 1 or some only of the conditions specified in the certificate, will not continue to be an eligible employee until he attains his maximum retiring age - the Commissioner shall revoke the certificate and issue in substitution for the certificate a new benefit classification certificate specifying only the condition or conditions in respect of which the Commissioner is of the opinion referred to in this subparagraph; and (c) in any other case - the Commissioner shall refuse the request to revoke the benefit classification certificate or to revoke the benefit classification certificate and to issue in substitution for the certificate a new benefit classification certificate. (9) Where, under subsection (8), the Commissioner revokes a benefit classification certificate on a particular day: (a) the revocation has effect on and from that day; and (b) the new certificate (if any) issued in substitution for the revoked certificate is to be taken to have been issued on that day."

7. Prior to 1988, s 16 included the following sub-section which related to service of BCCs: "16.(12) A copy of a benefit classification certificate, and a copy of any revocation or variation of such a certificate, and notice of refusal by the Commissioner of a request to revoke or vary such a certificate, shall be served on the person to whom the certificate relates or, if the person has died, on the personal representatives of the person or such other person or persons as the Commissioner, in his discretion, determines."

8. That section was repealed by s 20 of the Superannuation Legislation Amendment Act 1990 (No 40 of 1990) with effect from 1 July 1990. Section 66(2) of the Act sets out the effect of a BCC on the pension entitlements of an eligible employee who retires before attaining maximum retiring age.

9. As set out above, s 16(6) provides no time limit for a request by an eligible employee that a BCC be revoked. However there is a time limit in s 154 of the Act which provides a general power to seek review of a decision of the Commissioner. It provides as follows: "154. (1) In this section: 'decision' has the same meaning as in the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975; 'reviewable decision' means a decision of the Commissioner, or a delegate of the Commissioner, under this Act, under the superseded Act or under the regulations made under either of those Acts, and includes a decision of the Superannuation Board, or a delegate of the Superannuation Board (other than a decision under section 141 of the superseded Act); 'Superannuation Board' means the Superannuation Board established by the superseded Act. (2) A person affected by a reviewable decision who is dissatisfied with the decision may, by notice in writing given to the Commissioner within the period of 30 days after the day on which the decision first comes to the notice of the person, or within such further period as the Commissioner allows, request the Commissioner to reconsider the decision. (3) There shall be set out in the request the reasons for making the request. (4) Upon receipt of the request, the Commissioner shall reconsider the decision and may confirm or revoke the decision or vary the decision in such manner as the Commissioner thinks fit."

10. The respondent contends that, as Mrs Howarth did not request the Commissioner to reconsider the decision to issue a BCC within thirty days after the decision came to her notice, as specified in s 154(2), she requires an extension of that period. Mr Keely contended that, as s 154(2) sets a time limit of thirty days "after the day on which the decision first comes to the notice of the person", and as Mrs Howarth would give evidence that the decision to issue the BCC had not come to her notice as a result of the posting of the July 1981 letter and in fact only came to her notice on 17 June 1995, she did not require an extension of time under s 154(2).

11. During the hearing the Tribunal expressed surprise that neither the parties' representatives at the hearing nor the delegates of the Commissioner who considered Mrs Howarth's request had referred to s 16(6) of the Act. That provision allows an eligible employee to request the revocation of a BCC or revocation and issue of a new BCC at any time. In her letter of 25 June 1995 Mrs Howarth wrote: "I therefore believe the grounds on the 'BCC' to be too harsh and query there [sic] validity . . ."

12. That seems to us to be lay language for a request to revoke the BCC or to revoke it and issue in substitution a new BCC deleting some of the specified conditions. We find that Mrs Howarth did not have the BCC in front of her when she wrote her letter of 25 June 1995. Her solicitors wrote in the letter of 16 January 1996: "Our client did not sight the BCC until she saw the first page of the certificate which accompanied your letter to her dated 26th October, 1995. We received a copy of both pages of the certificate attached to your letter of 11th December, 1995."

13. Thus Mrs Howarth did not have the benefit of note (b) on page 1 of the BCC or of the summary of s 16(6), 16(7) and 16(8) which appears on page 2 of the BCC to direct her to the precise wording used in s 16(6) of the Act. We are of the view that the words she did use clearly convey her request that consideration be given to revoking the BCC, or at least to revoking it and issuing in substitution a new BCC which would specify fewer conditions and thus be less harsh in its application to her.

14. Superannuation legislation, like compensation legislation, is beneficial legislation. That is apparent from the long title of the Act, "An Act to make provision for and in relation to an Occupational Superannuation Scheme for persons employed by the Commonwealth, and for certain other persons, and for other purposes", and from the terminology such as "benefit classification certificate" used throughout the Act. The beneficial nature of the Act was referred to by the Full Court of the Federal Court in Commissioner for Superannuation v Benham (1989) 86 ALR 239, at 244: "This is, after all, beneficial legislation to which the established principle mentioned by Fullagar J in Wilson v Wilson's Tile Works Pty Ltd applies as it does in relation to a Workers Compensation Act that 'where two constructions . . . are possible that which is favourable to the [employee] should be preferred'."

15. The rule that remedial provisions are to be beneficially construed so as to provide the most complete remedy of the situation with which they are intended to deal within the confines of the language employed within the statute has been approved by the High Court in Khoury (M & S) v Government Insurance Office of New South Wales [1984] HCA 55; (1984) 165 CLR 622 at 638. There is no requirement in s 16(6) for the use of any approved form or precise form of words in making a request under s 16(6). The matter is therefore analogous to Comcare v Willems (1996) 43 ALD 253. In Willems the Full Court said, a p258: "To insist that a letter which fairly admitted of an interpretation in the particular circumstances, that it was an application for reconsideration, was not a 'request for reconsideration' would be to insist upon formality without regard to the provisions of s 72 and without regard to the nature of the SRC [Safety, Rehabilitation and Compensation] Act as beneficial legislation."

16. Mrs Howarth's solicitors, Wilson Potter Nicholson, foreshadowed another request under s 16(6) in their letter of 21 November 1995 (T236, pp545-550). They wrote: "Our client did not receive the Benefit Classification Certificate dated 2nd July, 1981 until this year. As a result she has not had an opportunity to request the Commissioner to revoke or vary the certificate as provided for in Note (b) on the certificate attached to the Committee's recommendation. Given the cut-off date for submissions is 23rd November, 1995, as stated in your letter, our client will not be in a position to obtain the material required to make such a submission prior to that date.

17. The material required in order to request the Commissioner to revoke or vary the certificate is: (a) A copy of the summary referred to in Note (b) on the certificate. (b) A copy of all documents used by the Commissioner in formulating the certificate. (c) Medical reports from the doctors involved in our client's treatment who would be asked to view the material relied upon by the Commissioner at the time the certificate was formulated. Items (a) and (b) will need to be supplied by you to enable us to seek reports from the treating doctors as per paragraph (c)." (emphasis added) In their letter of 16 January 1996 (T17, p56) the solicitors wrote: "(g) As we understand the many medical reports in existence there is no evidence that our client ever has been diagnosed as suffering from spondylitis. In our submission this should be deleted from the BCC. (h) The description 'disorganised pelvis' is not a diagnosis. We note that you have cited Re: Cass -v- Commissioner for Superannuation in support of the proposition that a condition specified in a BCC need not constitute an entirely accurate medical diagnosis of a condition. We submit that the situation here goes further than that. We do not know what a disorganised pelvis is and none of the doctors who wrote reports have elucidated that problem. We submit that the proper conclusion to be reached, particularly as the doctor seems to have had in mind some undefined connection between this and the spondylitis, is that it should be deleted from the BCC. For the reasons set out in this letter we submit that the BCC should be amended so as to delete: 'Post traumatic spondylitis of the lumbar thoracic and cervical regions with disorganised pelvis. Post traumatic arthritis L knee.' " (emphasis added)

18. Although s 16 does not refer to variation or amendment of a BCC, it does provide for a request to revoke a BCC and then issue in substitution for that BCC a new BCC specifying only one or some of the conditions specified in the first BCC. That is in substance the same as varying or amending the original BCC. Thus we consider that the requests made by Wilson Potter Nicholson in their letter of 16 January 1996 were to be understood as including a request under s 16(6) of the Act for revocation and issue in substitution of a new BCC, deleting the conditions set out in that letter.

19. In view of those requests by Mrs Howarth and by her solicitors we cannot understand why the delegate, on 23 January 1996, failed to consider Mrs Howarth's request under s 16(6) of the Act and, instead, refused to grant her an extension of time under s 154(2) of the Act in which to request the Commissioner to reconsider the decision to issue a BCC in respect of her. Mrs Howarth, in her letter of 25 June 1995, requested the Commissioner to revoke or vary the existing BCC, as well as to reconsider the application of the BCC to her invalidity retirement. Her solicitors, in their correspondence of 21 November 1995, referred to the s 16(6) procedure referred to in note (b) on the BCC, and then requested that certain conditions be deleted from the BCC in their letter of 16 January 1996. We asked Ms Munoz (see transcript pp74 and 83) to explain why the Commissioner had not acted on the s 16(6) requests but she did not seem able to respond to that question.

20. During the hearing it was suggested that perhaps s 16(6) in effect contains a time limit in that only an "eligible employee" may apply for revocation of a BCC under s 16(6). At that stage there was some question whether Mrs Howarth's request for reconsideration under s 154(2), or for revocation under s 16(6), was made before or after she ceased to be an "eligible employee". The definition of that term in s 3 of the Act includes: "'eligible employee' means: . . . (b) a person who is a permanent employee . . ." 21. Later on during the hearing (at transcript p78) Mr Keely and Ms Munoz advised the Tribunal that they had agreed that Mrs Howarth did not retire from her employment until 17 April 1996. That means that not only her letter of 25 June 1995 but also her solicitor's letters of 21 November 1995 and 16 January 1996 were sent to the respondent while Mrs Howarth was an eligible employee. So long as Mrs Howarth was an eligible employee she could make a request under s 16(6) and, in our view, the Commissioner has not yet responded to that request as he is obliged to do under s 16(8) of the Act.

22. It may perhaps be considered practical and reasonable by the respondent that the s 16(6) procedure be required to be used prior to the making of any decision under s 66(2) of the Act, but the sub-section does not contain any express limitation to that effect. We can see no reason why Mrs Howarth's letter of 25 June 1996 and her subsequent solicitor's letters were not treated as requests under s 16(6).

23. Until the Tribunal raised with the parties the possible use of s 16(6) of the Act, the matter had been considered by the respondent to be one where Mrs Howarth was seeking a reconsideration under s 154(2) of the Act of the decision of the Commissioner to issue a BCC in respect of her. We consider that to have been inappropriate in view of the fact that s 16(6) is a specific provision allowing an eligible employee to request that a BCC be revoked or that a new certificate be issued in substitution and Mrs Howarth and her solicitors had made two such requests, one on 25 June 1995 and one on 16 January 1996.

24. Section 154 is a general provision allowing review of any decisions made by the Commissioner or a delegate of the Commissioner under the Act. It appears to us that there is a repugnancy or inconsistency between s 16(6) which places no time limit on an eligible employee's right to request that a BCC be revoked or that a new BCC be issued in substitution specifying fewer conditions, and s 154(2) which requires a person affected by a reviewable decision to make a request for a reconsideration of a decision within thirty days after the day when the decision first came to the notice of the person.

25. The general rule of statutory construction in such cases is as explained by Dawson J in Downey v Trans Waste Pty Ltd (1991) 99 ALR 402 at p412 that the more specific provision must prevail. His Honour explained: "Where there is a repugnancy between the general provisions of a statute and provisions dealing with a particular subject matter, as a matter of general construction the latter must prevail. As Deane J pointed out in Refrigerated Express Lines (A/asia) Pty Ltd v Australian Meat and Live-stock Corporation (No 2) [1980] FCA 38; (1980) 44 FLR 455 at 469; [1980] FCA 38; 29 ALR 333 at 347: 'Repugnancy can be present in cases where there is no direct contradiction between the relevant legislative provisions. It is present where it appears, as a matter of construction, that special provisions were intended exhaustively to govern their particular subject matter and where general provisions, if held to be applicable to the particular subject matter, would constitute a departure from that intention by encroaching on that subject matter.' And in Anthony Hordern & Sons Ltd v Amalgamated Clothing and Allied Trades Union of Australia [1932] HCA 9; (1932) 47 CLR 1, Gavan Duffy CJ and Dixon J observed, at 7: 'When the legislature explicitly gives a power by a particular provision which prescribes the mode in which it shall be exercised and the conditions and restrictions which must be observed, it excludes the operation of general expressions in the same instrument which might otherwise have been relied upon for the same power.' Dixon J referred to the same principle in R v Wallis [1949] HCA 30; (1949) 78 CLR 529 when he said of the Commonwealth Conciliation and Arbitration Act 1904, at 550: 'If it confers a specific power with respect to a limited subject or specifies a manner of dealing with it or otherwise provides what the duty or authority of the arbitrator shall be, then upon ordinary principles of interpretation the provision in which that is done should be treated as the source of his authority over the matter, notwithstanding that otherwise the same or a wider power over the same matter might have been implied in or covered by the general authority given by s 38. This accords with the general principles of interpretation embodied in the maxim expressum facit cessare tacitum and if the proposition that an enactment in affirmative words appointing a course to be followed usually may be understood as importing a negative, namely, that the same matter is not to be done according to some other course."

26. We consider that the Commissioner was obliged to deal with Mrs Howarth's s 16(6) request as provided for in s 16(6), (7) and (8). However there is no reviewable decision made under s 16 of the Act before us. The delegates of the Commissioner who considered Mrs Howarth's request treated it as a request under s 154(2) of the Act and ignored the s 16(6) procedure. This Tribunal only has jurisdiction under s 154(6) of the Act to review reviewable decisions that have been confirmed or varied under s 154(4) of the Act. There is such a decision before us, being the decision made on 23 January 1996 and confirmed on 22 March 1996 not to grant Mrs Howarth a further period beyond the period of thirty days specified in s 154(2) of the Act. That is the only decision we can review in these proceedings.

27. Having regard to our views as to the operation of s 16 of the Act and as to the effect of Mrs Howarth's letter of 25 June 1996 and her solicitors' letter of 16 January 1996, it is not necessary for us to decide whether an extension of time should have been granted under s 154(2), if s 16(6) had not applied to the requests. However, we have concluded that the evidence as to when the decision to issue a BCC specifying certain conditions came to the notice of Mrs Howarth was so inadequate that she would not have required any extension of time even if her request was properly to be dealt with under s 154 of the Act. In view of the detailed consideration given by the delegates of the Commissioner to this issue in their decisions to refuse an extension of time, it seems appropriate for us to explain that conclusion.

28. A party seeking to rely on a statutory time limit must be able to prove that the other party is out of time. The evidence we consider fell short of establishing that fact. Ms Munoz's prime submission was that the decision to issue the BCC specifying certain conditions came to Mrs Howarth's notice as a result of the posting to her of a letter enclosing the BCC dated 2 July 1981 and addressed to Mrs Howarth at 32 Thomson Street, Belmont. Mr Keely contended that the evidence showed that the decision did not come to Mrs Howarth's notice until 17 June 1995.

29. Mrs Howarth joined Telecom on 17 April 1979 and attended the Commonwealth Medical Officer ("CMO") for her superannuation medical examination in August 1979. As is set out in the T documents, she had what may fairly be described as a significant medical history prior to joining Telecom. First, she had had a motor accident on 30 June 1969 in which she suffered head injuries, loss of consciousness, fractured pelvis, soft tissue injuries to her neck and back, a fractured big toe, numerous lacerations and cuts, shock, and some trauma to the left knee which subsequently led to a menisectomy. Mrs Howarth had a further motor car accident in December 1975 when she was hit by a car. Mrs Howarth had also been diagnosed as having an under functioning pituitary gland in early 1977.

30. Mrs Howarth said she gave that history to the CMO. She said there was nothing said to her by the CMO to suggest that there might be any problem with her superannuation. She said she heard nothing after the medical examination, "and I just assumed that I had passed my medical and there is no reference to superannuation at all".

31. Mrs Howarth said that in July 1981 when the letter advising her of the decision to issue a BCC was posted to her at 32 Thomson Road, Belmont, she no longer lived at that address, although her family were still there. She said she visited approximately fortnightly, usually over the weekend, until she left for twelve months overseas on a Rotary Scholarship. She said she was away from late July 1981 until August 1982.

32. Mrs Howarth said that she believed that she first saw a copy of the BCC when it was sent to her former solicitor, Mr Potter. As set out above, in his letter of 16 January 1996 (T17, p56) Mr Potter wrote at p57: "Our client did not sight the BCC until she saw the first page of the certificate which accompanied your letter to her dated 26th October, 1995. We received a copy of both pages of the certificate attached to your letter of 11th December, 1995."

33. Mr Keely asked Mrs Howarth whether there could be any doubt about the fact that she had not seen the BCC in 1981. She replied: "No, because if I had have - had have seen T6 - I mean first of all I would have been pretty amazed about what it said and secondly, I would have - I [am a] person who takes official mail fairly seriously and I would have read what it said and - and found out what I needed to do about it and what it meant"

34. Mrs Howarth said that she did not recall any official mail being sent to her by her family while she was overseas on the Rotary Scholarship between July 1981 and August 1982.

35. Mrs Howarth said that after receiving the copy BCC in October 1995 she had looked for any copy which might have been sent to her earlier and had not found a copy of the BCC but had found two information statements from ComSuper to which reference will be made later in these reasons. She said (in conflict with her evidence quoted in para 28 of these reasons) that she did not pay much attention to those statements.

36. In cross-examination Ms Munoz endeavoured to establish that Mrs Howarth had not, between April 1979 and July 1981, advised ComSuper or Telstra that she had moved from Thomson Street, Belmont, to Parkville. However there is nothing in the Act which indicates that if it was reasonable for the respondent to send the BCC to an address, it shall, for the purpose of s 154(2), be deemed "to have come to the notice" of the eligible employee in respect of whom it is issued.

37. While the letter dated 2 July 1981 advising Mrs Howarth of the decision to issue a BCC in respect of her may well have been posted to her at the Thomson Street address in July 1981, the evidence does not establish that Mrs Howarth received notice of the decision to issue a BCC in respect of her as a result of that letter being posted to her.

38. Mr Keely submitted that we should accept as true Mrs Howarth's sworn evidence that she did not have notice of the decision to issue a BCC until June 1995. He suggested that the evidence showed that, if Mrs Howarth had known of the existence of the BCC prior to June 1995, there was no reason why she would not have challenged it then. We accept that the evidence does show that Mrs Howarth did successfully challenge an Army medical classification that she was medically unfit after a car accident in November 1979. On the other hand, even if Mrs Howarth had known of the decision to issue a BCC which applied to her in July 1981, she may not have challenged it then because she knew that she had a number of medical problems resulting from her medical history prior to her joining Telecom and she may, in 1981, have thought that there was justification for the decision to issue the BCC. Further, she may, in view of her history of significant recovery from serious injuries and in view of her return to the workforce, have believed that she would not be retired for reasons associated with her prior health problems before she reached retiring age. As she wrote in her letter of 25 June 1994: "Had I been aware of the 'BCC' grounds prior to this I would have followed the matter up however equally I wouldn't have anticipated my health status would be what it is today!" (emphasis added)

39. In addition, as Mrs Howarth was about to leave Australia for one year, she may have felt that she had more pressing things to do.

40. There is evidence supporting Ms Munoz's contention that Mrs Howarth may be mistaken in saying she never received the BCC. She gave evidence that she was not aware of the fact that she had received the information statements from ComSuper until her solicitor told her that ComSuper claimed she would have been sent annual statements from June 1992. When she then looked at home she found she had two such certificates. That shows that Mrs Howarth, like "the generality of mankind", can overlook or forget the receipt of official documentation, even in a much shorter period than fifteen years. In Eagle Trust plc v SBC Securities Ltd [1992] 4 All ER 488 at p498, Vinelott J quoted the following observation of Megarry V-C in Re Montagu's Settlement Trusts [1987] Ch 264 at 284: "I suppose that there may be some remarkable beings for whom once known is never forgotten; but apart from them, the generality of mankind probably forgets far more than is remembered."

41. In circumstances such as this there is, as the Federal Court pointed out in McDonald v Director-General of Social Security [1984] FCA 57; (1983) 6 ALD 6, assistance to be derived from the terms of the relevant legislation. Woodward J said at p11: "If the AAT finds itself in a state of uncertainty after considering all the available material, unable to decide a question of fact either way on the balance of probabilities, it will be necessary for it to analyse carefully the decision it is reviewing. If, for example, it is a decision whether or not to cancel a pension in the light of changed circumstances, then it has failed to achieve the statutory requirement of reaching a state of mind that the pension should be cancelled. If, on the other hand, it is a decision, to be made in the light of fresh evidence, whether or not the pension should ever have been granted in the first place, then it has failed to be satisfied that the person ever was permanently incapacitated for work. . . . The present case falls within the former category - there was some evidence of improvement in the applicant's medical condition - and the Director-General purported to act under s 46(1) of the Act. Therefore a state of indecision by the AAT (if it had existed) should have been resolved in the applicant's favour."

42. Jenkinson J said at p21: "There is, however, in my opinion a dilemma in which either a court or an administrative authority determining rights or liabilities may find itself, for the resolution of which the same principles are applicable by each tribunal. Either tribunal may find itself unpersuaded either that a circumstance exists or that it does not exist. (The same may be said of a past or a future circumstance.) The court or the administrative authority will determine, by reference to the substantive law, whether it is the existence or the non-existence of the circumstance which is determinative of the question for decision."

43. Applying those principles to the decision we are reviewing, we must refer to the terms of s 154(2) of the Act. The factors which make us unable to find that the decision to issue a BCC did come to the notice of Mrs Howarth as a result of the posting to her of the letter of 3 July 1981 are: (a) Mrs Howarth's sworn evidence to that effect; (b) the fact that Mrs Howarth did not, prior to that date, seek review of the decision to issue a BCC that applied to her, even though it included the condition "disorganised pelvis". Mr Potter in his letter dated 21 November 1996 (T236, p546) said that his client, "a qualified nurse", was "mystified" by that diagnosis; (c) the fact that the letter advising of the issue of the BCC dated July 1981 was sent to Mrs Howarth's father's address where she was no longer living; (d) the fact that shortly after the date of the letter Mrs Howarth left Australia for a period of one year; (e) the possibility that the letter might not have come to Mrs Howarth's notice before her departure from Australia, if it arrived at her father's home shortly before that departure; and (f) the possibilities that the letter might have been lost while Mrs Howarth was overseas, or mislaid when sent to her in England, or overlooked when she returned from overseas a year later.

44. The evidence failed to satisfy us that the decision to issue a BCC in respect of her came to Mrs Howarth's notice either in July 1981 or at any later date as a consequence of the posting on or about 2 July 1981 of the letter (T7) addressed to her at Thomson Street, Belmont, advising her of the decision to issue the BCC.

45. Ms Munoz sought to rely on s 28A and s 29 of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901 as requiring the Tribunal to deem service of the BCC to have been effected on Mrs Howarth. Those provisions, so far as relevant, read as follows: "28A. (1) For the purposes of any Act that requires or permits a document to be served on a person, whether the expression 'serve', 'give' or 'send' or any other expression is used, then, unless the contrary intention appears, the document may be served: (a) on a natural person: (i) by delivering it to the person personally; or (ii) by leaving it at, or by sending it by pre-paid post to, the address of the place of residence or business of the person last known to the person serving the document; or (b) . . . (2) Nothing in subsection (1): (a) affects the operation of any other law of the Commonwealth, or any law of a State or Territory, that authorizes the service of a document otherwise than as provided in that subsection; or (b) affects the power of a court to authorize service of a document otherwise than as provided in that subsection. 29 (1) Where an Act authorizes or requires any document to be served by post, whether the expression 'serve' or the expression 'give' or 'send' or any other expression is used, then unless the contrary intention appears the service shall be deemed to be effected by properly addressing prepaying and posting the document as a letter, and unless the contrary is proved to have been effected at the time at which the letter would be delivered in the ordinary course of post. (2) . . ."

46. Ms Munoz submitted that the Tribunal should find that the document was served on Mrs Howarth by post, under s 29 at the time at which the letter would have been delivered to Thomson Road, Belmont, in the ordinary course of post. She pointed out that, as set out above in paragraph 6 of these reasons, in 1981 the Act specifically provided in s 16(12) that a copy of a BCC should be served on the person to whom the certificate related. However, even if that section were taken to have provided for service of the document on Mrs Howarth by post under s 28A of the Acts Interpretation Act, s 29, which deems service to have been effected at the time at which the letter would be delivered in the ordinary course of post, that would not establish that the letter "came to the notice of" Mrs Howarth. Spender J pointed out in Repatriation Commission v Gordon [1990] FCA 417; (1990) 100 ALR 255 at 263, that although the object of service is to bring the contents of documents served to the attention of the person served, one cannot confidently expect that aim to be achieved whenever a document is served. The requirement that a decision "comes to the notice of the person" is a different concept from service of a copy of a decision. Mrs Howarth's evidence was that the decision to issue the BCC specifying certain conditions did not "come to her notice" at the time when the letter of 2 July 1981 would have been delivered in the ordinary course of post, or at any other time.

47. Ms Munoz said that the only case of which she was aware, where the words "comes to the notice of" in s 154(2) of the Act had been applied was Re A L and Commissioner for Superannuation (1987) 12 ALD 308. Deputy President Thompson there said, at p310: "It is sufficient within the terms of s 154(2) that the employee know of the decision, and he or she need not receive formal or written notification. While there was no express statement to the applicant that he was accepted into the provident account, it was clearly implicit from the minute concerning his arrears. Therefore, the time commenced running against the applicant from 1967."

48. That passage supports the view that deemed service of a document advising of the decision would not satisfy the requirement of s 154(2) that a decision "come to the notice" of a person. Deputy President Thompson seemed to accept that one must have knowledge of the decision on the part of the person affected by it for the decision to have come to the notice of that person. In Re A L there was evidence that the applicant, when he received information that he was ineligible for the superannuation fund, sought advice from a clerk in the Personnel Section about appealing against the decision. Thus, it was clear the decision had come to his notice.

49. The decision of Re A L may seem at first sight to be inconsistent with a passage in the reasons of Megarry V-C in Re Montagu's Settlement Trusts [1987] Ch 264 at 277: "In the books and the authorities the word 'notice' is often used in place of the work 'knowledge', usually without any real explanation of its meaning. This seems to me to be a fertile source of confusion; for whatever meaning the layman may attach to these words, centuries of equity jurisprudence have attached a detailed and technical meaning to the term 'notice', without doing the same for 'knowledge'. The classification of 'notice' into actual notice, constructive notice and imputed notice has been developed in relation to the doctrine that the bona fide purchaser for value of a legal estate takes free from any equitable interests of which he has no notice."

50. However it appears to us that there is a distinction between "having notice" of a fact and that fact "coming to the notice of" a person. The use of the words "comes to the notice of the person" seems to indicate that this is not a case where constructive or imputed notice are relevant.

51. In this matter, unlike Re A L, there is no evidence that Mrs Howarth took any steps prior to June 1995 which showed that she was aware of the decision to issue a BCC in her case. Neither s 28A nor s 29 of the Acts Interpretation Act 1901, nor the decision in Re A L persuade us that the decision to issue the BCC came to the notice of Mrs Howarth as a result of the posting of the 2 July 1981 letter addressed to her at Thomson Street, Belmont.

52. There is, however, evidence from Mrs Howarth that she received June 1992 and June 1994 information statements from ComSuper. The statements (T8 and T9, pp24-27) are annual statements provided to contributors by ComSuper. Mrs Howarth said that when she had looked among her papers at home she had only found statements for 1992 and 1994. She believed she had not received any others. The statements contain details about the contributor to whom they are delivered, such as the period of contribution and amount of the contributor's equity in the fund and the components of that equity. The information statement as at 30 June 1992 (T8) also contains on p2 a section headed "Additional information to use in conjunction with Ready Reckoner". In that section there is an item reading "Benefit Classification Certificate - Yes". The information statement as at 30 June 1994 (T9, p26) contains a section with an almost identical heading on p1. It also includes the entry "Benefit Classification Certificate - Yes".

53. Mr Keely asked Mrs Howarth what she did with the statements when she received them. She replied: "Well from my recollection I just basically - I really didn't pay much attention to them, to be honest. I basically I think made a cursory look at the monetary value and - and just put it aside. Did you read the fine print, to the best of your recollection?--- No, I didn't. I didn't really have any need to, I didn't think."

54. Mrs Howarth said in evidence that in her later time with Telstra she was Occupational Health Care Manager, Human Resources. She had also spent some time seconded to Comcare helping them establish a preventative occupational health adviser position and also helping with the drafting of the occupational health and safety legislation which was to go before Parliament.

55. The evidence is that Mrs Howarth had been off work because of her health for significant periods from 1989 to 1992 and, by June 1994, she had been off work for two years because of ill health. Mrs Howarth wrote in her letter of 25 June 1995: "I subsequently had to cease work in June 1992 following suffering a spontaneous pelvic muscle wall prolapse for which I required corrective surgery. I have not worked since as the surgery was unsuccessful and I developed complications, . . ."

56. Mrs Howarth's evidence was that, from December 1993, she was aware that the CMO had recommended her for invalidity retirement, although she did not herself want to retire. We consider that, as a senior occupational health nurse, she must have known the significance of having a BCC. Bearing in mind that when the June certificate was prepared she was off work sick, and that by 1994 her invalidity retirement had been recommended, we find that when she received the June 1992 and 1994 information statements she would have been aware that it was important to her whether or not she had a BCC.

57. When the Tribunal put to Mrs Howarth that in those circumstances it was difficult to accept that she would not have looked at the documents to see whether there was a BCC applying to her, she replied: "Yes, I can understand how you can see that. I mean on reflection I can see that myself. But I honestly - all I can say is honestly that perhaps it was because I also wasn't really well. I mean all I know is that I just looked at - I didn't even recall that I had them until it was pointed out to me that they were there, and then I thought they were only statements about the dollars and I didn't know that the BCC boxes were even there. It wasn't something that was obvious to me until it was pointed out to me."

58. We consider the wording in Mrs Howarth's letter of 25 June 1995 (T14, p33) to be significant. She did not claim there that she was not aware of the existence of a BCC that applied to her until 17 June 1995. What she wrote was: "I write in request for a 'reconsideration' of the decision to apply my Benefit Classification Certificate (B.C.C.) to the grounds of my incapacity for my invalidity retirement, as outlined in a letter to me by Pam Kyle dated 7th June 1995 received 17th June 1995.

59. I have discussed this matter with Ms. Kyle who advised me to write to you if I wished to have the decision reconsidered.

60. In the letter of the 7th June I became aware for the first time of the ground determined for my invalidity retirement namely 'Degenerative Arthritis' and further the conditions specified in the 'B.C.C.' issued on 2nd July 1981 some two years after my pre employment medical for Telecom in 1979, namely 'Post traumatic spondylitis of the lumbar, thoracic and cervical regions, with disorganised pelvis and post traumatic arthritis left knee'." (emphasis added)

61. On the second page of her letter she repeated: "Had I been aware of the 'BCC' grounds prior to this I would have followed the matter up . . ." (emphasis added)

62. Nowhere in that letter did Mrs Howarth say that she did not know prior to 17 June 1995 that the Commissioner had issued a BCC in respect of her. The letter is consistent with her not having received the BCC in 1981, but having seen "Benefit Classification Certificate - Yes" on the information statements without following it up.

63. The fact that Mrs Howarth said she did not know that she had received the information statements until she looked amongst her material at home and found them there, indicates that it is possible that she could have read the reference to "Benefit Classification Certificate - Yes" when she received the statements and then have forgotten about it, when she forgot about the receipt of the statements.

64. Mr Keely submitted that before we could be satisfied that the decision to issue the BCC had come to Mrs Howarth's notice so as to start time running under s 154(2), if that section had any application to the matter, she would need to have had sufficient notice to allow her to make a proper assessment as to whether or not she was "dissatisfied" with the decision to issue the BCC. That would require that she have notice not only of the fact that there was a BCC, but also of the conditions specified in the BCC and of the fact that she could seek to have the BCC revoked, or could in some way request reconsideration or review of the decision to issue the BCC or of the conditions specified in the BCC. We consider there is considerable merit in that suggestion but because of our conclusions as to other issues, it is not necessary for us to express a final view in regard to that submission.

65. A major problem facing the respondent in seeking to rely on the receipt of the information statements was that Ms Munoz produced no evidence to establish when Mrs Howarth received those information statements. There was no signed record of receipt of the statement, nor was there any copy of a covering letter which might have been sent to Mrs Howarth with the statements. Ms Munoz attempted to repair that deficiency in the evidence by asking Mrs Howarth: "Mrs Howarth, you recall my questions about the information statements for 1992 and 1994 which you said you received. I just [want] to clarify this. I know that all these information statements were issued in late 1992 and in late 1994, so would you accept that you received a copy of this information statement in August of - sorry, October of 1992 and the one for 1994 in October of 1994?---I can't say. I mean, in October of 1992 I was actually in hospital and I possibly in October of 94 was, too. So I can't say. All right?---I mean I accept that they are there, but when they actually came and when I actually got them - - - Okay. But when you say they are there you are saying you in fact received them?---Yes." Mrs Howarth said that she was in hospital for most of the latter part of 1992. She added: "[D]id I get them in the October, well, I was in hospital for most of the latter half of 1992, and it's a bit like - I recently had seven and a half weeks in hospital and when I got home my neighbour presented me with a bag of mail, and it took me about three weeks to get through it. And you tend to sort of just focus on the things that really need attention like phone bills and electricity and gas and so on."

66. The Tribunal asked Ms Munoz whether there was not a procedure whereby beneficiaries signed for the receipt of their superannuation information statements. Ms Munoz replied: "There is a procedure adopted by the office of the respondent of making people sign, but unfortunately I inquired into it . . . and it looks like they have not kept records of these documents where they ask the members to sign evidencing receipt of the information statement."

67. The evidence was in such an unsatisfactory state that no finding could be made as to when Mrs Howarth received either of those statements. Thus they could not be relied on by the Commissioner as showing that the decision to issue a BCC came to the notice of Mrs Howarth at any particular time. Hence it was not established that Mrs Howarth would have needed an extension of time even if her requests were properly to be dealt with under s 154(2) of the Act.

68. Although the Commissioner does have power to grant or refuse extensions of time under s 154(2) of the Act, we have concluded that to refuse an extension of time in response to Mrs Howarth's request of 25 June 1995, or in response to her solicitor's request of 16 January 1996, was an inappropriate exercise of that power, as the correct or preferable decision in response to those requests would have been to deal with them under s 16 of the Act, under which no extension of time was required. We have considered what decision we should make in those circumstances.

69. In Re Brian Lawlor Automotive Pty Ltd and Collector of Customs (New South Wales) (1978) 1 ALD 167 at pp175-176 Brennan J considered the powers of the Tribunal. He said: "It is, I think, easier to divine the scheme of the Act if consideration is first given to the Tribunal's power under s 43(1), which reads:- 'For the purpose of reviewing a decision, the Tribunal may exercise all the powers and discretions that are conferred by any relevant enactment on the person who made the decision and shall make a decision in writing - (a) affirming the decision under review; (b) varying the decision under review; or (c) setting aside the decision under review and - (i) making a decision in substitution for the decision to set aside; or (ii) remitting the matter for reconsideration in accordance with any directions or recommendations of the Tribunal.' The Tribunal is not a primary administrator. It is not the original repository of powers and discretions under an enactment. When it makes an order under s 43(1) to take effect under the 'relevant enactment', the grant to the Tribunal of the original repository's powers and discretions makes its order effective under the enactment. . . . In the present case, the absence of power to support the Collector's decision to revoke the applicant's licence does not prevent the making of an order merely setting aside the Collector's decision, for an order in those terms is authorized by s 43(1)(c). That provision requires, however, that the Tribunal, in setting aside a decision, makes a decision 'in substitution for the decision so set aside' (sub-para (i)) or remits the matter for reconsideration: sub-para (ii). Sub-paragraph (i) assumes that there is some power to make a substituted decision vested in the Tribunal, and that power can be found only in the grant of the 'powers and discretions that are conferred by any relevant enactment on the person who made the decision'. In the present case, the Collector performs the normal administrative functions of his office pursuant to s 8 of the Customs Act, and it was entirely within his power to decide not to take any action in purported revocation of the licence. And that is a decision which the Tribunal is empowered to make to dispose of this matter if the Collector's original decision is a reviewable decision. The Tribunal may exercise its powers with the same flexibility as the administrator whose decision is under review. All that is necessary is that the administrator whose powers and discretions are conferred upon the Tribunal should perform his functions under a relevant enactment, for the Tribunal may then make the decision which that administrator ought to make if he were then substituting a fresh decision for the decision which is set aside."

70. It was entirely within the Commissioner's power to consider Mrs Howarth's request as a request, under s 16(6) of the Act, to revoke the BCC or to revoke it and substitute a new BCC. In fact we consider that was not only the correct or preferable course, but was one that the Commissioner was obliged to adopt under s 16(8) of the Act.

71. The decision under review will be set aside and the matter will be remitted to the Commissioner for reconsideration in accordance with the direction that the Commissioner deal with Mrs Howarth's request of 25 June 1995 and her solicitor's request of 16 January 1996 as provided for in s 16(7) and s 16(8) of the Act by deciding whether to: (a) revoke the BCC; (b) revoke the BCC and issue in substitution a new BCC; or (c) refuse the requests under s 16(6) of the Act.
