Robert Erwood Chalk v Commissioner of Superannuation [1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 19 Aar 450 (1994) 33 ALD 420 (4 May 1994)

FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

ROBERT ERWOOD CHALK v. COMMISSIONER FOR SUPERANNUATION

No. G161 of 1993

FED No. 246/94

Number of pages - 11

Superannuation - Administrative Law - Time

[1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 19 AAR 450

[1994] FCA 1063; (1994) 33 ALD 420

COURT

IN THE FEDERAL COURT OF AUSTRALIA

QUEENSLAND DISTRICT REGISTRY

GENERAL DIVISION

BLACK CJ(1), DAVIES(2) AND COOPER(3) JJ

CATCHWORDS

Superannuation - appeal from AAT - whether decision under s.157 of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) should be set aside for error of law - factors relevant to the s.157 discretion - effect of failure to advise contributing employees of right to elect - whether Commissioner for Superannuation and Board of Trustees in breach of fiduciary duties - circumstances in which time for election should be extended.

Administrative Law - ground of unreasonableness

Time - interpretation of beneficial legislation to extend time

Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth), ss.137(1), 138(1), 140(2)

Australian Broadcasting Tribunal v. Bond [1990] HCA 33; (1990) 170 CLR 321

Hospital Products Ltd v. United States Surgical Corporation [1984] HCA 64; (1984) 156 CLR 41

Fouche v. The Superannuation Fund Board [1952] HCA 1; (1952) 88 CLR 609

Superannuation Fund Investment Trust v. The Commissioner of Stamps (SA) [1979] HCA 34; (1979) 145 CLR 330

Hunter Valley Developments Pty Ltd v. Cohen [1984] FCA 176; (1984) 3 FCR 344

Outboard Marine Australia Pty Ltd v. Byrnes: Bauknecht (1974) 1 NSWLR 27

Comcare v. Bryan A'Hearn [1993] FCA 498; (1993) 119 ALR 85

Re State of Queensland and Australian National Parks and Wildlife Services [1986] AATA 224; (1986) 13 ALD 158

HEARING

BRISBANE, 3 December 1993

4:5:1994

Counsel for the Appellant: Mr. N.J. Thompson and Mr. H.L.

Alexander

Solicitor for the Appellant: Poteri Woods

Counsel for the Respondent: Mr. F.H. Callaway QC with Mr. P.J.

Hanks

Solicitor for the Respondent: Australian Government Solicitor

ORDER

THE COURT ORDERS THAT:

1. Leave to file an amended notice of appeal is granted.

2. The appeal be dismissed.

3. The applicant pay the respondent's costs.

NOTE: Settlement and entry of orders is dealt with in Order 36 of the Federal Court Rules.

DECISION

BLACK CJ I agree with Davies J, for the reasons he gives, that the applicant should have leave to file an amended notice of appeal, but that the appeal should be dismissed with costs.

DAVIES J This is an appeal from a decision of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal constituted by the Honourable Justice R.J. Bulley, Senior Member K.L. Beddoe and Member A.M. Brennan. The decision, given on 2 September 1993, affirmed a decision of a delegate of the respondent, the Commissioner for Superannuation, made on 22 November 1991 and confirmed on review on 15 October 1992.

2. The decision of the Tribunal affirmed the primary decision that the discretion conferred by s.157 of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) ("the Act") should not be exercised in favour of the applicant, Mr Robert Erwood Chalk.

3. Section 137 of the Act provided inter alia, in 1976:-

"(1) Where a person ceases to be an eligible employee and

is not entitled to pension under this Act, he may, not

later than 21 days after he ceases to be an eligible

employee, elect, by notice in writing to the

Commissioner, that this Division apply in relation to

him."

4. Section 138(1) of the Act provided inter alia, in 1976:-

"(1) Subject to this Division, where -

(a) a person makes an election under section 137; and

(b) within the period that is the prescribed period in relation

to him, he

(i) becomes employed in public employment; and

(ii) becomes a member of an eligible superannuation scheme

that is applicable in relation to persons employed in

that employment,

a transfer value in respect of the person is payable to the person

administering that scheme."

(2) Subsection (1) does not apply in relation to a person

unless the person administering the superannuation scheme

agrees to accept the transfer value and, under the rules of

the scheme, the first-mentioned person will become entitled

to retirement benefits under the scheme based upon the

transfer value."

5. Section 140(2) provided in 1976:-

"(2) Where -

(a) lump sum benefit has been paid under section 80 to a person

who has ceased to be an eligible employee; and

(b) after the lump sum benefit was paid, the person makes an

election under section 137,

the election does not have any effect unless an amount equal to

the lump sum benefit is paid to the Commissioner within 7 days

after the date of the election or within such further period as

the Commissioner in special circumstances allows."

6. Section 157 now provides:-

"Notwithstanding anything contained in this Act, where an

election under this Act is made by a person after the

expiration of the period allowed by or under this Act for

the making of the election, and the Commissioner is

satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is

desirable that the election should be recognized, the

Commissioner may direct that the election be treated as if

it has been made within the period allowed and the election

shall have effect accordingly."

Although there have been changes to these provisions over the years, the substance of their effect has not altered.

7. In 1957, Mr Chalk became an employee of the Postmaster General's Department, which later became Telecom. At that time, he also became a contributor to the Superannuation Fund established under the Superannuation Act 1922 (Cth), and his entitlements were transferred to the Scheme established under the Act. Mr Chalk continued in that employment for many years and in 1974 he was employed as a technician stationed at a small country depot located at Cooroy in Queensland.

8. In 1974, Mr Chalk's wife gave birth prematurely to a daughter who suffered heart problems. The daughter had a very difficult time and the mother's health also suffered. In 1975, Mr Chalk found it necessary to take leave from his work in order to look after his family. Mr Chalk took his wife and daughter to the Wamuran District where his sister, who could help, was resident. This was too far away from Cooroy to enable Mr Chalk to travel to and from his workplace. Mr Chalk took leave without pay and remained on leave until he resigned in August 1976. During this period he purchased a farm near Wamuran.

9. On 29 October 1975, a letter was sent to Mr Chalk, drawing his attention to the fact that his liability to continue superannuation contributions would continue during his absence and seeking his advice as to his intended method of payment of $371.75, the contributions which would be payable during the year from 28 August 1975 to 27 August 1976. When asked in cross-examination about this matter, Mr Chalk gave this evidence:-

"Probably I would have not contributed that $300 because

there was no point; I was leaving anyway. There would have

been no benefit for me to contribute that money."

10. On 26 July 1976, Mr Chalk resigned from his employment by a letter which read:-

"As discussed with you by telephone I wish to advise that I

cannot resume duty as a Telecom Technician in your division.

The medical conditions which have affected my family and which

necessitated my taking special leave, are still present.

For this reason it is still necessary for me to be available

when required.

I would like to tender my resignation and for it to be

effective from the date that I was due to resume.

Could any monies owing please be sent to the above address."

11. It is not known whether Mr Chalk subsequently filled in a formal application for a refund or whether, if he did so, he completed a form which provided for the alternatives of an application for a refund or an election for preservation of superannuation rights, or a form appropriate to a refund under the 1922 Act, which did not contain an election for preservation of rights. In any event, a payment of $1949.41 was made to Mr Chalk on 5 January 1977. He received a second cheque for $597 in December 1978. The first sum at least appears to have been used by Mr Chalk to pay off part of the bank loan obtained for the purchase of the Wamuran farm.

12. When he purchased the farm, Mr Chalk intended to derive his livelihood from it. He gave this evidence to the Tribunal:-

"Right. Now about this farm. What was the produce of your

farm?--At that stage, small crops. We were supplying salad

vegetables to Coles.

Right. And I would imagine that you were expecting to be

self-sufficient with the farming business - in the farming

business?---Moneywise?

Moneywise, yes---Oh, certainly."

However, Mr Chalk's expectations were not fulfilled. He gave this evidence:-

"Okay. Well, what happened as far as the farm was

concerned?---Well, we just - we couldn't make a go of it.

We couldn't make as much money as we'd hope to make.

So, what did you do?---I wasn't a good farmer, I suppose."

As a result, in 1978, Mr Chalk approached the principal officer at the Caboolture telephone exchange, which was closer to the farm than the Cooroy depot, and sought employment again with Telecom. Mr Chalk was taken back into employment at a lower grade, that of assistant technician, but he has since remained in employment with Telecom, or Telstra Corporation as it now is.

13. Mr Chalk thus rejoined Telecom in 1978 and, after he was again appointed as a permanent officer, he contributed once more to the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme. In 1990, he became aware of the right to make a late election to preserve benefits, subject to the favourable exercise of the s.157 discretion by the Commissioner. By letter dated 25 August 1990, Mr Chalk wrote to the Commissioner requesting permission to make a late election to defer benefits. I take that letter to be the exercise of the election subject to the granting, by the Commissioner, of permission to do so.

14. On 22 November 1991, a delegate of the Commissioner refused to exercise the s.157 discretion favourably to Mr Chalk. That decision was confirmed on internal review on 15 October 1992. The Administrative Appeals Tribunal affirmed that decision.

15. The case, as principally put to the Tribunal by counsel for Mr Chalk, was that Mr Chalk had not been advised of his right to elect that he should have been so advised, and that, if he had been, he would have made the election in 1976. It was not alleged that Mr Chalk had been misled by any document but that, in 1976, he and other officers of the Cooroy depot were not aware of the right conferred by s.137 of the Act. It was also put that, on Mr Chalk's return to employment in 1978, he was not informed of the s.157 discretion.

16. The Tribunal accepted Mr Chalk's evidence that he was not, in 1976, fully informed of his right to elect to leave his moneys in the Scheme. The Tribunal said:-

"We are satisfied, however, that there was a general

insufficiency of information promulgated to contributors by

the respondent and by the employing agency Telecom. One

need only refer to Telecom's own documents such as T27/62

and Exhibit 15 to establish a failure to fully explain

rights. The administration of resignations from Telecom in

the late 1970's seems to have proceeded on the incorrect

assumption that a resigning contributor to the

Superannuation Fund was only entitled to a refund of

contributions, plus interest, and we so find."

However, the Tribunal rejected Mr Chalk's evidence that, if he had been advised of his right, he would have elected to retain his funds in the Superannuation Scheme. The Tribunal said:-

"We are not satisfied that if the applicant had been given

adequate advice about preservation of benefits on the day he

resigned that he would have done any differently than accept

a refund of his interest in the Superannuation Scheme."

The Tribunal came to this conclusion primarily because Mr Chalk had in 1975 and 1976 shown no interest in continuing in the Scheme. He did not continue his contributions whilst he was on leave and, before retirement, he made no enquiry of any person who would have been in a position to give him informed advice as to his rights in relation to the Scheme. Moreover, he had a use for the Superannuation moneys for he had borrowed to purchase the farm.

17. No question of law arises out of this finding of fact by the Tribunal, which made its finding after hearing the evidence of Mr Chalk. Counsel for Mr Chalk has submitted that the Tribunal's conclusion was unreasonable in the Wednesbury sense, (Wednesbury Corporation v. Ministry of Housing and Local Government (1965) 1 WLR 261). However, neither the finding nor the decision of the Tribunal based upon the finding was perverse, irrational or unreasonable. The Tribunal was the decision-maker of fact. See Australian Broadcasting Tribunal v. Bond [1990] HCA 33; (1990) 170 CLR 321 at 355-360. As s.44(1) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 (Cth) limits an appeal to a question of law, the challenge to the decision on this ground must fail.

The Tribunal also held:-

"Nor can we be satisfied that with adequate advice he may

have sought to make a late election to preserve benefits

when he returned to employment with Telecom."

This conclusion was founded on the Tribunal's rejection of Mr Chalk's evidence. The Tribunal's view was that:-

"... we are satisfied that the applicant had no real

interest in the matter until later in his life when he

realised he had made a mistake (whether wittingly or

unwittingly) with the benefit of hindsight."

This finding of fact was also a matter for the decision of the Tribunal. It was not perverse or unreasonable, though another decision-maker may have come to a different view. The Tribunal's view received support from the fact that, by the time he returned to Telecom in 1978, Mr Chalk had already expended the moneys he had received from the Scheme.

18. Before the Tribunal and this Court, counsel for Mr Chalk submitted that the Commissioner for Superannuation and the Board of Trustees under the Act were in breach of fiduciary duties owed to Mr Chalk. In this Court, counsel referred to authorities such as Hospital Products Ltd v. United States Surgical Corporation [1984] HCA 64; (1984) 156 CLR 41; Fouche v. The Superannuation Fund Board [1952] HCA 1; (1952) 88 CLR 609 and Superannuation Fund Investment Trust v. The Commissioner of Stamps (SA) [1979] HCA 34; (1979) 145 CLR 330. The submissions led counsel for the Commissioner into a debate as to the function and duties of the Commissioner and of the Board of Trustees.

19. Those submissions were largely irrelevant to the task at hand. If a relevant provision confers a discretion, as does s.157 of the Act, the discretion should be exercised by reference to the words of the statute and the context within which the discretion is conferred. Section 157 uses simple words and the issues which arise under it are invariably issues of fact, the ultimate issue being whether it is desirable that a late election should be recognised. Reference to authorities such as the Hospital Products case, which enunciate principles of law and equity providing relief in circumstances entirely different from those within the contemplation of s.157, simply distract attention from the matters which ought to be considered under s.157. The operation of s.157 is not dependent upon the existence of fault on the part of officers of the employer or of the Scheme, although matters such as lack of advice may well be relevant.

20. Mr Chalk's case failed before the Tribunal because the Tribunal did not accept his evidence on what were presented as the vital points. Having regard to the way in which the case was presented to the Tribunal, I see no error of law in the Tribunal's decision or reasons for decision.

21. During the course of the hearing of the appeal, counsel for Mr Chalk sought leave to file an amended notice of appeal. I would grant leave to amend. Nevertheless, I would reject all the grounds of appeal which are stated therein. These grounds concentrate upon the lack of advice given by Telecom to Mr Chalk with regard to the provisions of the Superannuation Act as it applied to him. The Tribunal found as a fact that there was "a general insufficiency of information". But the Tribunal did not accept that Mr Chalk would have acted differently if he had been otherwise advised. That was because Mr Chalk used his refund to assist with the purchase and running of his farm. At the time, he had a need for the moneys and he expended them. As these were the Tribunal's findings of fact, all the grounds of appeal must fail.

22. I should, however, not close without observing that the classic exposition by Wilcox J in Hunter Valley Developments Ltd v. Cohen [1984] FCA 176; 3 FCR 344 of the principles on which time for the performance of an act may be extended, to which the Tribunal understandably referred, is sometimes misunderstood. Wilcox J referred to the general principles governing this area of jurisprudence. But his Honour did not limit the factors which might arise from the context in which a particular statutory discretion to extend time arises.

23. Most provisions which authorise an extension of time are instances of beneficial legislation which, accordingly, should be applied beneficially. With respect to such discretions in rules of court, Reynolds, Hutley and Bowen JJA said, in Outboard Marine Australia Pty Ltd v. Byrnes: Bauknecht (1974) 1 NSWLR 27 at 30:-

"We appreciate that the Rules of Court, particularly those

relating to time, should never be allowed to be an

instrument of tyranny. They do, however, have purposes, one

of which is that the parties may know where they stand and

regulate their affairs accordingly. It is also appreciated

that where genuine issues ought to be litigated, if such can

be done with fairness to all concerned, it is appropriate to

take a benign view of applications to extend time."

Those remarks indicate the importance of forming a view as to whether it is in the interests of justice that time be extended. A recent decision to like effect is that of Black CJ, Gray and Burchett JJ in Comcare v. Bryan A'Hearn [1993] FCA 498; (1993) 119 ALR 85 in which their Honours held, in the context of the lodgment out of time of an application to review a decision under the Commonwealth Employees Rehabilitation and Compensation Act 1988 (Cth), that it was not a precondition for success that an acceptable explanation for the delay be given. The general approach of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal accords with this. See, eg., Re State of Queensland and Australian National Parks and Wildlife Services [1986] AATA 224; (1986) 13 ALD 158. See also Ferocem Pty Ltd v. Commissioner of Patents (Burchett J, 24 March 1994, 135/94).

24. Many such provisions, including s.157, are not concerned with rights. If an act is not performed within the prescribed time, there is no right to have it performed. But there is a discretion to permit time to be extended should it be appropriate in the circumstances to do so. In the exercise of this discretion, it may be more important to consider the consequences of extending or refusing to extend time than to debate the reasons why the act was not done in time. Section 157 merely requires that the Commissioner "be satisfied that in all the circumstances of the case it is desirable that the election should be recognised". "All the circumstances" include all relevant circumstances existing at the time the discretion is to be exercised.

25. In the application of s.157, it may be of little consequence that the employee did not enquire as to his or her right to elect under s.137 or even that the employee positively decided not to elect. Section 157 confers a discretion which operates in either such case. Frequently, the discretion is exercised favourably because the facts which the employee took into account, when he or she decided not to elect, subsequently turned out differently. Oftentimes, the discretion is favourably exercised when the employee has encountered health problems of which he or she was unaware at the time of retirement. One of the objects of s.157 is to alleviate the consequences of such a change in circumstances. The circumstances need not be of a medical character. In Mr Chalk's case, the change in circumstance was that he resigned from Telecom intending to establish himself in an entirely new career as a farmer but was unsuccessful in doing so. And so he again took up employment with Telecom, after a lapse of two years.

26. The Superannuation Scheme operates in the context of Commonwealth employment. The rights which the Act confers are statutory rights. But the entitlement to participate in the Scheme and to receive the benefits of the Scheme do not merely have a statutory base. They form an incident of the employment. Thus, a decision-maker exercising the s.157 discretion could think it fair to the employer, to the employee, and to the Scheme that the benefit should be calculated by reference to the totality of the employee's service and to the totality of his contributions, including any sums which must be refunded.

27. When Mr Chalk resigned, he intended to leave his employment with Telecom permanently. This was not because of any dissatisfaction with his service, but because of the severe medical problems from which his daughter suffered and the stress and strain it placed on his wife. But Mr Chalk was disappointed with the rewards of farming. Matters did not turn out as he had hoped. And so he again took up employment with Telecom. In total, he has been an employee of Telecom from 1957 to the present, save for the two years from 1976 to 1978 when he tried his hand as a farmer.

28. Under the Act, the calculation of the benefit takes account of the employee's years of contributing service, final annual rate of salary and accumulated contributions. Section 55(1) provides:-

"Where a person ceases to be an eligible employee on or

after the attaining the age of 65 years, otherwise than by

reason of death, then, if the person does not make an

election under section 62 and subject to subsection (3) of

this section, the person is entitled to:

(a) standard age retirement pension in accordance with

subsection 56(1), (2) or (3);

(b) if he does not make an election under section 64, additional

age retirement pension in accordance with subsection 57(1);

and

(c) lump sum benefit (if any) in accordance with subsection

57(2)."

Section 56 specifies the standard retirement age and differentiates between contributory service of less than 20 years, of less than 30 years, of more than 30 years and of more than 40 years. Section 57 provides for an additional age retirement pension and a lump sum calculated by reference inter alia to accumulated contributions.

29. Mr Chalk has in fact served approximately 35 years going back to 1957. If his service between 1957 and 1976 were not recognised, the employer could have an undue gain. The late return of the contributions may not be a matter of concern. In his reasons for decision, the Commissioner's delegate, Mr R.C. Whithear, who has had long experience in these matters, explained:-

"If a late election is allowed, Mr Chalk will have had the

use of his refunded contributions (or, at least, the net

amount if any tax was paid on them) from 1976, and the

Superannuation Fund will have been deprived of their use;

and to that extent there is an element of advantage to him

and of disadvantage to the fund in such a course. On the

other hand, he will not have the advantage of receiving

interest on his contributions over the period during which

they were not in the fund."

Thus, because the moneys which would have to be repaid under s.138(11)(b) would not commence to accrue interest until repaid, it may be that no unfair advantage would result from a late election.

30. However, the additional matters I have been discussing were not examined before the Tribunal or in the appeal and neither the original nor the amended notice of appeal raised them. In these circumstances, they provide no ground for allowing the appeal.

31. For the reasons I have given, I would dismiss the appeal with costs.

COOPER J I have had an opportunity to read the draft reasons of Davies J. I agree with the orders he proposes for the reasons he has given. As a substantial part of the argument on the appeal concerned the relevance of any duty owed by the Commissioner for Superannuation as a trustee or a fiduciary to inform the appellant as a contributor to the Superannuation Scheme of available choices under the scheme and any alleged breach of that duty to the exercise of the discretion under section 157 of the Superannuation Act 1976 (Cth) ("the Act") it is appropriate to give some short additional reasons of my own.

2. The appellant sought to argue before the Tribunal and on appeal that the Commissioner for Superannuation was a trustee of the scheme which he administered under the Act. As trustee, it was submitted, the Commissioner was under a duty to ensure that the appellant as a contributor of the scheme was fully and correctly informed as to choices available to the appellant at the time he left, and later returned to, employment with Telecom. Alternatively, it was argued that if not a trustee, then the relationship between the Commissioner and the appellant was such as to give rise to a fiduciary duty on the part of the Commissioner to so inform the appellant. It was also submitted that in the circumstances which occurred the duty contended for was breached by the Commissioner. The existence of the duty and its breach, it was argued, were relevant legal and factual circumstances to be taken into account as part of the background matrix against which the discretion under section 157 of the Act was to be exercised. However, assuming without deciding, that there was such a duty and such a breach as contended for, what relevant use can be made of either or both circumstance by the Commissioner when called upon to exercise the discretion to direct that the appellant's election on 25 August, 1990 to defer benefits be treated as if made within time?

3. The existence of a duty and its breach by the Commissioner at an earlier point in time cannot give the decision-maker scope for the exercise of a discretion which would otherwise not be available under section 157 of the Act (Glass v. Defence Force Retirement and Death Benefits Authority [1992] FCA 558; (1992) 38 FCR 534 at 539). Nor can either circumstance estop the Commissioner exercising the discretion adversely to the appellant for proper reasons (Formosa v. Secretary, Department of Social Security [1988] FCA 291; (1988) 9 AAR 260 at 268 - 269). Similarly whether or not the appellant had actionable rights against the Commissioner at law or in equity for breach of the alleged duty does not fall for adjudication by the Commissioner as decision-maker or the Tribunal (Kedzierska v. Comcare (Unreported, Full Court, NG398 of 1993, Sydney, 27 September, 1993); nor are those rights, if any, relevant as such to the discretion to extend time under section 157 of the Act.

4. The conduct of the Commissioner is only relevant to the exercise of the discretion to treat the election as within time under section 157 if it helps to explain why the appellant did or did not make a timely decision in a particular way if the conduct of the appellant is itself a relevant consideration. In the instant case the Tribunal found that the appellant did not know of the choices open to him under the Act and that neither Telecom nor the Commissioner advised him of the available choices. The Tribunal correctly treated as a fact relevant to its deliberations that the appellant was ignorant of the choices available and that ignorance was partly explicable by the failure of Telecom and the Commissioner to correctly inform the appellant of the available choices. However, section 157 does not require that the Commissioner determine whether there exists attributable fault on the part of the appellant or any other person giving rise to the need to extend time; nor that the discretion be exercised on the basis of the existence or absence of attributable fault. Rather, as explained by Davies J in his reasons, the section merely requires that the Commissioner "be satisfied that in all the circumstances it is desirable that the election should be recognised". Those circumstances are all the relevant circumstances existing at the time the exercise of the discretion falls for consideration and will include circumstances of the type identified by Davies J as relevant to the statutory purpose for which the discretionary power in section 157 to treat an election as within time was enacted.

5. It was unnecessary for the Commissioner and the Tribunal as part of the exercise of the discretion under section 157 to decide whether the Commissioner was under a legal or equitable duty to provide to contributors to the scheme information as to choices available to them under it. The relevant fact was that the Commissioner did not provide the information. The legal questions of whether the Commissioner is a trustee of the statutory scheme, or stood in the position of a fiduciary to the appellant, and all that flows from a determination of those questions, only bear on the question whether there was a positive duty to inform; they do not bear directly on the exercise of the discretion. Accordingly, they are not questions of law the answer to which may lead to the decision of the Tribunal being set aside for error of law. These being the only legal questions which the appellant advanced on the appeal beyond contending that the Tribunal's decision was unreasonable in the Wednesbury sense (which argument is dealt with by Davies J), the appeal must fail.
