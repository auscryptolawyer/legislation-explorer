CARMEN MELITO and COMMISSIONER

FOR SUPERANNUATION

AT SYDNEY, MONDAY, 14 MARCH 1994



MR ALLEN: The applicant in this matter was appointed to the Commonwealth Public Service on 5 March 1984. On 11 July 1984 following her permanent appointment on probation she became an eligible employee for the purposes of the Superannuation Act 1976. On 24 September 1984 she was examined by a commonwealth medical officer for both medical fitness to be appointed as a permanent officer of the Commonwealth Public Service and for the purposes of whether she was eligible to contribute to the Commonwealth Superannuation Scheme.



As a result of that examination the commonwealth medical officer recommended that any decision as to permanent appointment and entry to the superannuation scheme be postponed for four months. One of the reasons for this recommendation being the applicant's condition of tenosynovitis. The applicant took three months leave without pay and was then re-examined on 23 April 1985. At that time an examining commonwealth medical officer determined she was not medically fit for appointment to the Commonwealth Public Service.



The applicant was advised of this recommendation and requested to provide further material prior to a decision being made as to her continued employment by the Commonwealth Public Service. Although additional medical reports were supplied, by notice dated 10 September 1985 the Commonwealth Public Service Board terminated the applicant employment with effect from 20 September 1985. Section 47 of the Public Service Act of the Commonwealth states inter alia, subject to sub section (2):



The appointment of a person to the service as an officer other than a secretary shall in the first instance be an appointment on probation. The relevant secretary may at any time after an officer is appointed to the service on probation and before the appointment is confirmed terminate the appointment.



At the time her employment was terminated the applicant was on leave following the certification by her local general practitioner that she was suffering from a repetitive strain injury. Following the termination of

her appointment the applicant applied to the respondent for payment superannuation on the grounds that she should be deemed to have retired on the ground of physical incapacity to perform her duties. That applicant was refused and on 8 July 1992 a reconsideration of that decision pursuant to section 154 of the Superannuation Act 1976 affirmed the original decision. By a letter received 28 July 1992 the applicant sought further review by the Administrative Appeals Tribunal.



The said application for review came on for hearing before this tribunal on 14 March 1994. The applicant who currently resides in the United Kingdom was represented by her sister, Miss G. Melito, and the respondent by Mr Whithear, the delegate who had made the reconsideration decision. At that hearing the following documents were taken in as exhibits, namely documents T1 to T50 being the documents prepared pursuant to section 37 of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975. Exhibit A is a report of the Southern Chiropractic Clinic dated 19 April 1993; B, report of Dr Yo dated 14 April 1993, and C, a bundle of documents being the applicant submission.



Having regard to the material before us, particularly what are pages 14 to 21 inclusive of exhibit C, we are satisfied that although the applicant's appointment was as a typist grade 1, immediately prior to her commencing the sick leave during which her employment was terminated the applicant had been redeployed to a position with the Receiver of Public Moneys as a receptionist, a position which, to use her own statement:



Involved very little hand movement.



In Commissioner for Superannuation v Trimnell, his Honour Mr Justice Olney said:



It is my opinion that the tribunal erred in addressing the question of what office or position the respondent held at the time of his resignation and not the duties he had been directed to perform at that time. Section 7(2) of the Superannuation Act does not refer to the duties of the eligible employee's position or office but rather refers to his duties which in my opinion must relate to his actual obligation to perform duties at the time his services are terminated.



See the unreported decision being DG 362 of 1992, Melbourne on 23 December 1993 at page 7. The applicant must therefore be regarded as performing the duties of a reception at the Receiver of Public Moneys although her actual appointment was that of a typist grade 1. Sub section (2) of section 7 of the Superannuation Act 1976 states:



melsupj 14.3.94 2



Where a person has before attaining his maximum retiring age ceased to be an eligible employee otherwise than by reason of death or by reason of his having been retired or his services having been otherwise terminated on the ground of physical or mental incapacity to perform his duties, but the commissioner is satisfied that at the time he ceased to be an eligible employee he was by reason of physical or mental incapacity unfit to perform his duties, the commission may direct that the person shall for the purposes of this act be deemed to have been retired on the ground of that physical or mental incapacity.



Although the tribunal accepts, as was conceded by the respondent, that the applicant did at the time of her termination suffer from a regional pain syndrome as to which see the report of Dr Richards at document T14, page 67. We are also satisfied that at the date of her termination she was capable for performing her duties she had been performing, namely that of a receptionist. It is no doubt mystifying to the applicant as to how she can be dismissed from her employment for failing to meet medical standards when the medical conditions which led to that failure have been work caused, yet failed to be entitled to superannuation.



The answer to that question is that medical fitness for permanent employment and incapacity to perform the duties allocated to one are two different matters. For the reasons set out by the tribunal in re Morajon v Commissioner for Superannuation, unreported tribunal decision number 7931, we find that the provisions of sub section (1) of section 7 of the Superannuation Act 1976 do not apply to an employee on probation whose services are terminated for failure to meet medical standards. As we have found that the applicant does not meet the criteria in sub section (2) of section 7 in that she was capable of performing the modified duties made available to her we do not need to go further and address what has rather objectionably been referred to as moral grounds.



A matter we do emphasis for the benefit of the applicant is that she has been badly advised in not seeking a further award of workers compensation. She was originally awarded workers compensation for her repetitive strain injury and if she remains incapacitated or partially incapacitated because of that condition, she should make a further claim. A letter, together with an accompanying medical certificate to any Comcare office and the address of such offices can be found in the telephone book, should be sufficient to re-activate that claim. For the reasons just expressed, however, the decision under review will be affirmed.
