Forsyth and Federal Privacy Commissioner and Anor [2004] AATA 175 (23 February 2004)

Last Updated: 24 February 2004

DIRECTIONS AND REASONS FOR DIRECTIONS [2004] AATA 175

ADMINISTRATIVE APPEALS TRIBUNAL )

) No A2003/214

GENERAL ADMINISTRATIVE DIVISION

)

Re

GUY VINCENT JOHN FORSYTH

Applicant

And

FEDERAL PRIVACY COMMISSIONER

Respondent

And ACT GOVERNMENT SOLICITOR

Party Joined

DIRECTIONS

Tribunal

Michael Sassella, Senior Member

Date 23 February 2004

Place Canberra

Decision

That the summons issued on 24 November 2003 addressed to the Federal Privacy Commissioner is discharged.

That the tribunal is to serve on the Federal Privacy Commissioner a notice in accordance with s 37(2) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 requiring the filing, within 28 days of the date of receipt of that notice, of copies of any correspondence and any legal advice held in the Federal Privacy Commissioner's Office relevant to the determination made under s 52(1)(b)(iii) of the Privacy Act 1988 other than any document already provided.

That the Federal Privacy Commissioner is to file with the tribunal any written submissions accompanying the additional documents provided pursuant to s 37(2) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 regarding the access that various parties should have to the additional material.

...........M Sassella (Sgd).................

Senior Member

CATCHWORDS

PRACTICE AND PROCEDURE - discharge of summons seeking additional material directed to decision-maker - decision-maker has obligation to provide to tribunal copies of all documents relevant to making of decision - decision-maker has obligation to provide privileged document to tribunal if document relevant and taken into account by decision-maker - directions issued requiring filing of additional material

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ss 35(2)(b), 37(1)(b), (2), (3)

Privacy Act 1988 s 52(1)(b)(iii), 61

Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1989 (ACT) s 37

Waterford v Commonwealth of Australia [1987] HCA 25; (1987) 163 CLR 54

Re Posetti and Comcare [2002] AATA 773

Re Thomas Cook Australia Pty Ltd and Collector of Customs (1994) 34 ALD 301

Re Fahey and Psychologists Board of the ACT (1996) 44 ALD 394

Re Takeshi Pty Ltd and Commissioner for ACT Revenue [1997] ACTAAT 169

REASONS FOR DIRECTIONS

23 February 2004

Michael Sassella, Senior Member

BACKGROUND

1. On 2 April 2003 the Federal Privacy Commissioner ("the respondent") determined in accordance with s 52(1)(b)(iii) of the Privacy Act 1988 ("the Privacy Act")[1] that Mr Guy Vincent John Forsyth ("the applicant") was entitled to $1,000 as compensation for loss of his privacy as a result of an unlawful disclosure of his name to a third person by an officer in the office of the ACT Government Solicitor ("the party joined"). Mr Forsyth was also allowed $1,643 for legal costs. Mr Forsyth applied to the Administrative Appeals Tribunal ("the tribunal") for a review of that decision. The tribunal has jurisdiction under s 61 of the Privacy Act but only as regards the compensation determined, not the finding of an interference with the privacy of an individual.

2. On 24 November 2003, at the ACT Government Solicitor's request, the tribunal issued a summons requiring from the Privacy Commissioner all files, correspondence, notes, statements and legal advices relating to the investigation of a breach of privacy complaint against the ACT Government Solicitor by Mr Forsyth.

3. On 8 December 2003 the Privacy Commissioner's legal representative wrote to the tribunal seeking to have the summons set aside by the tribunal, or to have the Privacy Commissioner excused from compliance. The reasons were:

* The summons is frivolous or vexatious.

* The summons serves no legitimate forensic purpose and amounts to a "fishing expedition".

4. The tribunal heard the parties on these matters on 13 February 2004.

RESPONDENT'S ARGUMENTS

5. Mr A Markus of the Australian Government Solicitor's Office repeated the arguments in [3] above. He said also that the party joined had used the summons procedure instead of doing as it should have done and apply to the tribunal for a notice under s 37(2) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act")[2] requiring the production of additional materials by the Privacy Commissioner. This would require the party joined to show that there had been a failure by the Privacy Commissioner in complying with s 37(1)(b) of the AAT Act.

6. Mr Markus suggested that the party joined misapprehends the function of the tribunal in reviewing a decision. This relates to the arguments suggesting that the finding of an interference with Mr Forsyth's privacy was unsafe for reasons in [10] below.

7. Mr Markus argued that the s 37 material provided in this matter met the statutory requirements. The suggestion that materials are missing from the s 37 documents was the merest assertion.

8. He argued that there is no evidence to suggest that the Privacy Commissioner's investigation was invalid.

9. Mr Markus argued that there is nothing in any material not included in the s 37 documents relating to Mr Forsyth's credibility (see [10] below) and that excluded material dealt with whether the third party was to be prosecuted (for a reason unconnected with his receipt of information identifying Mr Forsyth). Some material excluded from the s 37 documents related to administrative dealings between the Commissioner's office and Mr Forsyth. They did not canvas matters of substance.

PARTY JOINED ARGUMENTS

10. Mr D Mossop of counsel, representing the ACT Government Solicitor, alleged that the Privacy Commissioner had failed to investigate properly the factual basis for the privacy complaint by Mr Forsyth. He had not "used his statutory powers" and had not approached the third party who had been given Mr Forsyth's name. He argued that the material summonsed would assist regarding the adequacy of the investigation and the credit of Mr Forsyth.

11. It was suggested that the third item in Mr Forsyth's statement of facts and contentions saw him relying on the Privacy Commissioner's reasons. Thus, Mr Mossop said, it was only fair that the party joined should have access to all the material considered by the Privacy Commissioner.

12. A third point was that where, as here, the decision-maker, the Privacy Commissioner, is not actively involved in the proceedings it is for the active parties to identify the Privacy Commissioner's material of relevance.

13. Reference was made to page 124 of the s 37 documentation where, in an email message, mention is made of the Privacy Commissioner's legal counsel providing the Commissioner's investigator with advice in respect of the quantum to be awarded in the determination. Legal counsel's advice does not appear in the T documents.

FINDINGS ON MATERIAL QUESTIONS OF FACT WITH REFERENCE TO THE EVIDENCE AND OTHER MATERIAL IN SUPPORT OF THOSE FINDINGS

14. A particular issue that arises relates to the privileged status of certain of the material sought by the ACT Government Solicitor. A majority in the High Court of Australia case, Waterford v Commonwealth of Australia [1987] HCA 25; (1987) 163 CLR 54, 63-64, 74-75, held that legal advice procured for the purpose of advising a government agency as to the nature, extent and manner of exercise of its functions, powers or duties was subject to legal professional privilege. Mr Markus explained that the Commissioner had access to advice from the legal professionals working for the Human Rights and Equal Opportunity Commission under a service agreement. In my view the context of that relationship, and the independence of the providers of legal advice, connote a relationship attracting privilege.

15. The Privacy Commissioner could refuse to waive privilege over that advice and the summons would not operate to compel production.

16. Section 37(3) of the AAT Act, however, appears to require the provision of privileged documents to the tribunal. I found as much in Re Posetti and Comcare [2002] AATA 773. The tribunal can, however, be asked to direct that access to those documents not be accorded to the applicant or, as here, another party or parties (see s 35(2)(b) of the AAT Act).

17. Discussion of these matters shaded into a discussion of several decided cases. Mr Markus relied on the tribunal's decision in Re Thomas Cook Australia Pty Ltd and Collector of Customs (1994) 34 ALD 301. In that decision Senior Member J Handley refused to make a direction under s 37(2) of the AAT Act to require the production of a legal opinion by an in-house lawyer in relation to the reviewable decision. Senior Member Handley found that the legal advice was provided in the context of there being a solicitor/client relationship in operation. He found that the document was available to the primary decision-maker when he made his decision and influenced him in his decision-making. The learned senior member, however, refused to issue a notice under s 37(2) of the AAT Act because the legal opinion was not a document recording any fact relevant to the decision-maker in the making of his decision. It was said not to be in the same category as documents a party seeks to tender in evidence. Senior Member Handley said at page 305, "Nor could it be said that if [the writer] was called as a witness, his evidence, in so far as it related to the content of his opinion, would be relevant to the review by the tribunal of the decision being reviewed. The opinion has no weight, it would be inadmissible as irrelevant and could not be probative of any fact sought to be proved or established."

18. There is some force in this attitude. The tribunal does not engage in judicial review and so the factors surrounding the decision-making process resulting in the decision under review are either irrelevant or of only background relevance to a tribunal review. The tribunal's focus is on the decision to be reviewed. The tribunal's function is then to review that decision de novo and make up its own mind as to the correct or preferable decision. Nevertheless, there is quite powerful authority counter to that in the Thomas Cook case.

19. The ACT Administrative Appeals Tribunal ("the ACTAAT") considered these matters in Re Fahey and Psychologists Board of the ACT (1996) 44 ALD 394. This was a decision by President Curtis whose administrative law credentials are of the highest order by virtue of his role as a senior public servant responsible for implementation of much of the Commonwealth's administrative law reforms in the 1970s and 1980s. In that case an in-house lawyer had provided legal advice to the primary decision-maker on how a statutory power was to be interpreted. No copy of that legal advice was provided with the s 37 statement (the ACTAAT legislation, the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1989[3], closely replicating the AAT Act). The ACTAAT had been approached to determine whether the legal advice should have been provided in accordance with s 37.

20. President Curtis held that the advice was to be provided. In doing so he dismissed at page 396 an argument that the principle behind legal professional privilege would be compromised if those giving and receiving confidential legal advice did so in the knowledge that it was liable to disclosure under s 37. He said that that submission "entirely misunderst[ood]" the role and function of the ACTAAT. He continued:

"... This tribunal has been established as part of the administrative process of government in the territory. Although independent of government direction in its decision-making processes as it reviews primary decisions made by government agencies, it is nevertheless part of an administrative continuum. In reviewing primary decisions by officials, ministers and agencies, it stands, as the final decision-maker on merits (as distinct from the right of appeal to the Supreme Court on questions of law), at the end of that continuum. In reviewing primary decisions, its powers include all of the relevant powers and discretions of the primary decision-maker: s 44 of the AAT Act. It is not a stranger from which agency secrets must remain hidden; on the contrary, by virtue of s 37 of the AAT Act it must have access to all of the material before the primary decision-maker that is relevant to the review by the tribunal of the preliminary decision-maker.

"(7) Section 37 of the AAT Act is constructed on this basis. The requirement in the section to lodge documents with the tribunal applies notwithstanding any rule of law relating to privilege or the public interest in relation to the production of documents: s 37 (7). Even documents subject to the highest levels of government secrecy are not to be withheld from the tribunal if they are relevant to its task of reviewing a decision: s 35.

"(8) The interests of the primary decision-maker in having access to legal advice for the purposes of his or her functions therefore suffer no damage by reason of that advice being revealed to the tribunal. Whether, having been disclosed to the tribunal, the advice should then be made available to the applicant before the tribunal is a separate issue."

21. He also disposed of the approach adopted in the Thomas Cook case ([17] above) at pages 397-399:

"(15) It is, of course, a trite observation that the tribunal stands in the shoes of the primary decision-maker. Section 37 of the Act is designed to ensure the tribunal has all of the material before it that was before the primary decision-maker and which is relevant to the tribunal's review. It can readily be understood that not all of the material before the original decision-maker is necessarily relevant to the review. Where, as in many cases, the review is to take place on the basis of the factual situation at the date of the review, past circumstances, although relevant to the primary decision, may no longer be relevant. Even in those cases, however, inclusion of documents setting out those past circumstances may still be relevant as providing the historical content for the primary decision. Or there may be a case where the primary decision had two operative parts, and only one part is subject to appeal to the AAT. In that case, documents relevant to the primary decision in respect of the other part may have nothing to do with the part under review.

"(16) I do not agree with the proposition implicit in the Thomas Cook decision that only documents of a factual nature or dealing with matters admissible as evidence can be relevant to the tribunal review for the purposes of s 37. This misunderstands the function of the tribunal, as distinct from a court. The tribunal is not limited to a consideration of matters admissible in evidence in proceedings in a court. Just as the primary decision-maker is entitled to take note of advice given by experienced legal advisers of the government so, it seems to me, is the tribunal entitled to do so as standing in the shoes of the primary decision-maker.

"(17) Implicit in the arguments put by Mr Walker, as is also implicit in the decision in Thomas Cook, are the assumptions that the tribunal is composed of lawyers and that legal arguments will be presented by both sides. On those assumptions, the tribunal does not need to know on what view of the law the primary decision-maker acted. The assumptions involve a view of the tribunal as being in the position of a court deciding matters in an adversary system of administering justice, with the opposing parties being represented by lawyers.

"(18) The major premise, that the tribunal will have its own expertise, is not necessarily true in fact. Two senior members of this tribunal are not lawyers, yet as senior members they may be expected to preside over hearings. Neither does the AAT Act require that senior members be lawyers. The minor premise is that the parties will be legally represented. It would be a sad day for the working of this tribunal if only those who can afford legal representation can come before the tribunal to seek a remedy for administrative error. An applicant who knows on what view of the law the primary decision-maker acted will have an opportunity to get his or her own advice on whether that is a correct view of the law. Obtaining an opinion in this way, which the applicant can then put to the tribunal, will be much less costly then being represented before the tribunal for what might prove, especially in a case such as this, to be several days of hearing. The tribunal has a proper concern with enabling applicants to appear without legal representation and to present their cases. Access by the applicant to a document which enables this to happen is plainly relevant to the review of the primary decision by the tribunal.

"(19) The issue can also be looked at in this way. An important feature of the AAT Act is the power of the tribunal to direct the holding of a conference: s 33. One purpose of the conference procedure is to explore the possibility of reaching an agreed settlement. At that stage, the tribunal has usually not received submissions on issues of law; these are normally put at the hearing. For the tribunal to assess the prospects of settlement at a conference, it is relevant for the tribunal to understand the legal basis on which the primary decision-maker acted. There may, for example, be authority, known to the government's legal advisers and communicated to the primary decision-maker, that is conclusive of the issue. It cannot be expected that a non-legal member of the tribunal conducting a conference would be familiar with that authority.

"(20) I cannot emphasise too strongly that, in this tribunal, proceedings for the review of decisions are not adversary proceedings. They are not to be conducted as if they were adversary proceedings, according to the rules and presuppositions that apply in adversary proceedings. The primary decision-maker is compelled by s 37 of the Act to provide the tribunal with a statement giving the reasons for the decision. Where those reasons include the conclusion reached by the decision-maker of critical legal issues, then the statement must say so, and set out the conclusion. If it does not do so, it is not adequate. This is quite different from the position in ordinary litigation. A plaintiff who serves a demand for payment due under a contract is not required to give reasons for his or her view that payment is legally required under the contract. A government agency that baldly asserts that an applicant for a benefit has failed to satisfy the requirements of the statute where that opinion involves forming an opinion about the effect of the statute will have failed to comply with the statutory requirement to give reasons. Such a decision will be accorded more respect if it is shown to be based on responsible legal advice.

"(21) Hence the view that legal advice provided to a decision-maker on how a statutory power should be exercised is not relevant to review of the decision by the tribunal simply does not wash. It certainly does not do so in this case, where the documents already provided to the tribunal reveal on what matters the respondent board sought advice and the decision of the board discloses that it took a particular view of statutory provisions that are admittedly complex and not easy to understand."

22. President Curtis has revisited the issue in later cases, but the results have not always been to require production of legal advice. In Re Takeshi Pty Ltd and Commissioner for ACT Revenue [1997] ACTAAT 169 he refrained from requiring that legal advice be provided under s 37 for two reasons. The first was that the advice related to a version of the law that had altered by the time the decision-maker made the decision. It was thus irrelevant to the ultimate decision. The second was that, even if related to the correct provision, the decision-maker made up his own mind regarding the legal principles and explained his thought processes in his reasons for decision. President Curtis distinguished this case from Fahey which he still regarded as correctly decided.

23. I find President Curtis's interpretation of what is required of a decision-maker under s 37 of the ACT legislation convincing in relation to s 37 of the AAT Act. I therefore find that the correct approach to the question of the possible production of addition material from the files of the Privacy Commissioner to be:

(a) Any legal advice or other documentation that the decision-maker took into account in framing the determination regarding Mr Forsyth's complaint should be provided to the tribunal in accordance with s 37(2) of the AAT Act. I will issue a notice requiring that action.

(b) The Privacy Commissioner is invited to provide written submissions regarding the access that various parties should have to the material in (a).

(c) The summons issued on 24 November 2003 is to be discharged.

(d) The Privacy Commissioner will be permitted 28 days in which to comply with the notice issued in (a) in order to permit the work required in (b) to be done and in case he should wish to test my directions by appeal to the Federal Court.

DIRECTIONS

24. The following directions are issued:

(a) That the summons issued on 24 November 2003 addressed to the Federal Privacy Commissioner is discharged.

(b) That the tribunal is to serve on the Federal Privacy Commissioner a notice in accordance with s 37(2) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 requiring the filing, within 28 days of the date of receipt of that notice, of copies of any correspondence and any legal advice held in the Federal Privacy Commissioner's Office relevant to the determination made under s 52(1)(b)(iii) of the Privacy Act 1988 other than any document already provided.

(c) That the Federal Privacy Commissioner is to file with the tribunal any written submissions accompanying the additional documents provided pursuant to s 37(2) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 regarding the access that various parties should have to the additional material.

SCHEDULE

The notice to be sent to the Federal Privacy Commissioner is as follows:

The tribunal is of the opinion that documents additional to those provided to the tribunal already under s 37(1)(b) of the Administrative Appeals Tribunal Act 1975 ("the AAT Act") may be relevant to the review of the decision under review by the tribunal in application A2003/214.

The tribunal hereby notifies the Federal Privacy Commissioner that it is of that opinion.

The tribunal therefore requires the Federal Privacy Commissioner to lodge with the tribunal, within 28 days of the date of receipt of this notice, two copies of any document in his possession or under his control that answers the description in the next paragraph.

The documents to be provided are copies of any correspondence and any legal advice held in the Federal Privacy Commissioner's Office relevant to the determination made under s 52(1)(b)(iii) of the Privacy Act 1988 in relation to the complaint by Mr Guy Vincent John Forsyth, other than any document already provided to the tribunal.
